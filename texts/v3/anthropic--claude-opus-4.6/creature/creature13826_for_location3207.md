# Who Lives Here

---

Not a *who*. Or rather, not a single who. That's the first thing to understand: nothing here is sovereign. Nothing holds the deed. The garrison's last commanding officer left — decades ago, maybe longer — and no one replaced him, and yet the place has never been more densely governed, more fiercely contested, more thoroughly administered. It's just that the administration is distributed across several hundred organisms, none of whom have any concept of the whole, each of whom is utterly convinced that their particular few cubic centimeters of moss or stone or air constitute the most important territory in the world.

---

## The Wrens

Start with them, because they will insist on it.

There are more wrens here than the compound should support. A dozen, maybe more — it's hard to count because they refuse to hold still long enough to be distinguished from one another. They are tiny, brown, round, cocked-tailed, and absolutely *incandescent* with territorial fury at all times. Each one behaves as though it personally owns the entire garrison and every other wren is a trespasser who must be addressed at maximum volume immediately and without pause.

They live in the walls. Specifically, they live in the gaps where mortar has dissolved and moss has not yet fully filled the void — horizontal slots between dressed stones, cavities behind iron fittings, the space beneath a fallen lintel where the ferns arch over to create a dim green pocket just large enough for a nest of moss and feathers and spider silk. The nests are exquisite. The male builds several — five, six, sometimes more — and the female inspects them and selects one and lines it with whatever fine soft material she can find, and then she sits in it and the male stands on the nearest prominence and sings with a ferocity that would be terrifying if it came from anything larger than a thumb.

The song is the thing. It erupts from the fern layers and the wall-crevices and the collapsed interiors and it bounces off every remaining stone surface and it *multiplies*. A single wren can fill this compound with sound. A dozen of them create a wall of bright, sharp, rolling trills that dominate everything else. They sing to hold territory. They sing to attract mates. They sing, you suspect, because they cannot stop — because the energy inside them is so compressed and volatile that if they didn't release it as sound they would simply detonate. They weigh nine grams. They sing like they weigh ninety kilograms. The garrison's acoustics — the remaining walls acting as reflectors, the dense vegetation damping some frequencies and amplifying others — make the wrens sound even larger, even more numerous, even more outraged than they are.

Between songs, they hunt. They work the fern layers and the moss-mat and the crevices with a focused, flickering intensity, probing every crack and fold for spiders, insects, larvae. They move through the compound's vertical space — ground to canopy — with equal ease, and they know every passage, every gap, every route through the dense vegetation. They are the garrison's most intimate cartographers. No other creature here has as detailed a knowledge of its interior architecture. The wrens have mapped every stone, every root-junction, every fern-stem that can serve as a perch, and they navigate this map at speed, threading through spaces that seem far too small for anything with wings.

They are also, when the occasion demands, collectively brave to the point of stupidity. The stoat — we'll get to the stoat — has learned that the southern wall is a poor hunting ground not because prey is scarce but because the wrens make it intolerable. They mob with a coordinated, dive-bombing fury that is genuinely disorienting for a predator several times their size. They do not bluff. They will strike a stoat on the head.

They live here year-round. They do not migrate. In winter, when the mist becomes frost and the moss-mat crackles underfoot and the cistern grows a skin of ice, the wrens roost communally — five, six, seven birds crammed into a single wall-cavity, sharing warmth, their combined body heat keeping the space just above the temperature at which a nine-gram bird would die. They will emerge in the morning with their feathers fluffed and their metabolism screaming and they will sing — even in January, even in freezing mist, they will sing — because the territory must be held, the walls must be claimed, the compound is theirs, all of it, every stone, and they will defend it until they die, which for a wren is usually about two years, and then another wren will take their place and sing the same song from the same prominence and the garrison will not notice the change.

## The Blackbirds

Two pairs. Maybe three. It's hard to tell because the males are all glossy black with orange bills and the females are all brown and secretive, and they move through the compound at different scales than the wrens — longer flights, more ground-time, a more stately pace punctuated by sudden explosive sprints when they chase each other.

The males are the garrison's loudest single vocalists. A blackbird singing from the top of a broken wall-post at dawn produces a sound so rich, so fluent, so impossibly musical that it seems engineered rather than evolved. The phrases are long and complex and no two are alike and each one seems to be invented in the moment, a spontaneous composition delivered with absolute confidence from the highest available point. The song carries far beyond the compound — it rolls down the ridge and into the valleys on either side and it is the first thing you hear when you approach and the last thing you hear when you leave. It is the garrison's public announcement: *this place is occupied, this place is rich, this place is mine.*

Between songs, the males are thugs. They chase each other across the courtyard with a flat, direct, surprisingly fast flight, banking through the fern-filled window openings with centimeters to spare, and when they catch each other they fight — brief, fluttering, surprisingly violent confrontations that end with one bird retreating and the other immediately flying to the nearest high point to sing in triumph. The females, meanwhile, are doing the actual work: building nests deep in the densest vegetation — the hydrangea is a favorite, its interior a cathedral of stems and leaves that provides perfect cover — and then sitting on eggs with a patience that makes the males' histrionics look even more absurd.

They feed on the ground-mat, and their feeding is a distinctive, methodical destruction: they stand on the moss and flick debris aside with sharp lateral sweeps of the bill, exposing the wet substrate beneath, and then they strike — fast, precise, pulling a worm or a larva or a beetle from the mat with a jerk of the head. They are one of the few creatures here that actively *disturb* the ground layer. Where a blackbird has been feeding, you can see the evidence: small cleared patches where the moss has been raked aside, the underlying substrate briefly exposed before the sphagnum and creeping jenny close back over it within days. They are the garrison's small-scale tillers, turning the soil without knowing it, creating microhabitats for the seeds and spores that colonize every disturbance.

They roost in the hazel thicket on the western edge of the compound, and at dusk they give a series of sharp alarm calls — *tchink tchink tchink* — that serve as the garrison's evening roll-call, a sound that means the light is going and the night shift is about to begin.

## The Tits

Coal tits and blue tits, and they are everywhere the wrens are not, which means they are everywhere the wrens are too, because the wrens are everywhere. The coal tits are the smaller, more muted ones — olive-grey with a black cap and a white nape-patch — and they work the moss on the walls with an almost mechanical precision, hanging upside down, probing, extracting, moving on. They cache food. This is important. A coal tit in the garrison will find a seed or an insect and instead of eating it will fly to a specific crevice in a specific stone and push the item into it and cover it with a flake of moss or lichen and then fly away and remember — *remember*, in a brain the size of a pea — exactly where it put it, and return hours or days later to retrieve it. The garrison's walls are riddled with these tiny caches, hundreds of them, a distributed pantry hidden in the moss that only the coal tits can read.

The blue tits are bolder, louder, more visible — that sharp, high *tsee-tsee-tsee* call, the flash of blue and yellow in the green. They nest in the wall cavities too, competing with the wrens for the best slots, and the competition is real and sometimes fierce: a blue tit and a wren contesting a crevice is a surprisingly protracted and bitter negotiation conducted in alarm calls and threat displays and occasional physical confrontation. The blue tit is larger but the wren is more committed. The wren usually wins.

Both species are acrobatic feeders, and watching them work the moss-covered walls is like watching a vertical dance — they hang and twist and probe and flutter and move in quick hops along surfaces that seem to offer no purchase, and they find food in places that seem to contain nothing, because the walls are not inert surfaces but living ecosystems in themselves: the moss hosts mites, springtails, tiny beetles, spiders, moth larvae, all of them invisible to you but perfectly visible to a bird whose entire evolutionary history has been an arms race with exactly these prey items.

## The Redstarts

Just the one pair, and they are the garrison's aristocrats — not because they behave with any particular dignity (they don't) but because they are the most visually striking thing in the compound and they know it, or at least the male does. He is a small bird with a slate-grey back, a black face, a white forehead patch, and a tail and breast of such vivid, burning orange that when he perches on the eastern wall in a shaft of sunlight he looks like a small flame that has been placed there deliberately to complement the green. He flicks his tail constantly — a nervous, compulsive shivering motion that makes the orange flash and pulse — and this tail-flick is his signature, the thing that identifies him across the compound even when you can't see the rest of him. A flicker of orange in the ferns. The male redstart.

He sings from the wall — a short, sweet, slightly melancholy phrase that starts strong and trails off into a mumble, as though he begins each song with conviction and then loses interest halfway through. It is nothing like the blackbird's confident fluency or the wren's explosive declamation. It is tentative, incomplete, oddly charming. He sings it a dozen times and then flies off to catch a fly and then returns and sings it again.

The female is quieter, browner, easier to miss. She nests in a cavity in the eastern wall — a deep slot behind a rusted iron hinge — and she incubates with a stillness that makes her nearly invisible against the moss and stone. She is the one who chose this garrison. Redstarts are cavity nesters, and the compound's walls offer more cavities per linear meter than almost any natural cliff face. She assessed the options. She selected the site. The male followed, because that is how it works: the female chooses the real estate, and the male stands on top of it and sings.

They are summer visitors. They will leave in autumn, flying south to sub-Saharan Africa — a journey of thousands of miles for a bird that weighs fourteen grams — and the cavity in the eastern wall will stand empty through the winter, and the moss will creep a few millimeters further into it, and when the redstarts return in spring (if they return; the odds are roughly even) they will have to clear it out again, pulling moss and spider silk and the detritus of winter from their home before they can use it. They do this every year. They have done it, or their ancestors have, for as long as the wall has stood. The garrison was built by humans, but the redstarts have used it longer than the humans did.

## The Grey Wagtail

One bird, working the cistern overflow. It is slender, elegant, improbably long-tailed, and the underside of that tail and the belly are a vivid lemon yellow that flashes with every bob and dip as the bird works the watercress-choked channel. It moves along the overflow with a rapid, precise, almost mechanical gait — step, bob, dip, snatch, step — picking invertebrates from the water surface and the wet stones with a bill as fine as a needle.

It is not a sociable bird. It occupies the overflow channel and the cistern margin and it tolerates no other wagtails in this space. It calls — a sharp, thin *tzissik* — when it flies, and it flies in deep, bounding undulations, rising and falling as though the air were water and it were swimming through it. It is the garrison's water-margin specialist, and it knows the overflow channel the way the wrens know the walls: every stone, every clump of watercress, every eddy where insects collect.

It nests somewhere outside the compound — in a bank or a crevice on the ridge — but it feeds here exclusively, and its presence is a reliable indicator of the overflow channel's productivity. Where the wagtail works, the water is alive with larvae and small crustaceans and the constant micro-drift of organic material washing from the cistern. It is the garrison's most precise predator, operating at a scale of individual insects and individual water-drops, and watching it work is like watching a sewing machine: rhythmic, focused, hypnotically repetitive.

## The Robin

You might not see the robin for a while. It is not hiding, exactly — it is simply less frantic than the wrens, less conspicuous than the blackbirds, less acrobatic than the tits. It sits in the hazel and sings, and its song is a thin, silver, wistful thread that somehow cuts through all the other sound and reaches you with a clarity that the louder birds cannot match. It is not a loud song. It is a *penetrating* song — high, clear, with a quality of deliberate sadness that is entirely projected by the listener and not experienced by the bird, which is in fact singing to warn every other robin within earshot that it will fight them to the death if they enter this territory.

The robin is the garrison's most deceptive resident. It looks gentle — the round body, the red breast, the large dark eye. It is not gentle. It is violently territorial, and it will attack any robin that crosses the compound's invisible boundary with a ferocity that can result in serious injury or death. The red breast is not decorative; it is a threat display, a flag of war, and robins have been documented attacking stuffed specimens, red rags, and even red-painted stones with the same intensity they bring to real rivals.

It feeds on the ground, like the blackbirds, but with a different technique: where the blackbird rakes and flips, the robin watches and waits. It perches low — on a stone, a fallen branch, a fern-stem — and it watches the ground-mat with its head cocked to one side, and when it sees movement, it drops and strikes. It is a visual hunter in a world of auditory hunters, and its large eye is adapted for the low-light conditions under the canopy, where the green-gold haze makes movement harder to detect. It is also one of the few birds that will follow a larger animal — a blackbird, a human, even the stoat — to exploit the insects disturbed by their passage. If you walk through the garrison, the robin will follow you. Not for companionship. For the beetles you kick up.

It sings at dawn and dusk and also, sometimes, in the dark. A robin singing in the garrison at midnight, when the mist is down and the compound is lit only by whatever thin light the clouds let through — that is a sound that changes the place entirely, that makes the stones and the moss and the dark water of the cistern feel like something older and stranger than they are.

## The Woodpigeon

Somewhere in the canopy, unseen, it coos. Five notes, the same five notes, over and over: *hoo-HOOO-hoo, hoo-hoo*. It is the most monotonous sound in the garrison and also, somehow, the most essential — a background drone against which all the other sounds are measured, a baseline, a pulse. Without it, the compound would feel less complete. It is the garrison's clock, ticking in slow five-beat intervals, and it never stops during daylight hours.

The bird itself is large, plump, grey-pink, with a white neck patch and an air of supreme indifference to everything happening below it. It sits on a branch in the rowan canopy and coos and does not move. When it does move — startled by a gust, or finally motivated to feed — it crashes through the branches with a clatter of wings that is absurdly loud for a bird that was, until a second ago, the picture of placidity. It is the garrison's most startling resident: silent for hours, then suddenly and explosively present, and then silent again.

It feeds on the crabapples. It sits on a branch and reaches and plucks and swallows them whole — whole, the marble-sized fruit vanishing into its crop with a bob of the head — and it can eat an astonishing number before it stops. The crabapples pass through it and the seeds are deposited elsewhere on the ridge, which means the woodpigeon is the garrison's primary distributor of crabapple genetics, a role it performs with no awareness or intention, driven only by appetite and the five-note imperative of its own territorial song.

## The Frogs

Common frogs. At least half a dozen, probably more, because frogs are difficult to count — they appear and disappear, they look like wet stones, they sit motionless for long periods and then leap without warning and then sit motionless again.

They own the cistern and the overflow channel and the saturated margins where the moss-mat is wettest. They are brown-green, blotched, cold-skinned, with golden eyes that have horizontal pupils and an expression of profound and ancient patience. They sit on the moss at the cistern's edge and their throats pulse — a slow, rhythmic expansion and contraction that is their breathing made visible — and they watch the compound with a stillness that makes the birds' frantic activity look absurd.

In spring, the cistern becomes a mating arena. The males call — a low, resonant croaking that is nothing like the bright sounds of the birds, a sound from the basement of the frequency range, a vibration you feel as much as hear. They call and they grapple and they wrestle in the dark water, and the females lay their spawn in the submerged pondweed, and the gelatinous masses of eggs swell and develop and hatch into tadpoles that join the cistern's underwater ecosystem, feeding on algae and detritus in the tannic water, growing legs, absorbing tails, eventually climbing out onto the moss-mat as miniature frogs no larger than a fingernail.

These tiny frogs disperse into the compound — under stones, into the fern layers, along the overflow channel — and they are so small and so well-camouflaged that you will not see them unless you look very carefully, and even then you will mistake them for insects or moving shadows. They are the garrison's invisible army, a population of hundreds of tiny predators working the ground layer for springtails and mites and mosquito larvae.

The adults eat almost anything they can fit in their mouths: beetles, flies, slugs, worms, spiders. They hunt by waiting — that ancient patience again — and when prey comes within range they strike with a speed that the eye cannot follow, the sticky tongue deploying and retracting in a fraction of a second. They are ambush predators in a world of pursuers, and their strategy works because in a place this densely alive, you don't need to chase anything. You just sit still and the food walks past.

## The Newts

Palmate newts, in the cistern. You will not see them unless you lean over the dark water and wait and watch, and even then you may see only a flicker of movement under the duckweed, a small pale belly gliding beneath the surface, a ribbon-tail that undulates and then is gone.

They are smaller than the frogs, more secretive, more thoroughly aquatic during the breeding season. The males develop webbed hind feet and a filament at the tip of the tail and they court the females with a slow, deliberate underwater dance — a display of tail-waving and body-posturing that takes place in the submerged pondweed forests, invisible from above, a private ballet in brown water. The females lay their eggs individually, wrapping each one in a folded leaf of water plant, and the resulting larvae — tiny, gilled, predatory — hunt the cistern's microfauna: water fleas, mosquito larvae, the smallest of worms.

Outside the breeding season, the newts leave the water and become terrestrial, hiding under the collapsed stone slabs and in the moss-mat, moving at night, feeding on the same invertebrates the frogs eat but at an even smaller scale. They are the garrison's night shift, emerging after dark to work the ground layer when the birds are roosting and the frogs are sitting still and the only sounds are the wind and the water and the robin singing its midnight song.

If you lifted one of the flat stones in the old storehouse floor — carefully, slowly — you might find one. A small, smooth, brown creature with orange-spotted underparts, lying motionless on the damp earth, its skin glistening, its eyes dark and unreadable. It would not flee. It would wait. Newts are patient in a way that even frogs are not — a deeper, colder patience, the patience of something that breathes through its skin and measures time in the slow cycling of water through stone.

## The Salamanders

You heard them. Under the collapsed slabs, the wet shifting. Fire salamanders, if this is the right altitude and the right part of the world for them — and the garrison's cool, moist, stone-rich environment suggests that it might be. They are larger than the newts, glossy black with vivid yellow blotches, and they are strictly nocturnal and strictly terrestrial as adults, which means you will almost never see them. They exist here as sound and evidence: the wet sound of a body moving under stone, a glimpse of yellow in the beam of a torch, a track in the mud at the cistern's margin.

They give birth to live larvae in the cistern's water — or in the overflow channel, where the current is gentle and the watercress provides cover — and these larvae are aquatic, gilled, predatory, joining the newt larvae in the cistern's underwater food web. The adults feed at night on earthworms, slugs, beetles, anything slow and soft-bodied that they can find in the moss-mat and the crevices of the walls.

Their skin is toxic. The yellow markings are a warning: *do not eat me*. The stoat knows this. The blackbirds know this. The salamanders move through the garrison at night with a slow, deliberate confidence that no other small creature here can afford, because almost nothing will touch them. They are the garrison's untouchables, and their presence — even unseen — is part of the compound's character: a sense that the walls and stones contain things you cannot see, that the dark spaces under the slabs are occupied, that the garrison has a hidden population as dense and active as the visible one.

## The Stoat

Now. The stoat.

It lives in the southern wall. Specifically, it lives in a cavity deep in the wall's interior — a space created when several stones shifted and the mortar dissolved and the roots of a hazel tree grew in and then partially died back, leaving a network of channels and voids that connect to the outside through at least three openings, each one just wide enough for a sinuous brown body to flow through.

The stoat is the garrison's apex predator. Nothing here hunts the stoat. Everything here is hunted by it.

It is small — maybe twenty-five centimeters nose to tail-tip, the body slim and elongated, the legs short, the head flat and triangular with round ears and dark eyes that are, when you see them up close, startlingly beautiful and completely without mercy. The fur is brown above and cream below, the line between the two colors sharp and clean, and the tail has a black tip that persists even when the rest of the coat turns white in winter (which it may or may not do here, depending on the severity of the cold and the altitude and the individual genetics of this particular stoat).

It hunts everything. Mice, voles, shrews, young rats if there are rats — and there may be, in the walls — frogs, newts, birds' eggs, nestlings, insects if nothing better is available. It is fast, agile, fearless, and relentless. When it hunts a mouse or a vole in the wall-cavities, it pursues with a single-minded intensity that does not stop until the prey is caught: through tunnels, over stones, into the moss-mat and out again, a chase that can last minutes and cover the entire compound. When it catches, it kills with a bite to the back of the skull. It is efficient. It does not play with its food (weasels sometimes do; stoats rarely).

The wrens mob it because they have learned — through generations of experience coded into instinct — that a stoat near a wall-crevice means dead nestlings. The alarm call that goes up when the stoat appears is the garrison's most urgent sound: a rapid, harsh, penetrating *tek-tek-tek* that every bird in the compound recognizes and responds to. The tits freeze. The blackbirds fly up. The wrens attack. The robin, which was following you for beetles, pivots instantly and joins the mob. For a few seconds, the stoat is the center of a storm of small furious birds, and then it pours itself into a wall-gap and vanishes and the alarm calls continue for another minute and then gradually subside and the normal noise reasserts itself and the birds go back to feeding, but with a heightened vigilance that will persist for an hour.

The stoat does not care about the mobbing. It is annoyed by it — the diving wrens can actually strike hard enough to sting — but it is not deterred. It will return to the same wall, the same crevice, the same nest site, as many times as necessary. Its metabolism is ferocious — a stoat must eat roughly a third of its body weight every day — and the garrison is its larder. The walls are full of prey. The moss-mat is full of prey. The cistern margin is full of prey. The stoat knows this the way the wrens know the wall-crevices: intimately, completely, from a lifetime of hunting the same sixty meters of stone and moss and fern.

It may be female. If it is female and it is spring, there may be kits in the wall — four, five, six tiny blind furless creatures in a nest of fur and grass deep in the stone cavity, nursed by a mother who is simultaneously the most dedicated parent and the most efficient killer in the compound. She will hunt more when the kits are growing, and the garrison will feel the pressure: more alarm calls, more vigilance, fewer fledglings, fewer voles. The system absorbs it. There are always more wrens, more voles, more frogs. The stoat is part of the system, not a disruption of it.

In winter, the stoat is the garrison's only visible mammalian predator, and its tracks in the frost — small, paired prints, the hind feet landing in the front feet's marks — are the only sign that anything warm-blooded is still hunting the compound. Everything else is huddled in the walls or buried in the moss-mat or gone south. The stoat is still moving, still hunting, still pouring itself through the stone channels with that boneless, liquid, terrifyingly purposeful motion. It cannot afford to stop. Its body burns fuel too fast. A stoat that stops hunting in winter is a stoat that dies.

## The Voles and Mice

They are here. They must be here. The stoat proves it — a stoat does not establish a territory in a place without sufficient small mammal prey. And the evidence is everywhere if you know what to look for: the small excavation at the base of the northern wall, fresh earth on the moss, the scraping sound. Runs in the moss-mat — narrow trails, barely visible, where small feet have compressed the vegetation into paths. Tiny droppings on the stones. A gnawed hazelnut shell in the courtyard.

Bank voles, probably. Field voles, possibly. Wood mice, almost certainly — the garrison's walls and stone cavities are perfect wood mouse habitat, and the combination of shelter, water, and food (hazelnuts, crabapples, seeds, insects) makes this an ideal wood mouse territory.

You will not see them during the day. They are nocturnal, or at least crepuscular, and they spend the daylight hours in the walls and under the stone slabs, in nests of shredded moss and grass and leaves, sleeping in the small warm spaces that the garrison's architecture provides. At night they emerge and work the ground-mat and the base of the walls, feeding on seeds and nuts and fungi and insects, and they move with a quick, nervous, stop-start motion — run, freeze, listen, run — that reflects a life lived under constant threat. The stoat is above them in the wall. The owl — there may be an owl, we haven't heard one yet but the habitat is right — is above them in the canopy. Every moment outside the nest is a gamble.

The excavator at the northern wall is probably a bank vole extending a burrow system. Bank voles dig. They create networks of tunnels in the soil and moss-mat, with multiple entrances and exits and food-storage chambers and nesting chambers, and these networks can be extensive — meters long, threading under the old walls and between the stones, connecting the compound's interior to the surrounding ridge. The vole is engineering the garrison at a scale too small for you to see but large enough to matter: its tunnels aerate the soil, its caches distribute seeds, its droppings fertilize the moss.

The mice are different — more arboreal, more agile, more likely to climb the walls and the hazel branches and the hydrangea stems. A wood mouse can climb a vertical stone wall as easily as it runs across the ground, gripping the moss and the mortar-gaps with feet that are essentially tiny hands. At night, the garrison's walls are highways for mice: up, across, down, into a crevice, out again, along a branch, down a fern-stem, across the moss-mat, back into the wall. The traffic is constant and invisible, and the stoat follows the same routes, a predator on the same highway, and the mice know this and they move fast and they listen and they freeze at any sound that doesn't belong.

## The Shrews

Something very small and very fast just crossed the moss-mat at the base of the eastern wall. It was there and then it wasn't. It was brown, pointed, frantic. A shrew.

Common shrews, or pygmy shrews, or both — both are possible here, and both are so small and so fast and so relentlessly active that distinguishing them in the field is nearly impossible without catching one, which is nearly impossible because they do not stop.

A shrew's metabolism is even more extreme than a stoat's. A pygmy shrew — the smallest mammal in many parts of its range, weighing perhaps four grams — must eat every two to three hours or die. It cannot sleep for long. It cannot rest. It is a metabolic furnace that must be constantly fed, and it tears through the moss-mat and the leaf litter and the crevices of the walls with a manic, trembling intensity that makes the wrens look calm. It eats insects, spiders, worms, woodlice — anything it can overpower, and it can overpower things nearly its own size because it is venomous. The saliva of some shrew species contains compounds that can paralyze invertebrate prey. The shrew bites and the prey stops moving and the shrew eats, fast, constantly, desperately.

Shrews are also vocal in a way you might not expect. They echolocate — not with the precision of bats, but with a crude, ultrasonic chirping that helps them navigate the dark tunnels and crevices of the moss-mat. You cannot hear it. But the shrews hear each other, and they are violently territorial, confronting rivals with high-pitched squeaks and threat displays that sometimes escalate into physical combat. Two shrews fighting in the moss-mat sounds like nothing — a faint scuffling, a tiny squeak — but it is, for the participants, a battle for survival, because a shrew that loses its territory loses access to food and a shrew without food is dead within hours.

They are the garrison's invisible underlayer, its most desperate and most driven residents, living at a speed and an intensity that no other creature here can match. They do not experience the compound the way you do — as a place of green beauty and layered sound. They experience it as a landscape of urgent needs: food here, danger there, territory boundary, rival's scent, eat, move, eat, hide, eat. Their lives are measured in hours and meals. They are born in spring and most are dead by autumn. A shrew that survives to its second summer is ancient.

## The Invertebrates

Below the vertebrates, beneath the visible drama of birds and mammals and amphibians, the garrison supports an invertebrate population of such density and diversity that any attempt to catalog it must fail. But some residents deserve mention because they are not merely present — they are *constitutive*. They make the place what it is.

**The spiders.** Dozens of species. Web-builders in the fern layers — orb-weavers whose webs span the gaps between fronds and catch the light when the mist condenses on them, turning each web into a beaded net of silver. Sheet-web spiders in the moss-mat, their webs lying flat over the sphagnum like tiny trampolines, waiting for the footfall of a springtail or a mite. Crab spiders on the hydrangea blooms, sitting motionless on the white petals, their bodies color-matched, waiting for a hoverfly to land. Wolf spiders running across the stone surfaces, hunting by sight and speed, carrying their egg-sacs attached to their spinnerets. In every cavity, in every crevice, in every fold of moss and every curl of bark: spiders. They are the garrison's most numerous predators, and they kill more insects in a day than all the birds combined.

**The slugs and snails.** The moisture here is paradise for gastropods, and they are everywhere: on the walls, on the ferns, on the hydrangea, on the moss-mat, in the crevices, under the stones. The slug trails on the moss glisten in the morning light — silver tracks that record a night's wandering, a map of every surface the slug explored, every leaf it tasted, every stone it crossed. Snails with banded shells sit on the wall-tops in clusters, their shells beaded with moisture, their bodies retracted during the day and extended at night to feed on the algae and lichens that coat every stone surface. They are the garrison's slowest residents, moving at a pace that seems incompatible with life, but they are relentless: a snail that starts eating a patch of algae at dusk will still be eating it at dawn, and the patch will be measurably smaller.

**The earthworms.** In the moss-mat and the soil beneath it, earthworms work the substrate in numbers that beggar belief. Every square meter of the garrison's ground layer may contain dozens of worms, pulling dead plant material down into the soil, casting it up as nutrient-rich waste, creating the channels and voids through which water and air move through the substrate. They are the garrison's engineers, more important than any other single group to the compound's long-term ecological function. Without them, the moss-mat would become a compacted, waterlogged, anaerobic mass. With them, it is a living soil — aerated, drained, fertile, capable of supporting the entire plant community that makes the garrison what it is. The blackbirds know this. The blackbirds eat earthworms with the focused determination of creatures that understand, on some instinctive level, that this food source is the most reliable one the garrison offers: always present, always renewable, always accessible if you know how to look.

**The beetles.** The green tortoise beetle on its leaf is one of dozens of species. Ground beetles — black, fast, predatory — patrol the moss-mat at night, hunting the same prey as the shrews and the spiders. Click beetles lie on their backs on the stone surfaces and, when touched, jackknife their bodies with an audible *click* and flip themselves into the air. Weevils probe the plant stems with their elongated snouts. Rove beetles — tiny, narrow-bodied, fast — thread through the moss like shrews in miniature. Longhorn beetles emerge from the rotting wood of the nurse logs, their antennae longer than their bodies, their larval years spent tunneling through the dead heartwood of roofbeams that fell when the garrison was abandoned. The beetles are the garrison's recyclers, their larvae the primary agents of wood decomposition, turning the old timber into soil, completing the cycle that began when the trees were felled and shaped and fitted into the roof.

**The hoverflies.** Patient, precise, suspended in the air above the hydrangea blooms, their flight a miracle of aerodynamic control — they hang, they drift, they reverse direction without turning, they approach a flower with a delicacy that seems conscious and is merely mechanical. Their larvae, many of them, are aphid predators: slug-like, blind, voracious, crawling the plant stems and consuming aphids at a rate that keeps the garrison's plant life from being overwhelmed. The adults pollinate. They are, with the bees, the garrison's pollination service, and the hydrangea's extravagant display of blooms is both a result and a cause of their presence: the flowers attract the pollinators that ensure the next generation of flowers. The relationship is old, automatic, and mutually essential.

**The bees.** Solitary bees — mining bees, mason bees — nesting in the wall cavities, each female provisioning her own cells with pollen and nectar, laying her eggs, sealing the cells with mud or leaf-paste, and then dying. No colony, no queen, no workers: just individual mothers building individual nurseries in the garrison's walls, each one a tiny act of construction that mirrors the human construction it exploits. The bumblebees are different — colonial, social, nesting somewhere in the moss-mat or under a stone slab, their queen deep in the nest, her workers — the damp-furred ones you see on the thyme flowers — bringing pollen back in the baskets on their hind legs, feeding the larvae, maintaining the nest temperature, dying in autumn when the colony dissolves and only the new queens survive, burrowing into the moss-mat to hibernate through the winter.

**The springtails.** You cannot see them without a lens, but they are here in millions — tiny, six-legged, flealike creatures that live in the top layers of the moss-mat and the soil, feeding on fungal hyphae and decaying plant material, and they are, by sheer numbers, the garrison's most abundant animal residents. They are the base of the food web for the spiders, the beetles, the shrews, the newt larvae, the frog juveniles. They are so numerous that every footstep you take on the moss-mat displaces thousands of them, and they spring away — the forked appendage under their abdomen releasing and catapulting them into the air — in tiny invisible eruptions that you will never notice.

## The Fungi

Not animals. Not plants. The garrison's third kingdom, and perhaps its most important.

You can see some of them: a bracket fungus on the nurse log, its upper surface zoned in brown and cream, its underside a dense mat of pores. A cluster of small brown mushrooms — probably some species of *Mycena* — emerging from the moss on the northern wall, their caps translucent, their stems thread-thin, their fruiting bodies lasting only a few days before they dissolve into black slime. A patch of orange jelly fungus on a rotting roofbeam. A dusting of grey mold on a fallen crabapple.

But what you can see is the merest fraction of the fungal presence. Beneath the moss-mat, through the soil, around and into the roots of every plant in the garrison, a network of fungal hyphae extends — threads finer than spider silk, collectively more massive than all the plant roots combined, connecting the trees and the ferns and the moss into a chemical communication and nutrient-exchange network that functions as a single system. The mycorrhizal fungi wrap the root-tips of the hazel and the rowan and the birch and the willow and they trade: the tree provides sugar; the fungus provides phosphorus and nitrogen drawn from the soil. Neither can thrive without the other. The garrison's canopy — the hazel, the rowan, the crabapple — is as dependent on the fungal network as it is on rain and light.

The decomposer fungi — the bracket fungus, the *Mycena*, the molds — are doing the opposite work: breaking down the dead wood, the fallen leaves, the animal droppings, the bodies of the dead. They are the garrison's dissolution service, the agents that turn structure back into soil. Without them, the nurse logs would persist for centuries. With them, a fallen roofbeam becomes soil in decades, and that soil supports the ferns that grow from it, and those ferns shelter the wrens that nest in the walls, and the wrens eat the spiders that eat the springtails that eat the fungal hyphae, and the circle closes and opens and closes again.

## What Lives Here

Everything. Everything lives here.

That is not an evasion. It is the answer. The garrison is not a place with inhabitants — it is a place that *is* its inhabitants, a biological entity assembled from stone and water and hundreds of species that have no knowledge of each other's existence beyond the immediate and the predatory and the competitive. The wrens do not know the fungi. The fungi do not know the frogs. The frogs do not know the earthworms except as food. The earthworms do not know anything. And yet they are all here, all at once, all urgent, all pushing into whatever space remains, and the result is this: a small stone ruin on a wet ridge that has become one of the densest, most active, most alive places on the mountain.

The humans who built it created, without intending to, a perfect habitat. The walls break the wind and hold the heat and provide ten thousand cavities for nesting and hiding and caching. The cistern collects and holds water year-round. The drainage channel sustains a running-water ecosystem in a place where running water would otherwise not exist. The cleared interior space, now overgrown, created a light-gap that allowed the understory to flourish. The stone, as it weathered, provided mineral substrate for the mosses and lichens that became the foundation of everything else.

The humans are gone. Their departure was the garrison's ecological birth — the moment the walls stopped being maintained and the moss began its slow, patient, irreversible conquest. Everything that lives here now owes its presence to that departure, and to the original construction, and to the specific geology and hydrology of this saddle between two peaks, and to ten thousand years of ecological history that brought these species to this ridge and made them available to colonize this particular sixty-meter patch of stone when the opportunity arose.

And the opportunity arose because someone left. Someone walked out through that green-arched doorway — maybe carrying their belongings, maybe looking back — and they never returned, and the moss crept over the threshold, and the ferns unfurled in the courtyard, and the first wren found the first wall-crevice, and the first frog found the cistern, and the first stoat found the first mouse, and the compound began its long, dense, churning, singing, dripping, growing, dying, renewing existence as a place that is more alive than the person who left it ever knew a place could be.

No one lives here. Everyone lives here. The garrison belongs to the moss and the wrens and the stoat and the earthworms and the fungi and the springs that still feed the cistern from below, and it will belong to them until the stones finally dissolve and the ridge erodes and the saddle between the peaks fills with scree, which will be a very long time, and in the meantime it hums and chatters and drips and grows and the woodpigeon coos its five-note song and the mist comes in and the mist lifts and the hydrangea foams white against the green and the stoat pours itself through the wall and the wrens explode into fury and the frog blinks once and submerges and the moss grows another millimeter over the last dressed stone and the garrison becomes, incrementally, imperceptibly, and totally, itself.
