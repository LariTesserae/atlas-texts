# Who Lives in Kethara

---

They don't call themselves anything collectively. That's the first thing to understand — and it takes a while, because you arrive expecting a nation, a people, a name. What you find instead are neighborhoods that function like organisms, thousands of them layered through the structure, each one a few hundred people bound together by proximity, shared work, and the particular microclimate of the terraces and corridors they tend. They are Ketharans only to outsiders. Among themselves, they are the people of the Seventh Eastward Stair, or the Southface Fig Terraces, or the Deep Cistern Halls, or the Lower Loom District. Their identity is architectural. They belong to the part of the building they maintain, and it belongs to them, and the relationship between those two belongings has become so deeply reciprocal that neither can be described without the other.

---

## The Terrace Keepers

Start with the ones you see first: the people on the terraces. You catch glimpses of them as you approach from the lowlands — small figures moving among the green bands, distinguishable from the foliage only by the rhythm of their movement, the way a hand lifts to adjust a branch or a body bends to inspect the soil. They are terrace keepers, and their work is the most visible face of Kethara's metabolism, though calling it *work* misrepresents it slightly. It is more like a sustained conversation with living systems that have been in progress for longer than anyone can remember.

They tend to be lean. Not gaunt — no one in Kethara goes hungry in any but the worst years — but wiry with a specific kind of fitness that comes from climbing, bending, carrying, and balancing on narrow stone paths at significant heights. Their hands are extraordinary. Broad-palmed, thick-fingered, stained permanently at the nail beds with a greenish-brown pigment that is some combination of soil, chlorophyll, and the tannins of the plants they handle daily. A terrace keeper's hands can tell you what they grow just by the particular shade of the staining. The turmeric growers of the lower eastern terraces have a yellow tinge beneath the green. The oil-seed pressers of the middle levels carry a permanent slickness in the creases of their palms. The herbalists of the upper terraces have hands that smell — that *always* smell — of compounds so layered and complex that sitting near one of them in an enclosed space is like being inside a pharmacy.

They dress practically, in loose-woven tunics and wrapped trousers of Ketharan cotton dyed with terrace-grown indigo or left in the natural cream of the fiber. The fabric is sturdy but breathable, designed for the warm humid air of the planted faces. Most go barefoot on the terraces — their feet are as calloused and capable as their hands, the soles thickened to a leather-like toughness that grips wet stone and navigates root-tangled paths without hesitation. Some of the upper-terrace keepers wear a kind of soft boot with a flexible sole for the cooler altitudes, but they take them off indoors, and there is a slight air of affectation about the practice, as though wearing shoes is a minor vanity they permit themselves.

They are not farmers in any conventional sense. A lowland farmer contends with weather, with pests, with the fertility of a field inherited or bought. A terrace keeper contends with *architecture*. The soil they work is manufactured, held in place by engineered walls, fed by designed water systems, warmed by the thermal output of the structure beneath. Every planting decision is also an engineering decision. Put a deep-rooted tree too close to a retaining wall, and in twenty years the roots will begin to shift the masonry. Overwater a terrace, and the excess moisture seeps into the stone courses below, weakening mortar joints that bear the weight of everything above. A terrace keeper must think in three dimensions and in decades. They are gardeners, yes, but they are also structural engineers of a slow, biological kind, managing the tension between the green things that want to grow and the stone that holds them.

This gives them a particular cast of mind. They are patient in a way that can read as passivity to outsiders but is actually something more active — a constant, attentive watchfulness. A terrace keeper on her morning rounds moves slowly not because she is leisurely but because she is reading the terrace the way a physician reads a body, noting the color of leaves, the dampness of soil, the angle of a leaning trunk, the hairline crack in a retaining wall that was not there last season. She carries a small leather case of tools — a knife, a trowel, a probe for testing soil depth, a piece of chalk for marking stones that need a mason's attention — and she makes notes in a personal shorthand on a tablet of waxed wood that she wears at her hip. These tablets are everywhere in Kethara. Nearly everyone carries one.

The terrace keepers organize themselves into what might be called guilds but feel more like extended families that have incorporated strangers over many generations. A terrace district — a contiguous section of perhaps fifty to a hundred terraces on a single face of the structure — is tended by a group that shares meals, living quarters, childcare, and a body of accumulated knowledge about the specific conditions of their section. This knowledge is oral and practical, passed from elder keepers to apprentices in a process that takes years and consists mostly of being present — walking the terraces alongside someone who has been walking them for decades, absorbing the thousand small observations that add up to understanding.

But it would be wrong to suggest the terrace keepers are insular. They are the most outward-facing people in Kethara, literally: their terraces are the structure's skin, and they are the ones who stand in the open air, who watch the weather come in, who feel the wind shift. Many of them develop an almost meteorological sensitivity — they can predict rain by the quality of the light on a morning, or gauge the moisture content of an approaching fog bank by its color and speed. They are also the ones who notice when something approaches Kethara from outside: a trade caravan on the lowland road, a flight of unfamiliar birds, a change in the vegetation patterns of the surrounding valley that might indicate shifting water tables. They pass this information inward, down the stairs, through the corridors, into the structure's interior networks, where it is absorbed and acted upon or, just as often, noted and filed away for future consideration.

## The Millers and Their Machines

Go inside. Down the stairs, through the arched openings that pierce the structure's sunward faces, into the warm stone corridors where the light changes from golden to amber to the deep orange of oil lamps and furnace glow. The sound changes too — the biological noise of the terraces drops away, replaced by the industrial heartbeat of the mills: rhythmic, percussive, never stopping.

The millers are the internal counterpart to the terrace keepers, and the two groups exist in a relationship that is part symbiosis, part friendly rivalry, and part something that doesn't translate easily — a structural interdependence so old it has become almost invisible. The terraces grow things; the mills process them. Grain into flour, fiber into thread, oil-seeds into oil, stone into dressed block. Without the terraces, the mills have nothing to work. Without the mills, the terrace harvests are raw material with nowhere to go. The two systems are a single metabolism, and the people who tend each half are joined at a level deeper than politics or preference.

The millers are physically different from the terrace keepers, shaped by different work. Where the keepers are lean and sun-browned, the millers tend toward a denser build — thicker through the chest and shoulders, heavier in the arms. They work in the warm, poorly lit interior, and their skin is paler, though "pale" here means something relative: a warm olive or deep umber untouched by the intense sun of the terrace faces, with a faint sheen of perspiration that is simply the baseline condition of existing in the heated interior. Many of them are partially deaf, especially the older ones. The mills are loud. A lifetime of working beside trip hammers and grinding stones takes its toll, and the millers have developed a rich vocabulary of hand signals and body language that supplements spoken conversation. In the noisiest districts — the stone-grinding levels near the base, the textile mills of the upper-middle structure — communication is almost entirely gestural, and a conversation between two veteran millers is a beautiful thing to watch: rapid, precise, eloquent in the hands and face while the mouth stays shut against the noise and dust.

They are covered in the residue of their work. Flour millers have a permanent white dusting in their hair and eyebrows that they don't bother to wash out because it will be back within hours. Stone-grinders carry a fine mineral grit in every fold of their clothing and in the lines of their skin, giving them a slightly granular texture when you shake their hands. Oil-pressers gleam. Textile workers trail lint and fiber fragments. The ceramic workers of the southeastern kilns have hands stained red-brown from the clay, and the heat of the kilns has given their faces a ruddy, baked look that ages them prematurely in appearance but apparently not in fact — kiln workers are famously long-lived, for reasons no one has adequately explained. Perhaps it is the clay itself, which is supposed to have mineral properties. Perhaps it is the dry heat, which keeps certain ailments at bay. Perhaps it is simply that kiln workers tend to be unhurried, methodical people who do not waste energy on worry, and this temperament preserves them.

The millers' relationship to their machines is intimate and proprietary. A mill — any of the hundreds of mills in the structure — is not just a device; it is an inheritance, a personality, a thing with moods and preferences. The grain millers talk about their stones the way a horseman talks about a horse. *This* stone cuts fine but heats quickly. *That* one is slow but never clogs. The stone pair on the fourth level east was dressed by Grandmother Peret's master sixty years ago and has never been re-cut because the grinding surface it developed over decades of use produces a flour of incomparable texture. To re-dress it would be to lose something irreplaceable. The millers maintain their machines with an obsessive care that borders on devotion, and the tools they use for this maintenance — specialized wrenches, shims, gauges, calipers — are themselves heirlooms, made specifically for specific machines and passed down through lines of millers who may or may not be biologically related but are certainly family in every way that matters.

There is a hierarchy among the millers, but it is technical rather than political. The most respected are the millwrights — the people who understand not just how to operate a mill but how to build one, how to diagnose its failures, how to integrate a new machine into the existing network of shafts and water channels and gear trains that connect the mills to their power sources. A senior millwright is one of the most valued people in Kethara. They move freely through the structure in a way few others do, because their expertise is needed everywhere, and they carry a status that is visible in the deference they receive: people step aside for them in corridors, offer them the best seat at communal meals, ask their advice on matters far removed from mill engineering. This is not because millwrights are politically powerful — Kethara doesn't really have politics in the conventional sense — but because they embody a kind of knowledge that everyone depends on. The mills are the structure's organs. The millwrights keep the organs working.

## The Masons

If the terrace keepers tend the skin and the millers tend the organs, the masons tend the skeleton. They are the people who maintain and extend the structure itself — the stonework, the arches, the retaining walls, the load-bearing columns, the entire engineered geology that makes Kethara possible. And they are, in some ways, the strangest people here. The deepest ones. The ones whose sense of time has been most profoundly altered by the work they do.

A mason in Kethara does not build in the way a mason elsewhere builds. There are no blueprints, no master plans, no architect's vision being executed. The structure has grown beyond any single design. What the masons do instead is more like tending a living geology — they observe the behavior of stone under load, monitor the slow deformation of arches over decades, track the migration of stress through the massif as new weight is added above or old material compresses below. Their knowledge is empirical, accumulated over generations of observation, and it is encoded not in drawings or calculations but in physical intuition, in the ability to *feel* through a hand pressed flat against a wall whether the stone behind it is in compression or tension, whether the mortar is sound, whether the subtle vibration conducted through the masonry is the healthy pulse of the mills or the ominous signal of a structural shift somewhere below.

They work in teams of three to five, moving through the structure on irregular circuits that can take weeks to complete. A team might spend a day on the sunlit eastern face, inspecting the retaining walls where fig roots have been working their way into the mortar joints, then descend through interior stairs to a corridor seven levels down where a hairline crack has been reported by a water-clerk, then drop further still to the deep load-bearing levels where no daylight reaches and the stone is warm to the touch from compression and the residual heat of the furnaces. They carry their tools in leather rolls — chisels, hammers, plumb bobs, levels, a coarse-grained powder for testing mortar hardness, a collection of thin iron probes for exploring cracks. Some of the older masons carry a tuning fork, which they strike against the stone and listen to with their eyes closed, reading the harmonic response for information about the stone's internal condition. This practice is considered slightly eccentric by the younger generation, but no one denies it works.

The masons are often quiet people. The work selects for it. You spend days in the deep interior where speech is unnecessary, where communication happens through the tap of a hammer or a chalk mark on a wall, and you develop a comfort with silence that carries over into the rest of your life. At communal meals, the masons tend to sit together, eating steadily and without much conversation, their chalk-marked, dust-grayed clothing setting them apart from the flour-white millers and the green-stained terrace keepers. They are respected but slightly apart, as though the time they spend in the deep structure — the lightless levels where the oldest masonry has fused into something barely distinguishable from natural rock — has given them a perspective that doesn't quite align with the rhythms of everyday life above.

There is a thing the masons know that they rarely discuss with outsiders. It comes from working in the deepest levels, the substructure vaults where the weighted survey lines were lowered and never found bottom. Down there, in the darkness below the furnaces and the deep cisterns and the oldest mill rooms, the masonry changes. The chisel marks become less regular. The blocks are shaped by different hands — hands that worked to different proportions, different aesthetic sensibilities, different structural logics. The masons descend through strata of building tradition the way a geologist descends through strata of rock, and what they find at the lowest accessible levels is work so old and so different from contemporary practice that it raises questions no one in Kethara is entirely comfortable answering. Who built the lowest levels? How long ago? What were they for? The masons develop their own private theories, share them among themselves in the deep corridors, and mostly keep them to the guild. It is not secrecy exactly. It is more like discretion — the sense that some knowledge is structural, load-bearing, and should not be disturbed without understanding what it supports.

## The Water-Clerks

The water-clerks are the nervous system. They sit at desks in small stone offices distributed through the structure at irregular intervals, each one responsible for a section of the water network: the channels, cisterns, condensation surfaces, fog-catch arrays, and irrigation schedules that keep the terraces alive and the mills turning. Their work is administrative and obsessive, and they are among the most meticulous people in a structure full of meticulous people.

A water-clerk's office is a small, cool room, usually situated near a junction in the channel system where the sound of running water is constant. The desk is piled with ledger books — enormous volumes bound in oiled leather, their pages dense with columns of numbers recording water levels, flow rates, consumption figures, and maintenance notes going back decades. Some offices contain ledgers from centuries ago, their pages fragile and their ink fading, kept not as active records but as reference — because the water system is so old and so complex that understanding its current behavior sometimes requires knowing what it was doing two hundred years ago.

Water-clerks tend to be precise, somewhat fussy people who express deep emotion through the careful management of resources. They are the ones who ration irrigation during the dry weeks, and they take no pleasure in it — each decision to reduce flow to a terrace is a small anguish, carefully noted in the ledger along with the projected impact on crop yield and the expected date of relief. They are also the ones who open the sluice gates when the rain comes, feeding the cisterns and the mill-races, and there is a visible satisfaction in this — a loosening of the shoulders, a softening around the eyes — that tells you these are people who experience generosity as a form of joy and scarcity as a form of pain.

They communicate with each other through the water system itself. A clerk upstream can signal a clerk downstream by briefly interrupting the flow in a specific channel — a kind of hydraulic semaphore, with different interruption patterns encoding different messages. The system is slow (a signal might take minutes to propagate through the channel network) but reliable and difficult to intercept or misinterpret. The water-clerks have been signaling this way for generations, and their code has accumulated complexity over time, so that a modern clerk can transmit surprisingly nuanced messages through the water: not just "flow reduced" or "cistern full" but "the third terrace on the eastern face needs supplemental irrigation next week" or "the millrace on level twelve is losing pressure, possibly due to a blockage at junction seven."

They are, as a group, the most literate people in Kethara. Not because others can't read — most Ketharans have basic literacy — but because the water-clerks' work requires a sustained, intimate relationship with written records that most other occupations don't demand. This makes them the default archivists and historians of the structure, even though they would not describe themselves that way. They simply keep records. It's just that the records they keep, taken together over centuries, constitute the closest thing Kethara has to a written history.

## The Children

They are everywhere, and they are loud, and they are the most Ketharan thing about Kethara. The children of the structure grow up in a three-dimensional world — not the flat horizontal plane of a field or a street but the vertical, layered, labyrinthine space of the massif itself. They learn to climb before they learn to walk steadily. They navigate the interior staircases with a casual agility that makes visitors grip the walls in terror. A five-year-old will scramble up a retaining wall that would give a grown outsider pause, will traverse a narrow terrace edge with the fruit trees dropping away beneath, will duck through a low passage into an interior corridor and emerge three levels down and a quarter-kilometer away, having followed a route that exists in no map but is perfectly legible to every child in that district.

They are educated, but not in schools. There are no schools in Kethara, or rather, the entire structure is a school. Children attach themselves to adults — their parents, or other adults in their neighborhood group, or sometimes adults in entirely different districts whom they've encountered during their wanderings and found interesting — and they learn by being present. A child spending a season with a terrace keeper learns the names of plants, the feel of healthy soil, the way to read the color of a leaf for signs of water stress. A child spending a season with a millwright learns to listen to machinery, to feel vibration through stone, to understand the relationship between water pressure and grinding speed. They rotate through these attachments casually, following curiosity, and by the time they reach adolescence they have a broad, practical familiarity with most of the structure's systems.

What they do not have, and what is the most striking absence to an outside observer, is any strong sense of Kethara as *unusual*. To a child born here, the building-mountain is the world. The lowlands are a theoretical place where trade goods come from. The idea that people might live on flat ground, in structures that don't extend beneath them for unknown depths, in communities that don't wrap around them in all three dimensions — this is an interesting concept, the way the idea of living underwater is an interesting concept. Possible, maybe. But why would you?

They play games that reflect their environment. Hide-and-seek in Kethara is a three-dimensional sport that can last hours and involve vertical chases through stairwells, crawls through ventilation ducts, and ambushes from terrace overhangs. Racing games take advantage of the structure's interior slides — smooth stone chutes originally designed for moving grain or rubble between levels, now also serving as the fastest (and least parent-approved) way to descend through the structure. There is a ball game played on the flatter terraces of the middle levels that involves bouncing a dense rubber ball off the retaining walls at angles calculated to send it to a teammate on the terrace above or below — a game that requires spatial reasoning in three dimensions and that adults also play, competitively and with considerable passion.

The children's relationship with the structure's fauna is particularly intimate. Every child has, at some point, attempted to keep a gecko as a pet, built a small enclosure for a beetle, tried to hand-feed one of the parakeets (the parakeets do not cooperate), or sat motionless by a water channel trying to catch a glimpse of the jewel-green tree frogs. The older children develop more sophisticated relationships with the animals — they assist the beekeepers, help manage the draft-animal stables, know which lizard species are harmless and which of the larger ones should be given space. A child of ten can usually identify every bird species on their section of the structure by call alone, a skill that would impress an ornithologist and that the child regards as roughly as notable as knowing how to breathe.

## The Old Ones of the Deep Levels

Go down. Past the working mills, past the furnace districts, past the deep cisterns where the water-clerks keep their oldest ledgers, down into the levels that are used for storage, and then below those into levels that are used for nothing at all. This is where you find the old ones.

They are not a separate people. They are Ketharans who have, in the later decades of their lives, gradually migrated downward through the structure, drawn by something they describe variously as curiosity, restlessness, a sense of unfinished business, or simply the desire for quiet. The deep levels are quiet. The mill vibrations attenuate with depth, the sounds of birds and children and commerce fade, and what remains is the drip of condensation, the faint groan of compressed stone, and the enormous, patient silence of the structure bearing its own weight.

The old ones live simply down there. They maintain small rooms — cells, almost — furnished with a sleeping mat, a lamp, a water vessel, and whatever personal tools or artifacts they've carried with them from the upper levels. They eat food brought down by younger relatives or by a network of runners who serve the deep levels as a kind of informal supply chain. Some of them continue to work, in their way: an old mason examining the oldest stonework, running her fingers along chisel marks cut by hands dead for a thousand years, building a mental model of the lower structure that she will never complete. An old miller tending a small hand-mill in his cell, grinding grain more out of habit than necessity, the sound of stone on stone a comfort. An old terrace keeper who has brought soil samples down from every level and arranged them in ceramic jars on a shelf, studying the manufactured earths of Kethara as a naturalist might study the strata of a geological formation.

But many of the old ones are simply present. They sit. They listen to the stone. They develop, or claim to develop, a sensitivity to the structure's deeper movements — the glacial compression of the lowest levels, the slow tectonic adjustments as the massif settles and shifts on its own mass. They speak about this in language that hovers between the technical and the mystical, and it is hard, listening to them, to tell where observation ends and metaphor begins. "The building is breathing," an old mason once said to a visitor, "but each breath takes eleven years." Was she speaking literally? Was she reporting data — some incredibly slow cycle of expansion and contraction detected through a lifetime of tactile attention to stone? Or was she simply old, and deep, and speaking in the way that old deep things speak?

The old ones' rooms are sometimes visited by children, who come down in small groups on dares or out of genuine curiosity, bringing food and news from the upper levels and receiving in return stories that may or may not be histories. The old ones remember things — or they remember stories about things, which in Kethara amounts to the same thing. They remember the Great Cistern Collapse of six generations ago, when a structural failure on the seventh sub-level sent a cascade of water through the lower corridors and the entire structure thrummed like a struck bell for three days. They remember the Year of White Birds, when an unknown species arrived in enormous flocks and nested on every terrace, displacing the parakeets and then vanishing the following spring, never to return. They remember the names of legendary masons and millwrights and water-clerks, and they tell stories about these figures that are part biography, part engineering case study, and part myth.

The deepest of the old ones — the ones who have descended to the lowest accessible levels, where the worked stone grades into something indistinguishable from bedrock — sometimes stop coming back up at all. Their rooms are found empty, their lamps cold, their personal effects neatly arranged. The assumption is that they have descended further, into corridors and chambers that the working population does not use and has not mapped. Whether they die down there, or continue living in some attenuated way sustained by the structure's residual warmth and the thin seepage of water through ancient channels, or simply become part of the stone — this is a question the Ketharans do not press too hard. It is enough to know that the old ones are *down there*, somewhere, in the deep foundations, adding their weight to the weight that holds everything up.

## The Traders and the Threshold People

Kethara is not sealed. It trades with the outside world — the lowland communities that exist in the haze at the structure's margins, other settlements that are rumored to exist at varying distances. The trade is in finished goods: the dark, complex honey; the distinctive red-brown ceramics; textiles of unusual quality; flour ground to an almost supernatural fineness by the ancient stones. These goods leave Kethara through the lower openings, the broad arched gateways at the structure's base, carried by porters and pack animals along roads that descend into the humid lowlands and eventually connect to — somewhere. Wider networks. The outside.

The people who manage this trade are the threshold people, and they occupy a peculiar social position. They live near the base, in the lowest inhabited levels above the storage districts, in quarters that face outward through the great arched openings toward the lowland haze. They are the ones who speak outside languages, who understand foreign systems of weight and measure, who can negotiate with traders from communities that have entirely different ideas about value and exchange. They are, in a sense, translators — not just of language but of worldview. They explain Kethara to the outside and the outside to Kethara, and in doing so they occupy a liminal space that makes some Ketharans slightly uncomfortable, the way a door makes a wall uncomfortable.

The threshold people tend to be more cosmopolitan in dress and manner than other Ketharans. They wear garments that mix Ketharan cotton with imported materials — a silk sash here, a leather belt of lowland manufacture there. Some of them have traveled outside, which is rare enough in Kethara to be noteworthy, and they carry the experience with a certain quiet authority. They have seen flat ground. They have slept in structures that had roofs but no upper levels, buildings that were *only buildings*, not worlds. They found it — and this is consistent across accounts — disorienting. Exposed. The sky was too large. The ground was too stable, in the wrong way — it didn't vibrate, didn't hum, didn't carry the distant pulse of mills through the soles of their feet. They came back, all of them. Some of them came back very quickly.

There is a specific experience that threshold people report, and that other Ketharans confirm in their own way: stepping outside Kethara, even just to the base of the structure where the lowest arched openings give way to open ground, produces a physical sensation of *loss*. Not of safety, exactly, though there is that. More a loss of density, of dimension, of the constant tactile and auditory input that the structure provides. Inside Kethara, you are never alone. The building is always there — humming under your feet, pressing warm stone against your shoulders in narrow corridors, dripping water into channels beside your path, breathing its designed winds past your face. Outside, there is just air, and silence, and a horizon so far away it makes your eyes ache. The threshold people have learned to tolerate this. Other Ketharans prefer not to.

## The Nameless Relationships

There are marriages in Kethara, or something like them — pair bonds and group bonds and long-standing arrangements of mutual support and affection that are recognized by the neighborhood groups and formalized in small ceremonies that vary from district to district. But the most important relationships in Kethara are the working ones: the bonds between people who tend the same system.

A terrace keeper and the mason who maintains her retaining walls. A miller and the water-clerk who controls the flow to his millrace. A beekeeper and the terrace keepers whose plantings feed her hives. These relationships are not romantic (though they sometimes become romantic) and not familial (though they sometimes involve family). They are something else — something that Kethara's language has a word for but that translates badly. The closest approximation might be "structural intimacy," the knowledge that your work and someone else's work are load-bearing, that if either of you fails, something real collapses.

People in these relationships develop a deep, non-verbal understanding of each other's needs and rhythms. A water-clerk knows, without being told, that the terrace keeper on the fourteenth eastern shelf is struggling this season — the crops are stressed, the soil is compacting, something is wrong — and adjusts the irrigation schedule by a fraction, a few extra liters per day channeled to that section, noted in the ledger with a marginal annotation that might read: *Shelf 14E, supplemental, until conditions improve. K. looks tired.* This is governance in Kethara. Not laws, not councils, not elected officials — though there are occasional assemblies for decisions that affect the whole structure — but the continuous, fine-grained adjustment of systems by people who know each other's work intimately and act on that knowledge with a discretion so practiced it has become reflexive.

## What They Believe

They don't have gods, exactly. Or they have one god, and it is the structure. Or they don't think of it as a god — they think of it as a *fact*, the way gravity is a fact, something so fundamental it doesn't require veneration, only acknowledgment. The building exists. It has always existed, as far as anyone knows. It will continue to exist, as long as people maintain it. The relationship between the people and the building is not worship; it is maintenance. But maintenance, in Kethara, has been elevated to something very close to a spiritual practice — the daily, repetitive, attentive work of keeping a thing alive that is so much larger and older than any individual that caring for it becomes a form of self-transcendence.

There are rituals, of a sort. When a new section of wall is completed, the masons press their hands into the final coat of mortar, leaving prints that will be visible for centuries. When a terrace is replanted after a major renovation, the keeper pours the first water by hand, from a ceramic vessel made for the purpose, while the neighbors watch in silence. When a mill stone is replaced — a rare event, weighted with significance — the old stone is not discarded but carried, with considerable effort, to a specific place on one of the lower terraces and set upright in the soil, where it becomes a kind of marker, a monument to the work it did. The terrace where these spent millstones accumulate is dense with them, a forest of stone wheels standing on edge, their grinding surfaces worn smooth, some of them so old they are covered in lichen and barely distinguishable from natural boulders. The children play among them. The old ones sit beside them and rest their hands on the worn stone and feel, or imagine they feel, the faint residual vibration of the grain they once ground.

And there is the matter of the lowest levels. The old ones who descend and do not return. The chisel marks in stone so ancient it has fused under its own weight. The survey lines that found no bottom. Kethara does not have a creation myth in the conventional sense — no story of a first builder, no divine architect, no moment of founding. What it has instead is the building itself, extending downward past the reach of memory or measurement, implying that the question "who built this?" may not have a meaningful answer — that the building has always been building itself, through the hands of whoever happens to live in it, and that the people who live in it are, in some sense, the building's way of maintaining itself.

This is not said aloud very often. It is not a doctrine. It is more like an atmospheric condition — a suspicion that pervades Kethara the way the mill-vibration pervades the stone, felt more than heard, always present, shaping behavior in ways that are hard to isolate but impossible to ignore. The terrace keepers tend their plants. The millers tend their machines. The masons tend the stone. The water-clerks tend the water. The children climb and play and learn. The old ones descend into the depths and become, eventually, part of the foundation. And Kethara continues to arrive at itself — still becoming what it already is — turned and sustained by the people who live inside it the way a body is sustained by the cells that compose it, each one brief, each one essential, none of them the point but all of them, together, the living fact of the thing.

A gecko watches you from a crevice. Somewhere below, the mill turns. You feel it in your feet. You have been here only hours, and already the flat, quiet, unvibrating world outside has begun to seem like a memory — distant, implausible, a place you might have dreamed. You press your hand against the warm stone wall beside you and feel, just barely, the pulse.

You understand. You are in the building. The building is in you. You are, for this moment, part of what it is.

The mill turns, and turns, and turns.
