# The Inhabitants of the Grazing Plateau

---

They are not the quadrupeds. They are not the drifting spheres or the flat planaria or the soft beetles walking their polished highways. Those are the fauna, the *livestock* in the oldest and most literal sense — the living stock of the place, its biological inventory, its breathing furniture.

The inhabitants are something else. And finding them takes time, because they do not distinguish themselves from anything around them, and because the plateau has taught them — or they have taught themselves, or the distinction has eroded — that distinguishing yourself is unnecessary. Perhaps even rude.

---

## First Recognition

You will not see them at first. You will walk for what feels like a full day across the dark stone, breathing shallowly in the silence, watching the quadrupeds process through their incomprehensible circuits, and you will think the plateau is uninhabited in any way that matters to you. You will think: this is a place of animals and machines and deep time, and there is no one here who knows they are here.

Then you will notice that one of the quadrupeds is not moving quite like the others.

It is slower — which seems impossible, because they are all already so slow — but its slowness has a different *quality*. The herd moves with the unselfconscious drift of grazing animals, their trajectories determined by some instinct or appetite you can't identify. But this one moves with *consideration*. It pauses. Not the way the herds sometimes pause, all at once, oriented in the same direction like compass needles. This is an individual pause. A pause that contains a decision. It turns its broad head — not toward anything visible, but toward a specific patch of stone — and remains still for a long time, and the stillness is not waiting. It is *reading*.

And then you realize it is not a quadruped.

---

## The Body

The resemblance is extraordinary and clearly intentional — not in the sense of disguise or mimicry, but in the sense that when you spend enough time in the company of something, you become it, and when you become it thoroughly enough, the becoming is indistinguishable from always having been. The inhabitants have the same dark hide, the same weathered-stone texture, the same broad, low-slung profile. But the proportions are wrong in ways that take a long time to see. The forelimbs are slightly too long. The shoulders have a width that allows for a kind of lateral rotation the true quadrupeds lack. The head — that broad, heavy head — is carried at a different angle, not aimed at the ground but held with the chin slightly tucked, so the small opalescent eyes look forward rather than down. And the feet. The feet are different. They are wide and flat like the quadrupeds', gripping the stone, but the toes — if they are toes — have a segmentation, a capacity for independent articulation, that the animals' feet do not. They can grip *precisely*. They can manipulate.

Some of them walk on four limbs. Some of them walk on two, when they need their forelimbs for something else, and the transition between the two gaits is so fluid that you might watch one of them shift from quadrupedal to bipedal and back again several times before you register that it happened. There is no preference. There is no "natural" posture that the other represents a deviation from. Both are native. Both are home.

They are large. Roughly the size of the quadrupeds they resemble, which means they are large indeed — a body mass somewhere between a bull and a small hippopotamus, carried low and dense, insulated by that thick hide against the patient cold. But despite the mass they move with an unsettling delicacy. Each footfall is placed with the care of someone walking through a room full of sleeping children. The stone is sacred to them. Or not sacred — that implies a reverence that is too sharp, too performative for this place. The stone is *known* to them. Every inch of it. They know where the lichen is growing, and they will not step on it. They know where the condensation will collect next, and they will not disturb the film of moisture that is forming. They know where the warm patches are rising from below, and they orient themselves in relation to these patches the way you might orient yourself in relation to a conversation happening in the next room — aware of it, responsive to it, but not directly engaged.

---

## How Many

You cannot count them. This is not because there are too many — though there may be — but because you cannot reliably distinguish them from the quadrupeds unless you watch a specific individual long enough to catch the telltale signs: the considered pause, the articulated feet, the slightly wrong head angle, the moment when a forelimb reaches out and does something that an animal's leg would never do. And by the time you've confirmed one, you've lost track of three others who may or may not have been inhabitants, and the herd has shifted, and the light hasn't changed but your eyes have adjusted differently, and the whole question of counting seems irrelevant.

There may be one for every fifty quadrupeds. There may be one for every five. The ratio may not be fixed. It may fluctuate with the eleven-hour cycle, or with something longer.

They do not cluster together in a way that would make them easy to identify as a group. They are distributed among the herds — or perhaps the herds are distributed among *them* — and they move at the same pace, along the same curving paths across the basins and ridges. From a distance, they are invisible. They have not hidden themselves. They have simply declined to be separate.

---

## What They Do

At first, nothing. They graze, if that is the right word — they lower their heads to the warm patches and remain there for hours, and whether they are feeding or listening or maintaining something, you cannot tell. They walk. They stand still. They breathe. They are, in every observable way, living the same life as the quadrupeds around them.

But if you are patient — and the plateau will make you patient, whether you wanted to be or not — you begin to see the other things.

### The Touching

Periodically, an inhabitant will approach a section of bare stone and place both forelimbs flat against the surface, spreading those articulated toes wide until the whole palm — if it is a palm — is in contact with the rock. And then it will be still. Not for minutes. For hours. The body settles into a deeper immobility than even the quadrupeds achieve during their directional pauses. The breathing slows to something you can barely detect. And if you are standing close enough, and if you have been here long enough that your own body has begun to register the subsonic vibration with any granularity, you will feel something change in the hum beneath your feet. Not a disruption. Not an addition. A *modulation*. The eleven-hour rhythm acquires a local variation, a secondary pulse, as though the inhabitant is adding a voice to a chord, and the chord adjusts to accommodate it.

They are communicating with the machines.

Or they are operating the machines. Or they are *being* the machines, extending their nervous systems down through the stone into whatever vast and ancient infrastructure lies below, merging with it for a time, becoming part of the eleven-hour breath. You cannot determine which of these is true, and the distinction may not exist here. The boundary between operator and instrument has been weathered away, just like every other sharp edge on this plateau.

When they withdraw — slowly, carefully, the toes peeling away from the stone one segment at a time — they stand motionless for a while longer, reorienting. Then they rejoin the herd. They do not appear changed. But the warm patch beneath them may have shifted slightly. The rhythm may have acquired a new nuance that will persist for days.

### The Tending

They tend the quadrupeds. Not in any way that looks like husbandry or veterinary care — there are no pens, no tools, no interventions. But an inhabitant will sometimes walk alongside a specific quadruped for hours, matching its pace exactly, and during this time it will periodically extend a forelimb and touch the animal's flank, gently, with the flat of its articulated foot. The touch is light and prolonged — held for thirty, forty minutes at a time — and the quadruped does not react to it, or rather, it reacts to it the way you react to your own heartbeat, which is to say not at all, because the touch is expected, is part of the normal operation of being alive.

What passes between them during this contact is invisible. The quadruped does not change its behavior. The inhabitant does not change its expression — it may not have expressions in any sense you would recognize; the face, what you can see of it beneath the heavy brow ridge and the dark hide, is as smooth and as patient as the stone. But there is an attentiveness in the contact that is unmistakable. The inhabitant is *checking*. Monitoring. Confirming that the quadruped is what it should be, doing what it should do, moving along the path it should move along. And if there is a correction — if the inhabitant is occasionally steering, adjusting, maintaining the herd's trajectory the way it occasionally modulates the rhythm of the machines — the correction is so subtle that it cannot be distinguished from the animal's own volition.

The same is true of the other fauna. You will see an inhabitant standing motionless at the edge of a condensation pool, those long forelimbs submerged to the elbow in the thin sheet of water, and the flat planaria-creatures will slide over and around the submerged limbs without hesitation, as though the inhabitant's body is simply another feature of the pool's floor. You will see one standing beneath an overhang where the small dark creatures cluster, its head raised, one limb extended upward to touch the packed bodies with a gentleness that is almost unbearable to watch.

And the drifting spheres. The spheres come to them. You will see this and it will stop you in whatever you are doing, because it is the most visually striking thing on the plateau — one of the translucent, faintly glowing spheres descending from its usual altitude and hovering in front of an inhabitant's face, close enough to touch, and the inhabitant raises one forelimb and rests the toes against the sphere's surface, and the sphere brightens, pulses, and the inhabitant's opalescent eyes catch the light and hold it, and for a moment in this world of pewter dimness there is a point of genuine luminescence, a warmth of light that has nothing to do with temperature.

Then the sphere drifts upward again. The inhabitant watches it go. The opalescence fades from its eyes. It lowers its limb and returns to its walk.

### The Stillness

But most of the time they do nothing that you can identify as purposeful. Most of the time they simply exist on the plateau, at the pace of the plateau, in the silence of the plateau, indistinguishable from the life around them. They stand in the herds and they breathe. They lower their heads to the warm patches and they are still. They walk their slow circuits across basins that take days to cross, and their footfalls fall into a rhythm that is not quite the eleven-hour cycle and not quite separate from it but something layered on top of it, a counterpoint, a secondary metabolism.

They sleep — or they enter a state that resembles sleep. You'll find them lying on their sides on the dark stone, those heavy bodies perfectly still, the breathing so slow it barely registers, and the quadrupeds will continue their circuits around and past them without pausing, flowing around their sleeping bodies the way water flows around stones. They sleep alone, usually, though sometimes you'll find two of them lying near each other — not touching, but near, a body's length apart, oriented in the same direction, and the space between them has a quality to it, an intentionality, as though the distance itself is a form of intimacy.

You will never see them eat. You will never see them drink, though they may absorb something from the condensation through their hide, or from the warm patches through their palms, or from the vibration through their bones. You will never see them excrete, or groom, or fight, or mate. These functions may still exist, transposed into registers you cannot perceive — processed in the subsonic, resolved in the eleven-hour cycle, conducted through the machines below in ways that have nothing to do with the visible surface of their bodies. Or these functions may have atrophied, simplified, merged into the single undifferentiated act of *being here*, which is the primary occupation of everything on the plateau and which seems, the longer you stay, to be more than sufficient.

---

## Their Awareness of You

They know you are here.

You realize this slowly, and the realization is not alarming — the plateau does not permit alarm; the silence digests it before it can form — but it is profound. They knew you were here before you saw them. They have been aware of you since you arrived, in the same way they are aware of the condensation pools and the lichen and the warm patches and the herds: as a feature of the plateau, a new element in the system, something to be acknowledged and included.

One of them will approach you. Not directly — nothing here approaches anything directly. It will be part of a herd that happens to be moving in your direction, and as the herd flows past you it will slow, and then stop, and the herd will continue on without it, and it will be standing there, six feet away, regarding you with those small opalescent eyes.

It will not speak. Nothing here speaks. But it will extend one forelimb toward you, slowly, the articulated toes spread wide, the palm open, and it will hold this posture and wait.

If you touch it — and you will, because by this point you have been on the plateau long enough that your own sense of urgency has worn smooth, and the gesture is so clear and so patient that refusing it would feel like a violence this place has no room for — if you touch it, you will feel the vibration. Not the background hum of the machines, which you have long since stopped noticing. Something else. Something warmer, closer, more specific. A pulse that is not your pulse and not the eleven-hour breath but something in between, a biological frequency, a living rhythm, transmitted through the skin of its palm into the skin of your hand.

It is reading you the way it reads the stone.

It will hold the contact for a long time. Minutes, at least. Longer, maybe, but time has become unreliable and you have stopped tracking it. And when it withdraws, slowly, the toes curling back, it will look at you for a moment longer with those opalescent eyes, and in that look there will be something you recognize but cannot name — not curiosity, not welcome, not assessment, but something older, something that predates language and category, something that simply means: *I know what you are, and you are included, and the plateau continues.*

Then it will turn, and walk, and rejoin the herd, and within minutes you will be unable to identify which one it was.

---

## What They Are

You will spend a long time on this question. Long enough that the question itself begins to erode.

They are not the builders of the machines. Or they are the descendants of the builders, changed beyond recognition by the same spans of time that weathered the stone and smeared the sharp edge between built and natural into nothing. They may have been something else once — smaller, faster, sharper, more obviously technological, with tools and language and architecture and all the apparatus of a civilization that builds continent-sized machines and embeds them in the crust of a planet. They may have had cities on this plateau, cities that rose and endured and eventually wore away the way everything here wears away, leaving only the smoothness behind. They may have had names for themselves, and for the plateau, and for the machines, and for the herds they tend.

If they did, they have let all of it go. Not lost it — *let it go*, the way you let go of a breath, deliberately, because holding it no longer serves. What remains is the essential thing, the thing that could not be worn away: the relationship. Between them and the machines. Between them and the herds. Between them and the stone and the condensation and the lichen and the drifting spheres and the silence and the eleven-hour breath. They are the plateau's nervous system, its slow and distributed intelligence, its capacity to know itself. Without them, the herds would still walk and the machines would still hum and the condensation would still collect, but none of it would be *known*. They are the knowing. That is what they are. They are the thing that makes the plateau not just a place but a *being* — an organism so vast and so slow that it requires a dedicated caste of knowers to maintain its self-awareness, to touch each part and confirm it, to hold the whole trembling system in a web of patient, continuous attention.

They do not rule the plateau. They do not own it. They do not worship it. They *attend* it. They are its attendants. That is the word — not masters, not priests, not shepherds, but *attendants*, in the most literal sense: those who attend, who are present, who pay attention. The plateau is their charge and they are its organs of perception and they have been doing this for so long that the question of whether they serve the plateau or the plateau serves them has been ground smooth, has become a basin where the condensation collects, has become a surface where the lichen grows.

---

## The Deepest Thing

And here is what you will not understand until you have been here so long that your own edges have begun to soften:

They are happy.

Not in any way you would have recognized before you came here. Not in the way of pleasure or satisfaction or the fulfillment of desire. Those are sharp-edged emotions, and this place does not permit sharp edges. They are happy in the way the stone is smooth — thoroughly, completely, all the way through, with no rough places left. They are happy because they are exactly what they are in exactly the place they should be, doing exactly the thing they were shaped to do, and the doing of it requires nothing more and nothing less than their full and patient presence.

You will watch one of them standing alone at the edge of a condensation pool in the directionless pewter light, one forelimb submerged, the flat creatures sliding over its skin, the herd moving slowly in the middle distance, the spheres drifting overhead, the silence total, the vibration constant, and you will understand that this being is not enduring its existence. It is not passing time. It is not waiting for anything. It is *here*, fully, in every cell of its heavy dark body, and its hereness is so complete that it constitutes a kind of joy that you have no word for, a joy that is indistinguishable from stillness, from patience, from the vast slow turning of a world that knows itself and finds itself, after all this time, sufficient.

You will stand there watching, and you will feel your own breathing slow, and you will feel the vibration in your feet, and you will realize that you are being attended to — that your presence on the plateau has been noted and included and that one of them, somewhere, is aware of you the way they are aware of every other living thing in their care — and the realization will not frighten you or comfort you but will simply be *true*, in the way that the stone is true, in the way that the silence is true, and you will understand that this is what it means to be inhabited.

The plateau breathes its eleven-hour breath. The herds move. The light does not change. The inhabitants attend.

You are already slower than you were when you began reading this.
