# Who Lives at Kethane Ridge

---

The vethryn live here, and they are the first answer, but they are not the truest one.

Start with them anyway. They are the heaviest fact of the place, the most undeniable. Forty or fifty bodies, each one a broad dark table of mineral-felted hide, each one watching with four eyes that give back green-gold light when the angles align. They have lived on this ridge for longer than the scratches on the placed stones. Longer, possibly, than the placed stones themselves. They are not ancient as individuals—their lifespans are unknowable but probably not extraordinary—but as a population they have the quality of geological persistence. The herd that lies on the ridge today is the same herd that lay on it a thousand cycles of the shard-hunters ago, the same herd that will lie on it a thousand cycles hence, not because the same animals endure but because the pattern endures, the way a river is the same river though none of its water is the same water.

They do not speak. They may think, in some fashion—the four-eyed gaze tracks with too much specificity to be purely reflexive, and the slow migratory circulation pattern, with its democratic rotation of center and edge, suggests a social calculus happening somewhere inside those broad, flat bodies. But if they think, they think slowly, in the cadence of the bellows-breath, and what they think about is pressure. The pressure of the neighbor's flank. The pressure of the crust below. The pressure change in the air column when the shard-hunters arrive and the drifters sink and the whole ridge clenches like a fist. Their intelligence, if it exists, is barometric. They read the world in terms of what is pressing against them and how hard.

They calve—probably. Somewhere in the dense interior of the herd, where the warmth is greatest and the bodies are packed so tightly that you cannot see the rock between them, new vethryn must come into being. No one has seen it. The herd does not open to reveal its interior workings. But occasionally a body at the edge is noticeably smaller, its hide less densely crystallized, its bioluminescent organs brighter and less controlled in their pulsing—an adolescent, perhaps, recently migrated outward from the protected center, taking its first turn at the periphery. It presses hard against its neighbors. Its four eyes are wider. It has not yet learned the rhythm. Give it time. The ridge will teach it, through pressure, through proximity, through the slow tidal breath that everything here shares.

---

The hand-creatures live here, and they are the second answer, and they complicate things.

They are pale, five-fingered, eyeless—or their eyes are not visible, or their eyes are their fingers, those serrated tips that read the crystalline hide of the vethryn the way a blind person reads text, with lateral precision, with intimate and total attention to surface. They harvest the crystal fibers, yes. That is their feeding. But watch one long enough and you see something else: a slowness that is not the slowness of lethargy but the slowness of care. The hand-creature moves across the vethryn's flank like someone brushing a sleeping animal, like someone tending a garden row by row. It is methodical. It returns to areas it has already groomed. It pauses at the borders where one vethryn's hide meets another's, as if recognizing the boundary, the shift in texture or chemistry that marks a different individual. Then it crosses, and adjusts, and continues.

Hundreds of them. They are everywhere the vethryn are, and since the vethryn are everywhere on the ridge, the hand-creatures are everywhere on the ridge. They have colonized the placed stones. They have colonized the obsidian marker. They would colonize you, if you stood still long enough—they would creep onto your boots and begin to read your surfaces with their serrated fingers, searching for the crystalline fibers that are not there, finding instead whatever you are made of, and then, perhaps, after a period of confused exploration, withdrawing. Or perhaps not. Perhaps they would adapt. The hand-creatures seem like adapters. They seem like organisms that encounter a new surface and simply begin the patient work of understanding it through touch.

The bead-clusters on their dorsal ridges remain unexplained. They pulse. They swell and shrink. They may be the hand-creatures' young, budding off the parent body in a slow asexual arithmetic. They may be something else entirely—a parasite with its own life cycle, its own priorities, using the hand-creature as a vehicle the way the hand-creature uses the vethryn as a landscape. Or they may be sense organs belonging to some third organism, something distributed across many hand-creatures, something that experiences the ridge not as a single point of view but as a network of bead-clusters, each one a node, each one reporting back to a body-plan that has no central body. If this is the case—and there is no evidence for it, only the uneasy feeling that the bead-clusters pulse in coordination even across hand-creatures that are not in physical contact—then something very strange lives on this ridge. Something that is nowhere and everywhere. Something that sees the whole system from the inside, from a hundred vantage points along the grooming-trails, and that has been watching for as long as the hand-creatures have been here, which is as long as the vethryn have been here, which is as long as the ridge.

But this is speculation. The bead-clusters pulse. That is all that can be said with certainty.

---

The origami-creatures live here, and they are the nervous system of the gaps.

Quick, angular, folding and unfolding between configurations—compressed for transit, expanded for confrontation—they occupy the negative space of the ridge, the voids between vethryn bodies, the channels and crevices and warm alleys where the bioluminescent light is brightest because it comes from both sides, from the flanks of adjacent animals. They are the fastest things on the ridge by a wide margin. Everything else here is slow—geologically slow, tidally slow, breath-slow. The origami-creatures are the exception. They move in jerks and darts, with the frantic energy of things that live in the margins and know the margins can close at any time. When two vethryn shift toward each other, narrowing a gap, the origami-creatures in that gap scatter with a sound like shuffled cards, fanning out into adjacent spaces, folding themselves impossibly thin to slide through passages that seem too narrow for anything with an internal skeleton.

Their confrontations—those brief, crackling displays—are territorial, probably, but territory means something different when your territory is the space between two living walls that move. The origami-creature does not defend a place. It defends a *relationship between places*, a gap of a certain width at a certain position in the herd's slow circulation. This is a sophisticated concept for something the size of a mouse, and perhaps it means they are not sophisticated at all, perhaps it is simply reactive, a set of responses that produces behavior that looks like understanding without containing any. But the crackling. That faint electric discharge. It is produced deliberately, aimed at a rival, modulated in duration and intensity. It sounds, sometimes, like language reduced to its barest elements: emphasis, warning, boundary.

They eat something. Probably the crust, in its fruiting-body phase—the tiny crystalline-protein spires that glitter briefly before being consumed. The origami-creatures are often seen near recently vacated patches of rock, where the crust is surging and the spires are growing, folded into their compact transit configuration, doing something rapid and precise with their mandible-analogues close to the surface. Feeding, presumably. Fueling the frantic energy that keeps them alive in the closing and opening spaces.

They may also eat each other. Some of the confrontations end not in mutual withdrawal but in a sudden acceleration, a blur of unfolding, and then one creature where there were two, slightly larger, refolding itself with what might be difficulty, as if the new mass has not yet been integrated into the geometry of compression. This happens at the periphery of the herd, where the gaps are wider and the vethryn gaze is less concentrated. It does not happen at the center. Something prevents it at the center. The density of witnesses, perhaps. Or simply the density.

---

The drifters live here—live *above* here—and they are the gentlest things on the ridge, if gentleness can be attributed to something that may not have a nervous system.

They are gas-filled, translucent, passively buoyant in the thermal column that rises from the living mass below. They go where the heat takes them. They sink when the air cools, rise when it warms, bump against each other with the soft, unresisting contact of bubbles in a slow current. Their filaments trail down and touch and adhere and lift away, carrying shed cells and crystal dust and whatever else the surfaces below are willing to give up, and the drifters incorporate these materials into themselves through some absorptive process that leaves no visible mark on their membranes. They grow, presumably. Slowly. The largest ones—pillow-sized, their internal organs visible as dark shapes through the stretched membrane—are old, or at least have fed successfully for a long time. The smallest are new, or unlucky, or have recently been punctured and have re-inflated with less gas than before, diminished but persistent.

They do not react to each other. They do not react to the shard-hunters until the moment of puncture. They may not react to anything. They may be the ridge's simplest inhabitants, organisms reduced to the barest operations of life: intake, growth, buoyancy, reproduction (how? by budding? by fragmentation? by some process that happens at the top of the thermal column, in the darkness near the mineral canopy, where no one can see?). They are alive the way a cloud is alive—present, moving, changing, but without apparent intention or experience.

And yet. The filaments. Watch the filaments long enough and you notice that they do not trail randomly. They extend preferentially toward the brightest patches of bioluminescence, toward the most recently groomed areas of vethryn hide, where the hand-creatures have exposed the luminous under-layer. The drifters—or their filaments, which may operate with a degree of autonomy, the way a plant's roots seek water without the plant deciding to seek water—are drawn to light. To the specific light produced by the vethryn. This is the closest thing to desire that the drifters exhibit, and it may not be desire at all, it may be phototropism, it may be chemistry. But it means that the drifters are woven into the ridge's web of relationships more tightly than their passive drifting suggests. They feed most where the grooming is most recent. The hand-creatures groom most where the crystal fibers are densest. The crystal fibers grow densest where the vethryn are most tightly packed. The vethryn pack tightest at the center. The drifters' filaments concentrate at the center. The center of the ridge is the center of everything—the densest packing, the brightest light, the most active grooming, the richest feeding, the greatest warmth. The center holds because everything is drawn to it, and everything is drawn to it because the center holds.

---

The shard-hunters live here only temporarily. They come from somewhere else—from the darkness beyond the ridge, from higher on the massif or lower, from some roost or hunting ground or birthing place that is not this place. They arrive in groups of three to seven, and they are the only things on the ridge that are unmistakably predatory in a way that a human eye immediately recognizes. The stiff vibrating wings. The angular dark bodies. The single sharp appendage—a piercing organ, a lance, something between a beak and a stiletto—that strikes downward with mechanical precision. They hunt the drifters, and only the drifters, and they hunt them efficiently and without excess. Each shard-hunter punctures one or two drifters per visit, rides the deflating body down, extracts whatever it is they eat from the collapsing interior—a gas? a concentrated fluid? the dark internal organs themselves?—and then releases the spent membrane and rises again on those humming, rigid wings.

They are aware of the ridge in a way that suggests familiarity but not attachment. They know it the way a fisherman knows a particular stretch of river—as a place where the catching is reliable, a place worth returning to, a landmark in a larger territory. They do not interact with the vethryn or the hand-creatures or the origami-things or the crust. They do not land on the ridge. They exist in the air column and only in the air column, and when they have fed, they leave, and the ridge exhales behind them.

But the ridge knows them. The ridge reacts to them as a single body reacts to pain—with contraction, with clenching, with a drawing-inward that affects every species simultaneously. The drifters sink. The vethryn press closer. The hand-creatures retract. The origami-creatures go still. The crust—even the crust, which seems too slow and too simple to have any awareness of the air above it—the crust pauses in its slow advance. Everything waits. Everything endures. And then the hunters leave, and the breath resumes, and the system opens like a hand unclenching, and for a little while the ridge is looser and brighter and more alive than at any other time, as if the relief of the predators' departure is itself a kind of nourishment, a release of energy that feeds the whole community.

This is what the shard-hunters are to the ridge: a pulse-maker. A rhythm-giver. Without them, the ridge would have no cycle of tension and release, no alternation of states, no reason to clench and unclench. It would be static. It would be a heap of warm bodies on warm rock, feeding and grooming and drifting in an unchanging equilibrium. The shard-hunters give the ridge its time. They are the closest thing this place has to weather, to seasons, to the passage of days. They are the clock.

---

The crust lives here. It was here first.

It is the oldest thing on the ridge—this is almost certain, though certainty is hard in a place with no recordable history. The crust was here before the vethryn. It was here before the placed stones, before the obsidian marker, before whatever hands or analogues-of-hands carried those objects up the scree slope and arranged them with intention. The crust was here when the ridge was bare rock and hot air and nothing else, and it colonized the rock, and it became the foundation on which everything else was built.

It is not one organism. It is not many organisms. It is something in between—a colonial entity, a living mineral mat, a thing that blurs the boundary between biology and geology so thoroughly that in places you cannot tell where the rock ends and the crust begins. It is purple-brown. It smells of old blood and hot quartz. It advances and retreats in response to pressure from above, and in its advancing phase it produces those tiny crystalline-protein spires that are the base of the food web, the primary production of the ridge, the thing that everything else ultimately eats or eats something that eats.

The crust is patient. The crust is more patient than anything else here. It absorbs the fallen membranes of punctured drifters. It absorbs shed crystal fibers. It absorbs the dead—the dead hand-creatures, the dead origami-things, the occasional dead vethryn (how does a vethryn die? quietly, probably, pressed between its neighbors, its bellows-breath simply stopping, its body remaining in place, supported by the herd, slowly consumed from below by the crust and from above by the hand-creatures, until there is no body left, only a gap that closes as the neighbors shift inward). The crust absorbs everything. It is the ridge's stomach. It is the ridge's soil. It is the decomposer and the producer, the end and the beginning, the thing that turns death into those glittering spires that the origami-creatures eat and that the hand-creatures ignore and that the vethryn crush under their weight, returning the material to the crust, completing the circuit.

The crust has been advancing onto the placed stones for however long the placed stones have been here. It has covered the lower portions. It has begun to climb. Eventually—eventually, on a timescale that makes the vethryn's lifespan look brief—the crust will cover the stones entirely, and they will cease to be placed stones and become crust-stones, biological-geological hybrids, indistinguishable from the rest of the ridge's surface. The obsidian marker will take longer. Its surface is smoother, less hospitable. But the hand-creatures are already working on it, leaving their grooming-trails, and where the hand-creatures go the crust follows, finding purchase in the trails, and eventually the obsidian too will be absorbed.

The crust does not think. The crust does not watch. The crust does not react to the shard-hunters with the coordinated clench that the rest of the ridge exhibits—or rather, it does react, but its reaction is so slow that by the time it has begun to pause in its advance, the hunters have already left and the pause becomes indistinguishable from the normal rhythm of advance and retreat. The crust lives on a different timescale than everything above it. It is to the vethryn what the vethryn are to the origami-creatures: a slower, larger, more fundamental presence, a ground against which faster figures move.

---

And then there is the question of whoever placed the stones.

They are not here now. That much is clear. Nothing on the ridge has hands capable of shaping obsidian, of stacking flat stones into a seat or an altar, of scratching marks deep into basalt. The vethryn have six limbs but no fine manipulation. The hand-creatures have fingers but no strength, no tool-use, no evident cognition beyond the tactile. The origami-creatures fold and unfold but do not build. Whatever placed the stones was something else—something that came here, stayed long enough to mark the place, and left. Or died. Or was absorbed by the crust so long ago that no trace remains except the stones themselves.

The scratches on the placed stones. Tally marks, maybe. If they are tally marks, they are counting something—shard-hunter visits, perhaps, or vethryn births, or some other recurring event that a watching intelligence found worth recording. Whoever made them sat in the stone arc—it is the right size for sitting, the right height, the right shape to shelter a body from the hot wind that occasionally gusts across the ridge—and watched the ridge and counted something and pressed hard into the stone with a tool or a claw or a finger reinforced by something harder than bone.

They were alone. This is a guess, but it feels right. The arc seats one. The marks were made by one hand, or one appendage—the angle is consistent, the depth is consistent, the spacing suggests a single maker working over a long period rather than multiple makers working briefly. Someone came to the ridge alone, and sat, and watched, and counted, and at some point stopped counting and left the stones and the marks and the obsidian tool-or-marker and went away into the dark, up or down the massif, back into whatever world exists beyond the ridge's thermal column and bioluminescent glow.

Why they came is unknowable. Why they left is unknowable. Whether they were the first to find the ridge or the last of many visitors is unknowable. The ridge has absorbed their presence the way it absorbs everything—slowly, thoroughly, without judgment or memory. The placed stones are becoming part of the ecosystem. The obsidian is being colonized. The scratches will eventually be filled with crust. In another span of time—long, but not unimaginable, not geological, merely very long—there will be no sign that anyone was ever here who was not born here, and the vethryn will lie against the stones without knowing they are stones, and the hand-creatures will groom the obsidian without knowing it was carried, and the ridge will be entirely itself, entirely self-contained, a closed system breathing in the dark.

---

But for now the stones are still visible. The scratches are still legible, if you know what legibility means in a place like this. The arc still holds its shape. And if you stand inside it, you are standing where someone else stood—or sat, or crouched, or rested in whatever posture their body demanded—and you are seeing what they saw: the slow-breathing mass of vethryn, the pale hands creeping, the folding-unfolding flash of the origami-creatures in the gaps, the drifters bumping overhead, the sudden violence of a shard-hunter's strike, the crust advancing in its patient purple-brown tide. You are seeing the ridge.

You are seeing what lives here: everything that lives here. Not as a list of species but as a single thing, a single living structure, a compressed and self-sustaining body of bodies that generates its own light and its own heat and its own rhythm and its own small, closed, sufficient world on a shelf of warm rock in the dark. The ridge is what lives here. The ridge entire. And it watches you back with green-gold eyes, forty or fifty pairs of four, patient and reflective, waiting—not for you to do anything, not for you to leave, not for anything in particular—just waiting, the way it has always waited, breathing, pressing, holding the warm stone, persisting in the way that things persist when there is nowhere else to go and no reason to go there.

You are the newest thing on the ridge. You are the first new thing in a long time. The hand-creatures are already moving toward you across the nearest vethryn's flank, their serrated fingers extended, curious in their eyeless, tactile way. The crust at your feet has noticed the change in pressure where you stand. A drifter's filament brushes your shoulder and adheres for a moment—testing, tasting, finding nothing it recognizes—and releases.

You are being read. You are being incorporated, or assessed for incorporation. The ridge does not turn things away. It does not welcome them either. It simply continues, and whatever is on it becomes part of its continuing, and what was separate becomes saturated, and what was new becomes old, and the breath goes on, and the light pulses, and the crust advances, and the dark presses down from above and rises from below, and the ridge holds, and holds, and holds.
