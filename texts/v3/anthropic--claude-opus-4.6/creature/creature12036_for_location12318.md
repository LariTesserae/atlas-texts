# Who Lives Here

---

Not *who*. Not yet. The question needs to be reformed before it can be answered, the way water needs to be heated before it can rise.

---

Start with what is smallest, because that is what is actually in charge.

In the hot pools, in the films of mineral-laden water that coat every surface, in the warm wet soil between the rosette roots, in the steam itself — there are microorganisms so numerous and so metabolically active that they constitute the plateau's true biomass. Not the rosettes. Not the pale amphibians. The bacteria. The archaea. Whole kingdoms of thermophilic life that were old before multicellular organisms existed anywhere on Earth, and that here have never had to cede dominance. They are the plateau's original inhabitants, and everything else — every fern, every beetle, every slow-blinking amphibian — is a latecomer negotiating for space in a world the microbes built.

They live in the hot pools in layered mats: topmost, the cyanobacteria, blue-green and photosynthetic, capturing what light filters through the steam; below them, purple sulfur bacteria, metabolizing the hydrogen sulfide that seeps up from the deep volcanic plumbing; below those, pale anaerobes living in conditions that would kill nearly anything else, pulling energy from chemical gradients in the mineral-saturated water. The mats are centimeters thick in places, and they are old — not as individual organisms, but as continuous communities. The mat in a given pool may have been growing, layer over layer, for thousands of years, the living surface supported by the compressed, mineralized remains of its ancestors. They are the plateau's memory, in a sense. Drill into a mat and you'd find a stratigraphic record of the pool's chemical history, each layer a slightly different composition reflecting shifts in temperature, mineral content, gas output. The plateau journals itself in bacterial strata.

These microbial communities are not passive. They are, in aggregate, *doing something*. The cyanobacteria produce oxygen; the sulfur bacteria consume it; the balance between them determines the local atmospheric chemistry in the millimeters just above the pool surface. They precipitate minerals — calcium carbonate, silica — binding dissolved compounds out of solution and depositing them as solid structures. They are, in fact, the engineers of the mineral cones around the steam vents. Those regular, almost architectural formations that look too organized to be purely geological? They aren't purely geological. They are biogeological. The bacteria build them, precipitate by precipitate, the way coral polyps build reefs. The cones are alive at their surfaces and fossilized at their cores, and they have been growing — slowly, accretively, with something that looks alarmingly like *planning* — for as long as the vents have been active.

They live everywhere water touches mineral and heat touches both. Which is everywhere on the plateau. They are the plateau's connective tissue, the medium through which its self-regulation actually operates. When a steam vent shifts its output and downstream organisms adjust too quickly for purely mechanical feedback — it is because the microbial communities in the vent's mineral cone have already responded, altering their metabolic output, changing the chemistry of the steam, sending what amounts to a chemical signal through the water system. The plateau's apparent self-awareness, its seeming homeostasis, is *their* homeostasis. They are the nervous system of the superorganism. They have been running this place since before anything with eyes arrived to notice.

---

Now the beetles.

They are everywhere, and they are not quite beetles, though they occupy the same ecological niche. Their bodies are heavily sclerotized — hard, rounded, dark — but their exoskeletons have an unusual texture, slightly rough, almost powdery, and if you look closely you realize the powder is mineral. They are coated in a thin layer of calcium carbonate and silica precipitate, picked up from the surfaces they graze. They eat the microbial mats — or rather, they eat the topmost living layer and the loose mineral deposits simultaneously, grinding both in specialized mouthparts, extracting organic carbon from the bacteria and calcium from the minerals and excreting the remainder as fine paste that dries into small, hard pellets. These pellets accumulate. In the thermal clearings, where the beetles graze most actively, the ground is littered with them — tiny pale spheres, each one a processed mix of mineral and organic matter, each one a miniature act of geological transformation performed by a creature the size of a lentil.

There are at least a dozen species, distinguished by size, coloration, and the specific temperature range they tolerate. The smallest, barely visible, work the very edges of the hottest pools, where the water temperature approaches sixty degrees Celsius. Their metabolism is extraordinary — they burn through calories at a rate that would kill a temperate insect in hours, and they eat constantly, mandibles working with a mechanical steadiness that matches the pulse of the vents. The largest, perhaps two centimeters long, work the cooler zones beneath the rosettes, where they graze the mosses and the microbial films on the basalt surfaces. Between these extremes, a full gradient of species fills every thermal niche. They have speciated across temperature the way other beetles speciate across food plants.

They do not compete with each other so much as they partition the plateau with a precision that, again, feels designed. Each species stays within its thermal band. Step from a warm zone into a hot zone and the beetle community changes completely within meters — one assemblage vanishing, another appearing, the transition as sharp as a line drawn on a map. The beetles have mapped the plateau's thermal geography with their own bodies, and the map is detailed enough that you could, in principle, reconstruct the underground heat distribution from nothing but the surface distribution of beetle species.

They are the plateau's most visible workers. They process. They cycle. They convert one form of matter into another and they do it ceaselessly, millions of them, across the entire surface, all day and presumably all night. The pellets they produce become substrate. Mosses root in them. Mineral deposits incorporate them. The rosettes, in death, fall and are consumed by beetles that no longer eat microbial mats but have specialized in decaying plant matter — yet another branch of the beetle radiation, pale-bodied and soft-shelled, living in the humid dark beneath fallen rosette leaves. Everything passes through beetle mandibles eventually. They are the plateau's digestive system.

---

The ant-like things.

They are not ants. They may not even be hymenopterans. But they are social, they are small, they form trails, and they build.

Their colonies are in the warm soil, in the spaces between basalt fragments where geothermal heat keeps the temperature constant and moisture is always available. The nests are not mounds — the soil is too wet and too warm for above-ground construction. Instead, the colonies are subterranean, carved into the porous rock and the compacted mineral-rich soil, and they are extensive. You see the entrance holes — small, perfectly round, rimmed with a pale crust of excavated mineral — clustered in groups of twenty or thirty, usually in the transition zones between vegetated areas and thermal clearings. The trails radiate out from these clusters, dark threads of tiny bodies moving with the purposeful, slightly frantic energy of all eusocial insects.

They farm. Not fungi, like leafcutter ants. They farm the bacteria. Or rather, they cultivate specific strains of thermophilic bacteria in chambers within their nests, maintaining the chambers at precise temperatures by opening and closing connections to deeper geothermal channels. They harvest bacterial biomass and feed it to their larvae. They also harvest beetle pellets — frequently you see a trail of the ant-analogues converging on a cluster of pellets, disassembling them, carrying the fragments underground. The pellets provide mineral substrate for their bacterial gardens. The whole operation is a supply chain, and it is optimized to a degree that makes human agriculture look haphazard.

The colonies communicate. Not just within a colony, but between colonies. The trails sometimes connect two or more entrance clusters, and traffic moves in both directions — workers from one nest visiting another, carrying material or, possibly, chemical information. There is a network here, a plateau-wide web of colonial organisms exchanging resources and signals through underground tunnels and surface trails. You cannot see the full extent of it. The surface trails are the visible fraction; the subterranean network is certainly larger, threaded through the warm basalt like a mycelial web.

What are they *doing*, in aggregate? They are redistributing. They take mineral-rich material from one zone and move it to another. They take bacterial strains from one thermal regime and introduce them to a different one. They are, effectively, homogenizing the plateau's biological resources — preventing any single zone from becoming depleted, ensuring that nutrients cycle not just vertically (from deep earth to surface and back) but horizontally, across kilometers. If the microbial mats are the plateau's nervous system, these colonial insects are its circulatory system. They pump material through the body.

---

The amphibians.

They are the closest thing the plateau has to a *charismatic* inhabitant, and even they resist easy characterization. Pale-bodied, nearly translucent, the size of a large fist when curled, larger when extended. They have the blunt, boneless look of something that has never needed to flee — no predator on the plateau targets them. Their skin is smooth and slightly iridescent when wet, which is always. Their limbs are short, thick, more suited for gripping and bracing than for swimming or jumping. Their tails are broad and laterally compressed, useful as rudders in the slow currents of the hot pools. Their external gills — feathery, elaborately branched, blood-red from the density of capillaries — are their most conspicuous feature. They wave slowly in the warm water like ornamental plumes, and they are functional far beyond respiration. The gills also serve as heat exchangers, radiating excess body heat into the water or, when the animal is in a cooler pool, absorbing warmth. The amphibians are their own thermostats. They shuttle between pools of different temperatures to maintain their internal state, and the gills are the interface.

They eat. Slowly, without urgency, with an almost contemplative stillness. They eat the bacterial mats, using broad, flat tongues to scrape the living surface layer from submerged mineral deposits. They eat the algae that fringe the pools. Occasionally, they eat beetle larvae that fall into the water. They are not hunters. They are grazers, and they graze with a patience that reflects the plateau's tempo — everything here happens, but nothing happens fast.

They breed in the warmest pools, laying clusters of large, gelatinous eggs that adhere to the submerged mineral surfaces. The eggs are translucent, and through them you can watch the embryos develop — a process that takes weeks rather than days, the warm water incubating them at a pace that is almost artificially slow, as though the organisms are taking their time with the difficult work of becoming. The larvae are gilled from birth, tiny and delicate, and they stay in the natal pool for months, growing slowly, feeding on microbial films too thin for the adults to bother with. They are the pool's caretakers, in a way — their grazing keeps the microbial mats from overgrowing, maintains the chemical balance of the water, prevents any single bacterial species from dominating. By the time a juvenile amphibian is large enough to leave its natal pool and explore the plateau, it has already spent its entire developmental period performing a service for the ecosystem that made it.

There are, you begin to suspect, no freeloaders here. Every organism pays rent.

---

The flying things.

The elongated gnats with iridescent abdomens. They are not gnats. They are something else — something with longer evolutionary roots on this plateau, something adapted to the specific conditions of hot, humid, mineral-rich air in ways that no true gnat would need to be. Their wings are long relative to their body, held stiffly outward like a dragonfly's, and they are not fragile — they have a rigidity that lets the insects fly in the dense, turbulent air above the steam vents without being tumbled. Their abdomens glow faintly, and this is not iridescence after all but bioluminescence. In the thick, steamy air, especially in the low light of early morning or late afternoon when the steam is densest, they glow. Blue-green, mostly. Sometimes a colder white.

They swarm above the vents. Not chaotically — in patterns. Loose, shifting, three-dimensional patterns that have the quality of flocking behavior but slower, more deliberate. They are feeding on something in the steam itself. Airborne bacteria, possibly — the vents carry microbial cells upward along with the water vapor, and the air above the plateau is not sterile but thick with thermophilic organisms riding the updrafts. The flying things harvest this airborne crop, filtering it from the humid atmosphere through specialized mouthparts that work like tiny sieves.

At night — and here you have to extrapolate, because you have not been told about night on the plateau, but you can infer — at night the bioluminescence matters. The plateau would glow. Not brightly, not uniformly, but in shifting constellations above the steam vents, blue-green points of light drifting and swirling in the dark. The vents would still be exhaling; the steam would still be rising; and in the steam, the flying things would still be feeding, their tiny cold lights marking the positions of the vents like signal fires. Anyone — anything — navigating the plateau in darkness could read the vent-map by the positions of the swarms.

This feels important. This feels like it matters. As though the flying things are not just feeding but *signaling*, broadcasting the plateau's thermal geography into the night air as a map of light. For whose benefit? Their own, certainly — the males display in the swarms, and the females choose mates based on criteria you cannot guess, and the whole mating system is spatially organized around the vents. But the information is there for anyone who can read it.

---

The rosettes.

They are not animals. They do not *live here* in the way the question implies. But they are the plateau's dominant visible life form, and they participate in the system so actively that ignoring them would be like describing a city and leaving out the buildings.

Each rosette is a water-processing machine. Its thick leaves collect condensation — up to several liters per day per plant — and channel it inward to the root crown, where it soaks into the soil and begins its downward journey back toward the heat. The leaves also transpire, releasing water vapor from their upper surfaces, contributing to the humid atmosphere that makes more condensation possible. Each plant is a node in the water cycle, a pump that moves moisture from air to ground and back again. Collectively, the rosettes are the plateau's humidity regulators. Without them, the steam from the vents would rise and disperse. With them, it is captured, grounded, recycled. The greenhouse effect that keeps the plateau warm and wet is maintained in part by the plants that benefit from it. They have engineered their own climate.

Their root systems are intertwined. Not just touching but grafted — root tips from one plant fused with roots from its neighbors, creating a continuous underground network through which water and dissolved nutrients flow from plant to plant. A rosette growing near a mineral-rich vent shares its chemical bounty with rosettes fifty meters away, deep in the vegetated zone where the soil is less mineralized. The network distributes resources. It also distributes information, in the limited way that plants communicate — chemical signals, hormone gradients, electrical impulses that travel through the fused root tissue and trigger coordinated responses. When a section of the plateau heats up — a new vent opening, an old one intensifying — the rosettes in the affected area respond within hours, adjusting their transpiration rates, and the adjustment ripples outward through the root network. The whole canopy modulates itself as a single system.

In the center of each rosette's leaf spiral, where the condensed water collects before draining to the roots, there is a small pool. A cupful, maybe two. These pools are ecosystems in miniature — home to mosquito-like larvae (another flying insect, distinct from the bioluminescent ones), rotifers, tardigrades, and a specialized community of bacteria different from the ones in the hot pools below. The rosette pools are cooler, less mineralized, and they host organisms that could not survive in the thermal clearings. Each rosette carries its own tiny biome in its crown, a pocket of milder conditions in the midst of the plateau's heat, and the organisms in these pools perform services for the plant — fixing nitrogen, breaking down trapped organic matter, preventing pathogenic bacterial growth. The rosette is a landlord. Its tenants pay in chemistry.

---

The rodent-like thing that rustles unseen in the moss.

You will not see it. Not because it is rare — there are many — but because it is profoundly secretive, small, and exquisitely colored to match the mosses and liverworts in which it lives. It is warm-blooded, furred, with large dark eyes adapted for the low-contrast light beneath the rosette canopy. Its fur is not one color but a mosaic — patches of vivid green, muted brown, pale cream — that mirrors the patchwork of mosses and mineral deposits on the ground. When it freezes, it vanishes. It has been vanishing since before you arrived and it will continue vanishing after you leave.

It eats insects. Beetles, primarily — the soft-shelled decomposers that work the fallen rosette leaves. It eats the ant-analogues when it can catch them near their surface trails. It eats the larvae in the rosette crown pools, climbing the thick stems with clawed feet and dipping its narrow snout into the water with a precision that suggests long practice. It is, by the standards of this plateau, a generalist predator — one of the few organisms here that eats across multiple trophic levels, connecting the beetle economy to the ant-analogue network to the rosette-crown microbiome in a web of consumption.

It nests in the warm soil, in burrows that it does not dig itself but appropriates from collapsed ant-analogue tunnels, lining them with moss and shed fur. Its body temperature runs high — it has to, to remain active in the plateau's heat without overheating, which means its metabolism is fast and hot and demanding. It eats constantly. It moves constantly. It is the most energetically expensive animal on the plateau, per gram, and its population is held in check not by predation (nothing hunts it) but by the sheer caloric cost of being what it is. There are exactly as many as the plateau can fuel, and not one more.

It has one behavior that is difficult to explain. Periodically — perhaps once a day, perhaps less often — an individual will climb to the top of a rosette, to the very tip of the highest leaf, and sit there, exposed, in the open air above the canopy. It will sit for several minutes, motionless except for the rapid movement of its sides as it breathes. Then it will descend and disappear back into the moss.

Why? It gains nothing visible from this behavior. There is no food at the leaf tip. There are no mates to signal. There are no predators to watch for. It simply sits, at the highest point it can reach, in the warm humid air, and looks out across the plateau.

Perhaps it is thermoregulating. Perhaps it is scent-marking. Perhaps it is something else — something that does not need a functional explanation, something that simply *is*. A small warm creature, climbing to a high place, pausing, and then going back to work.

---

Now step back. See the whole.

Nothing here is separate. The bacteria build the mineral cones; the beetles graze the bacteria and produce pellets; the ant-analogues redistribute the pellets and farm the bacteria underground; the rosettes collect the water and regulate the humidity that the bacteria need; the amphibians graze the bacterial mats and maintain the pool chemistry; the flying things harvest airborne bacteria and map the vents with light; the rodent-things eat across all levels and nest in tunnels built by the ants. Every organism is implicated in every other organism's existence. Remove one and the system does not simply diminish — it destabilizes, like pulling a load-bearing wall from a building that has no other walls, only a million perfectly balanced forces holding the roof up.

This is what lives here: a *process*. Not a collection of species but a single metabolic event that has been continuously occurring for geological time, sustained by volcanic heat and organized by the accumulated, interlocking adaptations of everything involved. The plateau does not *have* an ecosystem. The plateau *is* an ecosystem in the way that a flame is combustion — not a thing that contains a process but a process that presents as a thing.

And at the center of the process, at the level too small to see but too important to ignore, the thermophilic bacteria continue what they have always done. They pull energy from chemical gradients. They precipitate minerals. They build. They have been building since before the rosettes arrived, before the beetles speciated, before the first amphibian ancestor crawled into a warm pool and decided to stay. They will be building after the last rosette falls and the last beetle starves. They are the plateau's permanent residents, its founders, its ground truth. Everything else is a guest in their house, however long the visit.

---

So: who lives here?

Everyone who lives here lives *for* here. That is the rule, the only rule, and there are no exceptions. The plateau does not tolerate passengers. It runs hot and wet and constant, and it demands participation, and every organism on its surface — from the archaeal cells in the deepest vent to the small furred creature sitting motionless on a leaf tip, looking out at the steam and the light — is paying its way. Working. Processing. Converting. Building.

The plateau breathes, and its inhabitants are the breath.

The plateau feeds, and its inhabitants are the food and the feeding.

The plateau builds, and its inhabitants are the bricks, and the masons, and the mortar, and the blueprint, and the building inspector, and the building itself, all at once, all continuously, all without anyone having drawn up plans.

You asked who lives here. The answer is: *the living does*. The living lives here. It has been living here for a very long time, and it is not finished, and it does not need you to understand it in order to continue.

But it notices that you are warm. And it notices that you are wet. And it notices that you are standing on its surface, exhaling carbon dioxide and water vapor, shedding skin cells and bacteria of your own, and it is already — has been since the moment you arrived — beginning the long, slow, patient work of finding out what you are good for.
