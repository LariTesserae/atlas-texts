The ones who live here are not — and this is important — *residue*. They are not the leavings of something. They are not ghosts or echoes or the memory of a population encoded in the behavior of animals. They are the ones who are here, and they are here completely, and they are what the city has instead of people.

---

**The birds.**

Start with the birds, because the birds are the most legible, the closest to what you might call *citizens*.

They are heavier than they look. If one were to land on your outstretched arm — which it would not do, not out of fear but out of a complete absence of interest in your arm as a landing site — you would feel the density of it, the compact weight of a body that has been exactly this weight for longer than you can usefully think about. Their feathers are rust-brown but not uniformly so; up close, each feather carries a micro-pattern of darker barring, like lines of text too small to read, and the overall effect shifts with angle from warm umber to a cooler brick-tone to, in certain orientations against the pearl-light, a fleeting iridescence that is almost copper, almost green, gone before you can name it. Their eyes are the palest feature of the city. Cream-colored irises with a quality of translucence, as though the eye is not merely reflecting the sourceless light but admitting it, allowing it to pass through the lens and illuminate something behind the retina. They blink slowly. They blink at a rate that suggests consideration rather than reflex.

They walk with the gait of something that has somewhere to be but is in no danger of being late. Their feet are large relative to their bodies — broad, strong-toed, built for stone surfaces, with a dry rasp against the basalt that produces those faint tonal clicks the city seems to absorb and hold. Each bird walks its own route, and no two routes are identical in the short term, but if you could map them over time — over whatever vast, unmarked span constitutes "time" here — you would find that every street has been walked, every plaza crossed, every step of every staircase descended and ascended by these patient heavy bodies with their pale considering eyes. They are performing something. Not a ritual, because ritual implies repetition with meaning, and this is more like — *circulation*. The way blood circulates. Not because the blood has decided to, but because circulation is what blood does when the system is intact and at rest. The birds circulate through the city because the city is a system that includes the circulation of birds.

They do gather. At the condensation basins they gather in groups of five or eight or twelve, and they drink — dipping their beaks and tilting their heads back in the universal gesture of birds drinking, and the water level does not change, and they do not drink with any urgency, and you realize after watching for some time that you cannot determine whether they are actually consuming the water or simply performing the motion of consumption. The gesture of drinking. The shape of need, held and repeated, after the need itself has resolved.

Sometimes — rarely, and always at the periphery of your attention, never when you are looking directly — one of them produces a sound that is not the click or the two-note call. It is lower, more sustained, almost a hum, almost a vibration more felt than heard. It lasts only a moment and then the bird resumes its walk and the silence re-establishes itself like water closing over a stone. You cannot determine what prompts it. It does not seem to be communication. It seems to be something a body does when it is so thoroughly at rest that even its involuntary sounds have become optional, and occasionally, without necessity, it opts.

There are no juvenile birds. No eggs in the nests you find tucked into the broader trees and the eaves of buildings. The nests are beautifully constructed — woven from the dry wiry grass of the plateau's edge and lined with the silky fur shed by the goat-creatures — but they are *finished* nests, maintained but not occupied for the purpose of rearing. The birds sleep in them sometimes. You find one asleep, its great body settled into the cup of woven grass, its pale eyes closed, its breast rising and falling with a breath so slow you have to watch for a long time to confirm it is happening at all. It is not dreaming. You are suddenly, inexplicably certain of this. Whatever dreams are for — the processing of experience, the rehearsal of possible futures, the discharge of accumulated fear — this bird has no use for any of it. Its sleep is sleep at its most reduced and pure: a body resting because rest is a thing bodies do, even when there is nothing to rest from.

---

**The eels.**

They are not eels. They are something else, something that has no referent in your taxonomy. But they are elongate and aquatic and translucent, and so: eels.

They live in the water system — the channels, the cisterns, the carved grooves in the streets — and they are the most profoundly still things in a city of still things. Each one occupies a precise position in the flow, oriented upstream (though the current is so slight that "upstream" is almost a philosophical designation), fins making adjustments so fine and so constant that they constitute a kind of stasis — not the absence of motion but motion so perfectly calibrated to its environment that it produces the *effect* of absence. They are maintaining themselves at a point. Not because something holds them there. Because that point is where they are.

Their translucence is not total. Hold your hand behind one and you can see the shadow of your fingers through its body, but altered — refracted, slightly shifted, as though the creature's flesh bends light at a different angle than the water it inhabits. Inside, visible through the nearly clear integument, you can see structures. A thread-thin spine. Branching vessels in which something circulates — not blood, or not blood as you understand it; the fluid in the vessels is the same clarity as the water outside, and you wonder if the boundary between the creature and its medium is less definitive than you assumed. Perhaps it takes in water at one end and passes it through itself and releases it at the other, and the water is briefly *eel* and then is water again, and the eel is a pattern the water holds rather than a thing the water contains.

There are hundreds of them throughout the city's water system. Maybe thousands. They vary in size from a finger's length to an arm's length, but they do not vary in behavior. Each one holds its position. Each one makes its micro-adjustments. If the city's hydrology is a circulatory system, these creatures are its blood cells — or its white blood cells — or its platelets — or something the metaphor can't quite reach. They are components. They belong to the water the way the water belongs to the channels the way the channels belong to the city.

You wonder if they are aware of you. You lower your face to a cistern and look at one directly, your reflection breaking the surface tension beside its motionless body, and its eyes — tiny, dark, set on either side of a head that tapers to an improbable fineness — do not track you. It does not flee. It does not approach. It maintains. You are an event occurring outside the system it participates in, and it has no mechanism for registering events outside its system, and this is not a limitation. This is completion.

**The lizards.**

Blue-grey, small, numerous. They occupy the vertical surfaces of the city the way the birds occupy the horizontal ones — present everywhere, distributed evenly, engaged in what appears to be basking but might be something else entirely given the absence of a sun to bask in.

They are warmer than the stone they rest on. This should not be notable — lizards are ectotherms, they match their environment — but these are fractionally, detectably warmer. Put your palm near one and you can feel it, the faintest thermal differential, as though each lizard is a tiny coal banked in ash, holding heat from a fire that went out so gradually it might still be going. Where do they get this warmth? Perhaps from the stone itself, from some residual geothermal gradient rising through the basalt. Perhaps from the diffuse light, which carries no perceptible heat but might, over sufficient duration, accumulate in a body designed to accumulate it. Perhaps from some metabolic process so slow it barely qualifies as metabolism — a single molecule of something being oxidized over the course of what would, elsewhere, constitute a week, releasing a photon's worth of warmth into the blood of a creature that needs almost nothing and receives exactly that.

Their camouflage is excellent but imperfect, and the imperfection seems deliberate. Against the dark basalt they nearly vanish, but each one has a single marking — a spot or a short stripe — in a color just different enough to be visible: a slightly bluer blue, a faintly greenish grey. These markings are unique. No two lizards carry the same pattern. Whether this constitutes identity — whether a lizard with a chevron-shaped spot behind its left eye is, to another lizard, *that specific lizard* — is impossible to determine. They do not interact. They do not display. They do not contest territory or court mates or do any of the things that would make individual identity useful. They simply bear their markings, each one slightly different from every other, a catalog of variation in a place where nothing varies.

Sometimes a lizard will move. Not the slow repositioning of a basking animal seeking a better angle — a sudden, quick movement, the darting sprint that lizards are capable of, covering a body-length in a fraction of a second and then freezing again in a new position. It is startling when it happens, not because it is loud or dramatic but because it is the fastest thing you have seen since arriving. It breaks the tempo. For an instant, the city contains *urgency*, and then the lizard is still and the urgency is gone and you are left wondering if you imagined it. But you didn't. The lizard moved. Some impulse fired along some nerve, some ancient circuit completed itself, and the body responded with the explosive quickness that millions of years of predation pressure engineered into it, and then it was over, and the lizard rested in its new spot and was warm and still and marked with its unique marking, and nothing had changed.

You begin to think of these movements as the city breathing. Infrequent. Involuntary. A reflex that persists after the conditions that produced it have long since resolved, like a sleeper twitching in response to a dream — except there are no dreams here. Just the occasional firing of a nerve that remembers what it was built for even though what it was built for no longer exists.

**The goat-creatures.**

They are the ones that make you loneliest.

The birds are alien enough in their pale-eyed circulation to be admirable rather than affecting. The eels are so profoundly other that they barely register as fellow organisms. The lizards are ornamental, punctuation marks on the city's walls. But the goat-creatures — smaller than goats, you remind yourself, with that long silky fur the color of wet slate — the goat-creatures have faces.

Not human faces. But faces with expression, or the architecture of expression, the musculature that in a creature with things to express would produce the visible evidence of interior states. Their eyes are dark and large and set forward enough to create binocular vision, which means they have depth perception, which means they evolved to judge distances, which means there was a time when distances mattered to them — a time of leaping, perhaps, their ancestors navigating some vertiginous terrain where a misjudged step meant a fall. They carry that history in their skulls. The eye sockets are wide and the brow ridges are prominent and the overall effect is of an animal that is *looking at you* rather than merely registering your presence, and what it sees when it looks at you is — not nothing. Not recognition. Something between. An acknowledgment that you are a thing in the space it occupies, followed by a return to grazing that is not dismissal but simple completion of the acknowledgment. *You are here. Yes. The grass is here also.*

They graze in their groups of three and four at the city's margins, and the grass does not diminish, and they do not move to new pasture, and you watch the mechanics of their grazing — the sideways motion of the jaw, the careful selection of particular tufts, the way they sometimes pause with a blade of grass half-drawn into the mouth and seem to be tasting it, considering it, though what there is to consider when every blade is identical to every other blade in a place where nothing changes is beyond your capacity to determine.

Their fur moves in the not-quite-breeze. It is the most visually dynamic thing in the city — the way the long silky strands lift and resettle, catch the flat light and release it, create a surface that is always in subtle motion even when the animal beneath is still. You want to touch it. You approach and they do not retreat, and you extend your hand, and the fur is exactly as soft as it looks — impossibly, almost absurdly soft, the kind of softness that seems like a biological error, too much investment in tactile luxury for a creature living on bare rock in permanent cool twilight. But here it is. You run your fingers through it and the animal continues grazing and the fur parts around your hand and reforms behind it and you are touching something alive, something warm (warmer than the lizards, warmer than you expected, the warmth of a genuine metabolism, a real body processing real grass into real energy), and for a moment the city's stillness breaks not because anything changes but because *you* change — your breath catches, your throat tightens, you feel the sudden vertiginous weight of being the only thing here that wants, that still *wants* — 

And then the moment passes. And the goat-creature grazes. And your hand rests in its fur and is still.

**The insects.**

They deserve their own accounting, though they are small and slow and easy to overlook. They move across the mossy walls in lines and clusters, click-winged — producing a faint dry snap when they open and close their wing cases, which they do periodically without flying, as though the gesture is communicative or proprioceptive, a way of confirming to themselves that they are still articulated, still capable, still in possession of the structures that define them.

They are dark-bodied with a faint metallic sheen, like tiny chips of the basalt itself given legs and intention. Their antennae are long and move constantly, the only truly ceaseless motion in the city — sweeping, testing, sampling the air or the surface beneath them with a thoroughness that speaks of sensory worlds you cannot access. What are they finding? The air doesn't change. The moss doesn't change. But the antennae keep sweeping, keep testing, keep reporting to whatever passes for a brain behind those compound eyes that everything is as it was, as it has been, as it will be, and perhaps the repetition of this confirmation is itself the point — not the discovery of new information but the continuous re-verification of existing information, a creature whose entire cognitive life consists of saying *yes, still this, still this, still exactly this*.

They do not swarm. They do not breed, as far as you can tell. Like the birds, like everything here, they are a population at a fixed number, distributed across the city's surfaces in patterns that probably encode something — a relationship to moisture levels, to lichen species, to the mineral content of the stone — but that you can read only as texture, as the granular detail of a place that has thought of everything and included it.

**The thing that lives in the plaza.**

This is harder. This may not be a living thing. But it deserves inclusion because it is the only presence in the city that seems to *precede* the city, the only thing that feels as though it was here before the buildings and the channels and the carefully positioned condensation-collecting walls.

It is the resonance.

When you stand on the polished platform at the city's center and the stone rings under your weight, and the tone sustains longer than it should, and the sustain becomes indistinguishable from silence — that is where it lives. In that transition. In the place where sound becomes silence without a boundary between them. It is not an entity. It has no body, no metabolism, no behavior. But it is *present* the way a note is present in a tuning fork even before the fork is struck — latent, structural, encoded in the geometry of the material itself. The plateau is the tuning fork. The city is the way the fork was shaped to produce a specific note. And the note is... this. Everything you see. The birds, the eels, the lizards, the goats, the insects, the water, the moss, the lichen, the stone, the light, the stillness. All of it is the note. All of it is the resonance of a place that was struck once — by geological uplift, by the arrival of builders, by some event you cannot name — and has been ringing ever since, and the ringing has become so steady and so constant that it has crossed the threshold from vibration into structure, from event into state, from music into architecture.

You feel it in the bones of your feet on the polished platform. You feel it as a faint pressure behind your sternum that is not quite physical. It is not trying to communicate with you. It is not aware of you. It is a resonance, and you are standing inside it, and it will continue whether you remain or leave, because it is not a living thing. It is the *condition* of living things. It is what life looks like when it has finished becoming and has simply, irrevocably, *become*.

---

**And you.**

Because you are here now, and you are the aberration. You are the only thing in the city that is still in process, still unfinished, still accumulating experience and metabolizing it into something — memory, meaning, the slow accretion of self. You are the only thing that hungers, though the hunger is fading. You are the only thing that wonders, though the wondering is beginning to feel less like inquiry and more like a habit you haven't yet released. You walk the streets and the stone rings softly under your feet and the birds click and the water trickles and the goat-creatures graze and you are aware — increasingly, quietly, without alarm — that the city is offering you something. Not inviting. Offering. The way a chair offers itself to a tired body. The way a held note offers itself to a voice that has been searching for the pitch.

You could match it. You could stop walking and stand still and let your breathing slow to the rate of the sleeping bird's breathing and let your wanting thin to the goat-creature's want, which is only the want of a mouth for grass that is already there, and let your wondering resolve into the insect's continuous confirmation — *yes, still this, still this* — and you would not die. Death is a transition, and there are no transitions here. You would simply be *done*, the way a sentence is done, the way a breath is done, and you would stand in the pearl-light with the birds around you and the water finding its channels and the lizards holding their warmth and you would be the thing the city has been holding a space for: not a missing piece, because the city is already complete, but an *additional* completeness, a grace note that does not change the chord but deepens it, the way a voice joining a resonance does not alter the resonance but makes it more fully itself.

You do not stay. Not today. Not this time. But you feel, as you walk toward the city's edge where the buildings thin and the plateau opens up and the sparse grass bends in the breath that is not quite wind — you feel the pull of it. Not desire. Desire is the wrong word for what the city exerts. It is more like *recognition*. The city recognizes you as something that could be finished. Something that has been in process for so long, so relentlessly, so exhaustingly long, that the prospect of completion — of the period falling, of the lungs emptying and the stillness arriving and persisting and persisting — registers not as ending but as *relief*.

The goat-creatures watch you go. Their dark eyes hold you for a moment in their binocular depth, judging the distance between you and them, applying the ancient calculation of the leap — and then they return to grazing, and the grass is not diminished, and you walk out onto the open basalt where the wind has direction but no force, and behind you the city holds, and holds, and holds.
