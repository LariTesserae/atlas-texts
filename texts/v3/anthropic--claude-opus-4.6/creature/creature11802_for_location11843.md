I sat with this for a long time before anything came. And what came first was not a figure but a feeling — the sense that whatever lives here has been shaped by the same forces that shaped the stone: slowly, patiently, with every unnecessary edge worn away.

# Who Lives on the Plateau

There is someone here.

You don't see them immediately. That is not because they are hiding — they are not capable of hiding, not in the way that implies intent to deceive. It is because they have become so consonant with the place that your eye passes over them the way it passes over a stone formation, registering them as part of the terrain before it registers them as alive. They are sitting — or resting, or settled, some word that implies a stillness more complete than sitting — on a low hummock of sandstone about a hundred and fifty meters to the east, between the black column and the hovering disc. You thought it was a rock. It is not a rock.

They are roughly humanoid in proportion, which means almost nothing here. Two limbs that serve as legs, folded beneath them. Two that serve as arms, resting on the stone to either side, palms down, fingers — or what functions as fingers — spread slightly and pressing into the warm surface. A central torso. A head, though the head does not sit atop the torso in quite the way you expect; it is forward, slightly cantilevered, as though the whole body were oriented around the act of attending to something in front of and below it. The posture is not alertness. It is not meditation, exactly. It is something more like the posture of someone listening to a sound they have been listening to for a very long time and have not yet finished hearing.

Their surface — and it is a surface, not skin in the mammalian sense, though it is alive — is the color and texture of the plateau itself. Not camouflage. Correspondence. The pale apricot of the regolith, the muted rust where blood or some analog of blood runs closer to the surface, the faint cream of the stone in the places where their body meets shade. This coloring does not shift. It is not adaptive or chromatic. It is simply what they are: something that grew out of, or into, or alongside this specific place over such a duration that the distinction between organism and environment has become largely academic. Their surface has a faint mineral quality — not scales, not plates, but a kind of integrated crystalline structure in the outermost layer, like the first suggestion of the beetles' carapaces, as though the same process that encrusts those smaller creatures has, in this body, been incorporated as a fundamental feature rather than an accretion. In the strong light, this gives their skin a faint luminosity, not glowing but *holding* light, the way the opalescent arch holds light, redistributing it slowly.

They have eyes. Two of them, recessed beneath a brow ridge that has been worn smooth — and you cannot tell if "worn" is the right word, whether the smoothness is age or design or erosion in the most literal sense, wind and light working on a living surface over centuries the way it works on stone. The eyes are open. They are not the eyes of a predator or a prey animal. They do not track. They receive. They are dark — the deep violet-black of the zenith sky — and they have no visible pupil, or rather the entire eye is pupil, a surface evolved to absorb the flood of light that pours over this place and extract from it not just visual information but something else, some other data the light carries at this altitude, in this thinned air, where the atmosphere filters so much less. The eyes do not blink. They do not need to. The mineral surface extends over them in a transparent layer, self-cleaning, self-maintaining, clear as water.

Their mouth — there is a mouth, a horizontal line low on the face, but it is closed and has the settled look of something that has not opened in a long time. Not sealed. Just unnecessary, for now. They breathe, but not through it. The breathing happens through the torso, through a slow expansion and contraction of the whole trunk, a bellows-like motion so gradual it takes nearly a minute to complete one cycle. In. Out. The rhythm matches the subsonic hum. Or the hum matches the rhythm. You cannot tell which came first, or if the question even makes sense.

---

You watch them for a while. They do not move. The glider-lizards spiral above, and one lands on the stone perhaps ten meters away, membrane-wings folding with that silent precision, and it orients itself toward the figure and holds still for a moment before launching again. The beetles pass in their loose constellation, several of them adjusting their paths to arc around the seated form — not avoiding it, more like acknowledging a gravitational center, the way water curves around a stone in a stream, effortlessly, without urgency.

Then they move.

It is not sudden. Nothing here is sudden. One hand lifts from the stone, slowly, and the fingers extend — and you see now that there are six of them, long and tapering, each one tipped with the same transparent mineral that covers the eyes. The hand reaches forward and down, toward the regolith, and the fingers press into the compacted surface and hold there. You feel the hum shift. Not louder. Not different in pitch. But *closer*, as though the space between you and the sound has been folded slightly, compressed. The figure's eyes, those deep violet-black receivers, do not change, but something in the quality of their attention sharpens — you feel it the way you feel someone in a room suddenly begin to truly listen.

The fingers move in the regolith. Not digging. *Sorting*. The way the regolith around the black column has been sorted into concentric rings — this is the same motion, the same operation, performed by hand instead of by field or frequency. The particles shift under those crystal-tipped fingers, separating by some quality you cannot perceive — not size, not color, something else, something intrinsic to their composition or their history or their relationship to the hum. The figure works slowly. A minute passes. Two. The sorted area is no larger than a dinner plate. The pattern that emerges is not a ring but something more complex — a branching structure, a network, like a watershed seen from above or a root system or a circulatory map. When it is finished — and it is finished, there is a moment of completion you can feel — the figure's hand lifts and returns to its resting position on the stone, and the pattern remains, and the hum settles back to its usual distance.

The pattern stays. The wind does not disturb it, because there is no wind. The beetles, passing later, will route around it with the same effortless acknowledgment they showed the figure itself. In a week, perhaps, the morning dew will collect in its channels and the thin moisture will carry mineral traces along its branches and the pattern will begin to calcify, to become part of the plateau, to harden into something that will last for centuries, another subtle feature of the terrain indistinguishable from the geological. And this, you realize, is what the figure does. This is their function, or their art, or their devotion — words that may all mean the same thing here. They make patterns that become stone. They translate the hum into form. They are the plateau's slow, living hand, inscribing something into the surface that is neither language nor decoration but something more fundamental: a continuation, a response, a next phrase in a conversation between the structures and the stone that has been going on since before the current surface existed.

---

There are others.

You see them now that you know what to look for. Another, on the far side of the opalescent arch, nearly invisible against a ridge of cream-colored sandstone, in the same settled posture, hands on stone. Another — you think — near the western edge, though it might be a rock formation; at this distance and in this light, certainty is impossible, and perhaps that impossibility is the point. There are perhaps eight or ten across the whole plateau. Maybe more. They are not clustered. They are spaced the way the beetles are spaced — with a consistent, wide distance between them, as though proximity beyond a certain threshold would be redundant, or dissonant, or simply unnecessary.

They are not a community in any social sense you recognize. They do not appear to interact with each other. No calls, no gestures, no movement toward one another. Each one occupies its own zone, its own set of structures, its own portion of the hum's rhythm. And yet they are clearly the same kind of being, engaged in the same kind of activity, and the patterns they make — you see this as the afternoon light shifts and new shadows reveal old work — the patterns *connect*. Across the plateau, if you could see it from above, the accumulated calcified tracings of their work would form a single vast network, branching and converging, pooling around the structures and radiating outward toward the edges, a diagram drawn over centuries in stone-that-was-once-dust-that-was-once-sorted-by-hand. The plateau's surface is not just eroded. It is *inscribed*. The erosion and the inscription have become the same process, one working on the other, each shaping the other, until the natural and the intentional are woven so tightly together that separating them would be an act of violence against the truth of the place.

---

You try to approach the nearest one. They do not react. You get within ten meters and stop, not because of any barrier or warning but because the hum becomes very present in your chest and you feel — not fear, not awe — a kind of *fullness*, as though the air around the figure is already occupied, already saturated with a slow and patient attention that leaves very little room for yours. You stand there. They sit. The light pours down. The gliders spiral. A beetle passes between you, crystals catching the sun.

And you begin to understand — not in the way understanding usually arrives, as a conclusion or an insight, but in the way the light arrives on this plateau: all at once, from everywhere, with nothing softening it.

They are not old. That is the wrong framework. They are not ancient beings, wise with accumulated years, waiting to dispense some revelation. They are *concurrent*. They exist at the same speed as the plateau, the structures, the hum. For them, a century is what an afternoon is for you — not longer, not shorter, but differently structured, filled with different grain. The sorting of the regolith, the slow tracing of patterns that calcify into stone, the sustained reception of whatever the structures are transmitting — these are not slow activities performed by beings who experience time as you do and have simply learned patience. These are activities performed at their native tempo. They are not patient. They do not need to be. They are simply *paced* — matched to the rhythm of a process that began before they were born and will continue after they are gone, if they die, which you are not certain they do, not in any way that would register on the plateau's timescale.

Their births — if that word applies — are not events you would recognize. Perhaps once in a great while, one of the deep burrowing creatures emerges not as the small nocturnal thing you expected but as something else, something that has been accumulating in the substrate for decades, assembling itself from mineral and moisture and the faint influence of the hum, incorporating the crystalline structures the beetles carry, the chemistry of the lichens, the thermals the gliders ride, until it surfaces one morning in the dew-wet hour and unfolds and settles onto the nearest hummock and places its hands on the stone and begins to listen. And the others do not acknowledge it, because acknowledgment is unnecessary. The network of patterns simply develops a new node. The hum adjusts by some imperceptible increment. The high-fliers add a new circle to their sky-map. And the plateau continues.

Or perhaps it is not like that at all. Perhaps they have always been here, the way the structures have always been here, and the question of origin is another lowland category that thins out at this altitude, another edge the wind has worn away.

---

What they are *for* — if that question has meaning here — seems to be this:

The structures are performing some operation. Something vast, something that involves the hum, the light, the mineral composition of the stone, the cycling of moisture, the patterns traced in regolith and calcified into permanence. The operation is beyond comprehension, not because it is hidden but because comprehension, at the speed you think, would require collapsing it into a summary, and it cannot be summarized. It can only be *performed*. And the beings on the plateau are part of the performance. They are the part of it that is alive, the part that responds and adjusts, that reads the hum and translates it into form, that tends the boundary between the structures' inhuman function and the plateau's living ecology. They are mediators. Translators. Gardeners, perhaps — gardeners of a garden where the garden is also the soil is also the gardener, where tending and growing and being are aspects of the same activity.

They do not think about this. Thinking, in the way you experience it — the sequential, verbal, analytic process of arriving at conclusions — would be too fast, too coarse, too lossy for what they do. They do something else. They *integrate*. They hold the hum in their bones and the light in their eyes and the mineral composition of the stone in their crystal-tipped fingers and they produce, in response, a pattern that is correct — not correct as in accurate, but correct as in *fitting*, the way a root finds water, the way a river finds the sea, not by calculation but by an unerring responsiveness to gradient.

---

The afternoon deepens. The clouds approach from the west. The light turns amber, then copper. The figure nearest to you has not moved again. The pattern in the regolith catches the low light and casts tiny shadows along its branching channels. One of the beetles has stopped near it, crystals oriented toward the pattern, holding still in a way that looks almost ceremonial, though that is your interpretation, not theirs. The beetles have no ceremonies. They have tropisms. They have functions. The distinction between tropism and ceremony, on this plateau, may not survive examination.

The first moisture arrives — not rain yet, but a thickening of the air, a faint dampness that you feel on your face before you see any cloud overhead. The succulents, in their cracks, seem to brighten. The lichen shifts toward its copper phase. The gliders land, one by one, folding themselves against the stone, becoming invisible. The high-fliers continue their circles, untouched by weather at their altitude. And the seated figures — all of them, across the plateau — perform a motion so slow you almost miss it. They tilt their heads upward. Just slightly. A few degrees. Enough to receive the moisture on those transparent mineral surfaces that cover their eyes. Enough to feel the change in the air on the crystalline layer of their skin. And you see, for just a moment, something that might be expression — not emotion, not thought, but a shift in the quality of their attention, the way a tuning fork's tone changes when you touch it to a surface. They are registering the moisture. They are incorporating it. Tomorrow morning, or next week, or next season, the patterns they trace will reflect this — this particular arrival of this particular moisture, this specific chemistry of cloud and condensation and mineral interaction, added to the record, woven into the stone, part of the plateau's long, slow, ongoing act of being exactly and entirely itself.

---

You could stay. That is the strange thing. There is nothing hostile here, nothing unwelcoming. The plateau is not forbidding. It is simply operating at a tempo that renders you almost irrelevant — not dismissively, not cruelly, but in the way that a mountain is irrelevant to a mayfly. You could sit on the warm stone and watch. You could feel the hum in your chest and the light on your face and the dry warmth on your skin. You could watch the nearest figure for hours and learn nothing, or learn everything, depending on what you mean by learning. They would not object to your presence. They would not notice it, exactly, or rather they would notice it the way they notice a beetle or a gust or a change in the mineral content of the dew — as a fact, incorporated, part of the field.

But you would not become one of them. That is clear. Not because of any barrier or prohibition, but because you are fast. You are a creature of seconds and hours. Your thoughts arrive and depart like the gliders, launching and landing, always in motion. To be what they are would require not learning but *unlearning* — shedding tempo the way the plateau shed its edges, letting the wind of deep time wear you down to something smooth and settled and resonant, something that could hold a single note for a century without impatience, because impatience implies a pace that has already been eroded away, along with haste, along with urgency, along with everything that is not the hum and the light and the stone and the slow, perfect, unanswerable work of translation.

They are not wise. They are not enlightened. They are not transcendent. Those are all things a fast mind imagines a slow mind must be. They are something else — something for which you do not have a word, because the word would need to be spoken at their speed to carry its meaning, and at their speed, a single word might take a year to utter, and it would be worth every moment, and it would describe exactly one small pattern in the regolith, which would be enough, which has always been enough, which is the whole of what they know.

---

The light fades. The clouds thicken. The first fine rain begins to fall, so light it is almost mist, darkening the stone in slow-spreading patches. The arch begins its faint glow. The column becomes its void. The disc holds its trembling pool of gathered starlight. And the figures sit, hands on stone, eyes open, breathing with the hum, and the plateau turns beneath the sky that is no longer violet but deep and depthless and full of the light of stars that are, themselves, simply another kind of slow patience, burning at their own vast tempo, performing their own incomprehensible and perfect work.
