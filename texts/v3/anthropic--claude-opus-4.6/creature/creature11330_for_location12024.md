# Who Lives on the Shelf

---

She comes up twice a year, and she has always come up twice a year, and her mother came up twice a year, and before that it gets uncertain—not because the tradition is young but because the memory is honest. She doesn't know how far back it goes. She knows the wall. She knows the handprint. She knows that the hand is smaller than hers, and she has never pretended it belongs to an ancestor. It might. It might belong to someone from an entirely different direction of people, someone who found this ledge from the other side of the mountain, someone who has no descendants, someone whose line ended in a cough during a hard winter six centuries ago. She places no claim on it. She rebuilds the cairn when it has fallen, and she does not know if she is the only one who does this, and she does not need to know.

She is not old but she is becoming old. Her knees know the route before she does—the way the body memorizes a path not as a sequence of directions but as a sequence of efforts, a grammar of strain and relief. The last hour of the climb is the worst, a near-vertical scramble up a gully choked with loose stone, and she manages it more slowly each time. Her hands are thick-knuckled, dark from weather, the skin across her fingers cracked in places that have stopped healing completely and have simply become features of her hands, like the grain in wood. She carries very little. A skin of water. Dried meat that she chews slowly, that lasts. A pouch of something—tsampa, maybe, or its local equivalent, a roasted grain flour that she mixes with water or tea into a paste and eats with her fingers. And a smaller pouch, stained with ochre, that she handles carefully.

When she arrives on the ledge she does not rest immediately, though she is exhausted. She goes first to the cairn. She studies it. If it stands, she touches the quartz capstone briefly—palm flat, a gesture so practiced it has lost the boundary between ritual and habit. If it has fallen, she rebuilds it, choosing the stones not randomly but by some criterion she could not articulate if asked. Rightness. The way they sit. She has never stacked them the same way twice, and this doesn't bother her. The cairn is not a monument. It is a *conversation*—a call she leaves, and someone or something answers by leaving it standing or knocking it down, and she responds again. She has been having this conversation for thirty years. She does not know the other speaker. She suspects the wind. She is not entirely sure the wind is not a person.

---

Then she rests. She goes to the alcove behind the stacked wall, and she sits where the rock is smooth, and her body fits into a slight depression that may or may not have been shaped by bodies like hers over uncountable sittings. She leans against the cliff. She closes her eyes. She does not sleep but she enters a state adjacent to sleep—a wide, unfocused awareness in which the sounds of the ledge arrange themselves around her like furniture in a familiar room. The pika sentinel calls: she knows them individually, not by any conscious identification but by the way they land in her attention, some closer, some farther, some from the direction of the pool. The wind moan through the upper rocks: she has heard it since she was a girl, when her mother first brought her, and it sounded then like a voice and it sounds now like a throat, like the mountain is not speaking but preparing to speak, perpetually clearing itself for an announcement that never comes.

She thinks in images more than words, or rather, she thinks in a mode that preceded the division between images and words and has never fully accepted it. When she considers the ledge, she does not see it from above like a map; she feels it as a set of relationships. The pool is not *there*, at the eastern end. The pool is *that feeling*—cold water on the lips, the sharp mineral taste, the way the water seems to resist being drunk, as if it would prefer to remain still. The cliff face is not behind her. The cliff face is the pressure on her back, the exhalation of cold, the slow weeping of condensation that she sometimes wakes to find has dampened her hair. The ledge is not a place she visits. It is a set of textures and temperatures and sounds that she *enters*, like entering a body of water, and when she leaves she carries it on her skin for days.

---

She is—what is she? She is a herder, or she lives among herders. She is something else also, something the herders need but regard with an uneasy respect that is not quite fear and not quite reverence and contains elements of both. She knows things about plants. She knows which of the cushion-plant species on this ledge, when its leaves are dried and crushed and steeped, will ease the labored breathing that comes to the old people in winter. She knows that the saxifrage root, chewed raw, stops bleeding—not because someone told her, though someone did, but because she has *seen* it stop bleeding, has pressed the masticated root into a wound on a child's foot and watched the red fade to pink to nothing. She knows the gentians are not medicinal but are necessary; she picks a small number each visit and carries them down and places them in a cup of water by the hearth and they last for days, bluer than anything in the settlement, bluer than the sky, and people come to look at them. They don't say why they come. They say they came to discuss a goat, or to return a borrowed tool. But they look at the gentians, and something in their faces loosens, and she understands that beauty is not separate from medicine, that there is a pathology of the spirit that responds to color the way a wound responds to a poultice.

She has a name, but it is not one name. She has a daily name that the herders use, and she has a name her mother used for her in private, and she has a name she has never spoken aloud that she considers her true name, the one that describes not who she is to others but who she is on the ledge, alone, with the marmots watching her and the lammergeiers inscribing their circles overhead. This last name is more of a sound than a word. It is related to the wind-moan. She found it—or it found her—during her third or fourth solo visit to the ledge, when she was still young enough to be frightened by the altitude and the solitude and the way the emptiness beyond the edge seemed to *want* something from her. She had been sitting in the alcove and the wind had moaned and she had moaned back, not in imitation but in *response*, the way you answer a question you didn't know you'd been asked, and the sound that came out of her was the name, and she knew it immediately the way you know your own face in still water.

---

She is aware of the snow leopard.

Not in the way the marmots are aware of it—as a lethal probability, a thing to watch for. She is aware of it as a *neighbor*, an entity with whom she shares the ledge on different terms. She has seen it twice in thirty years. Both times it was already looking at her. Both times the encounter lasted only a few seconds before the animal turned and became stone, became lichen, became a rumor of movement at the edge of vision. But those seconds were—she has no word for it. The closest she comes is that it was like being *read*. Not watched. Read. As if the leopard's gaze moved through her and understood not her intentions or her threat level but her *content*, the way you understand a passage in a text, and having read her, found her neither interesting nor uninteresting, simply *known*, and turned away to unknow her, to return her to the category of things that do not require attention.

She leaves offerings for it. This is perhaps the wrong word. She leaves the remains of her meals—a bone with some meat still on it, a smear of fat on a rock—in a specific place near the eastern edge of the ledge, not at the site of the scrape marks (she would not presume) but nearby, in a neutral zone. She does not think of this as worship. She thinks of it as *manners*. You share a place, you share what you have. The offerings are always gone when she returns months later. She does not know if the leopard takes them or the choughs or the elements. It doesn't matter. The gesture is the thing. The gesture maintains a relationship that exists whether or not both parties acknowledge it, the way gravity exists whether or not you believe in it.

---

She paints. This is perhaps too grand a word. She *marks*. Twice a year, when she comes, she opens the ochre pouch and mixes the pigment with water from the pool—always the pool, never the water she carries, the pool's water has a quality she trusts more, a mineral authority—and she applies it to the cliff face in the sheltered area near the old marks. Her additions are modest. A line. A cluster of dots. Once, a shape that might be a marmot or might be a stone or might be the concept that contains both marmot and stone, the idea of *a round thing on the ledge that is alive in a way you can't be sure about until it moves*.

She does not try to match the style of the older marks. She does not try to differ from them. She makes her marks in the language of her own hand, and if they rhyme with the older marks it is because the ledge teaches a certain vocabulary to anyone who stays long enough. The horizontal. The scattered. The single emphatic point of presence in a field of space. These are the ledge's grammar, and anyone who paints here will find themselves painting these things, the way anyone who speaks a language long enough begins to think in it.

The handprint. She thinks about the handprint. She has placed her own hand over it—not touching, hovering, feeling the cold of the stone an inch from her palm. The print is smaller. A child's hand, or a small adult's. She imagines sometimes the moment of its making: the hand dipped in ochre, pressed to the rock, held there. The heartbeat in the palm transferring to the stone. The deliberateness of it—not a casual touch but a *declaration*. I was here. This is the shape of my reaching. She has not made her own handprint beside it. She has considered it. She may yet. There is a specific spot, to the left and slightly above, where the rock is clean and smooth and seems to be *waiting* for a hand, the way a sentence sometimes seems to be waiting for its next clause. But she is not ready. Or the ledge is not ready. Or the readiness is not hers to determine.

---

She knows things about the ledge that nobody else knows, and she knows things about the ledge that everybody who has ever been here knows, and she cannot always distinguish between these two categories.

She knows that the pool is deepest after the equinox rains, when moisture saturates the cliff face and the weeping becomes a trickle becomes, for a few days, an actual rivulet that fills the basin to its lip. She knows that during these days the pool becomes a mirror so perfect that the lammergeiers, passing over, sometimes startle at their own reflections—a tiny flinch in their flight, a barely perceptible correction, that she finds unspeakably moving. These vast, ancient birds, masters of the air, undone for a half-second by the sight of themselves. She holds this knowledge the way she holds the ochre pouch: carefully, aware of its fragility, uncertain who to pass it to.

She knows that the pikas at the western end of the ledge are not one colony but two, and that the boundary between their territories runs along an almost invisible seam in the granite—a slight change in color, a mineral boundary, that the pikas respect absolutely and that no human eye would notice without being told. She knows because she has watched for thirty years and has seen pikas approach the line and stop and turn back as if striking glass. She has seen young pikas cross it accidentally and be driven back by residents of the other colony with a fury disproportionate to the incursion. The line is real. It is as real as a wall. It is more real than a wall because it exists only in the minds of pikas, and yet it governs their world completely. She thinks about this often. She thinks it might be the most important thing she knows.

She knows that on certain evenings, when the clouds clear just before sunset and the light comes in low and horizontal and turns the granite the color of bread crust, the ledge becomes so beautiful that it is almost unbearable. Not beautiful in the way a painting is beautiful or a face is beautiful—not composed, not balanced, not *meant*. Beautiful in the way that reality is sometimes beautiful when it forgets to be ordinary. The light on the moss. The last pika call hanging in air that has turned golden. The lammergeier banking into the sun, its wings translucent for a moment, backlit, every feather a finger held up to a candle flame. The shadow of the mountain reaching across the valley below like a dark hand laid gently on the earth. She sits in the alcove at these moments and her chest aches and she does not know if the ache is grief or gratitude or if these are the same thing experienced from different directions.

---

She will die, of course. Sooner rather than later now—she feels it in the way the climb takes more from her each time, the way her body has begun to treat the altitude as an adversary rather than a condition. One year she will start the climb and not finish it. Or she will finish it and not be able to descend. Or she will simply not start, will wake on the morning she always leaves and find that her legs have voted against her.

When this happens, the cairn will fall and not be rebuilt—or it will be rebuilt by someone else, someone she has never met, a traveler or a shepherd who finds the ledge by accident or by a different tradition and sees the fallen stones and feels the impulse to stack them and does so, not knowing they are continuing something. The ochre marks will persist under their mineral glaze, growing slowly more abstract, more ambiguous, until they could be anything or nothing, until a future eye sees them and thinks: *geological staining, probably iron oxide, natural discoloration of the rock*. The handprint will be the last to go. It is the most protected, the most clearly intentional, the most resistant to re-interpretation. It will remain a hand for a long time.

The ledge will not notice her absence. This is not cruelty. The ledge is not capable of cruelty or kindness. It is capable of being a ledge—of holding weight, of collecting water, of providing a horizontal surface in a vertical world. It will go on doing these things. The pikas will go on observing their invisible boundary. The marmots will go on pressing their bodies to sun-warmed stone. The leopard will go on being a possibility that reshapes the space around it. The lammergeiers will go on dropping bones. The frost will go on forming and subliming. The gentians will go on being bluer than anything has a right to be.

And twice a year, at the times she used to come, the ledge will be exactly as it always was—which is to say, it will be complete, lacking nothing, a world unto itself, sufficient—

Except that the alcove will be empty. And the quartz capstone will be lying on its side among the other fallen stones. And the ochre pouch, which she left on her last visit tucked into a crevice at the back of the shelter because she knew, the way you sometimes know, that she was not coming back—the ochre pouch will be there, and the ochre inside it will still be good,ite powder still vivid, still the red of the earth's deep interior, still waiting for a hand to mix it with pool water and press it to the rock.

Still waiting for the next sentence in a conversation that has been going on longer than anyone remembers, on a ledge no larger than a thought, halfway between the valley floor and the stars.
