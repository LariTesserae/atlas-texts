What lives here is not any single creature. What lives here is the hollow itself — the whole of it — and everything inside is an organ of that living.

---

Begin with what's oldest, because the oldest thing sets the terms.

**The water lives here.** Not as a visitor, not passing through. This is water that has been in conversation with this stone for so long that the distinction between them has grown philosophical. It rises through the sandstone's pores as a kind of geological capillary action, warmth pulling it upward from deep aquifers that have never seen light, and it arrives in the chamber already changed — mineralized, silky, carrying the dissolved memory of every stratum it's passed through. It doesn't flow. It *manifests*. It gathers on the underside of the ceiling as condensation and hangs there in fat drops that catch the amber light before falling. It seeps through hairline fractures and emerges as a film on the walls, a dampness you notice only when you touch the stone and your fingers come away not wet exactly but *moistened*, as though the rock were perspiring. And then it pools — that central black mirror, that smaller alcove basin — and waits, and warms, and gives itself to everything that drinks. The water is the blood of this place. Everything else is downstream of it.

**The stone lives here,** though you wouldn't call it alive. But it participates. It has yielded itself to the logic of this chamber so completely that it functions as a body. Its undulations are not random erosion; they are *shaped* — shaped by the same slow hydrology that shapes a riverbed, except here there is no river, only permanence and patience. The stone holds heat the way a body holds heat, and radiates it upward at a rate so constant that the chamber's temperature varies by less than a degree across seasons. The sandstone has become, over millennia, a kind of thermostat. A cradle. It has made itself into the conditions for life to occur, not through intention but through the accumulated logic of water and warmth and time. The worn depressions are not wounds. They are accommodations. The stone has made room.

---

Now the roots.

**The roots live here more than the trees they belong to.** Whatever grows on the surface above — and it must be substantial, old-growth something, broad-canopied and deep-seeking — has sent its most ambitious architecture down through the ceiling and into the hollow, and what has happened is a kind of inversion: the trees above are, in a meaningful sense, *rooted* in this place rather than the surface. This is where they drink deepest. This is where the minerals are richest, where the water is warmest, where the microbial communities in the root-mat have had the longest uninterrupted tenure. The roots have been here so long they've forgotten they're roots. They behave more like the structural members of the chamber itself — columns, walls, vaulting. They hold the ceiling together in places where the sandstone alone might have long since crumbled. They channel water along their surfaces, creating vertical highways for the millipedes, the beetles, the bronze snakes. They've merged into one another so thoroughly that the hollow's root system functions as a single organism, a mycorrhizal network made visible, every tree above connected to every other tree through this underground cathedral of braided wood.

And at their tips, at their finest filaments, they're doing something peculiar. They're reaching *into* the pool. You can see this if you look carefully at the edge of the water — the thinnest root-threads, almost hair-fine, extending down into the dark warmth and disappearing. They're drinking directly from the heated mineral water, and what they're pulling up must be extraordinary, because the rosette plants growing on the guano mounds nearest the pool are grotesquely lush, their leaves thick as leather, their cups brimming. Whatever is dissolved in that water, the roots are pumping it upward and outward, into the trees above, into the mosses and ferns that grow on the roots themselves, into the whole cascading architecture of the hollow's botany. The roots are the circulatory system. The water is the blood. The stone is the body. You see how this works.

---

**The moss lives here** as a kind of atmosphere. It is the thing you breathe when you breathe down here — not literally, but almost. It oxygenates the air. It softens every surface. It catches the amber light and holds it, extends it, distributes it into the crevices where ferns can't grow and roots can't reach. The moss is the most democratic organism in the hollow: it grows on stone, on root, on the shells of the largest millipedes, on the lip of the pool, on the placed cobble's underside. It is the green skin of this place. Without it, the hollow would feel like a cave. With it, the hollow feels like an interior — something enclosed and carpeted and *inhabited*. The phosphorescent impression it gives, that not-quite-glow, comes from the structure of its cells, which are arranged in a way that captures and internally reflects even the faintest photon, bouncing light around inside itself like a hall of mirrors, so that the moss appears lit from within even in near-darkness. In the hours after sunset, when the ceiling cracks go dark, the moss must be the last visible thing — a faint chartreuse haze clinging to every surface, mapping the hollow's architecture in bioluminescent suggestion. The creatures that move at night move through a world defined by moss-light.

---

Now the animals. And here you must slow down, because they are not simply *in* this place. They are *of* it. The distinction matters.

**The millipedes.** They are the fundamental currency. Everything eats them or depends on something that eats them. Their translucent bodies are the hollow's most visible evidence of its own cycling — you can literally watch the economy through their shells, see the green of moss being converted into animal matter, see them carrying that converted matter along the roots in their slow columnar processions, depositing frass that feeds the moss that feeds the next generation of millipedes. They are not quite any species known from the surface. They have been down here too long. Their translucency is not a youthful trait but an adaptation — in a world of near-darkness and no predation pressure from sight-hunters, pigmentation was a cost without a benefit, and they let it go. Their softness, too, is an adaptation. The hard chitinous armor of surface millipedes is unnecessary here; the bronze snakes swallow them whole regardless, and the energy saved by not mineralizing their exoskeletons can be redirected into reproduction. They breed prodigiously. They must. They are the base of everything. When you see thousands of them on the walls, know that this represents the hollow's metabolism made visible — its rate of primary consumption, its caloric throughput, its *pace*. If the millipedes slow down, everything above them in the chain slows down. If they proliferate, the frogs get fat, the snakes hunt more often. The millipedes are the hollow's pulse.

**The beetles.** These are harder to read, and they trouble the easy narrative of the food chain, because they don't seem to eat anything visible and nothing seems to eat them. They are not prey for the frogs — too hard-shelled, too bitter perhaps, and the frogs ignore them with a studied indifference that suggests long chemical negotiation. The snakes have no interest. The bats don't take them. So what are they? Watch them more carefully. Their movements are not random. Each beetle emerges from its crevice, trundles a short distance along the root bark, pauses, and returns. But the root bark where they pause is different afterward — slightly rougher, slightly more textured, as though the beetle has done something to it. Scraped it. Scored it. Opened it. And behind each beetle's path, within hours, a fine thread of fungal mycelium will appear, white and delicate, growing into the scored bark and from there into the root's interior. The beetles are *inoculating* the roots. They carry fungal spores in specialized structures on their legs — this is a known behavior in some surface beetle families, ambrosia beetles and their kin — and they are farming. The fungi they plant break down the root's outer bark, creating a nutritive paste that the beetles harvest on subsequent trips. But the fungi also penetrate deeper, connecting to the mycorrhizal network that links the roots, and in doing so the beetles are inadvertently *maintaining* the network, repairing breaks, establishing new connections. They are the hollow's maintenance crew. Their wing-cases, old-blood dark, are stained with spores. They have been doing this for so long that the roots have adapted to them — the bark grows back in exactly the consistency the beetles prefer to score, a co-evolutionary handshake repeated ten million times until neither party could function without the other. The beetles, in their humble trundling, are the hollow's immune system.

**The frogs.** The silent frogs. This is the strangest population in the hollow, and the one that most suggests something organized beyond simple ecology. Their silence is not a deficit. Surface frogs call to locate mates across distance, to claim territory, to warn. These frogs have no need for distance — the hollow is a single room, and everyone is visible to everyone else. But they have not simply *stopped* communicating. They've shifted medium. Press your palm to the stone and feel that faint, irregular pulsing. That's them. They're communicating through the rock itself, through substrate vibration, a method known in some surface species but here refined into something approaching language. Not language — that's too much — but a system of nuanced signals that coordinate their behavior in ways that look, from outside, like collective intelligence. The synchronized reorientation when the pool surface bulges. The coordinated waves of repositioning that ripple across the colony. The way they space themselves with geometric regularity around the water's edge, each frog equidistant from its neighbors within a tolerance of inches. They're reading the stone the way a spider reads its web — every vibration carries information about location, intention, identity. Each frog knows where every other frog is. Each knows when a snake moves. Each knows when the pool's mysterious occupant stirs. And they know these things not through sight or sound but through the stone, through the very body of the hollow, which serves as their shared sensorium. The copper stripes on their backs may be visual identifiers for the few situations where sight matters — distinguishing mate from rival at close range — but most of their lives are lived in a world of vibration. They are the hollow's nervous system. They feel it from the inside.

**The bronze snake** is fast and thoughtless, a metabolic arrow, pure appetite threaded through the root tangles. It eats millipedes constantly, one after another, threading through the curtains with a fluid efficiency that suggests it has internalized the root architecture so completely it could navigate it blind — and probably does, in the dark hours. It is young, or it acts young: quick, reactive, always hunting. There may be several of them, hard to distinguish in their flickering passages. They are the hollow's *speed* — the fast part of its metabolism, the quick conversion of millipede into snake into energy into motion. They keep the millipede population in check the way a thermostat keeps temperature in check, not by eliminating them but by harvesting exactly enough to maintain the balance. If there were no bronze snakes, the millipedes would overrun the moss. If there were too many bronze snakes, the millipedes would collapse and the snakes would starve. The number you see is exactly right. It has been exactly right for a very long time.

**The gray snake** is something else entirely. It is the hollow's apex predator, but that term does it no justice, because apex implies activity, dominance, aggression, and this animal is none of those things. It is *patience made flesh*. Draped along the thickest root near the ceiling, moving so slowly that hours pass between one visible change in its position and the next, it is the closest thing the hollow has to a deity — something ancient, slow, all-seeing, that takes what it needs so rarely that its kills feel less like predation and more like *tithing*. It eats a frog perhaps once every few days. Maybe less. It has learned — across generations, across centuries of this population occupying this exact chamber — the precise rate at which it can harvest the frog colony without diminishing it. It is not conserving. It is not being strategic. It simply *is* the rate. Its metabolism has slowed to match the hollow's metabolism. Its body temperature is the hollow's temperature. Its stillness is the hollow's stillness. When it does move — when the glacial curl of muscle finally resolves into a strike — the event has the quality of something geological, a slow process suddenly completing itself, like a stalactite finally dropping. The frogs know it's there. They orient away from it. But they don't flee, because fleeing would disrupt the colony's spacing, which would disrupt the vibrational network, which would cost more than the occasional loss of a member. The gray snake and the frogs have reached an equilibrium so refined it looks like agreement.

**The bats.** They are the hollow's only real connection to the outside world, and this makes them something like diplomats, or merchants, or lungs. Every night they leave through an exit you haven't found — some crack or chimney in the ceiling's far reaches, hidden behind root-mass — and they hunt the surface, gorging on moths and flying insects in the scrubland above. When they return, they bring the outside *in*. Their guano is the hollow's primary nutrient import, the subsidy that keeps the whole system richer than it could be on mineral water and rock alone. Without the bats, the rosette plants would be smaller. The moss would be thinner. The millipedes fewer. The frogs leaner. Everything would still function, but at a lower intensity, a quieter hum. The bats crank the volume up. Their guano mound is the hollow's richest real estate, and everything near it is oversized, overgreen, almost obscene in its vitality. The bats themselves are small, drab, unremarkable except for those enormous ears, which are tuned not just for echolocation but for the hollow's particular acoustics — the way sound behaves in a space padded with root and moss, where echoes are swallowed almost instantly. They listen to a world that absorbs sound. They've learned to read silence. The one that hangs awake, rotating its head, is not a sentinel in the military sense. It is *listening to the hollow*. Taking its pulse. Registering the tik of the drip, the faint vibration of the frogs, the somewhere-shifting of a gray coil, the slow breathing of the pool. If anything changes — if the air current shifts, if a new animal enters through the crack, if the pool's mysterious disturbance intensifies — this bat will know first.

---

**The thing in the pool.**

This is what the hollow keeps closest. This is what everything else is arranged around, though not in service to — arranged around the way a forest is arranged around a spring, not worshipfully but gravitationally.

It is not gas. Gas would bubble and dissipate. This *surges* — a slow, deliberate upwelling that deforms the pool's surface into a dark lens, holds for several seconds, and subsides. It happens irregularly. Sometimes minutes apart, sometimes an hour. And when it happens, the frogs orient. The watching bat tilts its head. The moss seems to brighten, though that's impossible, or nearly so.

It is an amphibian. Or it was, once, in the way that the roots were once roots and the stone was once just stone. It is large for this place — the size of two cupped hands, perhaps larger — and it lives at the bottom of the pool, in the warmest water, in the deepest depression, where the mineral seepage is richest and the water is almost briny with dissolved calcium and silica. It is very old. Not individually old, perhaps, though perhaps that too — but *lineally* old, the latest in a line of large amphibians that have occupied this pool for longer than the tally marks on the wall, longer than whatever human found this place and counted something and left a thumb-worn cobble. It breathes at the surface in those slow upwellings — or it may be absorbing oxygen cutaneously, through its skin, and what you're seeing is not breathing but *moving*, a slow repositioning that displaces water upward. It feeds on the things that fall into the pool — millipedes that lose their footing, the occasional frog, organic detritus carried in by the hair-thin channels. It does not hunt. It does not need to. The pool delivers.

Its skin is dark, darker than the water, and covered in a fine network of capillaries that may serve as external gills, or may serve some other function — absorbing the minerals directly, perhaps, incorporating the dissolved stone into its body in a way that makes it, over time, partly calcium, partlyite. When it dies — if it dies — it will mineralize in the pool's warm water and become part of the sediment that the roots drink and the trees above are built from. It will become the chamber. This has happened before. The pool's bottom, if you could feel it, is not stone but compacted layers of calcium-enriched organic sediment, the compressed remains of a hundred generations of pool-dwellers, each one dissolved back into the system, each one contributing to the mineral richness that feeds everything above.

The frogs know it. They orient toward it not as prey orients toward a predator but as — and this is the only word that fits, even though it's wrong, even though it projects too much — as *congregants* orient toward something central. It is the oldest continuous presence. It is what the pool is *for*. When it surges, the hollow acknowledges it, in its distributed, unconscious, non-sentient way, the way a body acknowledges its own heartbeat: not with attention, but with the faint systemic recognition that the rhythm continues, that the center holds.

---

**And the one who left the marks.**

They are gone, but they lived here too, for a while, and the hollow remembers them in the only way it can — in stone.

They came down through the crack. They found the hollow. They did not build a fire — there's no carbon on the ceiling — which means they came and sat in the amber dimness and the heat and the silence, and they stayed long enough to make twenty marks on the wall and wear a depression into a cobble with their thumb.

What were they counting? Days is the obvious answer. Twenty days in the hollow. But twenty days without fire, without apparent food stores, without tools, suggests something other than habitation. They were not *living* here. They were *being* here. Twenty visits, perhaps. Twenty descents through the sideways crack, twenty sessions of sitting in the warmth, thumb on the cobble, watching the frogs orient and the roots sway and the pool surge and subside.

They were watching the hollow live. They were counting their witnessings.

The cobble is a river stone, brought from elsewhere — the nearest river is probably miles away. They chose it deliberately. Its egg-shape fits the hand perfectly, and the thumb-depression is not a tool-wear pattern but a comfort-wear pattern, the kind of groove that forms when someone holds a thing for solace, for grounding, the way you might worry a smooth stone in your pocket during a long wait. They held this stone the way you hold something when you're trying to stay present in a place that is very large and very old and very indifferent to your presence.

They understood, probably, that they were a temporary anomaly. A warm thing in the hollow, briefly. The millipedes investigated their ankles. The frogs reoriented around their stillness. The gray snake noted them and dismissed them. And after twenty visits — or twenty days, or twenty whatever-units they were measuring — they stopped coming. They left the cobble on the shelf, placed it precisely, and climbed back through the crack for the last time, and the hollow closed over their absence the way the pool closes over the amphibian's surge — a brief disturbance, a slow subsiding, a return to the continuous present tense of the place.

The lichen is growing over the tally marks now. In another century they'll be illegible. In another millennium the root-mass will reach them and begin to pry the stone apart along those scored lines, and the marks will become part of the root architecture, incorporated, dissolved, converted. The cobble will last longer — river stones are denser than sandstone — but eventually the moss will cover it completely, and the condensation will begin its patient chemistry, and the stone will soften, and it too will become the hollow.

---

This is what lives here: **a system so integrated that no single part of it can be meaningfully separated from the whole.** The water lives here. The stone lives here. The roots and moss and ferns and rosettes live here. The millipedes and beetles and frogs and snakes and bats live here. The ancient thing in the pool lives here. The memory of whoever made the marks lives here, diminishing. And the hollow itself lives here — the hollow as a single metabolic entity, breathing its mineral breath, cycling its warm water, converting sunlight into moss into millipede into frog into snake into bat into guano into rosette into root into the slow deep drink that feeds the trees above, which hold the soil, which holds the sandstone, which holds the crack, which holds the hollow, which holds the water, which holds the pool, which holds the thing at the bottom, which holds the center, which holds.

It is complete. It has been complete for a very long time. It will be complete after you leave. The single ticking drip will continue. The frogs will reorient. The gray snake will make its glacial shift. The pool will surge and subside, surge and subside, marking time in a rhythm older than the marks on the wall, older than the thumb-worn stone, older than the idea that time is something you might want to count.

You are welcome here the way any warm thing is welcome — not invited, not refused, simply *included*, briefly, in the hollow's long, slow, self-sustaining breath.
