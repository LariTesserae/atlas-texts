# Who Lives in Vael

---

**Marek** is the switchman, and he is the one you'd notice first if you stood at the edge of the yard long enough — not because he's loud or large but because he *moves*. He is always crossing from one place to another, always in the middle distance, a figure with a long iron switch lever over one shoulder and a gait that looks unhurried but covers ground. He is perhaps forty-five. Perhaps fifty. He has the kind of face that doesn't resolve into an age so much as a condition: weathered, attentive, not unkind but not seeking your approval either. His hands are extraordinary — oversized for his frame, with thick fingers that have been broken and healed at angles that suggest he doesn't visit doctors, or that there are no doctors to visit, or both. He sets points, checks couplings, signals with his body when a car needs to be shunted. He does not use a whistle. He uses his arms, held at specific angles that the locomotive engineer reads from the cab without hesitation, a private semaphore that predates any official system. He developed it with her in the first weeks of the yard's operation, and it has never been written down.

Marek sleeps in the second house from the water tower, the one with the chimney that draws poorly, so that on still mornings a haze of coal smoke leaks from the joints in the stovepipe and hangs around his roof like a personal weather system. He lives alone. There is no evidence of a previous arrangement — no second chair pushed to the side, no curtain dividing the single room. The house contains a cot, a stove, a table, a hook for his coat, and a wooden crate turned on its side that holds, in no particular order: a folding knife, a tin of grease, a rail gauge, two books in a language that uses a alphabet you almost recognize, a single thick wool sock with no partner, and a fist-sized piece of granite from the eastern cut. He picked this up during the blasting, months ago. He does not display it or think of it as a keepsake. But he has noticed that it is not quite the same shape it was when he brought it inside. The change is so small he can't be certain. He is a man who is comfortable with not being certain.

He talks to the dog. Not in the way people talk to dogs — no baby-voice, no commands, no performance of affection. He talks to it the way you'd talk to someone sitting next to you on a bench: offhand remarks about the weather, about the quality of the coal this week, about the fact that the third rail clamp on track four keeps working itself loose. The dog does not respond to these remarks in any way that would satisfy a skeptic. But Marek is not making a claim. He is simply talking, and the dog is simply there, and Vael is the kind of place where the distinction between audience and furniture is allowed to remain blurry.

He is the one who first documented the rail hum. Not officially — Marek does not write reports. But he was the one who walked into the dispatching office that first morning, set both hands flat on the counter, and said, in his plain way, *The rails are singing. There's nothing on the line.* He said it the way you'd report a gate left open. He was not frightened. He has not become frightened since. But he listens for it every morning now, and on the mornings it comes, he stands very still for a moment with his hand on the nearest rail, feeling it through his palm, and his face does something that is not quite a frown and not quite recognition. More like the expression of a person hearing a familiar voice speaking unfamiliar words.

---

**Sonja Kael** is the locomotive engineer, and she is the reason the train will move tomorrow.

She is compact — that word again, but it's the right one, because she is *compressed*, everything about her giving the impression of density. Short-limbed, wide through the shoulders, with forearms that could belong to a blacksmith. She wears her hair cut close, almost shaved at the sides, longer on top where it falls forward and she pushes it back with a wrist, leaving a smear of soot at her hairline that she washes off every third or fourth day, or when it rains, whichever comes first. She has light eyes — gray, or green in certain lights — set in a face that is almost aggressively plain, the kind of face that portrait painters would reject and that you would remember in a crowd long after forgetting more beautiful ones. She is thirty-one. She looks both older and younger than that, depending on whether the fire is in her eyes or the tiredness is.

She came to Vael with the locomotive, rode it in on the inaugural run when the line was completed six months ago, and she has not left since. This was not the plan. The plan was that she would drive the engine in, spend two weeks training a local crew, and then return east to the main depot for her next assignment. But there were no locals who knew engines — Vael was a settlement of trappers, a chestnut seller, and a handful of people who had drifted to the place for reasons they mostly didn't articulate — and the two weeks became four, became eight, became *she lives here now*, in the third house from the water tower, the one with the window that faces the yard so she can see the locomotive from her bed. She has not officially informed anyone that she is staying. Her assignment paperwork is presumably sitting in a file somewhere in the eastern depot, growing a bureaucratic mold. She doesn't think about it.

She thinks about the engine. Her engine — she has begun to think of it that way, which she knows is a professional failing, the first symptom of what older engineers call *getting married*. The locomotive is a Class 4 mountain freight unit, built in a foundry she has visited, from iron smelted in a region she can name. She knows its tolerances, its moods, the specific sound of its boiler at every pressure within operating range. She knows that the left injector sticks when the ambient temperature drops below a certain point. She knows that the whistle, if opened slowly rather than in a sharp pull, produces a lower harmonic that carries better through the valley. She knows the firebox prefers the coal that arrives in the second delivery of the month — slightly harder, slightly less sulfurous — and she has begun sorting the loads, keeping the better coal for days when she needs steady, sustained heat.

What she does not yet know, or rather what she knows but has not yet assembled into a thought she's willing to hold still and examine, is that the engine behaves differently in Vael than it did anywhere else on the line. The differences are small. The boiler holds pressure a fraction longer than the math says it should. The steam, when vented, disperses faster than the humidity would predict, as if the air *wants* it, pulls it apart eagerly. And once — just once, three weeks ago, early morning, heavy dew — she was in the cab stoking the fire when she felt the entire locomotive shift on its springs, a gentle rocking, as if something very large had leaned against it companionably and then straightened up. She went outside and walked the full length of the engine and tender and found nothing. No animal, no person, no debris on the track. The brindled dog was on the platform fifty meters away, watching her with its head raised, and she had the irrational, fleeting thought that the dog was *checking on her*, the way a neighbor checks on a neighbor after a strange noise in the night.

She bought chestnuts that morning too, and stood looking at the rock face, and the rock face looked back, or didn't, and she ate the chestnuts and went back to work.

Sonja does not talk to the dog. She talks to the engine. She has full conversations with it, inside the cab, where no one can hear her over the boiler noise. She tells it about the grade they'll face tomorrow, about the load, about the weather. She tells it about a lake she swam in as a child that was so cold it made her bones ache for hours afterward and she went back every day for a full summer. She does not know why she tells the engine this. She suspects the engine doesn't care. She suspects this is not entirely true.

**The two laborers** are brothers named **Piet** and **Danne**, and they are young — early twenties — and they are the loudest things in Vael that are not mechanical. They shout to each other across the yard in a dialect so thick with local idiom that even Marek, who speaks the same base language, sometimes has to ask them to repeat. They argue about everything: the best way to chock a wheel, whether the left or right side of a flatcar should be loaded first, whose turn it is to haul water. These arguments are not hostile. They are the ongoing, lifelong negotiation of two people who have never been apart for more than a day and who relate to each other through friction the way a wheel relates to a rail — the contact is what makes the motion possible.

Piet is the taller one. He is narrow and strong in the way that young men are strong when they have never done anything *but* physical labor: no bulk, just cable and sinew under sun-darkened skin. He has a narrow face, a sharp nose, and eyes that are always slightly narrowed, giving him a permanent expression of skepticism that is mostly accidental and partly earned. He is the one who notices things — the rail clamp that's working loose, the timber load that's shifted an inch to starboard, the way the foxglove is taller this week than last. He notices these things and announces them to Danne, who does not care, which does not stop Piet from announcing them. He is building, without knowing it, a detailed observational record of Vael in his head, a catalogue of small changes that no one has asked for and that he could not produce in written form if asked. It is simply the way his mind works: it measures, compares, notes deviation. He will someday — not soon, but someday — be the person in Vael who understands the place best, not because he sought understanding but because he couldn't stop paying attention.

Danne is shorter, wider, built more like a barrel than his brother. His face is open and round and he smiles easily, which in Vael makes him unusual. Not that the people here are unhappy — they aren't — but they are reserved in the way of people in cool, damp, isolated places, people for whom effusiveness feels like a waste of body heat. Danne smiles anyway. He smiles at the jackdaws. He smiles at the chestnut seller. He once smiled at a section of rail he was carrying and Piet told him he was losing his mind and Danne said the rail was warm and it felt nice and Piet said all rail is warm when you've been carrying it for fifty meters and Danne said *yes but this was warm before I picked it up* and they argued about this for twenty minutes and then forgot about it. Danne has forgotten about it. Piet has not. Piet noticed that it was a morning when the rails were humming. He has not connected these facts explicitly. They sit in his catalogue, adjacent, waiting.

The brothers live together in a structure that is barely a house — more of a shed with ambitions, knocked together from milled lumber and roofed in corrugated metal, with a single stove between two cots. They are from a settlement two valleys to the west, a place even smaller than Vael, a place that no longer has enough people to sustain itself. They came here because the railway is the future, or at least a future, and the alternative was slow dispersion into an increasingly empty landscape. They do not talk about this. They talk about rail clamps and timber loads and whose socks are whose.

---

**Thyra** is the woman in heavy gloves resetting the rail clamp on track four, and she is the closest thing Vael has to a chief engineer, a title she does not hold because the title does not exist here. What she holds is knowledge. She was part of the survey team that mapped the route through this region, two years before the first rail was laid. She walked the ground before it was cut. She knows where the rock is hard and where it's friable, where the water table is close to the surface and where it drops away into deep, unknowable channels. She chose the site for the switching yard — this specific depression, this geological palm — and she chose it for reasons she put in her survey report (drainage, grade, access to water, protection from prevailing winds) and for one reason she did not, which is that when she stood here for the first time, in the rain, before any tree had been felled or any stone displaced, the place *felt right*. Not mystically. Not in a way she would describe out loud. Just a sense that the land here was — the word she thought but did not write was *willing*.

She is tall, angular, somewhere in her late thirties, with dark hair going gray at the temples in two symmetric streaks that she does not dye or disguise and that give her the faintly lupine look of a person who spends more time outdoors than in. Her hands, even inside the heavy gloves, move with a precision that suggests training — technical training, the kind that involves drafting tables and mathematical instruments. She has such instruments in her quarters (the end room of the dispatching office, partitioned off with a canvas curtain). She uses them. At night, by the light of an oil lamp when the generator is off, she redraws the survey maps, updating them, because the distances keep changing.

Not by much. Centimeters. The kind of discrepancy you'd normally attribute to measurement error. But Thyra does not make measurement errors, and she has checked her work enough times to know that the eastern ridge is — the word fails her, the word she wants doesn't exist — *settling inward*. The depression is very slightly deeper than it was when she surveyed it. The slopes are very slightly steeper. The geological palm is, over the course of months, *closing*, tightening its grip by fractions so small they are at the threshold of detection. She has told no one this. She is waiting to be sure. She suspects she will be waiting a long time, because Vael is a place that resists certainty, that keeps its truths at the edge of the measurable, in the gap between what the instruments say and what the body knows.

She and Sonja eat dinner together most evenings. They do not have a word for what they are to each other. Vael is too new and too small for the usual social vocabularies. They eat, they talk about the work, they sit on the platform outside the dispatching office and watch the light leave the sky — which it does slowly here, the diffuse atmosphere prolonging twilight into a long, soft, blue-gray hour during which the switching yard becomes a place of silhouettes and the distant sound of the generator and the smell of coal and creosote and the turpentine sweetness of the conifers on the ridge. Sometimes Thyra tells Sonja about the survey maps. Sometimes Sonja tells Thyra about the lake. They are building something between them that is as new and provisional as the yard itself, as solidly made as the rails, as subject to forces neither of them fully understands.

---

**Old Maren** — the chestnut seller — is the fixed point around which Vael's history, such as it is, quietly orbits.

She was here before. Before the survey team, before the blasting, before the first stake was driven. She was here when this depression was just a place where two ridges didn't meet, a damp hollow full of alder and birch and the dark conifers, where a creek ran through in spring and dried to a muddy thread by late summer. Her cottage on the eastern ridge — the one that predates everything — is built from hand-hewn logs so old they've gone silver, with a sod roof that is less a roof than a continuation of the hillside, the grasses and mosses growing over it in an unbroken carpet, as if the land had simply decided to flow over this small human structure and absorb it.

She is old. How old depends on who is doing the estimating and what cultural framework they bring to the task. The laborers say she might be seventy. Thyra, who has looked at her hands and her eyes and the way she moves — slowly but without frailty, with the deliberate economy of a person who has learned exactly how much energy each action requires and wastes none — Thyra thinks she might be older than that. Sonja doesn't guess. Sonja has decided that Maren's age is one of those things that Vael keeps at the edge of the knowable, and she respects it the way she respects the boiler's tendency to hold pressure a fraction longer than it should.

Maren's face is a terrain. There is no other way to describe it. The lines are so deep and so numerous that they have their own topology — ridges, valleys, planes, a landscape of skin that has been shaped by decades of cold wind and damp air and squinting into the particular flat light of this region. Her eyes are dark, almost black, and they are sharp in a way that her age should not permit. She misses nothing. She sees the jackdaws and knows which ones are this year's brood and which are old residents. She sees the foxglove and knows it's three centimeters taller than it should be for this time of year. She sees the fresh-cut rock face and she watches it the way you'd watch a person sleeping — attentively, patiently, without expectation.

She roasts chestnuts over a small brazier on a cart that she wheels to a spot near the water tower each morning and wheels back to her cottage each evening. The chestnuts are not local — they come from somewhere else, possibly the same supply chain that brings the coal, possibly some other route that only Maren knows. She sells them in paper cones for a price that has not changed since the yard opened. She does not haggle. She does not advertise. She is there, the chestnuts are there, and the people of Vael come to her when they want chestnuts and she gives them chestnuts and takes their coins and that is the transaction. Except it isn't, because no one goes to Maren *only* for chestnuts. They go because she is the place where information gathers. Not gossip — Vael is too small and too busy for gossip — but *observation*. People stand at Maren's cart and say things they wouldn't say elsewhere. Piet stands there and tells her about the rail that was warm before Danne picked it up, and she nods and says nothing, and he feels heard. Sonja stands there and doesn't tell her about the engine rocking on its springs, but Maren looks at her and Sonja has the overwhelming sense that she already knows.

What Maren knows, actually knows, as in: what is the content of her understanding of this place — this is the thing that cannot be directly accessed. She has said the hill is *learning the shape of the wound*. She has said this plainly, without drama, and the people who heard her filed it under "local color" or "old woman's poetry" and moved on. But Thyra, who heard it secondhand from Marek, copied the phrase into her survey notebook in small, careful letters and has thought about it on many evenings since. *Learning the shape of the wound.* Not healing. Not resisting. *Learning.* As if the hill — the granite, the soil, the root systems, the drainage channels, the whole living and mineral body of the place — is engaged in an act of comprehension. As if what happened when the railway came through was not an injury to be recovered from but an *event to be understood*, and the understanding is ongoing, and the subtle movements of the rock and the inward lean of the willows and the rail hum and the slow tightening of the depression are all part of a vast, unhurried process of *making sense of what has arrived*.

Maren does not explain this. Maren offers chestnuts and the warmth of her brazier and the gravity of her presence. She has outlasted everything that was here before the railway and she will, probably, outlast the railway too. She is not opposed to it. She has no objection to the trains, the noise, the coal smoke, the people. She watches it all with the same patient attention she gives the rock face. Vael is learning, and she is part of how it learns — the part that remembers what was here before, and holds that memory without grievance, and waits to see what the place will become.

---

**The child** — the one who appeared on the slope above the warehouse, watched, threw a stone, vanished — is **Liss**, and she is ten, and she is feral in the way that only a child in a place like Vael can be feral: not neglected, not unloved, but *ungoverned*, free to move through the settlement and the surrounding hills with a liberty that would be unthinkable in a larger or more established place.

She is Maren's great-grandchild, or grand-niece, or some relation that is described in a single word in the local language that has no English equivalent, a word that means something like "the young person who belongs to the old person's household but by bond rather than blood." She lives in the silver cottage on the eastern ridge. She sleeps in a loft under the sod roof where she can hear the grasses growing — she says she can hear them, and no one corrects her, because in Vael the line between hearing and imagining is allowed to be permeable.

Liss is small for her age, dark-haired, quick-moving, with knees that are perpetually scraped and fingernails that are perpetually dirty. She has the watchfulness of a prey animal combined with the curiosity of a predator, a combination that makes her an extraordinary observer but a terrible student; she cannot be made to sit still, cannot be made to focus on anything she has not chosen to focus on, and what she chooses to focus on is the yard, the trains, the rock, the water, the birds, the dog, the willows, the everything. She is the inheritor of whatever Maren knows, not because Maren is teaching her — Maren is not a teacher, Maren is a presence — but because Liss is learning the way Vael learns: by proximity, by patience, by being there when things happen and absorbing them through her skin.

She has found something in the eastern cut. She hasn't told anyone yet. It's a place where the freshly blasted granite has a vein of something different — not mica, not quartz, something darker, with a surface that is slightly warm to the touch regardless of the weather. She visits it most mornings, before Maren wheels the cart down, when the dew is still heavy and the rail hum — if it comes — has just begun. She puts her hand flat against the dark vein and stands there, absolutely still, for as long as five or ten minutes, which for Liss is an eternity of stillness, a feat of concentration that she cannot muster for any human-imposed task but that comes naturally here, as if the rock has asked for her attention and she has simply agreed. What she feels — what she thinks she feels — is a pulse. Slow. So slow it might be imagined. A rhythm that is not mechanical, not exactly biological, but something else, some third category of movement that Vael is inventing or revealing or both. She does not have words for this yet. She is ten. She has time.

She threw the stone at nothing. That's what it looked like from the yard. But Liss was throwing it at a specific point on the slope, a patch of bare earth where she has been throwing stones for weeks, and the stones do not accumulate there. Each time she goes back, the patch is bare again. The stones are *absorbed*, or displaced, or taken somewhere — she doesn't know, and she is not afraid of not knowing, because not-knowing is the natural state of things in Vael and she has never lived anywhere else and she assumes this is how the world works everywhere: you throw a stone, the ground takes it, you throw another one. It's a game, or a conversation, or an experiment. She is not sure which and does not need to be.

---

**The cats** — there are three of them, and they deserve mention as residents because they have been here longer than most of the humans, which is strange given that Vael's human settlement is only months old. They were here before the railway. Maren knows them. They are not her cats, but they predate the yard, and they lived in and around her cottage and in the dense brush of the ridges, surviving on voles and the occasional bird and whatever else the land provided. They are large, long-haired, heavy-boned animals with tufted ears and dense undercoats, adapted to the cold and damp in ways that suggest either a very old local breed or a very successful feral population. Their eyes are amber. All three of them, the same amber, which is unusual enough that Piet has noticed it and added it to his catalogue.

They have moved closer since the yard was built. They now live in and around the warehouse, in the warm spaces behind the coal stores, in the gap beneath the dispatching office where the generator's waste heat keeps the ground dry. They hunt the rats that came with the supply trains — came from elsewhere, carried in with the crated equipment and the coal, alien rodents that are fatter and bolder than anything the local ecology produced. The cats dispatch them with a clinical efficiency that suggests not hunger but *policy*, a territorial imperative that has nothing to do with food and everything to do with maintaining the order of things. They leave the remains near the loading dock like reports filed in triplicate.

They do not interact with the dog. The dog does not interact with them. This is not tension; it is demarcation. They have divided Vael between them without conflict, the dog claiming the open spaces of the platform and the yard, the cats claiming the enclosed spaces, the dark warm gaps, the underneath. They coexist with the same pragmatic disinterest that characterizes most of Vael's relationships — not cold, not hostile, just *settled*, each party occupying its niche and allowing the others to occupy theirs.

---

**The dog** is named **nothing**. No one has named it. It arrived during the construction phase, or was already here, or materialized out of the local conditions the way the fireweed materialized out of disturbed soil — no one is certain, and the dog offers no autobiography. It is large, brindled — brown and gray in irregular patches — with a broad head, heavy paws, and a tail that neither wags nor tucks but simply *exists*, hanging at a neutral angle that communicates nothing except the dog's refusal to perform.

It sleeps on the platform outside the dispatching office. It eats what is offered and does not beg for what isn't. It follows no one. It watches everything. On mornings when the rails hum, it lifts its head and orients toward the sound and holds that orientation for the duration, ears forward, eyes half-closed, with an expression that — if you are being very honest, if you are being the kind of honest that Vael seems to require — looks like *listening* in the deepest sense, not just hearing but *attending*, the way Maren attends to the rock face, the way Liss attends to the dark vein in the granite.

When Sonja felt the locomotive rock on its springs that morning, the dog was watching. Sonja saw it watching. There was a moment — brief, unrepeatable, the kind of moment that dissolves under scrutiny — when she and the dog looked at each other across fifty meters of switching yard and she felt, with absolute, ridiculous, unjustifiable certainty, that the dog knew exactly what had happened and was not alarmed. That the dog's raised head was not alertness but *acknowledgment*. As if the dog were saying: *Yes. That happened. It's fine.*

She has not told anyone about this moment. She will not tell anyone. It sits in her the way the warm rail sits in Piet's catalogue: a fact without a frame, waiting to be part of a picture that hasn't formed yet.

---

**There are others.** The hammering man near the water tower — his name is **Gregor**, he is a carpenter and general fixer, an older man with a bad knee who came to Vael because it was the one place on the line where they needed someone who could build things out of wood and he can build anything out of wood. He is rebuilding a section of the water tower's platform that has warped — not from age, since nothing here is old enough to warp from age, but from something else, some asymmetric stress, as if the timbers were pulled gently in one direction. Toward the yard. He has not remarked on this because Gregor does not remark on things. He fixes them. He planes the warp out, re-seats the boards, moves on. If they warp again, he'll fix them again. This is his relationship with Vael: the place does what it does, and he corrects for it, and the process is neither adversarial nor cooperative but simply ongoing, a dialogue conducted in wood and nails.

There is a **telegraph operator** who works in the dispatching office when the generator is running and drinks tea when it isn't. Her name is **Venn** and she is the youngest adult in Vael, twenty-two, thin-wristed, precise, with handwriting so small and neat that the dispatching logs look like they were printed by a machine. She came from the eastern terminus, trained in a city she doesn't talk about, and she is the most formally educated person in the settlement, which means she is the one who struggles most with what Vael is doing. The others — Maren, Liss, even Marek and Sonja — have some capacity for accommodation, some willingness to let the unexplained remain unexplained. Venn wants explanations. She writes down the times and durations of the rail hum. She records the barometric pressure and the temperature and the humidity and the wind direction and she looks for correlations and she finds almost-correlations, patterns that are *nearly* patterns, regularities that hold for three or four instances and then break without apology. This frustrates her in a way that is productive: it keeps her sharp, keeps her recording, keeps her building a dataset that someone — maybe her, maybe Thyra, maybe some future person who hasn't arrived yet — will someday be able to read. She is, without knowing it, Vael's archivist. The place is being documented in her telegraph logs and her weather records and her small, precise notations in the margins: *Rail hum, 07:14–07:31. No scheduled traffic. Clear morning, heavy dew. Dog oriented NNE.*

---

And there are the **people who pass through**. Vael is a switching yard; its purpose is to be *between*. Crews from the timber operations come through with their loads, rough-handed men and women who smell of pine sap and mule sweat, who stay for an afternoon or a night, who eat Maren's chestnuts and drink whatever Gregor has distilled in the apparatus he keeps behind the machine shop and doesn't think anyone knows about (everyone knows about it). Supply drivers bring coal and equipment and crated goods, and they bring news from the eastern terminus and from beyond the terminus, news of a wider world that Vael's residents receive with polite interest and immediately forget, the way you forget a dream that wasn't yours. A surveyor comes through every few weeks, checks Thyra's work, finds nothing amiss (because the discrepancies are too small, are centimeters, are at the threshold of detection), and leaves again, reassured. He is reassured because he wants to be; Thyra lets him be because the alternative is a conversation she isn't ready to have.

Occasionally, someone arrives who doesn't leave. It happens the way Sonja happened: a person comes for a purpose, the purpose is fulfilled or modified, and the person *stays*, not out of inertia but out of some pull that they describe to themselves in practical terms — the work is steady, the air is clean, the pace suits them — but that is, underneath the practical terms, something else. Vael *collects*. Not aggressively, not hungrily, but with the same patient, inward pull as the willows on the slopes, the same slow tightening that Thyra has measured in the depression's contours. People arrive and they find that the place fits them, or they fit the place, or the distinction doesn't matter because fitting is a two-body problem and Vael is always adjusting, always learning the shape of what arrives.

---

This is who lives in Vael: a switchman who talks to a dog, an engineer who talks to an engine, a surveyor who redraws maps that won't stay still, an old woman who understands something she has no intention of explaining, a child who listens to rock, two brothers who argue as a form of love, a carpenter who fixes what the land undoes, a telegraph operator who measures the unmeasurable, three amber-eyed cats who enforce the local order, a dog that listens for a sound the rails make when nothing is coming, and whatever else moves in the brush upslope, unwilling to fully commit to visibility.

They are all — every one of them — in the process of becoming native to a place that is itself in the process of becoming. Vael is young, and so are they, even Maren, even the cats, because *young* in Vael doesn't mean new, it means *unfinished*, it means the shape hasn't settled, the relationship between the place and its inhabitants is still being negotiated, and the negotiation is slow, and patient, and conducted in a language that is partly iron and partly stone and partly root and partly the smell of coal smoke on a cool morning when the light falls cleanly through a sky the color of old milk and the rails begin to hum.

No one here is foolish enough to rush the process. The hill is learning. They are letting it learn. And in the letting, they are learning too — not what Vael *is*, because that is not yet determined, but how to live inside a question that has not yet finished asking itself.
