# The Keeper of the Cistern

---

At first you think it is part of the wall.

That's the correct response. That is, in some sense, the intended response, if intention can be attributed to something this far removed from conscious strategy. It is pressed into the corner where the eastern wall meets the ceiling at their closest convergence — the tightest space in an already impossibly tight space — and its body conforms to the junction so precisely that it reads as architecture. A thickening of the seam. A node where the mineral veining converges into something denser, darker, more deliberate. You would have to stare at it for a long time in this dim non-light, and you would have to know what you were looking for, and even then you might convince yourself you were looking at a natural accretion, a stalactite of biochemistry, a place where the slow mineral logic of the cistern simply… gathered.

But then it moves.

Not much. A shift in surface texture, a change in the way the wall-light interacts with its outer layer — and you realize that what you took for mineral stain is skin, or something that serves as skin, and what you took for a seam-shadow is a fold, and the fold has depth, and the depth has purpose.

---

It is larger than anything else in the cistern. Not large in any absolute sense — curled into its corner, it might be the size of two fists pressed together, and some of that volume is the space its body encloses rather than the body itself. It is built like a question mark that has been folded in on itself, a curved and partially hollow structure with no obvious head, no obvious tail, no obvious distinction between its dorsal and ventral aspects. It wraps the corner the way a vine wraps a post, except that a vine has a direction of growth and this creature seems to grow in all directions simultaneously and then stop, holding itself in a permanent state of almost-expansion, a tensed unfurling that never completes.

Its surface is where the confusion begins. The outer layer is not uniform. In places it is smooth and slightly reflective, with that same dark iridescence you see in the photosynthetic patches — and looking more closely, you realize that the photosynthetic patches on the ceiling, one of them, the one nearest to this corner, is not a separate organism at all. It is part of this creature. Or this creature is part of it. Or the distinction has been negotiated away over a timescale long enough that the negotiation itself has become the organism. The dark, almost-black film that you took for primitive photosynthetic life is an outermost layer of something more complex: a symbiotic mantle, alive in its own right but integrated so thoroughly into the keeper's body that removing it would be like removing a liver. It faces the wall-light. It drinks what the walls remember of brightness. It feeds the interior.

In other places the surface is textured like the composite walls themselves — the same ceramic-metal grain, the same warmth, the same mineral-vein patterning — and this is the most disorienting thing about it: that its body has, over time, become a material echo of its environment, or its environment has become an echo of its body, and you cannot determine the direction of causality. The smaller creatures treat parts of its surface as wall. They graze its edges the way they graze the photosynthetic patches. They traverse its body on their way to feeding seams, six or eight pale legs clicking across a surface they may not distinguish from architecture. It permits this. More than permits — there are channels on its surface, faint grooves barely visible, that seem to guide the crawlers along specific paths, that funnel them toward particular regions where the biofilm is thickest, where their grazing serves some function for the keeper itself: cleaning, perhaps, or stimulation, or the delivery of microorganisms from elsewhere in the cistern to seed colonies the keeper cultivates on its own body.

It is a terrain. That is the first true thing you understand about it. It is not an animal in the way you carry that word. It is a terrain that is also an animal, or an animal that has become so deeply settled into its niche that it has taken on the properties of a place.

---

The interior is where the sound comes from.

Not all of the sound. Not the bass throbs, not the mechanical breathing of the larger structure — those come from beyond, from the vast engineered systems whose vibrations permeate everything. But the higher register, the biological chorus, the clicks and trills and whistling oscillations that you attributed to the water and the smaller creatures — some of that, yes, comes from the crawlers flexing their ventral plates. But the sustained tones, the ones that weave through the roar like a thread of silver through dark fabric, the ones that seem to emanate from everywhere and nowhere — those come from inside the keeper.

Its body is partially hollow. Not empty — no cavity in the cistern is ever empty — but structured around a series of internal chambers connected by narrow passages, and through these chambers, warm water flows. Not the pool water exactly, though the pool water feeds it: the keeper draws condensate in through openings along its underside, openings so fine they look like pores, and the water passes through its interior architecture in a slow, deliberate circulation driven by cilia or peristalsis or some hybrid mechanism that partakes of both. As the water moves through chambers of different sizes and shapes, the vibrations from the larger structure — the omnipresent roar — interact with the keeper's internal geometry. The chambers resonate. They select certain frequencies and amplify them, dampen others, introduce new harmonics through the interference patterns created by water moving through branching passages at varying speeds.

The keeper is an instrument being played by the building.

Or the keeper is a filter, translating the building's raw vibrational output into something more complex, more information-dense, more — and this is where your thinking stalls, because the word that wants to come is *meaningful*, and you don't know if that's projection or perception.

The sound it produces is not random. You are sure of this. You have been crouching in this space long enough now that the roar has become your baseline, and against that baseline you can hear the keeper's contribution as a distinct layer, and the layer has structure. There are phrases — recurring patterns of oscillation that last three or four seconds and then repeat, with variations, like a theme being explored. There are responses — when the building's vibrations shift, when a new harmonic enters the ambient roar, the keeper's internal resonance adjusts, not passively like a tuning fork but actively, as though it is selecting which aspect of the new sound to engage with and which to ignore. There are, unmistakably, durations of silence — moments when the water inside it goes still and the keeper contributes nothing to the sound-field, and in those silences the ambient roar sounds thinner, less coherent, as though something necessary has been subtracted.

The smaller creatures respond to its sounds. You can see it now. When certain phrases emerge from the keeper, the crawlers on the nearby wall shift their patterns — clustering more tightly at a particular seam, or dispersing from a surface they were grazing, or producing their own clicks in a rhythmic pattern that seems synchronized to the keeper's oscillations. Whether this constitutes communication, or merely a bioacoustic tropism, or something in between that doesn't map to either category — you don't know. You suspect the keeper doesn't know either, in the way that a heart doesn't know whether its beating constitutes communication with the lungs. It does what it does. The system coheres.

---

It eats slowly. Everything about it is slow. The photosynthetic mantle feeds it sugars at a rate that would starve anything with a metabolism calibrated for movement, for pursuit, for the energetic urgency of animal life. But the keeper does not move. Has not moved, you suspect, in a very long time — years, decades, some unit of time that makes more sense in the language of geology than biology. Its energy budget is spent almost entirely on three things: maintaining the internal water circulation that produces its sound, maintaining the surface ecology that the smaller creatures inhabit, and — this last one you infer rather than observe — growing.

It is growing. Slowly. Millimeter by millimeter, it extends along the seam where wall meets ceiling, its leading edges advancing through the mineral stain, incorporating the composite surface into its own border, blurring the line between its body and the structure that houses it. In places where it has been longest, the wall behind it has changed. The composite is slightly softer there, its grain altered by the keeper's metabolic chemistry, by the sustained warmth of its body, by the slow enzymatic processes that break down the ceramic-metal hybrid into something more biologically available. The keeper is digesting the wall. Not consuming it — not leaving a void — but transforming it, converting inert engineered material into living substrate, into an extension of itself that is also still wall, still structural, still warm and sound-conducting and capable of weeping condensate. It takes what was made and makes it alive without making it stop being what it was.

This is what it does. This is its function, if a creature this far outside any designed purpose can be said to have a function. It is the agent of the cistern's slow transformation from engineered space to living space. It is the reason the walls feel warm in a way that exceeds their material properties. It is the reason the mineral veining has that particular branching pattern — the pattern follows the keeper's historical growth, its centuries-long traverse of the cistern's interior surfaces, and the veins are its footprints, the metabolic traces it left behind as it moved through its youth (it must have had a youth, must have once been small enough to be mistaken for one of the crawlers, must have once moved freely through the pool before finding its corner and settling and beginning the long project of becoming architecture).

---

You look at it and it does not look back. It has no eyes. It has no sensory apparatus that you can identify as analogous to eyes, ears, or nose — and yet it knows you are here. You are certain of this because the sound has changed since you entered. The keeper's phrases have shifted: a new pattern has entered the rotation, one with a lower fundamental frequency and a slower repetition rate, one that might — if you are willing to extend your interpretation — be described as interrogative. It is tasting you through the vibrations your body introduces into the sound-field. Your breathing, your heartbeat, the micro-tremors of your muscles as you hold your crouch — all of this is information, conducted through the water and the walls, and the keeper's internal chambers are resonating with it, processing it through a medium that is acoustic and fluid and structural all at once.

It is deciding whether you are wall.

That is the assessment it makes about everything in its world, you realize. Wall or not-wall. Part of the structure or foreign to the structure. And the way it makes this determination is not rapid and binary but slow and probabilistic and ongoing: it will spend hours, days, processing your vibrational signature, comparing it to the baseline, integrating the data into its model of the cistern's interior. If you stay long enough — truly long enough, longer than you can stay, longer than your body would tolerate this crouch — it would reach a conclusion. And if the conclusion were *wall*, you might feel the first faint enzymatic touch at the edge of your shoe where it meets the water, the beginning of a negotiation that the keeper conducts with everything it contacts, the opening offer in a transaction that takes decades and that ends with incorporation.

It does not want to eat you. It does not want anything, not in the way you experience wanting. It metabolizes. It resonates. It grows. It converts. It maintains the sound-field that organizes the cistern's ecology. It turns dead matter into living matter with the patience of a glacier turning mountain into valley. If you are wall, it will eventually make you more alive. If you are not wall, it will eventually lose interest, the interrogative phrase will drop from its repertoire, and the sound-field will re-stabilize around your absence.

---

There is one more thing.

In the deepest part of the pool — the center of the concavity, where the water is warmest and darkest — there is a pale shape just barely visible beneath the surface. It is the size of a grain of rice. It is not moving. It is soft-bodied, featureless, curled into a comma, resting on the textured floor in the accumulated sediment of the cistern's slow mineral rain.

It is young. Impossibly young for something whose adult form takes centuries to become what it becomes. It detached from the keeper — you don't know when, you don't know by what mechanism, budding or fragmentation or something with no name in your biology — and it drifted down into the pool and settled, and now it rests in the warmest water, in the most vibrationally saturated spot in the entire cistern, bathed in the sound its parent produces, fed by the motes that drift against its body and are absorbed through a skin so thin you can see the faint movement of fluid inside it.

It is waiting. Not the way the flat predator on the wall waits — not with the coiled tension of ambush — but with the absolute, serene, geological patience of something that has nowhere to be and ten thousand years to get there. One day it will begin to move. One day, long after you have left this space, it will find its own corner, its own seam, and it will press its soft body against the warm composite and begin the slow work of becoming indistinguishable from the structure that houses it.

The keeper produces a sound — a low, sustained tone that is also a vibration, that is also a current in the water, that passes through the pool and through the pale comma-shaped body resting at its center, and the tiny creature responds with a movement so subtle it might be imaginary: a faint contraction, a pulse, a first small proof of resonance.

The cistern breathes. The cistern hums.

The keeper holds its corner, and the walls weep, and the sound goes on and on and on, and the smallest thing in the pool stirs once and is still, dreaming in a language made of water and vibration and the slow conversion of what was built into what lives.
