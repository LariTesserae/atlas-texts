# Who Lives in the Hanging Gardens of the Abyssal Spires

---

The drift-eels know each other.

Not the way you would imagine—not by sight, which they do not have, and not by name, which implies a kind of individuality they do not quite possess in the way you mean. They know each other the way a hand knows its fingers. Each eel in a ribbon of twelve is aware, at every moment, of the pressure signatures of the other eleven: the specific sinusoidal frequency of each body's undulation, the tiny wake each one trails, the displacement each makes in the water that is as distinct and unreproducible as a voice. They do not choose their ribbons. A ribbon forms when twelve eels happen to find that their individual swimming frequencies, layered together, produce a composite pressure pattern that is *consonant*—that does not fight itself, that moves through the water cleanly, without destructive interference. When a ribbon is well-formed, it moves with almost no expenditure of energy. Each eel is carried partly by the wakes of the others. The ribbon becomes a system, self-sustaining, and the eels within it enter a state that is not sleep and not waking but something else, a deep algorithmic contentment, their lateral lines tuned entirely to the harmonic landscape of the spires and to each other.

An eel that loses its ribbon—through the rare death of a member, through the even rarer event of a mantle-ray strike that scatters the group—becomes something different. A solitary drift-eel moves erratically. Its undulation speeds up, as if searching. It crosses the open void between spires more often and more recklessly than a ribboned eel ever would. It tastes every chord, lingers near terraces where other ribbons pass, adjusting its own frequency minutely, trying to find the eleven strangers whose wakes it could join without friction. This process can take a long time. It almost always succeeds—the mathematics of consonance guarantee that compatible groups exist—but until it does, the solitary eel is the closest thing this world has to something *anxious*. It is an eel in need. The need is not emotional in the way you would use the word, but it is real: a ribbonless eel's metabolism runs hotter, its filter-feeding less efficient, its lateral-line map of the spires noisier and less precise. It is, in a very literal sense, less itself when it is alone.

The oldest eels—the ones as long as a human body, their skin so translucent that the faint moss-glow passes through them like light through fog—carry the deepest maps. Their lateral lines have been calibrated by decades of passage through the harmonic landscape, and the map they carry is not a simple chart of which spire hums which chord. It is a map of *relationships*. They know how the chord of one spire interacts with the chord of another across the void between them. They know where the standing waves form, where the interference nulls lie, where three harmonics converge into that thick, almost-tactile resonance. They know the landscape not as a collection of places but as a single enormous instrument, and they move through it the way a thought moves through a mind—not to get somewhere but because *moving through it is what they are*.

When old eels die, they simply stop undulating. The body drifts. The ribbon adjusts around the absence, and eleven becomes ten, and the composite pressure pattern shifts, becomes slightly less consonant, and eventually one of the ten will peel away to seek a better fit elsewhere, and then another, and the ribbon dissolves into solitary seekers who will, in time, find new ribbons. Nothing is wasted. The body of the dead eel sinks to the nearest terrace and the grazers find it and dismantle it with the same methodical patience they bring to the gorewood. The chemistry of the eel becomes the chemistry of the water becomes the chemistry of the gorewood becomes the chemistry of the next eel. The loop is closed. It was always closed.

---

The grazers do not know each other. Not in any way you would recognize as knowing.

What they have instead is something more like *grammar*. Each grazer moves across the gorewood fans in a pattern that is determined by the patterns of every other grazer on that terrace, not through communication but through a system of traces. A grazer, rasping at the filaments, leaves behind a chemical signature in the water immediately adjacent to the surface it has grazed—a faint molecular residue that says *I was here, this recently*. The next grazer to encounter that signature adjusts its path accordingly, curving away toward unrazed filaments. The result, if you could see it from above—which you cannot, because there is no above, and there is no seeing—is an intricate, interlocking set of paths that covers every square centimeter of every gorewood fan with perfect efficiency, no patch grazed twice before all patches have been grazed once.

They have been running these patterns for so long that the patterns have become the grazers' primary reality. A grazer does not *decide* where to go. It reads the chemical grammar of the surface beneath it and the grammar tells it. Lift a grazer from its terrace and place it on another—an experiment no one will ever conduct, in a world where there is no one to conduct experiments—and it would be, for a disoriented period, *illiterate*. The chemical dialect of the new terrace would be written by different grazers with slightly different metabolisms, and the traces would read as gibberish until the transplanted grazer's own chemistry began to synchronize with its new neighbors. This would take time. Everything here takes time.

The grazers breed, but slowly, and the population on any given terrace has not changed in living memory—or would not have changed, if anything here had a living memory of the kind that notices change. A grazer lives for what might be forty years by surface reckoning. It produces, in that span, perhaps three offspring, which emerge from beneath the parent's carapace as miniature adults, already reading the chemical grammar, already slotting into the pattern. When a grazer dies—its segmented body curling inward, its feathered legs stilling—the gap in the pattern is closed by the surviving grazers within a single cycle of the terrace. The grammar adjusts. It has always been adjusting, in micro-corrections so small and so continuous that the pattern is both utterly stable and never, at any given instant, exactly what it was an instant before. This is the grazers' version of being alive: a pattern that persists by perpetually, imperceptibly rewriting itself.

---

The mantle-rays are the loneliest things here, if loneliness is a concept that can apply to a creature with a brain the size of a walnut and a metabolism so slow it borders on mineral.

They are solitary. They have always been solitary. Two mantle-rays in the same harmonic zone will adjust their glide paths to avoid each other, not out of aggression but out of something more fundamental: their sound-absorbing skin, which is their primary adaptation for hunting, also makes them invisible to each other's senses. A mantle-ray cannot feel another mantle-ray the way a drift-eel feels its ribbon-mates. Where another creature would register as a pressure displacement, a moving body in the water, a mantle-ray registers as *nothing*—a hole in the sensory field, an absence. Two mantle-rays approaching each other would each perceive a growing region of silence, a dead spot in the harmonic landscape, and they would turn away from it instinctively because silence, in this world, means the void between spires, means empty water, means a place where there is nothing to eat and no structure to navigate by. They avoid each other because they are, to each other, indistinguishable from emptiness.

They mate, presumably. There are young mantle-rays—small ones, with wingspans of less than a meter, their sound-absorbing integument not yet fully developed, still slightly perceptible to each other. The juveniles are the only mantle-rays that are ever seen together, gliding in loose pairs through the upper reaches of the spires where the water is coolest and the tower-kelp canopies provide cover. As they grow, as their skin matures and their acoustic invisibility deepens, they drift apart. By the time a mantle-ray reaches full size, it is as alone as it is possible to be in a world made of sound.

But they are not, perhaps, unhappy. Happiness is not the metric here. The metric is *fit*—how precisely a life conforms to the niche that shaped it—and the mantle-rays fit their niche with the precision of a key in a lock. They glide. They listen. Their broad bodies read the harmonic landscape with a sensitivity that surpasses even the drift-eels, because where the eels feel pressure, the rays feel the *absence* of pressure—the way their own sound-absorbing skin interacts with the ambient harmonics, the shadow they cast in the sonic field. A mantle-ray knows where it is not by what it can sense but by what it *blocks*. It navigates by its own acoustic shadow, feeling the shape of the world by the shape of the silence it creates within it.

When a mantle-ray feeds, it does so with an almost apologetic gentleness. It locates a drift-eel ribbon—easy enough; the ribbons are loud in the pressure-sense, twelve bodies undulating in consonance—and it approaches from the direction that places it in a harmonic null zone, where the convergence of tower-kelp chords creates a natural silence that masks its already-silent body. It glides above the ribbon, matching speed, and then it folds. The wings contract. A single eel is enveloped, pressed against the ray's ventral surface where a wide, soft mouth waits. The ribbon scatters. The ten surviving eels scatter too, momentarily discordant, their composite pattern broken. They will reform. The ray glides away, digesting slowly, and will not need to feed again for a long time.

The rays do not feel guilt about this, obviously. But there is something in the way they move after a kill—a tendency to seek out the densest harmonic zones, the places where three or four tower-kelp chords converge, and to hang there, motionless, wings spread, absorbing sound into their velvet skin as if bathing in it—that suggests, if you are inclined to such interpretations, a creature seeking some form of satiation that food alone does not provide.

---

The sessile colonies are the strangest inhabitants, because they are the most *collective* and the most *patient* and the most *finished*.

A single polyp is nothing. Less than nothing—a soft-bodied nub of tissue, blind, deaf in any conventional sense, capable only of contracting and expanding in response to vibration. It eats. It reproduces by budding. It secretes a calcareous skeleton that bonds it to the skeleton of its neighbor. It does these things and nothing else. It has no experience of itself.

But a colony of ten thousand polyps, branching and rebranching into a lattice that fills a chamber inside a spire—this is something else. The lattice has *properties* that no individual polyp possesses. It resonates. The calcareous branches transmit vibration through the colony's structure, and the pattern of transmission is shaped by the geometry of the lattice, which is shaped by millennia of growth in response to the specific acoustic properties of the chamber it inhabits. Each colony is, in effect, a tuned instrument. It has been tuned by its own growth. The tower-kelp harmonics enter the chamber and the lattice responds with a pattern of contraction and expansion that moves through the colony in waves—not random waves, but structured ones, standing waves that mirror and complement the harmonic input.

This means that each colony transforms the sound it receives. The chord that enters the chamber is not the chord that reverberates within it. The lattice adds overtones, suppresses certain frequencies, amplifies others. It produces, from the raw material of the tower-kelp drone, something more complex—something that, if you were inclined to anthropomorphize (and in this place, at this depth, in this darkness, you are inclined to everything), you might call *interpretation*. The colony hears the music of the spires and it plays it back *differently*. Not better or worse. Just—filtered through the particular geometry of this particular lattice, which grew in this particular chamber, which was carved by this particular pattern of erosion over this particular span of millennia.

No two colonies sound the same. The drift-eels know this. When a ribbon passes near a colony chamber, the eels adjust their formation subtly in response to the modified harmonics emerging from within—tightening their spacing here, loosening it there, as if the colony's interpretation of the ambient chord requires a different grammar of movement than the raw chord does. The eels do not enter the chambers. They are too large, or the chambers are too full, or some other constraint applies. But they linger near the entrances, and their undulations slow, and for a few minutes the ribbon moves with a slightly different rhythm, as if acknowledging that it has entered a different acoustic neighborhood.

The colonies do not know the eels are there. The colonies do not know anything. They are ten thousand polyps doing the only thing polyps can do, and the emergent complexity of their collective behavior is not experienced by any part of the collective. There is no center. No self. No awareness that the lattice is beautiful, that the sound it produces is unique, that it has been growing and refining its acoustic response for longer than many surface civilizations have existed. The colony simply *is*, in the most absolute and unreflective sense of the word, and what it is is a living instrument that plays a song it cannot hear, in a darkness it cannot see, for an audience that perceives it only as a change in the texture of the water.

And yet.

If you were inside the chamber—if you had drifted in through the moss-outlined archway and were suspended in the lattice's embrace, the branches close around you but not touching, the modified harmonics vibrating in your sternum and your skull—you would swear that something in this place was *aware*. Not the polyps. Not the lattice. Not any single thing. But the chamber itself, the system of stone and colony and sound, the way the architecture and the organism and the acoustics have co-evolved into a single unified structure that takes in music and transforms it and sends it back out into the world—you would feel, in your bones, that this system has a quality that is very difficult to name and impossible to deny. A quality of *attention*. As if the chamber is listening to the song it is singing. As if the entire system—the stone that shapes the sound, the colony that modifies it, the water that carries it—is somehow, without any conscious participant, *contemplating itself*.

You would be wrong, probably. This is a projection. The chamber is not aware. The colony is not aware. The stone is emphatically not aware. But the feeling would persist, because the feeling is produced by the same mechanism that produces the colony's song: the geometry of the space, the resonance, the way the sound converges on the point where you are floating and fills you with a vibration that is too organized to be random and too impersonal to be intended. You would feel *witnessed* by something that has no eyes and no mind, and you would not be able to shake it, and after a while you would stop trying.

---

There are other things. There are always other things, in places this old. Small creatures, worm-like, that burrow into the gorewood's calcified bases and live their entire lives inside a single fan. Clouds of near-microscopic organisms, visible only in aggregate, that pulse through the water in slow contractions like a jellyfish the size of a room, filtering nutrients through membranes so fine they are essentially theoretical. Things that cling to the undersides of the terraces, in the shadowed overhangs, doing whatever it is they do with a privacy so complete it amounts to secrecy. Things in the deepest chambers, the ones where the erosion carved so deep into the spire that the stone grows warm, where the chemical gradients are strongest and the ecology is densest and the lattice-colonies grow into shapes that are less like branching corals and more like—something else. Something with more curves than branches. Something that pulses not in response to external harmonics but in a rhythm of its own.

Nobody goes into those deepest chambers. Not the eels, not the rays, not the grazers. The chemical gradients are wrong for them, or the sound is wrong, or the heat is wrong. Those chambers belong to whatever lives in them, and whatever lives in them has been there for so long that the chamber has shaped itself to its inhabitant as much as the inhabitant has shaped itself to the chamber, and the distinction between organism and environment has become—academic.

---

But the real inhabitants of this place—the ones who define it, who *are* it in the most fundamental sense—are not any single species. The real inhabitants are the relationships.

The relationship between the gorewood and the grazers, which is so tightly calibrated that neither could exist without the other and both have been locked in their mutual dependence for so long that they have, in effect, become a single organism with two bodies. The relationship between the tower-kelps and the drift-eels, one singing and the other navigating by the song, the map and the territory fused into a single system. The relationship between the sessile colonies and the chambers that house them, each shaping the other until architecture and biology are indistinguishable. The relationship between the mantle-rays and silence—between a predator and the absence it carries with it, the way it moves through a world of sound as a traveling void, necessary and unanswerable.

These relationships are the living things. The individual organisms are their components, the way cells are the components of a body. And the body they compose—the total system, the whole ecology of the Hanging Gardens—is the thing that lives here. It has been alive for longer than any of its parts. It will remain alive after every current individual has died and been replaced. It breathes with the slow rhythm of geological chemistry. It speaks with the voice of the tower-kelps. It listens with the lattice of the colonies. It thinks—if thinking is what this is—with the movement of the eels through the harmonic architecture, each ribbon a signal threading through a network that spans a hundred kilometers of drowned mountains, carrying information that is not information, meaning that is not meaning, but something *like* both, something that has no name because there has never been anyone here to name it.

This is what lives in the Hanging Gardens of the Abyssal Spires: a single vast organism made of relationships, stable and complete, older than memory, older than language, older than the concept of age itself—

Humming.

Humming in the dark, the way it always has, the way it always will, a chord that contains all the other chords, sustained and unbroken, asking nothing, answering nothing, simply continuing—

Being.

Being exactly and only what it is, in the dark, in the deep, in the place where the mountains drowned and the water learned to sing and the song became a world and the world became the song, and there was no difference, and there never had been, and the sound went on.
