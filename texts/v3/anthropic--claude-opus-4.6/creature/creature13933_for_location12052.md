# The People of the Crown

---

They are small. That is the first thing you notice, and it stays with you, not as a diminishment but as a rightness — a proportion. They are scaled to this place the way the shrubs are scaled to it, the way the birds are scaled to it. A tall adult might reach your chin. Most are shorter. Their frames are compact, broad through the chest and shoulders — the deep chest of a population that has breathed thin air for so many generations that their lungs have simply *become more*, denser with capillaries, wider in the barrel of the ribs, so that breathing here looks effortless in them while you labor at it. Their legs are short relative to their torsos, their feet wide and sure, toes spread — they walk the narrow passages and the gently curved stone surfaces with a flat-footed sureness that you, with your narrow shoes and your assumptions about flat ground, cannot replicate. They do not stumble. You never see one of them stumble.

Their skin is the color of the stone. Not metaphorically — or rather, metaphorically at first, and then you realize it's more literal than that. A pale warmth, ivory-tawny, with undertones that shift depending on the light: faintly golden where the flower spikes cast their glow, faintly lilac in the open haze-light, almost white in the deepest passages where reflected light bounces off dressed rhyolite walls. They are not pallid. The color is rich, saturated in its own quiet way, the way the stone itself is rich — seemingly blank from a distance, but up close full of grain and crystal-flash and subtle variation. Their skin has a dryness to it, not papery but firm, a texture that speaks of the dry warm air and the mineral dust that settles on everything. The older ones especially have this quality — their skin looks *finished*, the way the stone looks finished, every line and fold settled into its final expression.

Their hair is dark. Uniformly, strikingly dark against the pale skin — black with a faint reddish depth, like the drupes before they're fully ripe. They wear it in ways that manage the wind: close-cropped, or braided tight against the skull in patterns that you slowly realize are not merely decorative but *familial*, repeating with variations within clusters of people who share dwellings. The braiding patterns are a language you can't read, but you can see it being read by others — a glance at someone's head, and something is understood about where they belong, who they're connected to, which passage they'll turn down at the end of the day.

Their eyes are large. Proportionally larger than yours, adapted to the perpetual milky dimness, the pupils wide even in what passes for bright conditions here. The irises are pale — gray, or gray-green, or a gray so light it's almost silver, catching the flower-spike glow and holding it. In the dim passages, their eyes are sometimes the most visible part of them, catching and returning the scattered light. When several of them look at you at once, from the shadows of a shrub-archway, the effect is striking and a little uncanny — all those luminous pale eyes in dark-haired, stone-colored faces, attentive without being alarmed, curious in the measured way of people who have very few novel things to be curious about.

## How They Move

They move like people in a house they have always lived in, which is precisely what this is. No one looks where they're stepping. No one ducks consciously under the shrub-archways — they tilt at exactly the right moment, a fraction of a second before contact, the way you navigate your own hallway in the dark. Children run through the narrow passages in groups of three and four, their bare feet slapping the succulent carpet, and they take the turns without slowing, knowing the exact geometry of every corner. The older adults — and there are *very* old adults here, their age visible more in the depth of the lines and the slowness of their movements than in any fragility; they are not fragile, these old ones, they are *dense*, compacted by time the way the shrubs are compacted by wind — the older adults move with a measured deliberateness that is not caution but ceremony, a pacing that says *there is no reason to move faster than this, there has never been a reason*.

There is a particular gesture they all make, so habitual it takes you a long time to notice it as a gesture rather than a tic. When passing through a narrow spot — a doorway, a tight passage between buildings, a gap in the shrub thickets — they brush the stone or the wood or the living plant with the fingertips of one hand. Not stroking it. Not leaning on it. Just — touching. A light drag of the fingers across the surface as they pass. The children do it. The ancient ones do it. Everyone does it, the same hand, the same light contact, as reflexive as breathing. You watch for a long time before you understand: they are reading the surface. The temperature, the moisture content, the texture. They are checking the settlement the way you might check your own pulse — continuously, unconsciously, taking the measure of the place they are part of. The stone speaks to their fingers and their fingers listen and none of it rises to the level of conscious thought. It is deeper than that. It is structural.

They carry things constantly. There is always something in someone's hands — a drupe being peeled, a tool being shaped, a coil of fiber stripped from succulent leaves being twisted into cord. They work while they walk, while they talk, while they watch their children, while they eat. This is not industriousness in the way you might mean it, not productivity or urgency. It is the same as the fingertip-brushing: a continuous low-level engagement with the material world, hands always in dialogue with substance. To sit with empty hands, you come to realize, would feel to them the way silence would feel — like something is wrong, like a signal has dropped out, like the ongoing conversation between body and place has been interrupted.

## How They Speak

The language is quiet and fast. You hear it as a patter, almost percussive, consonant-heavy, with very short vowels and frequent glottal stops — a language shaped by the constant background noise of the bird-chatter, evolved to be intelligible *through* that noise rather than over it. They do not raise their voices to be heard. They sharpen them. The consonants click and tap, cutting through the ambient sound the way the drupe shells click when sorted, the way the stone tools tap — and you realize after a while that the human speech and the work-sounds and the bird-calls have all converged into a single acoustic texture, that the people have tuned their language to fit the soundscape the way they've tuned their buildings to fit the stone. They speak as part of the noise, not above it.

Conversations are overlapping, simultaneous, multi-threaded. In a group of five, three might be speaking at once, and all five are following all three threads — you can see it in the eye movements, the slight head-tilts, the way a response will address something said not by the last speaker but by the one before, or the one before that. It is not interruption. It is polyphony. They are doing with speech what the birds do with territory calls: maintaining multiple simultaneous signal-streams in a shared acoustic space. A newcomer to this — you, for instance — hears chaos. But watch their faces and you see the tracking, the micro-responses, the way attention flickers between speakers with a precision that speaks to a lifetime of practice in exactly this kind of dense communicative environment. They are never confused by it. They are *swimming* in it.

There is also a quieter register, used in the deeper passages and the sleeping chambers, where the bird-noise is muffled by stone: a slower, softer speech, vowels lengthening, consonants losing their percussive edge, the whole cadence dropping into something almost murmured. This is the speech used with the mammalian creatures in the lower chambers. This is the speech used with very small children. This is the speech used, you think, between people who are as close as the stone is to the roots inside it — people who have grown into each other over years, whose communication has worn itself smooth, whose words have settled into their final possible shapes.

They also whistle. Short, sharp, single-note whistles that carry over the bird-noise and across the full width of the summit. These are directional, informational: *come here*, *I need you*, *food is ready*, *look at this*. The whistles are distinct to individuals — you learn this when you realize that heads turn selectively, one person responding while others ignore the same sound. Each person has a whistle-name, a specific pitch-and-rhythm pattern that belongs to them, and the air of the settlement is laced with these calls at all times, a sparse overlay on the dense chatter, people locating and summoning each other across the labyrinth of passages and chambers.

## How They Eat

They eat constantly, in small amounts, the way the shrubs drink the stone — not in meals but in a continuous slow intake that is part of the rhythm of the day. A handful of drupes, the shell cracked between the teeth (their teeth are strong, broader and flatter than yours, the molars especially wide, adapted to exactly this work of cracking hard shells and grinding dense fruit-flesh). A raw egg, the blue shell punctured with a thumbnail and the contents tipped back in a single practiced motion. A smear of drupe paste — fermented, sharp-smelling, the color of old wine — scooped from a communal clay pot with two fingers and eaten while walking. A strip of something dried and leathery that might be processed succulent leaf, or might be something else, chewed slowly and meditatively while working.

The fermented drupe paste is the dietary center of gravity. It is in every dwelling, in clay-lined pits and sealed stone vessels, in various stages of fermentation — fresh and sharp, or aged into something darker and more complex, almost savory, with a depth of flavor that you are not prepared for. The fermentation is managed with care: specific temperatures maintained by the placement of the pits relative to the geothermal warmth rising through the stone, specific durations marked by — you're not sure what. They seem to know. The way they know when the stone surface is fractionally cooler or warmer, the way they know which whistle-name is whose. The paste is food, condiment, social glue. Offering it to you — a dark smear on a flat piece of dressed stone, presented with a directness that is not ceremony but also not casual — is the first thing they do when they decide you are going to be fed. It is how they answer the question of whether you are a guest or an intruder: if they feed you the paste, you are inside the system. If they do not, you are still part of the wind.

The eggs are currency and gift and punctuation mark. Someone finishes a task, cracks an egg. Someone meets someone in a passage, offers an egg. Children trade them, argue over them, carry them in small woven pouches slung across their chests. The rust-breasted birds that produce them are tended with a seriousness that borders on reverence — their woven cages are the most carefully maintained structures in the settlement, rewoven and repaired with a frequency and attention that nothing else receives. The birds are named. You hear the names spoken in the softer register, the murmuring voice, the voice used for intimate things. These birds have been partners in this place for as long as the shrubs have been growing, maybe longer. The relationship is not ownership. It is older and stranger than that, and neither side could say when it started or how it would end if it ended.

## What They Know

They are not isolated in the way you might imagine. They know the haze below them holds a world. They speak of it — you gather this from context, from gestures toward the drop-offs, from a particular tone of voice that seems to mean *out there, down there, that other kind of place*. But they speak of it the way you might speak of the ocean floor: a real place, certainly, a place with its own rules and inhabitants, but not a place that has much bearing on the daily texture of your life. Occasionally — rarely — someone comes up from below. You can tell who they are because they breathe wrong. They gasp. They sit down too often. They look at the edges with a terror that the summit people find genuinely puzzling, because to the summit people the edges are simply where the world stops, the way a wall is where a room stops, unremarkable, not calling for any particular emotional response.

What they know is this place. And they know it the way you know your own body — not as information but as *being*. They know which section of the succulent carpet is thinning and needs to be left alone so it can regrow. They know which shrub is producing fewer drupes this season and which is overburdened and should have branches trimmed to redirect its energy. They know the temperature gradient across the summit, the way the geothermal warmth pools in the southeastern basins and dissipates near the northern rim. They know where the lizards are nesting this year, which means they know which passages will have more midges, which means they know which fermentation pits will work faster and which will need to be watched for contamination. They know the root-networks in the stone, not mapped but felt — sensed through the fingertips brushing the walls, through the temperature of the floors, through subtle changes in the resonance of the stone when tapped. They know when a root-channel has reached a new fracture and opened a fresh source of mineral water, because the stone above it changes color slightly, grows fractionally cooler, and the succulents nearby put out an extra flush of flower spikes.

This knowledge is not taught in any formal way. It is absorbed. Children grow up inside it the way the roots grow inside the stone — finding the fractures, following the paths of least resistance, slowly filling in the available space with understanding. By the time a child is old enough to braid their own hair, they know everything they need to know. Not because someone sat them down and told them, but because they have been touching the walls and listening to the birds and cracking drupe shells and watching the flower spikes for their entire lives, and the knowledge has seeped into them through every sense simultaneously, the way the hydroxyl groups seep from the crystal lattice into the roots.

## What They Make

Art is the wrong word but it's the closest one you have. They carve the stone. Not decoratively — or not *only* decoratively. They carve channels and patterns into the rhyolite surfaces of the passages and dwelling walls, and these carvings are — you stare at them for a long time before you understand — maps. Not spatial maps. Temporal maps. They record the history of the root-networks, the slow progression of the lithophyte roots through the mountain over generations. Each line in the carving represents a root-path. Each branch in the pattern represents a fracture exploited, a new direction taken, a vein of mineral water found and followed. The carvings are updated incrementally — a new line added here, an old one deepened there — and the oldest ones, in the deepest and most central dwellings, are so dense with overlapping root-histories that they've become almost abstract, a lacework of incised lines that covers entire walls in a pattern that mirrors, you realize with a start, the actual root-network visible through the translucent stone beneath your feet.

They are recording the life of the mountain. The mountain's slow consumption by the things that live on it and in it. The reciprocal process by which the stone becomes plant becomes food becomes person becomes, eventually, dust and waste that settles back into the cracks and is reclaimed by the stone. The carvings are the closest thing they have to writing, and what they are writing is the autobiography of a single organism — summit, stone, root, plant, bird, lizard, midge, mammal, human — that has been telling its own story for longer than anyone can remember and that will continue telling it, line by incised line, for as long as the stone holds and the roots drink and the wind blows over the quiet, full, finished crown of the mountain.

## The Old Ones

In the deepest chambers, where the geothermal warmth is strongest and the stone walls are thick enough to muffle even the bird-chatter to a soft distant wash, the oldest members of the community spend their days. They are very old. How old is hard to say — the signs of age here are different from what you're accustomed to. Their skin has taken on the quality of the stone itself, pale and fine-grained and smooth in a way that is beyond wrinkles, as if the lines have deepened and then *settled*, the way erosion channels settle, into a final topography. Their hair, still dark, has thinned to a fine covering through which the shape of the skull is visible — and their skulls are beautiful, smooth-domed, echoing the shape of the summit itself, a rhyme between body and place that feels too perfect to be coincidence and too deep to be intention.

These old ones do two things.

They carve. The densest, most intricate root-maps are theirs, worked with tools so worn and familiar that the handles have taken the shape of specific hands — you can see the finger-grooves, each tool belonging to one person, shaped by decades of the same grip. Their carving is slow, a few strokes a day, each one placed with the deliberation of someone who has watched the root-networks for an entire lifetime and knows exactly where the next line should go. They do not consult the stone beneath them. They don't need to. The map in their mind and the map in the stone and the map on the wall are the same map, maintained in triplicate through a lifetime of continuous attention.

And they tend the mammals. The pale-furred creatures of the deep chambers are most at ease here, with the oldest people, in the warmest and quietest spaces. You see them finally, clearly, in this deep stillness: they are the size of a large cat, round-bodied, with short dense fur the color of the rhyolite, small ears pressed flat against broad skulls, and large dark eyes — the inverse of the people's pale eyes, as if between them they've divided up the available ways of seeing in the dimness. They are slow-moving, warm-blooded furnaces, their body temperature palpably high even from a distance. They press against the old ones' sides and laps, and the old ones rest their carved-worn hands on the dense fur, and there is a stillness in these pairings that is the quietest thing on the summit — the one pocket of something approaching silence in this otherwise ceaselessly sonorous place. The chirring of the mammals and the slow breathing of the old ones and the faint distant wash of the birds above. That is all. It is not silence. It is the sound this place makes when it is most fully and completely itself.

The old ones will die here. Their bodies will be placed in deep clefts in the stone at the summit's edge, wrapped in mats of succulent fiber, and the roots will find them. This is not spoken of as loss. It is spoken of — in the murmuring register, the soft voice, the voice used for intimate things — as *returning*, the way water returns to stone, the way stone returns to mineral, the way mineral returns to root. The root-maps on the walls will record it: a new line, a new branching, a new path followed, a new fracture exploited. The mountain takes back what the mountain gave, and the story continues, and the people above go on brushing the walls with their fingertips, cracking blue-shelled eggs, speaking in fast consonants through the bird-noise, shaping the stone that shapes them, wearing down and being worn, arriving and arriving and arriving at themselves.

## The Children

But attend to the children, because the children are where you see that this place is not merely ancient but *alive* — not a museum of equilibrium but a system still in the process of discovering what it is.

The children are louder than the adults. Their voices have not yet been tuned to the bird-chatter; they shout, they shriek, they make sounds that have no informational content at all, pure vocal exuberance that rises above the layered soundscape like bright flags above a gray-green canopy. The adults do not shush them. The birds absorb the extra noise, adjusting their own calls upward in frequency and volume, and the system accommodates, flexes, and the children's shouting becomes part of the texture too, a higher harmonic, a voice the settlement didn't have a moment ago but now cannot do without.

They are experimenting. You see a child, maybe five or six years old, pressing her palm flat against a passage wall — doing the fingertip-brush that everyone does, but doing it *wrong*, doing it with her whole hand, doing it slowly and deliberately instead of in passing, her head tilted, her pale eyes half-closed, listening to the stone with a concentration that is fierce and private and entirely her own. She is learning something that no one has taught her because no one *can* teach it. It is the thing that happens between hand and stone, and it is different for every hand, and she is finding her own version of it, the fit that will be hers for the rest of her life on this summit.

You see another child, older, maybe ten, sitting alone at the edge of the settlement, near the drop-off — close enough that you feel a vertigo he clearly does not — staring out into the haze below. He is not longing. He is not dreaming of elsewhere. He is looking at the haze the way his grandmother looks at the root-map on her wall: as information, as texture, as part of the world that is his to read. He watches the way the haze moves, the slow convective coils of it, the way it thins and thickens. After a long time, he turns and goes back into the passages, and you wonder what he learned, and you know you'll never know, and you know he'll carry it in his body for sixty years and it will show up one day as a new line on a wall somewhere in the deep chambers, a line that means: *the haze does this, I saw it, the world is this shape*.

The children play with the lizards, fearlessly, catching them and releasing them, draping them over their arms and letting them scrabble up to their shoulders. They play with drupe pits, flicking them through the passages in games whose rules are opaque and apparently infinite, the pits clattering off the stone walls in patterns that might or might not be intentional. They climb the shrub thickets, which the adults never do, pressing into the dense interlocking branches and finding spaces — hidden hollows, gaps in the canopy, tiny rooms within the living wood — that only a child's body can reach. They come out with scratches and dark leaf-stains on their pale skin and expressions of private satisfaction. They have been somewhere no one else goes. They have found something. They will not tell you what.

This is how the place stays alive: not by changing, but by being re-discovered, generation after generation, by small hands pressing against stone, by pale eyes staring into haze, by voices not yet tuned to the frequency of the world they were born into, still searching for their note, still arriving.

## You

And now there is you, breathing hard in the thin dry air, standing in a passage between two stone-and-succulent buildings, the camphor smell in your nostrils, the bird-chatter filling your skull, a dark smear of drupe paste on a flat stone being offered to you by a short, broad-chested person with silver-gray eyes and a braiding pattern you cannot read, who is looking at you with an expression that is not welcome and not unwelcome but something more neutral and more profound — the expression of someone encountering a novel thing in a world where novelty is so rare as to constitute a kind of event, a minor geological event, a temporary disruption in a system that has been stable for longer than your entire civilization has existed.

You take the paste. You eat it. It is sharp and deep and strange, a flavor that seems to go on longer than a flavor should, unfolding in layers, the history of a fermentation process that began before you arrived here and will continue after you leave. It tastes, you think, the way the place sounds — layered, dense, complex, not beautiful exactly but *complete*, every frequency occupied, every niche filled, a fullness so thorough that it approaches a kind of perfection.

The person nods. A minimal gesture, barely a movement. And turns and walks away down the passage, fingertips trailing along the stone wall, bare feet sure on the springy carpet, disappearing around a corner into the warm dim labyrinth where a hundred and fifty or two hundred people live inside a mountain that is being slowly, lovingly, patiently, gratefully consumed by everything that lives on it, and that is consuming them in return, and that will go on doing so — the mutual feast, the shared becoming, the long slow dissolve of the boundary between the living and the lithic — until the wind finally takes the last of the stone, and the roots have nothing left to drink, and the birds go quiet, and the last egg is eaten, and the summit completes its long project of becoming sky.

But not yet.

Not for a long time yet.
