# The Keeper of Section 9

---

She is not the first and she knows it. Somewhere in the swollen clipboard, in that brick of pulp that used to be pages, there are names of others. She has never tried to separate the sheets. It would be like performing an autopsy on a cloud. But she knows they existed because of the things they left: the survey stakes, the painted letters on the weir, the PVC channels that now serve as eel-roads. People came down here with intentions. They measured. They labeled. They believed, for a time, that the Floodworks was a problem to be managed.

She is not managing anything.

---

Her name is Dafne, and she comes down every eleven days, though not because of a schedule. Eleven days is simply how long it takes. How long what takes, she couldn't tell you precisely—something like *saturation*, something like the feeling you get when a word you've been trying to remember finally arrives at three in the morning and you have to get up and *do something* with it or it'll dissolve. Eleven days on the surface and the pressure builds, a kind of acoustic phantom limb, her inner ear reaching for a sound that isn't there, and she descends.

She is fifty-three. She has a bad left knee that she does not favor, because favoring it in the water changes how she moves, and how she moves matters here. She wears chest waders that she patches with a kit designed for bicycle tires. She carries a headlamp she rarely turns on. She has discovered that her eyes, given twenty minutes in the pewter light, can see more than the headlamp reveals—the headlamp *flattens* things, turns the basin into a set of surfaces, whereas the ambient dimness lets her see *through* the water, *into* the mineral murk, down to where the pale eels wind and the crayfish go about their clicking diplomacy.

She was a hydrologist. She might still be. The county still sends her checks, though the amount has decreased three times in the past decade, each decrease accompanied by a letter she has kept but not read, stacking them in a kitchen drawer with the same care one might give to seeds one has no intention of planting. The monitoring station—the one with the sickly yellow-orange glow—is technically her responsibility. She maintains the generator. She replaces the fuel line when the mineral deposits constrict it, which happens roughly every fourteen months. She downloads data from instruments that measure water temperature, flow rate, dissolved mineral content, pH, conductivity. She uploads the data to a server that she assumes someone, somewhere, looks at. She has never been asked about it. She has never been thanked.

These tasks take perhaps forty minutes out of the eight to fourteen hours she spends below on each visit.

---

The rest of the time she walks. Wades. Stands. Listens.

She has been coming down here for nine years and she has not mapped the basin. This is a deliberate choice, though it took her three years to realize it was a choice and not a failure. In the early days she brought survey equipment. A laser rangefinder. Waterproof notebooks. She tried to establish fixed points, to triangulate, to impose a coordinate system on the space. But the space resists coordinates. The mudflats migrate. The channels shift. A stalactite nub she used as a reference point in April was three centimeters longer by October and had begun to merge with a neighboring formation, changing the ceiling geometry in ways that invalidated her measurements. The basin is not a place. It is an *event*, one that happens to be occurring slowly enough that you can stand inside it.

So she stopped mapping. And something opened up.

What opened was this: the ability to be *in* the Floodworks without needing the Floodworks to be anything in particular. To stand in the warm water and let the frog-sound fill her skull and not *interpret* it, not translate it into data, not need it to mean something she could write down. This is harder than it sounds. She is a scientist. Her training is a set of reflexes that fire constantly—*measure this, compare that, what's the rate of change, where's the trend*—and the Floodworks defeats every one of those reflexes by being too much, too dense, too simultaneous. You cannot measure everything at once. You cannot track ten thousand frog calls and also the mineral deposition rate and also the migration pattern of the eels and also the slow sagging of a bread-dough ceiling bulge. You have to choose, and the moment you choose, you've lost everything you didn't choose, and what you didn't choose was most of it.

She has learned, instead, to *not choose*. To stand in the warm water with her eyes soft and her ears open and let the whole system enter her without filtering. She does not understand what she perceives when she does this. She could not publish a paper about it. But she knows things about the Floodworks that no instrument has captured—she knows its *mood*, for lack of a less ridiculous word, its temperament on a given day, the way the acoustic texture shifts when a new channel opens somewhere deep in the basin and redirects a population of callers, the way the eels respond to barometric changes on the surface that she can't feel but they can, the way the bats and the frogs exist in a kind of antagonistic symphony, each raising its volume against the other in a war that has produced, over time, the composite roar that greets you on the stairs.

She knows the Floodworks is getting louder. Not quickly. But measurably, if anyone were measuring, which no one is except her, and she's not measuring so much as *remembering*. Nine years ago the sound on the stairwell was a roar. Now it is a roar with more roar inside it. The basin is filling—not with water, which maintains a rough equilibrium, but with *life*, with biological density, with creatures packing into the warming darkness at a rate that seems to have no ceiling. Something about this place—the warmth, the mineral richness, the protection from surface weather, the absence of large predators—has created conditions for a population explosion that has been running for longer than she's been watching and shows no sign of stopping.

She wonders, sometimes, what the upper limit is. Whether there is one. Whether the Floodworks will simply keep filling with life until it is a solid mass of frogs and eels and salamanders and crayfish and midges, a living block, every cubic inch occupied, the sound so dense it becomes silence again—silence by saturation, the way white is every color at once.

She does not think this will happen. But she does not know *why* it won't, and that not-knowing is one of the things that brings her back every eleven days.

---

She has a routine, and the routine is a path, and the path is not fixed because the basin won't allow fixed paths, but it has a *grammar*—a set of waypoints she visits in a flexible order determined by what she encounters.

She starts at the old pumping station. She checks the diesel engine, not because she intends to repair it—she doesn't, it's beyond repair, and anyway the salamanders have claimed it—but because the engine serves as a kind of census station. The species of salamander that nests in the cylinder housings is one she has never been able to identify. She has photographed them. She has sent the photographs to a herpetologist at the state university, who replied with a single email: *"Where did you say this was?"* She replied. He did not write back. The salamanders are the color of raw liver, as described, but she has noticed that their coloration varies by cylinder—the ones in cylinder three are slightly more purple, the ones in cylinder five slightly more brown—and she suspects they are diverging, speciating, right here, right now, in the housings of a defunct diesel engine, separated by inches of corroded metal. Evolution at the speed of the Floodworks.

She names them. Not the species—she wouldn't presume—but individual animals, when she can distinguish them. The largest female in cylinder three is *Margaret*. The male with the damaged tail in cylinder one is *Hector*. She has watched Margaret for six years now. Margaret has not moved from cylinder three in all that time. Margaret has grown. Margaret is now larger than any salamander Dafne has seen in any field guide, and she sometimes wonders if Margaret is something new, something the Floodworks is making, something that hasn't existed before and exists only here, in this specific cylinder of this specific engine in this specific unfinished landscape.

---

From the pumping station she wades south—or what she calls south; there is no compass bearing that holds meaning down here, only relative direction between landmarks that are themselves impermanent. South takes her along a channel that, three years ago, was the main flow path and is now a secondary braid, the water having found a more persuasive gradient a few meters to the left. She follows the old channel anyway, out of loyalty, or stubbornness, or because the eels still follow it too and she likes the company.

The eels. She has a relationship with the eels that she has told no one about because she has no one to tell and because it would sound insane. The eels *know her*. She is certain of this. Not in the way a dog knows its owner—there is no affection in it, no eagerness—but in the way a river knows its banks. She is a feature of their environment. They have incorporated her into their understanding of the basin. When she wades the old channel, the eels adjust their movements around her with a fluency that is not avoidance and not approach but something more like *acknowledgment*. They flow past her boots. They rest against her waders. Once, standing still in waist-deep water in a channel she'd been monitoring for mineral deposition, she felt an eel wind slowly up her leg, over her hip, across her chest, and off her shoulder, using her body as terrain, as geography, as something the landscape had provided for it to navigate. She stood perfectly still for this. It took four minutes. She has never felt more included in anything.

The eels are pale—not albino, she thinks, but *tending toward* albino, the pigment leaching out of them generation by generation in the darkness. Their eyes are still functional but seem to be decreasing in size. She measures this with her memory, not with calipers. The eels she sees now have smaller eyes than the eels she saw nine years ago. This should take longer than nine years. Unless it's happening faster here. Unless the Floodworks accelerates things. Unless the pressure of all this life packed into all this becoming-space creates evolutionary urgencies that don't exist on the surface.

She doesn't know. She writes nothing down. The water would claim it anyway.

---

Halfway across the basin—she thinks of it as halfway, though she has never reached the far side, and for all she knows there is no far side—there is a formation she calls the Proscenium.

It is a place where the ceiling drops to about fifteen feet and the stone has deposited in a smooth, almost architectural arch, framing a view of a wide, shallow expanse of water beyond. The arch is accidental—geology doesn't have intentions—but it is beautiful in the way that accidental things sometimes are, when they happen to align with some template buried deep in the human visual cortex, some inherited sense of *threshold*, of *entrance*, of *here is where you pass from one kind of space into another*.

The acoustics at the Proscenium are extraordinary. The arch focuses the frog-sound like a parabolic reflector, compressing it, intensifying it, layering it into a chord so dense and rich that standing beneath the arch is like standing inside a bell that is being struck continuously from every direction. Dafne goes to the Proscenium when the eleven-day pressure is at its worst, when the silence of the surface has become unbearable, when she needs to be *annihilated* by sound, dissolved in it, broken down into a component of the choir rather than a listener to it. She stands under the arch and the sound enters her through her skin, through her chest wall, through the fluid in her inner ear, and for a few minutes she is not a hydrologist or a county employee or a woman with a bad left knee. She is a vibrating column of warm salt water, resonating at the frequency of the Floodworks, indistinguishable from it.

She comes back from this. She always comes back. But each time, she comes back a little more slowly, and the surface feels a little more foreign when she gets there, and the eleven days until the next descent feel a little more like exile.

---

She has thought about not coming back. About staying.

Not in a dramatic way. Not in a *walking into the sea* way. In a practical way. She has calculated: the water is warm enough to sustain her body temperature indefinitely. There is food—the crayfish are edible, the snails almost certainly, the eels definitely. The water, once filtered through the mineral deposits, is drinkable, if chalky. She could build a platform on one of the more stable mudflats, above the water line, using materials from the corrugated shed. She could live here. The generator has fuel. The instruments would keep running. She would become another feature of the basin, another organism in the choir, another thing the eels would flow around.

She has not done this. She doesn't think she will. But the thought sits in her like a stalactite nub—small, growing, accreting a little more mineral with each visit, a little more substance, a little more weight.

---

What keeps her on the surface, she thinks, is not attachment to the surface but a suspicion that the Floodworks needs her to come and go. That her oscillation—eleven days up, eight to fourteen hours down—is itself part of the system, a rhythm the basin has incorporated the way it incorporated the eels, the way it incorporated the diesel engine. She is a tidal function. She is a cycle. If she stayed permanently, she would stop being a rhythm and start being a constant, and the Floodworks has enough constants. What it doesn't have—what nothing down here provides—is *periodicity*, a reliable coming and going, a pulse.

She doesn't believe this, exactly. It's not a belief. It's more like a stalactite—a structure her mind has begun depositing around an empty space, building up slowly, not finished, not yet solid enough to bear weight. But it's there. It's accreting.

---

There are other visitors. Not many. Not often.

Twice a year, a team from the county comes down to inspect the monitoring station. They wear hard hats and carry powerful flashlights that ruin everything—that flatten the basin into surfaces, that scatter the bats, that silence the nearest frogs for hours, creating a spreading circle of quiet that takes a full day to heal. The team stays for ninety minutes. They check boxes on forms. They take water samples that Dafne knows will sit in a lab refrigerator until someone throws them out. They do not go past the pumping station. They do not see the Proscenium. They do not meet the eels. They climb the stairs with their hard hats and their flashlights and their samples, and they leave, and the Floodworks closes behind them like water closing over a stone, and within a day there is no evidence they were ever here.

Once, three years ago, a group of cavers found an entrance—one of the ceiling gaps, wide enough to rappel through—and descended into the basin about a quarter mile from the pumping station. Dafne was there that day. She heard them before she saw them: human voices, sharp and distinct and *alien* against the frog-sound, cutting through it like a blade. There were four of them. They had headlamps and ropes and wetsuits and the particular loud confidence of people who enter wild places believing those places exist for them to enter.

She watched them from a distance, standing still in thigh-deep water, her headlamp off. They didn't see her. They explored for about two hours, exclaiming at the formations, catching crayfish, recording the frogs with a microphone that would capture nothing usable—the sound down here defeats recording equipment the way it defeats everything, by being *too much*, by overwhelming the dynamic range of any device designed for the quieter world above. They found the weir and read the painted letters and took photographs. One of them said, *"Someone built all this?"* and another said, *"Looks like they gave up,"* and they laughed, and the laughter bounced off the wet stone and was absorbed by the choir and was gone.

They left through the same ceiling gap, hauling themselves up into grey daylight, and Dafne stood in the water and watched the pale circle of sky where they'd vanished and felt something she couldn't name—not relief exactly, not loneliness exactly, but something adjacent to both, something with the texture of a frog call she hadn't learned yet, a new frequency in the choir.

---

There is one more thing she has noticed, and it is the thing that frightens her, if *frightens* is the right word for a sensation so slow.

The Floodworks is rising.

Not the water level—that remains roughly stable, governed by inputs and outputs she mostly understands. The *basin* is rising. The mineral deposition, the slow accretion of calcium and silite on every surface, is building the floor up. Millimeters per year. Undetectable without instruments, and she has instruments, and she checks them, and the data is clear: the floor of the Floodworks is growing toward the ceiling. The stalactite nubs are lengthening downward. The ceiling bulges are sagging lower. The space is *closing*.

Not soon. Not in her lifetime. Not in several lifetimes. But the geometry is clear: the Floodworks is building itself shut. The warm mineral water that created this space is now filling it in, the same process that hollowed the basin now depositing material back into it, and in some number of centuries—she can calculate this, she has calculated this, on a napkin at her kitchen table with a glass of wine that sat untouched until it was warm—in some number of centuries the ceiling will meet the floor and the Floodworks will be solid stone again. A fossil. A record of a chamber that once existed, written in mineral layers, with the bones of frogs and eels and salamanders pressed between the strata like flowers in a book.

The animals will be gone. The sound will be gone. The warm water will find another way, another hollow, another basin to fill and then slowly, slowly fill in. The Floodworks is not permanent. Nothing down here is permanent except the process, the cycle of hollowing and filling, the geological breath.

And standing in the warm water, in the sound, knowing this, she feels not grief but something stranger—a kind of *privilege*, acute and unbearable, at being here now, at this exact moment in the breath, when the hollow is open, when the choir is at its loudest, when the eels still have eyes and the salamanders are still deciding what species to become and the ferns in the ceiling cracks are still startled to be alive.

She is here for this. She is here *during* this.

She climbs the stairs. The sound recedes. The condensation dries on her skin as she rises, and the air gets cooler, and the pressure drops, and by the time she reaches the surface she can hear individual things again—a car, a bird, the wind—and each individual sound is a loneliness, a single voice where there should be a choir, and the eleven days begin.

---

Below, in cylinder three of the diesel engine, Margaret blinks. The mineral water drips. An eel follows a channel that will not exist next year. A stalactite adds a molecule to itself. The frog-sound fills the basin to its brim and spills up the stairwell and dissipates into the surface air, where no one hears it, where it becomes just another component of the noise of a world too large and too quiet and too bright, a world that sits on top of this one like a lid on a box of sound, not knowing what it holds, not knowing what is singing beneath it, not knowing that the singing will outlast the singers, that the silence will outlast the singing, that the stone will outlast everything, and that even the stone is only temporary, only a breath, only one slow exhalation in a process that has no audience and no end.

Dafne drives home. She does not turn on the radio. She lets the quiet accumulate around her like mineral deposit, like calcium milk, like the slow patient accretion of absence.

Eleven days.

She'll be back.
