# Who Lives in Ketharn

---

The lizards live there. Let's begin with them, because they are the most certain inhabitants, and certainty is worth anchoring to before we move into murkier territory.

They are not a colony so much as a distribution. Each one occupies a specific territory — a particular drainage channel, a particular set of crevices, a particular basin — and these territories have been occupied by lizards of this species for so long that the stone where they rest most frequently is microscopically polished by the oils of their bellies, a smoothness you would never notice unless you were another lizard. They do not fight over territory because there is nothing to fight over. The territories are established. When one dies — which happens rarely, quietly, in the deeper crevices where the body desiccates and eventually becomes calcium and powder indistinguishable from the stone — another, younger one shifts into the vacancy, and the younger one's former territory is taken by one younger still, and so on, a slow cascade of minor relocations that might take a season to complete and constitutes the most dramatic social event in their world.

They know Ketharn more completely than anyone who built it ever did. They know which basins fill first. They know that the third terrace has a hairline crack in its back channel that allows a thread of moisture to seep into a crevice that no one carved, and that this crevice stays damp until midmorning, longer than any basin. They know the thermal map of every surface — which stones hold warmth longest after sunset, which cool fastest before dawn. They do not know these things the way a person knows a fact. They know them the way a root knows which direction is down. The knowledge is in their behavior, which has not changed in a very long time, which does not need to change, because Ketharn does not change.

They eat what lives in the deeper crevices. Small pale insects — soft-bodied things, translucent, that feed on the lichen and on traces of organic matter caught in the stone's texture. These insects are so small and so few that the lizards eat slowly, methodically, one at a time, the way someone reads a book they know they won't be given another of. The lizards are never full and never starving. They exist in the same state as everything else here: settled.

---

Now, the lichen.

The lichen lives there too, and deserves acknowledgment, because it has been there longer than the lizards and will be there after them. It does not experience habitation the way we mean the word, but it is *present* in a way that goes beyond mere attachment to a surface. It has shaped the stone it lives on — not dramatically, not even measurably by any human instrument, but at the scale of its own existence, it has etched faint acid trails into the limestone, has drawn minerals out and replaced them with its own slow body, has changed the texture of the walls it covers so that those walls now hold moisture fractionally longer than the bare ones, which in turn sustains the lichen, which in turn continues to alter the stone. This is a conversation between organism and substrate that has been going on for longer than the settlement has been a settlement. The lichen was on the ridge before the first cut was made. It simply continued onto the new surfaces as they were exposed, at its own pace, which is glacial, which is appropriate.

The lichen and the lizards do not interact. They share the space the way two thoughts share a mind — simultaneously, without contact, each following its own logic.

---

The insects live there. The grey-green grasses live there, in their cracks, in their patient way. The shrub on the uppermost terrace lives there, and it is worth pausing on the shrub because it is the largest living thing in Ketharn, and it has a quality that the other living things do not, which is *shape*. The grasses are tufts. The lichen is a film. The lizards are small and flat and press themselves into crevices. But the shrub has dimension — it rises, it branches, it occupies space in three directions. When the afternoon light hits it, it casts a shadow on the stone, and that shadow is the most complex shape in Ketharn: irregular, branching, organic in a way that nothing else here is. It is possible that if you lived in Ketharn long enough, you would begin to find the shrub beautiful, not for any aesthetic quality it possesses but simply because it is *different*, because it is the one thing that breaks the geometry of terrace and wall and stair and channel with a line that was not cut by a chisel or worn by water or wind.

The shrub does not know this. It lives there the way the stone lives there. Slowly, and without commentary.

---

But you are asking about people. You are asking whether someone comes home to these rooms.

Here is what can be said:

There are wear patterns on the floors of certain dwellings — the ones on the fourth and fifth terraces, mostly — that are not consistent with weathering alone. Weathering is even. It respects no path. But in these rooms, there is a faint track, a barely perceptible depression in the stone, that runs from the doorway to the back wall shelf, and another that runs from the doorway to the left side of the room (always the left side, always the side away from the prevailing wind, the side that would be slightly warmer and more sheltered), and these tracks suggest feet. Many feet, over much time, walking the same short routes. Doorway to shelf. Doorway to sleeping place. Shelf to doorway. These routes are so worn into the stone that they might be natural features to an untrained eye — slight undulations in the floor, meaningless — but they are not natural. They are paths. And paths imply walkers.

There are also — and this requires close looking, the kind of looking that the late afternoon light facilitates — faint marks on some of the interior walls. Not carved. Not painted. *Polished*. In two rooms on the fifth terrace, the wall beside the sleeping-height shelf has a patch, about the size of two spread hands, where the stone is marginally smoother and has a faint luster that the surrounding wall does not. This is the kind of mark left by skin. By a shoulder or a back resting against stone night after night, year after year, the oils and warmth of a living body slowly buffing the surface. The stone remembers this the way it remembers everything — passively, permanently, without interpretation.

Someone leaned here. Someone slept here, and in sleeping, shifted, and their shoulder blade polished a small region of wall into something almost soft.

These marks do not prove current habitation. They prove *habitation*. The tense is unclear.

---

Here is what the settlement suggests, if you let it speak in its own language of form and wear and negative space:

There were people here — are people here — who lived the way the lizards live. Within a system. Occupying specific spaces. Following specific routes. Taking water from the basins in the early morning. Returning to the same rooms. Engaging in something on the fifth terrace that involved water and the oval depression — bathing, perhaps, or the preparation of something, or a ritual whose meaning was inseparable from its mechanics. They were small, or they were comfortable with lowering their heads, which may amount to the same thing culturally. They did not accumulate possessions, or they accumulated only possessions that decay — fiber, skin, wood — which would be gone now, dissolved into the same pale dust that lies in the corners of everything.

They were not many. Perhaps fewer than the settlement could hold. It is possible that Ketharn was built for a population that it never fully contained — that it was designed with a margin, an expectation of growth that the water could not ultimately support, and so the upper and lower terraces were always less occupied, and the middle terraces, closest to the central water channels where the flow was most reliable, were always the most lived-in, which is consistent with the wear patterns, which are deepest on terraces four through six.

They did not write. They did not carve images. They did not mark their walls with symbols or their doorways with totems. Either they had no written language, or they wrote on materials that have not survived, or they simply did not feel the need to leave messages for the stone to hold. This last possibility is the most interesting. Most human habitations are *marked* — people scratch, paint, carve, press their handprints into wet plaster, leave something that says *I was here, I existed, remember me*. The people of Ketharn left nothing of the kind. Only the wear. Only the polish of a shoulder against a wall. Only the paths from doorway to shelf, shelf to doorway, doorway to basin, basin to doorway.

This suggests — and suggestions are all Ketharn offers — people who did not distinguish between themselves and the settlement. People for whom being in Ketharn was not an experience to be recorded but a state to be maintained. The way the lichen does not record its own growth. The way the lizard does not note the position of the sun. You do not document what you are inside of. You do not mark what you *are*.

---

So who lives in Ketharn now?

Walk through it in the early morning, when the condensation is still in the channels and the light has not yet come over the ridge. The basins hold their few inches of water. The air has that mineral almost-smell. A lizard is motionless at the rim of a basin on the third terrace, its snout just touching the surface, and it will remain like this for ten minutes, fifteen, in no hurry, because there is no such thing as hurry here.

Check the rooms. Duck through the doorways. Let your eyes adjust to the dim interiors. The shelves are empty. The niches are empty. The floor paths are there, if you know how to see them, faint as veins under skin. The shoulder-polish on the fifth terrace wall catches what little light enters and gives it back with a warmth that the rest of the stone does not have.

The rooms are not *abandoned*. That is the wrong word. Abandoned places have a feeling of interruption — a meal half-eaten, a door left open, a story stopped mid-sentence. These rooms have no interruption in them. They are *complete*. Whatever happened in them happened fully, all the way to its end, and the end was not dramatic, was not a catastrophe or a flight or a conquest. The end was the same as the middle, which was the same as the beginning. Someone slept here. Someone stopped sleeping here. The stone kept the polish.

It is possible — and this is the possibility that Ketharn most strongly suggests, the one that the wind and the water and the lizards and the lichen all seem to corroborate by their own continued, untroubled presence — that the people of Ketharn did not *leave*. That there was no departure. That the population, never large, simply diminished, the way a basin's water diminishes through the day: not by any sudden event, but by the ordinary slow process of evaporation. One generation slightly smaller than the last. A few rooms occupied, then a few fewer. The water still collecting, still sufficient for those who remained, still sufficient when those who remained were very few, still sufficient now, when those who remain may be —

What? None?

Or one?

---

Let me say what cannot be confirmed.

There is a room on the sixth terrace, the third from the left. It is a single-room dwelling, slightly smaller than average. Its doorway faces south-southwest, which means it receives the late afternoon light more directly than most, and the scalloped chisel marks on its back wall are lit up in the amber hour with particular clarity. Its shelf is at sleeping height. Its niche is at eye level from the shelf — the exact height at which someone lying on the shelf would see it without turning their head.

This room is marginally cleaner than the others. The dust in its corners is thinner. The basin nearest to its door — the second basin on the sixth terrace, set into the channel about four paces to the right of the doorway — shows, at its rim, a disturbance in the calcium crust that might be recent. A small section, about the width of two fingers, where the white deposit is thinner, as if something has been repeatedly placed there — the edge of a vessel, perhaps, or the heel of a hand, pressing down gently, cyclically, in the motion of someone scooping water.

This is not proof. The disturbance could be a natural variation in the mineral deposit. The cleaner floor could be an effect of airflow patterns particular to that doorway's orientation. These explanations are available. They are reasonable. The stone does not argue with them.

But the room has the shoulder-polish. And the shoulder-polish is — this is the thing that resists the tidy explanation — the shoulder-polish is *not dusty*. Every other surface in Ketharn, without exception, has a fine film of limestone dust, the perpetual slow shedding of the stone wearing against itself in the thermal cycle. The polish in this room on the sixth terrace has no dust on it. It is clean. It is as smooth and faintly lustrous as skin.

Something has leaned here recently. Something with warmth and weight and the patience to press against stone night after night in the same position, keeping the surface clear by the simple act of presence.

---

Perhaps it is this:

One person. The last one, or the latest one, or the only one — the distinction does not matter to the stone, and perhaps it does not matter to the person either. Small, as the doorways suggest. Thin, as the water supply demands. Dark-eyed, perhaps, like the lizards, though this is projection, is fancy, is the mind filling in what the stone does not record. Someone who sleeps on the shelf and rises before dawn and goes to the basin and scoops water with cupped hands and drinks and waits and watches the light move through its arc, the same arc, the same light, the same terraces emerging from blue shadow into amber glow and back into shadow.

Someone who does not feel alone because the concept requires a contrast that does not exist here. You cannot feel *alone* in Ketharn any more than a lizard can feel alone, or the lichen, or the shrub. You can only feel *present*. You can feel settled.

This person — if this person exists — does not wonder about the builders. Does not wonder about the others who came before. Does not think of themselves as the *last* of anything. They think of themselves — if they think of themselves at all, and they may not, not in the way you mean — as part of the system. Water collects. Water flows down. Water fills the basins. A person drinks. The stone wears. The light moves. These things are all one process, and the person is part of it, the way the channel is part of it, the way the calcium crust is part of it.

They have been here a long time. Or they have been here a short time and the people before them were here a long time and the distinction is not meaningful because the behavior is the same. Rise. Drink. Sit. Watch. Sleep. Lean against the wall. Leave no mark except the mark that leaning leaves, which is not a message but a consequence, which is the only kind of mark Ketharn accepts.

They may die here. They may already be dead. The room will not change. The basin will continue its cycle. The lizards will drink. The wind will come from the north, as it always has.

---

But there is one more thing, and it is small, and it may mean nothing.

On the flat platform at the top of the ninth terrace — the uppermost level, the one that might have been a watchtower or a ceremonial space or simply a place someone preferred to sit — there is a stone. A loose stone, about the size of a fist, that is not limestone. It is darker. It is dense and smooth, a river stone or a stone from some other geology, from somewhere that is not the ridge, not the plateau, not anywhere visible from Ketharn. It sits on the platform near the southern edge, where you can see the full sweep of the dry plateau stretching out below.

It was brought here. Carried up nine terraces, through forty-something stairways of softened steps, by someone who went somewhere else — somewhere with rivers, with different stone — and came back. And placed this one foreign object in the highest, most open, most wind-exposed spot in all of Ketharn, where it sits, darkly, being the one thing that is not pale yellowish limestone, the one thing that does not match, the one thing that insists, mutely, on the existence of an *elsewhere*.

It has been there a long time. The lichen has begun to grow on its north face. But it has not been there as long as the rest. Not nearly. It is the newest thing in Ketharn by what might be centuries, and it is the closest thing Ketharn has to a statement, and what it states is:

*Someone left. Someone came back. Someone set this down and stayed.*

The lizards do not go near it. It is not their stone. The wind moves over it the same as everything else. The light, in the amber hour, makes it glow faintly — a darker gold, a warmer note — and then the shadow comes, and it is just a shape on a platform, and Ketharn settles back into itself, which is the only thing Ketharn knows how to do, and whoever lives here settles with it, shoulder against the wall, breath as quiet as the draft over the terrace lips, present, neither the first nor the last, neither remembered nor forgotten, but *here*, in the way that the stone is here, in the way that the water is here each morning and gone each noon and here again, always here again, filling the basins to the same depth, leaving the same white crust, sustaining the same slow unmeasured unchanged and unchanging life.
