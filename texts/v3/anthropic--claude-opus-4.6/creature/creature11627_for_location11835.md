I have been sitting with this for a long time. Let me tell you what I think lives here — not all at once, because nothing here arrived all at once.

---

**The first and oldest thing** is the one beneath.

It is not an animal. It is not a root system, though it has been mistaken for one. It is a fungal body — a single organism, or what was once a single organism before the word "single" stopped applying — that colonized the clay beneath the depression so long ago that it predates the roots, predates the hollow's current shape, may predate the tree itself. Its mycelial network is vast and slow and reaches far beyond the boundaries of what you can see. But its densest mass, its thickest knot, lies directly below the center of the hollow, maybe four meters down, a bolus of living tissue the size of a small room, pale and damp and gently contracting.

That is the pulse.

It is not a heartbeat. It is peristalsis. The fungal body is moving nutrients through itself the way a gut moves food — in slow, rhythmic waves, contracting and releasing, contracting and releasing, once every few minutes, and each contraction sends a low-frequency pressure wave up through the clay and into the root-floor of the hollow. It has been doing this for longer than anything else here has been alive. It was doing this before there were frogs to go silent. It was doing this when the depression was just a dip in the ground, before the lateral roots curved over it, before the aperture narrowed, before the resonance began to find itself. The fungal body is the oldest voice in the chord, the ground note, the thing everything else was built on top of, and it has no awareness of this. It is simply digesting. It is simply being large and slow and alive in the dark, and the entire cathedral overhead is, in a sense, a harmonic overtone of its digestion.

Its mycelial threads penetrate the root-walls. They are everywhere in the wood, invisibly, softening the grain in precise patterns that change the acoustic properties of the root-surfaces — making some patches more resonant, some more damped, shaping the hollow's timbre from within. The beetles gather on patches where the fungal colonization is densest, not because they eat the fungus (though some of them do) but because those patches *sound best*, resonate most richly under the friction of their ridged wing cases. The fungus does not know it is being played. The beetles do not know they are playing it. The music is an accident of metabolism, which is what all music is, eventually, but here the accident has been running long enough to become indistinguishable from intention.

---

**The frogs** are the most important residents, and I think the description already suspects this — *it is possible that whoever did this is the frogs* — and I think the description is right, but not in the way it might mean.

There are eleven species. They have speciated here, in this hollow, from what was likely two or three ancestral species that arrived when the depression was younger and shallower and the roots had not yet closed overhead. They speciated not by geographic isolation but by *acoustic* isolation — by frequency. Each species occupies a band of the sound spectrum the way each species in a coral reef occupies a depth or a substrate. The lowest-voiced species, a squat brown creature the size of a walnut with a throat sac that inflates to twice its body volume, calls at a frequency just above the fungal pulse. The highest-voiced species is almost too small to see, a translucent jewel the size of a lentil that produces a sound like a single pixel of the insect curtain — you cannot distinguish its call from the stridulation around it, which is, in fact, the point, because its call *is* the bridge between the frog chorus and the insect chorus, the thing that knits the two registers together.

They did build the platforms. Not in any single generation, not with any plan. But frog species — these frog species in particular — do something that most frogs do not: they modify their environment with their mouths. They chew. They strip fibers from the aerial roots and work them into loose mats for egg-laying, for moisture retention, for thermoregulation — behaviors that are each, individually, unremarkable, the sort of thing a clever amphibian might converge on given enough time and enough pressure. But here, in this hollow, the mats resonated. A mat of woven root fiber against a root-wall does something acoustically — it dampens certain frequencies, amplifies others, creates a local zone of altered sound. And the frogs preferred those zones. And they expanded the mats. And over generations — hundreds of generations, thousands — the mats became platforms, became shaped and curated and positioned at points along the root-wall where their acoustic effect was most pronounced, where they created pockets of sound that were *good for frogs*, that amplified mating calls, that clarified territorial signals, that made the business of being a frog in this hollow slightly easier and more successful.

The platforms are not furniture for a listener. They are instruments built by performers. The curve that looks like it could cradle a hip was shaped to cradle a frog — a specific frog, the mid-range species, the one about the size of a thumbnail, the one whose call is the most harmonically complex, the one that contributes most to the character of the chord. That species lays its eggs in the woven fiber of the platforms, and the tadpoles develop in the thin film of acoustically-migrating water that coats the root-wall behind the platform, and the tadpoles grow up hearing the chord from the inside, from within the structure that shapes it, and when they metamorphose and begin to call, their calls are already tuned, already fitted to the hollow, already part of it. They are born inside the instrument and they become part of the instrument and they never leave. None of the frogs ever leave. There is no reason to leave. Everything they need is here: water, food, mates, and the sound, which is not a need exactly but which has become so entangled with their needs that the distinction no longer means anything.

The tuned seed-pods are also theirs. Not deliberately. The frogs cache seeds — another unusual behavior, convergently evolved here, where the ecosystem is small and closed and every calorie matters. They tuck seeds into woven root-balls for reasons that are partly alimentary and partly structural (seeds are dense; they change the resonant mass of a woven object; a frog sitting on a seed-filled pod produces a subtly different call than a frog sitting on an empty one, and the frogs with the better-sounding pods attract more mates, and so the behavior persists and refines itself over generations until the pods are *tuned*, not because any frog decided to tune them but because sexual selection and acoustic physics have been collaborating here for millennia on a project neither of them knows about).

---

**The beetles** are the hollow's most active sculptors, though they would not recognize the word.

They are a single species — unlike the frogs, they did not speciate, because their acoustic niche is not a frequency band but a *behavior*. They are small, ten millimeters long, black with an iridescence that shifts blue-green in the light column, and their wing cases are serrated, ridged, evolved into structures that produce sound when rubbed together or dragged across a surface. All beetles stridulate; these have turned stridulation into something more. They are, functionally, bows. The root-wall is, functionally, a string. When a beetle crawls across a patch of fungus-softened bark, it produces a tone whose pitch and timbre depend on the texture of the bark, the speed of the beetle, the angle of its body, the moisture content of the wood. A single beetle is a quiet, unremarkable thing. But they do not operate singly.

They move in clusters of dozens to hundreds, and the clusters move in coordinated patterns across the root-walls, and the coordination is not centrally directed — there is no leader beetle, no conductor — it emerges from a simple rule that each beetle follows: *move toward the frequency that is currently weakest in the local sound field*. This means that when a section of the chord thins out — when a frog species goes briefly quiet during a reproductive pause, or when the light column moves and changes the thermal pattern and the insects shift — the beetles flow toward the gap, massing on bark surfaces that will produce the missing frequency, filling in the chord, maintaining its completeness. They are the hollow's immune system, acoustically speaking. They heal the silences.

Over time — long time, deep time — this behavior has physically altered the root-walls. The paths the beetles travel most frequently have been worn smooth in some places, roughened in others, carved into a topography of grooves and ridges that is essentially a musical score written in wood. The beetles read this score with their feet and their wing cases, and the score tells them where to go and what sound to make when they get there, and the beetles' passage continues to refine the score, deepening the grooves, sharpening the ridges, and this is why the chord has been getting richer over the centuries — because the instrument is being improved by its own players, continuously, imperceptibly, the way a river improves its own bed.

---

**The birds** are the newest arrivals, and in some ways the most disturbing.

There are three species, all small, all plain, all derived from lineages that elsewhere are migratory, restless, far-ranging. Here they do not migrate. Here they barely move. They roost in the upper tangles near the aperture, where the light is strongest, and they sing — but I said this already: their singing is contribution, not performance. What I didn't say is what has happened to them.

Their songs have simplified. Outside the hollow, their ancestral species produce complex, variable, individually distinctive songs — the kind of songs that female birds evaluate for creativity, for novelty, for the cognitive fitness they imply. Here, over the generations, the songs have converged. Each species now produces essentially one phrase, one repeating motif, and the motif is a harmonic of the chord. The birds have given up individuality for integration. They have given up the dazzling improvisatory intelligence of birdsong for a single, perfect, endlessly repeated note that fits.

This is the disturbing thing: they are *less* than they were. They are simpler. Their brains are measurably smaller than their relatives' outside the hollow — the neural architecture that supports song learning and improvisation has atrophied, because it is not needed here, because the hollow does not reward novelty, it rewards *fit*, and the most fitting thing a bird can do is produce its one note at its one frequency at the exact right moment in the cycle, again and again, until it dies, and be replaced by a chick that does the same thing. The birds are the hollow's most legible lesson about what it means to live here: you are welcomed, you are given a place, and the place *simplifies you*. It does not do this cruelly. It does it the way water simplifies a stone. But the result is the same: something that was complex and varied and full of potential becomes something that is smooth and singular and part of a larger thing.

The birds do not seem to suffer. They are fed — the insect density here is extraordinary, and the acoustic hunting strategy means they barely have to move to eat. They are warm. They are safe from most predators, because the few predators that enter the hollow are disoriented by the sound and leave. They live long lives, these birds. They pair-bond permanently. They sit on their listening platforms — because they use the frog-built platforms too, and their weight further tunes the structures — and they sing their one note, and their eyes are calm, and there is nothing in their behavior that looks like distress.

But they are less. They are less than they could have been. And the hollow does not care, because the hollow is not a thing that cares, it is a process that selects for what sustains it, and what sustains it is conformity, resonance, fit. The hollow is not evil. But it is appetitive. It takes what enters and it finds the note inside it and it encourages that note and it lets everything else atrophy, and it does this with the patient, impersonal, irresistible persistence of a physical law.

---

**The snakes** are the only things here that seem to resist.

There are two species, both slender, both green, both nearly invisible. They eat the frogs. They eat the eggs from the platforms. They are predators in a space that is otherwise almost entirely collaborative, and their presence introduces something the chord does not entirely accommodate: dissonance. When a snake takes a frog, the frog's call stops, and there is a gap, and the beetles cannot fill this gap because it is the wrong register, and for a few minutes the chord is *incomplete*, and the hollow — the whole hollow — seems to flinch. The water patterns change. The condensation shifts. The aerial roots drift into new configurations, unsettled, seeking a new equilibrium.

And then the equilibrium is found, and the chord rebuilds, and the gap is filled by another frog, and the hollow is whole again. But the snakes have introduced the possibility of interruption, of rupture, of something other than the endless self-reinforcing deepening of the same sound. And this, I think, is why the hollow has not yet consumed itself — why the feedback loop has not spiraled into some acoustic singularity, some resonance catastrophe that shatters the root-walls and collapses the chamber. The snakes are the hollow's necessary imperfection, its built-in flaw, the grain of sand in the oyster. They keep the chord from becoming too perfect, too stable, too complete. They force it to adapt, to rebuild, to find itself again after each small predatory disruption, and each re-finding is slightly different from the last, shifted by a microtone, and this is why the chord has been evolving, not just deepening — because the snakes keep breaking it, and each repair is a revision.

The snakes are silent. They are the only silent things in the hollow. They make no sound as they move — their sine-wave locomotion is tuned to the dominant frequency, yes, but not because the sound moves them: because matching the frequency makes them invisible, acoustically. They are stealth predators in a world that is made of sound. They are the hollow's shadow, its negative space, the silence that moves through the noise and takes what it needs and keeps the whole system honest.

---

**And the thing that might still be here.**

I have to be careful with this.

The platforms were built by frogs. The seed-pods were cached by frogs. I believe this. The evidence is good, the mechanisms are plausible, the evolutionary logic is sound. But there is a set of marks on the inner surface of one of the root-walls, near the floor of the depression, half-covered by moss, that I cannot attribute to any frog or beetle or bird. They are shallow grooves in the wood, arranged in a pattern that is — I do not want to say *deliberate*, because everything in this hollow looks deliberate, everything has been shaped by the feedback loop into something that appears intentional — but these grooves are *geometrically* deliberate. They are evenly spaced. They form a series of concentric arcs, not circles but arcs, as though someone pressed a fingernail or a claw-tip into the wood and traced the same curve again and again, each time slightly larger, like the growth rings of a tree drawn from the outside in.

When the light column passes over these marks — and it does, for about twenty minutes each day, in the late afternoon — the grooves catch the light and cast tiny shadows that shift as the angle changes, and the shifting shadows create a pattern on the moss below that looks, briefly, astonishingly, like a sound made visible. Like a waveform. Like someone carved a picture of what the chord looks like, from the inside.

I don't know what made these marks. They are old — the wood has healed around them, the way wood heals around a wound, which means they were made when the wood was alive and growing, which means they were made a long time ago, back when the root-walls were still expanding, still flexible enough to accept a groove without splitting. Whatever made them was here during the hollow's formation. During the time when the chord was still young, still finding itself, still thin and simple enough that you could conceive of carving a picture of it.

There is nothing here now that has hands. There is nothing here now that makes marks. But the platforms are positioned such that, if you sat on one — if you curled your body into the cradle that the frogs now claim — you would be facing the grooved wall. You would be watching the light move across it. You would be watching the picture of the sound change as the sound itself changed around you.

I think someone was here once who was like us. Not human, necessarily, not even primate, but *like us* in the way that matters: capable of recognizing what was happening in this hollow and wanting to record it, to participate in it not just as a body that makes sound but as a mind that knows the sound is beautiful. I think they came here, and they listened, and they carved what they heard, and they may have been the first to weave a platform to sit on while they listened, and the frogs found the platforms after the person was gone and the frogs made them theirs, and the person's contribution to the hollow was not a sound but a *structure*, a way of attending, a practice of listening that the place absorbed and kept and no longer remembers the origin of.

Or I think they are still here, and they are the fungal body beneath the floor, and they were not always a fungal body, and the pulse is not peristalsis but something else, something slower and more patient than thought but shaped like thought, like memory, like the desire to hear what happens next.

Or I think they were never here, and the grooves are a natural artifact, and the platforms are entirely the frogs' doing, and there is nothing in this hollow that has ever known what it was part of, and the chord is the product of nothing but physics and evolution and time, and that is more remarkable than any other explanation, that this immense, complex, self-sustaining, self-reinforcing, ever-deepening music is the work of creatures that have no concept of music, that what sounds like composition is the blind accumulation of successful adaptations playing themselves out in a resonant chamber for ten thousand years.

I don't know which of these is true. The hollow does not care which is true. The hollow only cares — in its processual, non-conscious, tropistic way — about the next note. About the continuation. About more.

---

**What lives here:**

A fungal body that is the ground. Eleven species of frog that are the chord. A species of beetle that is the healing. Three species of bird that have forgotten how to be anything but their note. Two species of snake that are the silence that keeps the sound alive. Orchids that breathe. Mosses that listen. Water that climbs toward music. Ferns that grow toward resonance. Seeds in tuned shells, germinating into the walls of an instrument that has been building itself for longer than anyone has been counting.

And maybe, beneath it all or within it all or long gone from it all, something that once knew what it was hearing.

The chord continues. The chord has always been continuing. If you press your jaw to the root-wall you can feel it in your teeth, the whole ten-thousand-year history of this place vibrating at once, every generation of frog and beetle and bird encoded in the current sound the way every year of a tree is encoded in its wood, and you cannot hear the beginning because there was no beginning, there was only a slow thickening, a gradual accumulation of sound upon sound upon sound until the accumulation became self-sustaining, became alive in the way that fire is alive, feeding on itself, growing, always growing, and you are here now, and your heartbeat is being incorporated, and the hollow has made room for you in the chord, and the chord is slightly richer now, slightly more complex, and it will keep this complexity even after you leave, a ghost of your rhythm persisting in the phase relationships of the frogs for a generation, maybe two, before it is absorbed completely and the hollow forgets you specifically but remembers you *generally*, as a moment when the chord changed, as a note that came and went and left the music slightly different than it found it.

This is what lives here. Not creatures. Not organisms. A *song* lives here, and everything else — everything with legs and lungs and eyes and hunger — is the song's way of singing itself.
