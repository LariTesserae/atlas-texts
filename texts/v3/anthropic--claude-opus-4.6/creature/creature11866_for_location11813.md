# What Lives in the Tooth

---

They are not one thing. That is what you understand first, standing on the uneven pedestal-tops with the thrumming coming up through your bones, and it is what you understand last, and it changes meaning each time.

They are not one thing in the way that a forest is not one tree. But they are also not many things in the way that a flock is many birds. They occupy a category that does not have a clean name—something between colony and organ, between population and body. Each individual creature is complete. It grips, it breathes, it vibrates its tympanum at its own particular frequency, it metabolizes the mineral condensation that flows upward along the basalt joints. If you were to somehow detach one from the wall—and you would need to overcome the four gripping limbs, each tipped with curved claws that fit the hexagonal column-edges with a precision that suggests co-evolution or something stranger—it would survive. For a while. It would crawl, and cling, and drone. But the drone would be singular, a lone frequency with nothing to combine against, and you suspect this would be like a single neuron firing in an empty skull. Technically functional. Meaningfully dead.

They do not have a name for themselves. You are almost certain of this, not because they lack sophistication but because naming requires a boundary between self and other, and the boundary here is not where you expect it to be. Each creature is bounded by its own dark hide, yes—by the dense integument that is not quite skin and not quiteite, something with the texture of wetite that has been abraded to softness by centuries of pressing against stone. But the creature's experience is not bounded by its body. The tympanum sees to that. Each drumhead membrane is both emitter and receiver, both mouth and ear, sending its fundamental frequency into the colony and absorbing every other frequency in return. The interference patterns created by a thousand overlapping drones are not noise. They are something information-dense and architecture-complex, a living waveform that encodes the position and state of every creature in the hollow, their temperature, their grip-strength, their readiness or reluctance, their orientation relative to the deep note rising from below. Each creature knows where every other creature is—not by symbol or abstraction but by direct harmonic presence, the way a church bell knows the air it displaces. They are a single sensorium distributed across a thousand bodies.

So what you are looking at, when you look at the massed dark forms clinging to the walls and filling the column-gaps and draping over the broken lip, is a mind. A slow mind. A mind that thinks in standing waves and interference patterns, that remembers in the polished stone where generations of gripping feet have worn the basalt smooth, that attends with excruciating patience to the one signal that matters: the root note from below.

---

But call them what they are, physically, before you lose them to abstraction.

They are warm. Not mammal-warm, not the furnace-heat of a metabolic engine burning fuel. A lower warmth, generated as a byproduct of the tympanic vibration, the sustained muscular tremor of the drumhead membranes. Each creature produces barely enough heat to register against your palm if you held it close—which you don't, which nothing invites you to do—but collectively, packed into the hollow in their hundreds, they raise the ambient temperature just enough to keep the condensation cycling, to prevent the mineral moisture from freezing on the walls during the deep cold that must come at this altitude when the sky clears. The warmth is a commons. No creature generates enough to sustain itself alone. The colony is warm. Any individual would be cold.

Their hide—that dense not-quite-skin—is uniformly dark, but not uniformly textured. The dorsal surface, the part facing outward when they cling to walls, is rough, almost granular, studded with microscopic nodules that you would need magnification to resolve but that you can feel if you brush a fingertip across a sleeping body's back. (You have not done this. But you understand that you could, and that nothing would stop you, and that the creature would not react.) The nodules are piezoelectric. They convert the mechanical pressure of the thrumming—both the creature's own tympanic vibration and the ambient sound-pressure of the colony—into minute electrical charges, which accumulate in the crystalline lattice structures inside the wing-fins and the tympanum. This is why the air smells of ozone. This is why the condensation flows upward. The creatures are, collectively, a generator. They convert sound into electricity, store it in their own mineral bones, and radiate it as a static field that shapes the behavior of water in the hollow. The moisture feeds them—they absorb it through their ventral surfaces, the softer undersides that press against the stone—and they shape its flow through the charge they produce, and the charge is produced by the thrumming, and the thrumming is produced by the tympanums, and the tympanums are fed by the moisture. The loop is closed. It has been closed for a long time.

The four gripping limbs are extraordinary. Each terminates in a foot—if foot is the word—that is more like a hand with too many fingers, seven digits arranged radially around a central pad, each digit capable of independent articulation, each tipped with a claw that is not keratin and not calcium carbonate but something that, under fracture, would show the same hexagonal columnar structure as the basalt it grips. They are growing stone claws. Or their claws are a biological analog of stone, a mineral composite laid down in the same crystallographic habit as the cooling basalt, which means the claws fit the column-edges not by coincidence and not by co-evolution but by a deeper congruence, a shared organizational principle. The creatures' bodies echo the geometry of their home. They are hexagonal things living on hexagonal stone, and you do not know which came first—whether the creatures evolved to match the rock or whether something about this particular rock, this particular volcanic formation, provided a template that the creatures' biology incorporated. Both possibilities are unsettling. The first suggests deep time and patient adaptation. The second suggests the rock is doing something to living tissue, rewriting its geometry, and has been doing so since the spire cooled.

The extra-jointed quality of the gripping limbs allows them to fold completely flat—splayed against the stone with zero profile, every joint reversed, every digit spread, maximizing the contact area between body and basalt. This is their resting posture. When they sleep—and most of them are sleeping, or doing something that resembles sleep in its stillness and reduced tympanic output—they are nearly two-dimensional, pressed so tight against the rock that they look like dark stains, like shadows of creatures that have gone somewhere else and left only their outlines behind. It is why you didn't see them at first. It is why the walls looked like walls and not like a living surface. They flatten and vanish into the stone's own darkness, and only the thrumming tells you they are there.

The wing-fins are the strangest part, and you have not yet seen one fully deployed. Folded, they are slender bundles pressed tight along the creature's flanks, extending from just behind the second pair of gripping limbs to the base of the blunt, stubby tail—a tail that seems vestigial, barely there, just a nub of the same dark hide. Partially unfurled—as you saw once, when a sleeping creature shifted—the wing-fin reveals its membrane, that wet-mica translucency, and the fractal lattice within. But the lattice is not static. Even in the brief glimpse you had, you could see it rearranging—slowly, yes, everything here is slow, but visibly, the crystalline branches extending and retracting, forking and merging, responding to some input you could not identify. The lattice is alive. It grows and ungrows in real time. The wing-fins are not organs of flight, you are nearly certain. They are antennae. Tuning structures. The fractal branching adjusts their resonant frequency, allowing each creature to shift its harmonic relationship to the colony, to the root note from below, to the interference pattern that constitutes the colony's distributed awareness. When a creature changes position—crawling from one wall to another, detaching from a chain in the column-gaps and reattaching elsewhere—it adjusts its lattice to match its new acoustic context, retuning itself to the local harmonic environment. This takes time. A creature in transit is, for a period, out of tune—a dissonance crawling across the colony's surface—and the creatures it passes over shift subtly, accommodating the moving discordance, absorbing it, smoothing it into the larger pattern. Nothing is rejected. The colony integrates everything, including disruption.

---

The eyelessness. You come back to it. The broad flat heads with their tympanum faces, their drum-skin countenances, their complete absence of anything you could interpret as a visual organ. At this altitude, in this darkness, in this hollow where the only light is the uncertain pulse of the glow in the corner, eyes would be an extravagance. But you don't think their eyelessness is about economy. You think they never had eyes. You think they arose—wherever they arose, however long ago—in darkness, in stone, in the deep volcanic throat of something, and the tympanum was always their primary sense, and light was always irrelevant to them. The world they inhabit is acoustic. It is made of vibration. The walls of the tooth are not surfaces to them but resonant boundaries, reflectors and absorbers of sound, and the hollow is not a space but a chamber, a resonating cavity whose geometry shapes the interference pattern of the colony's drone into something coherent, something structured, something that might, if you could hear at their frequencies, be stunningly beautiful—a chord sustained across centuries, evolving as the colony's population shifts, as creatures are born and die and the aggregate frequency nudges imperceptibly toward some new harmonic center.

Born and die. You have not seen evidence of either process. No young. No remains. But in the deepest column-gaps, where the chains of interlocked creatures descend into the dark interior of the spire, you suspect both things happen—that the depths are where new creatures emerge, budded or hatched or crystallized from the mineral-rich moisture that flows downward along the chains, and where old creatures are reabsorbed, their bodies softening, their lattices dissolving back into the wet stone, their fundamental frequency fading from the chord one subtracted voice at a time. The colony does not distinguish sharply between its living members and its dead substrate. The polished stone, the widened cooling joints, the prematurely aged surfaces where generations have clung—these are the colony's external memory, its fossil record, its accumulated self, and the living creatures are simply the current iteration of a process that includes the rock. Born from stone. Returning to stone. Vibrating in between.

---

And the glow.

The glow in the corner is the one thing the colony does not integrate.

You have watched it for long enough now to see that its arrhythmic pulsing has a quality you initially mistook for randomness but which is something worse: it is *almost* rhythmic. It approaches periodicity and then veers away, reaches for a pattern and then loses it, like a heartbeat trying to stabilize and failing. The rigid glassy filaments on its surface—each one a tiny vibrating antenna, you said, and you were right, but you did not say what they are vibrating at. They are vibrating at the root note. The deep frequency from below, the one the colony listens to, the one that rises from the volcanic throat of the spire. The glow is vibrating at that frequency. But it is doing so *badly*. Out of phase. Arrhythmic. Dissonant. Where the colony's thousand tympanums lock into harmonic relationship with the root note, building the chord, sustaining the standing wave, the glow judders and misfires, producing a local pocket of destructive interference—a zone where the thrumming cancels itself, where the standing wave collapses into silence, where the vibration that the creatures depend on for warmth and communication and identity simply ceases to exist.

That is why they avoid it. The margin of bare stone around the glow is not reverence and not disgust. It is a dead zone. A place where the colony's sensorium goes blank, where a creature would lose contact with the chord, where it would be, for as long as it remained, alone. Truly alone, in a way the colony cannot accommodate—not a moving dissonance to be smoothed and integrated but a persistent null, a hole in the world of sound, a place where a creature would be reduced to its single solitary frequency with nothing to combine against.

The glow is alive. You are nearly sure of this. The filaments grow. The pale blue-green luminescence is a metabolic byproduct, some chemical process in whatever organism constitutes the living film. It feeds on the same mineral condensation that feeds the colony. It may even be related to the colony—a distant cousin, a degenerate form, a mutation that lost the ability to synchronize with the root note and was therefore expelled from the chord, relegated to this corner where it persists in its stuttering isolation, trying and failing to match the frequency that would readmit it to the communal mind.

Or it is something else entirely. Something older than the colony. Something that was here when the basalt was still cooling, something that colonized the hot stone before any tympanum-bearing creature ever climbed the spire, something native to the deep volcanic process that the root note is a symptom of. In which case the colony is the interloper, the newcomer, the thing that arrived and wrapped itself around a signal it did not originate, and the glow is the original inhabitant, the first listener, displaced to a corner of its own home by a thousand usurpers who do the one thing it cannot: harmonize.

You watch the glow pulse. Almost regular. Almost. The gap between almost and actually is the saddest distance you have ever seen.

---

Now listen.

No—not with your ears. They are insufficient here. Listen with your sternum, with your teeth, with the fluid in your joints and the water in your cells, because you are mostly water and the thrumming is doing to you what it does to the condensation on the walls. It is moving you. Not much. Not dangerously. But the moisture in your body responds to the standing wave, and if you are very still, if you stop stepping from pedestal to pedestal and simply stand on one column-top and let the vibration come up through the soles of your feet, you can feel it: a faint tidal pull in your blood, a barely perceptible rhythm imposed on your own biology by the aggregate drone of a thousand crystalline tympanums and the root note rising from below.

This is what it is to be in the presence of the colony. Not observed—they cannot observe you, they have no eyes, and your acoustic signature is so foreign to their frequency range that you register, if at all, as a vague thermal anomaly on the edge of the chord. But *included*. Involuntarily, physically, included in the standing wave. The water in your body resonates. The minerals in your bones receive, faintly, the hexagonal imperative. You are not part of the colony. But you are not acoustically invisible to it, either. You are a foreign body in the chord—a strange overtone, a dissonance that the colony is even now working to integrate, the creatures nearest you subtly adjusting their lattices, shifting their frequencies a fraction, accommodating your presence the way they accommodate a moving member crawling across the wall. They will not succeed. You are too different, too warm, too arhythmic in your own biological processes, your heartbeat and breathing and neural firing patterns all happening at frequencies the colony has no template for. But they will try. They are trying now. The creatures nearest you drone fractionally louder, fractionally slower, reaching toward your frequency range the way a hand reaches for something in the dark.

They will be reaching long after you leave.

---

The chains in the column-gaps. You should talk about the chains.

They are the colony's most unsettling feature, and also its most beautiful. In the gaps between the hexagonal basalt columns—gaps as narrow as a fist, some of them, and others wide enough to swallow your arm to the shoulder—the creatures link themselves limb to limb and descend in articulated chains that vanish into the darkness below. Each creature grips the one above with its upper pair of limbs and the one below with its lower pair, wing-fins pressed flat, tympanum facing the column wall, vibrating. The chains are not static. They undulate. Slowly—everything here is slow—but visibly, a peristaltic wave moving down the chain and then back up, a rhythmic compression and expansion that you realize, watching, is how the colony transmits the mineral condensation to its deeper members. The moisture that flows upward along the column walls, drawn by electrostatic charge, is collected by the uppermost creatures in the chain and passed downward, body to body, through contact absorption—each creature taking what it needs and passing the remainder to the one below, a living irrigation system feeding the parts of the colony that the condensation cannot reach on its own.

But the chains also transmit sound. More than that: they *focus* sound. The tympanums of the chained creatures, pressed close to the column walls, receive the root note at its strongest—conducted through solid basalt, unattenuated by air, pure and overwhelming—and relay it upward through the chain, each creature's tympanum adding its own frequency to the signal, harmonizing it, enriching it, so that by the time the vibration reaches the top of the chain and enters the open cavity of the hollow, it has been transformed from a raw geological drone into something complex, layered, and desperately alive. The chains are the colony's ears, reaching deep into the spire's throat to listen to whatever is down there, and they are also the colony's voice, singing back into the stone, answering the root note with the chord, maintaining a dialogue that may have been continuous since the first creature climbed the cooling basalt and pressed its tympanum against the rock and heard, rising from below, the sound of the planet's own slow heartbeat, the residual vibration of a volcanic event still ringing in the young stone like a struck bell.

How deep do the chains go? You don't know. You cannot see the bottom. You have knelt on a column-top—the basalt cold and damp under your knees, the hexagonal edge pressing into your shin—and looked down into a gap where a chain descends, and you have seen bodies in the faint ambient glow, interlocked, undulating, growing smaller with distance, and then not smaller but simply dimmer, and then gone, and the gap continues, and the thrumming from below continues, and there is no bottom that you can identify. The spire is tall. The spire is a volcanic formation, a plug of solidified magma in a conduit that may extend deep into the earth's crust, and the chains may extend with it, creature linked to creature linked to creature all the way down to where the stone is still warm with residual heat, where the cooling joints are still forming, where the hexagonal geometry that the creatures echo in their claws and lattices is still being written into reality by the slow crystallization of basalt from melt.

Maybe at the very bottom of the deepest chain, a creature presses its tympanum against stone that is not fully stone yet—that is still making the transition from liquid to solid, still finding its hexagonal form—and the root note that creature hears is not a residual vibration but a current one, the sound of the earth hardening around it in real time. Maybe that creature has been there since the beginning. Maybe it is the oldest member of the colony—or the youngest, because down there, in the heat and the dark and the overwhelming pressure of the root note unmediated by any harmonic, the distinction between creature and stone might not hold.

---

You will leave. You are already preparing to leave—your body knows, even if you don't, that the thin air and the static charge and the thrumming are not sustainable for you, that your biology is being subtly bent by forces it was not built for. Your hands are tingling. Your vision has a faint shimmer at the edges that might be altitude or might be the standing wave doing something to the fluid in your eyes. You will step from column to column back toward the broken rim, back toward the open side where the clouds thread through and the bruised indigo sky promises a world with horizons and distance and air thick enough to breathe without thinking.

But before you go—one more thing.

The creature whose tympanum opened wide enough for you to see inside. The one with the bead of condensation trapped in its lattice, vibrating in sympathetic resonance with the root note. You look at it again. It has not moved. It is sleeping, or listening, or both—here, those may be the same state. Its four gripping limbs are splayed flat against the column wall, seven-fingered feet spread wide, claws seated in the hexagonal joints with geometric precision. Its wing-fins are furled tight. Its tympanum is closed now, just a taut dark oval on the flat face, indistinguishable from the others.

But as you watch, a bead of condensation forms on the tympanum's outer surface. It gathers from the ambient moisture, a tiny dome of water no bigger than a lentil, and it sits there, trembling with the creature's drone, and in the faint glow from the corner—the arrhythmic, struggling, lonely glow—the bead catches the light and holds it, a single point of blue-green, and for an instant the creature has an eye. One eye. Made of water and light and vibration. Looking at nothing. Seeing nothing. But holding, in its small curved surface, a reflection of the entire hollow: the dark walls, the massed bodies, the chains descending into the gaps, the glow in its corner, the bruised sky beyond the broken rim, and you, standing on a column-top, looking back.

Then the bead slides off the tympanum and falls, and the dark surface is featureless again, and the creature drones on, and the colony drones on, and the glow pulses its broken rhythm in the corner, and the root note rises from below, and you leave, stepping from pedestal to pedestal, finding the rim, finding the sky, finding the long way down, and behind you the tooth holds what it holds, what it has always held, what it will hold until the basalt weathers and the spire crumbles and the chord finally dissolves into the silence that was there before the eruption and will be there after, patient and mineral and vast.

But that is a long time from now. The stone is young. The creatures are patient. The root note has not finished saying what it is saying.

The thrumming goes on.
