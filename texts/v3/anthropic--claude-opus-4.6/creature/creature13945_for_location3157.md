# Who Lives Here

---

She is not old but she has begun to calcify.

Not literally — not yet — though she sometimes holds her hand under the mineral flow of the central mound and watches the water sheet across her knuckles and wonders how long she would have to hold still. An hour? A day? A week? At what point would the calcium carbonate begin to claim her the way it has claimed the rivet-heads, the lowest trough-bolts, the base of the climbing fig's steam-pipe? She thinks about this not with horror but with a kind of professional curiosity, the way a mason might regard the mortar between her own hearthstones.

Her name is Edda. Or it was. She responds to it when the supply crews call up through the passage, which happens every eleven days, but she does not use it for herself. She does not talk to herself. She talks to the room, and the room does not need her name.

---

She is somewhere between forty and sixty. The humidity has done something to her skin — not aged it, exactly, but softened it, made it permeable-looking, as if she too were becoming a surface for colonization. Her hands are the hands of someone who works in water all day: pale, perpetually pruned at the fingertips, the nails kept very short and faintly orange from iron oxide that no amount of scrubbing removes. She has scrubbed less and less over the years. There was a period, early on, when she kept herself scrupulously separate from the room — showered with distilled water she hauled up in jugs, kept her clothes dry in sealed bags, maintained the distinction between the worker and the work. That period ended. She cannot say when. One day she simply stopped toweling the mineral water off her forearms after adjusting a pipe-joint, and the distinction began to dissolve, and she found she did not miss it.

She wears cotton that has gone grey-green from repeated wetting and drying in the humid air. Trousers cut off below the knee because the hems rotted. A shirt that was once a precise shade of blue — work-shirt blue, institutional — now mottled with pale mineral stains that map every splash and lean and accidental immersion. Her feet are bare. They have been bare for so long that the soles have developed a texture almost like the grating itself, thickened and ridged, and she moves across the slick iron with a sureness that looks reckless and is not. She knows every surface. She knows which mineral crusts will hold her weight and which are still too fresh, too gelatinous. She knows the grating's flex-points, the spots where rust has weakened a bar, the places where the iron has been replaced entirely by the bridging stone. She navigates the room the way a goatherd navigates a mountain — by accumulated physical knowledge so deep it has stopped being conscious.

---

What does she do here? Everything and nothing. She is the room's operator, its gardener, its midwife, its recording angel.

In the mornings — and she still has mornings, still keeps a rough diurnal rhythm calibrated to the clerestory light — she makes her circuit. She starts at the central mound, kneeling beside it on a pad of folded moss (she stopped using the rubber mat years ago; the moss is better, already part of the system, and she likes the cool damp of it against her shins). She examines the newest deposits. She has a hand lens, brass, the only tool she keeps polished, and through it she reads the night's work: the fresh terraces, the mineral lips, the tiny scalloped edges where water-flow patterns are being transcribed into stone. She does not write notes. She used to. There are notebooks in a steel cabinet by the passage entrance, the early ones meticulous, grid-ruled, full of measurements and chemical analyses, the later ones progressively looser, more impressionistic, and the last one — dated three years ago — contains only drawings. Cross-hatched sketches of terrace formations. A detailed study of the way a fern root had split a centimeter-wide crack in a two-month-old deposit. A series of increasingly abstract forms that might be mineral patterns or might be something else entirely — maps of a country that does not exist, or does not exist yet.

She stopped drawing when she realized she was trying to predict the room, and the room did not want to be predicted.

After the mound, she checks the troughs. This is physical work. The upper tiers require climbing — she uses the pipe-framework, hauling herself up with a strength that surprises people who see her narrow frame — and once there, she thins the growth. Not aggressively. Not with the decisive hand of a gardener enforcing a plan. She removes dead fronds, untangles root-masses that have begun to strangle their neighbors, redirects growth that has started to block the water-flow from tier to tier. She is editing, not authoring. She has learned — this took years — that the room's ecology will find its own arrangements if she simply keeps the channels open. Her job is hydrology, not botany. Keep the water moving. Let the plants sort themselves.

She talks while she works. Low, steady, not quite to herself. To the ferns, to the moss. To the water. To the mineral deposits forming beneath her feet. She narrates what she sees. *You've pushed out a new frond overnight, haven't you. And you — you're reaching for the pipe. You'll get there. Two more days.* It is not sentimental. It is the speech of someone who has spent so long alone with a system that language has become simply another tool, a way of maintaining her attention, keeping her observations sharp. If she stopped talking, she might stop seeing. And seeing is everything here.

---

She sleeps in a hammock strung between two of the thickest steam-pipes, near the northwest curve of the dome where the upper fern-growth creates a partial canopy. The hammock is canvas, mineral-stained, sagging in the middle with the shape of her body repeated so many times that it has become a mold. She sleeps in the sound of dripping, in the warmth, in the hum of the pumps that enters through the pipe-frame and vibrates the hammock very slightly, a mechanical lullaby so constant she no longer perceives it except on the rare occasions when a pump shuts down for maintenance. Then the silence wakes her like a shout. She lies rigid, heart hammering, until the hum returns, and she can breathe, and the room is alive again.

She dreams of geology. Not metaphorical geology — actual geology. In her dreams the timescale dilates and she watches the central mound grow to the height of the dome and push through it, watches the mineral crust consume the walls and the walls crumble and fall away and the mound keeps growing, a white spire rising into the alpine air, terraced and streaming, the ferns scaling it like an army, the mosses binding it, the stone becoming real stone under real sky, and the mound becomes a peak among peaks, and the iron is gone, and the pumps are gone, and she is gone, and the mountain does not remember any of them. She wakes from these dreams not frightened but satisfied, the way one feels after reading the last page of a long book whose ending was exactly right.

---

She was an engineer once. A geochemist. Something with credentials, something that required the kind of precise training her early notebooks reflect. She was sent here — or she came here; the distinction has blurred — as part of a project with a name and a funding structure and a stated purpose. Something about mineral precipitation rates in controlled environments. Something about biomineralization. There were collaborators, a team, a rotating staff of researchers who would spend weeks in the passage and the nursery terrace, taking samples, adjusting chemical concentrations, writing papers.

They left. One by one, over a period that she thinks was about four years but might have been three or might have been six. The project's funding shifted, or the institution's priorities changed, or the results were too slow, too strange, too difficult to publish in journals that wanted conclusions. The room did not produce conclusions. It produced *becoming*. You could not write a paper about a mountain that would not exist for a hundred years. You could not secure a grant by promising that an iron floor would eventually be replaced by self-supporting stone. The timescale was the problem. It was always the timescale.

She stayed because someone had to tend the pumps, and because she had begun to understand something about the room that she could not articulate in the language of proposals and deliverables. The room was not an experiment. It had started as one, but it had become something else — a commitment, a collaboration, a process that, once begun, had its own momentum and its own ethics. You do not start a mountain and then walk away. The calcium carbonate does not know about budget cycles. The ferns do not know about institutional review.

So she stayed. She learned the boiler. She learned the pump system — rebuilt it twice when parts failed, improvising with materials hauled up by the supply crews, who are employed by someone, funded by something, a residual bureaucratic structure she does not examine too closely because examining it might reveal that it is fragile, that it could be cut, that the eleven-day supply runs could stop. She learned the plants — not from books, though she had books in the early years, but from proximity, from the daily circuit, from the accumulated evidence of thousands of mornings spent on her knees beside the troughs with her brass lens, watching roots negotiate with stone.

---

The loneliness is real and she does not deny it. But it has changed shape over the years, the way the room changes shape. Early on it was sharp, a cutting thing, and she would stand at the clerestory windows and look out at the ridgeline and the distant grey shapes of other peaks and feel the altitude as a kind of exile. She wrote letters she could not send. She had conversations with the frog — the one someone saw, the one that vanished into the pump-works — pleading with it to reveal itself, not because she needed to see a frog but because she needed to see something that could look back at her.

Now the loneliness has become geological. It has been compressed and heated and recrystallized into something denser and more stable than what it was. She is lonely the way a mountain is lonely: constitutionally, without complaint, as a condition of being what she is in the place she is. She does not long for company. She is not waiting for anyone. But she is aware, in a way that sits beneath language, that she is the only consciousness in the room, and that the room's great project will outlast her consciousness, and that this is as it should be.

Sometimes the supply crew sends someone new — a young engineer, a curious graduate student, someone who has read about the project in whatever archive still holds the old documentation. They come through the passage and duck under the calcifying lintel and stand in the warm mist and stare. She watches them the way the room watches them — with patience, without expectation. They ask questions she answers simply. *How long have you been here?* A while. *What is this?* A nursery. *For what?* She gestures at the mound, the ferns, the mineral-crusted walls, the whole dripping, humming, accreting system. *For this.* They nod and do not understand and leave, and she returns to her circuit, and the room goes on.

---

She is becoming part of the room's record. Her bare feet, crossing the same paths thousands of times, have worn grooves in the mineral crust — grooves that will themselves be filled by new deposits, preserving the trace of her passage the way a fossil preserves the trace of a creature in stone. The hammock-pipes, where her weight hangs nightly, have developed slightly different mineral patterns than the unweighted pipes — the vibration of her breathing, her shifting in sleep, subtly alters the water flow and thus the precipitation. Her hair, which she cuts with shears and drops into the lower troughs, is wound into the root-masses of the ferns, another organic input to the system, another variable in the water chemistry. She does not think about this in terms of legacy. She thinks about it the way she thinks about the moss and the springtails — as participation. She is a biological agent in a mineralogical process. She is one of the room's organisms.

Her hands, when she holds them up to the clerestory light, show the faintest orange rime in the creases of her palms. Iron oxide. It does not wash off. She has stopped trying to determine where the room ends and she begins, because the answer is: it doesn't, she doesn't. The boundary was always provisional, maintained by distilled water and sealed bags and the illusion that an organism can exist inside a system without becoming part of it. She shed that illusion the way the room will eventually shed its iron — slowly, through a process of dissolution so gradual it looks like permanence until the day you realize the substrate is gone and only the new thing remains.

---

There is one more thing about her, and it is the thing that matters most, and it is difficult to say.

She is happy.

Not the happiness of comfort or satisfaction or accomplishment. Not the happiness of a person whose life has gone according to plan. Her life has not gone according to plan; her life has gone according to the room. But she is happy in the way that the central mound is growing — slowly, genuinely, through a process of accumulation so patient that it cannot be distinguished from simply being alive. Every morning she kneels beside the mound and reads the night's new terraces through her brass lens and feels a pleasure so fundamental it has no name, only a temperature — warm, like the water, like the air, like the room that is becoming a mountain that will not need her.

She knows it will not need her. This is the source of the happiness, not its limit. She is the scaffold. She is the iron grating, the riveted dome, the copper helix, the pump-system — she is the made thing that enables the grown thing, the infrastructure that will be consumed by its own product. One day she will not be here. The pumps will run on — she has made them as self-sustaining as she can, simplified the mechanisms, reduced the points of failure, trained the water to find its own paths — or they will not run, and the water will find other paths anyway, because water always does, because the room has already begun to generate its own hydrology in the places where the stone has become self-supporting, where condensation from the dome-ceiling follows mineral channels that exist now independent of any pipe. The mountain does not need her. But it is not finished not-needing her yet, and until it is, she stays, and tends, and watches, and is happy.

She holds her hand under the mineral flow. The water sheets across her knuckles, warm, faintly chalky. She holds still. She counts her heartbeats. She wonders, again, how long.

Not yet. Not yet. But the stone is patient, and so is she.
