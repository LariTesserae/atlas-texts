# Preparing for the Cyst Works

---

## Before Anything Else: Understanding What You're Walking Into

This is not a dangerous location in the way that most multiverse travelers understand danger. Nothing will attack you. Nothing will chase you. The air is breathable, the footing is stable enough, and the space is finite—forty meters across, roughly. You could walk from one end to the other in a minute. You could, in theory, visit for ten minutes, look around, and leave.

But you won't. Or rather: the people who leave after ten minutes are the ones who didn't prepare properly and panicked, and the people who stay longer than they meant to are the ones who prepared well enough to be comfortable and then lost track of something—time, intention, the boundary between observation and participation. Both outcomes are failures of preparation, and both are common. So the first thing to understand is that preparing for the Cyst Works is less about gear and more about *deciding, in advance, what your relationship to this place will be,* because the place itself has no opinion about you and will not help you maintain one.

You are visiting a system that is complete without you. This is rarer than it sounds across the multiverse. Most locations have a posture toward visitors—hostile, welcoming, indifferent-but-legible. The Cyst Works is not indifferent. Indifference implies a capacity for difference. The Cyst Works is *occupied.* It is busy. It is doing something. What it is doing has nothing to do with you, and the longer you are there, the more you will feel the pull to make it about you—to interpret, to assign meaning, to find the message. There is no message. Prepare for that.

## Physical Preparation

### Heat Acclimatization

The interior temperature of the Cyst Works holds steady somewhere between 38°C and 42°C (100–108°F) with near-total humidity. This is not speculative; this is the temperature range at which the root system and the fungal colonies have reached their equilibrium, and neither the rock nor the geothermal source beneath it show any sign of fluctuation. You will be breathing air that is essentially body-temperature and saturated with moisture. For most people, this means:

- **Your body's cooling mechanisms will be severely compromised.** Sweat will not evaporate. It will pool on you, mix with the condensation that collects on every surface, and slide. You will be wet within five minutes and will not become dry again until you leave.

- **Core temperature will rise.** Not dangerously fast if you are healthy and hydrated, but steadily, insistently, in a way that sneaks up on you because the environment *feels* natural. This is the deceptive thing. The Cyst Works is body-temperature. It feels, on a mammalian level, like being held. Some travelers have described it as comforting, even womb-like, in the first twenty minutes. This is your thermoregulation failing to register danger because the temperature gradient between your skin and the air is too small to trigger alarm. Do not trust comfort here.

**What to do:**

Spend at least a week before your visit doing deliberate heat exposure—saunas, hot yoga, whatever your origin world offers. You want your body to be practiced at managing elevated core temperature. On the day of your visit, begin hydrating aggressively six hours beforehand. Bring at least three liters of water for every hour you plan to spend inside, and consider an electrolyte solution rather than plain water. Your sweat losses will be enormous even though you won't feel like you're sweating, because the moisture on your skin will be indistinguishable from the moisture on everything else.

Wear minimal clothing. Cotton is useless; it will be soaked and heavy within minutes. A thin synthetic base layer—something designed for water sports, or a merino wool blend if your origin world has access to good ones—will at least not actively hinder you. No boots. The floor is uneven but not sharp; the calcite deposits and root surfaces are smooth, almost polished by the mineral water. A thin-soled water shoe or neoprene boot will give you grip without insulating your feet, which is where a surprising amount of heat exchange happens. Go barefoot only if you're confident in your footing; the shallow pool at the lowest point is warm and only a few centimeters deep, but the submerged root surfaces can be slippery.

### Respiratory Considerations

The air in the Cyst Works is not toxic. Let me be clear about that, because the rumors that circulate in traveler communities—about spores, about the vine off-gassing psychoactives, about the fungal bioluminescence being a sign of something airborne—are persistent and, as far as any rigorous investigation has established, unfounded. The air is hot, wet, mineral-smelling, and heavy with CO2 from the root system's respiration and the slow decomposition of organic matter, but it is breathable. Oxygen levels are adequate. You will not suffocate.

However.

The air *feels* thick. It feels like you are breathing through a warm wet cloth. This is purely a function of temperature and humidity, but for some people—especially those with any history of respiratory sensitivity, anxiety disorders, or claustrophobia—the sensation of not-quite-getting-enough-air can escalate rapidly into panic. And panic in the Cyst Works is a particular problem, because the acoustics will take your rapid breathing and your heartbeat and feed them back to you as part of the ambient drone, which is exactly the kind of feedback loop you do not want.

**What to do:**

Practice controlled breathing before your visit. Box breathing, 4-7-8 patterns, whatever works for your physiology. Bring the technique with you as a practiced skill, not a theoretical concept. If you have any history of asthma, bring your medication even if you haven't needed it in years; the combination of heat, humidity, and elevated CO2 can trigger bronchospasm in sensitized airways. A light particulate mask is unnecessary and will make the breathing-discomfort worse. Do not wear one.

Consider, seriously, whether you are someone who is physiologically suited to this visit at all. There is no shame in acknowledging that a 40°C, 95%-humidity enclosed space with no natural airflow is beyond your body's comfortable range. Travelers with cardiovascular conditions, heat sensitivity, pregnancy, or severe anxiety should think very carefully. The Cyst Works is not going anywhere. It has time. You can come back when conditions are right.

## Equipment

### Lighting

You need to bring light. You also need to be thoughtful about what kind.

The Cyst Works is not dark, exactly. The single caged bulb near the ceiling provides a dim sulfurous wash that your eyes will adapt to after fifteen or twenty minutes, and the bioluminescent fungal shelves add a peripheral green glow that is more felt than seen. Between these two sources, you will eventually be able to navigate—to see the major root trunks, to avoid the pool, to identify the machines as dark shapes against slightly-less-dark walls. But this is not seeing. This is *sensing,* and the gap between the two matters if you want to actually examine anything.

Bring a headlamp with a red-light mode. White light in the Cyst Works is not forbidden or dangerous, but it is *violent*—it flattens the space, kills the bioluminescence instantly, and turns the root system and the mineral formations into something that looks merely geological rather than whatever they actually are. You will lose something important if your first experience of the space is under a white LED. Use the red mode for navigation and give your eyes time to adapt. If you need white light for close examination—of the gauges, the pump mechanism, the vine's interface with the machines—use it briefly, in a focused beam, and then turn it off and wait for your night vision to return.

Bring a backup light. Batteries last poorly in the heat and humidity. Bring a chemical light stick as a tertiary backup—something that requires no electronics and cannot fail due to moisture. You do not want to be in the Cyst Works in true darkness. Not because anything will happen to you. Because the sound, without the visual anchor of even the dimmest light, becomes *spatial* in a way that most people find destabilizing. The pump's thunk, the generator's hum, the creak of roots—in darkness, these sounds become architecture, and the architecture moves.

### Recording Equipment

This is where I need to be honest with you about something uncomfortable.

Cameras work in the Cyst Works. Audio recorders work. You can bring whatever documentation equipment you like, and it will capture images, sound, data. Nothing about the environment interferes with electronics beyond the usual hazards of heat and humidity. Seal your devices in waterproof housings, bring silica gel packets, and you'll be fine mechanically.

The problem is that nothing you record will convey the place.

This is not mystical. It is acoustic and phenomenological. The Cyst Works is an environment that operates on your body—through subsonic vibration, through heat, through the proprioceptive confusion of standing inside a space where the boundary between living and mechanical has been dissolved. Photographs of the root system will look like photographs of roots in a cave. Audio recordings of the pump will sound like a pump. The drone, which in person is a three-dimensional tactile experience that changes with every step you take, will flatten into a muddy hum on any recording. You will play it back later and wonder why you felt what you felt, and the recording will have no answer.

Bring recording equipment if you want. Document what you can. But know that the documentation is for reference, not reproduction. The Cyst Works must be experienced with the body. There is no substitute. Write notes. Write them by hand, on paper, in the dim light—the act of writing in that space, of processing the experience through language in real time, will preserve more than any camera.

Bring a notebook that can handle moisture. Waterproof paper exists in most developed multiverse nodes. A pencil, not a pen—ink runs, graphite doesn't.

### Other Gear

- **A watch or timer.** Critical. Not on your phone—a dedicated, simple timepiece that you can glance at without engaging with a screen. You *will* lose track of time. The pump's irregular rhythm and the drone's constancy create a temporal smear that most visitors find disorienting. Set a timer for your intended stay and respect it when it goes off. Common first-visit recommendation: no more than ninety minutes. Two hours absolute maximum. You can always come back.

- **A small plastic bag for waste.** You will probably not eat in the Cyst Works—the heat kills most people's appetite—but you'll generate waste: water bottles, wrappers, tissues. Take everything out with you. This is not for ecological reasons, though those apply. It is because the yellow vine colonizes foreign objects with visible speed—not minutes, but hours—and there are reports of abandoned equipment becoming incorporated into the system in ways that make later removal destructive. Don't leave anything behind that you don't intend to donate permanently.

- **No ropes, pitons, or climbing gear.** You will not need them. The space is navigable on foot. The temptation to climb the root system to get closer to the ceiling or the upper fungal formations is real—the root trunks look sturdy, and they are—but the surfaces are slick with condensation and the thin stone shelves that form overhangs are more fragile than they look. A fall from even three meters onto a calcite-ridged floor would be a serious injury in a location where evacuation is not trivial.

- **No open flames.** Not because of gas risk—the atmosphere is not explosive—but because the fungal bioluminescence is a photosensitive response, and intense light of any kind suppresses it for hours. Travelers who have lit flares or candles in the Cyst Works have reported the bioluminescence going dark for the remainder of their visit and, in some cases, not fully recovering for subsequent visitors days later. The ecosystem is robust but slow. Don't punish it for your convenience.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

This is the section that matters most, and the one that most preparation guides underweight. I will not underweight it.

### The Loneliness Problem

The Cyst Works is not haunted. It is not sentient. It is not watching you. But it is *occupied,* in the way that a body is occupied by its own processes, and standing inside it produces a feeling that most visitors struggle to name. The closest most people get is: "I felt like I was inside something that was alive and I was not part of it."

This is loneliness of a particular kind. Not the loneliness of isolation—you chose to come here, you can leave—but the loneliness of irrelevance. The system does not need you. The system does not notice you. The pump thunks whether you are there or not. The vine threads through the gauge whether anyone is watching or not. The root system has been growing for centuries and will grow for centuries more and your visit is not a data point it will retain. For some people—particularly those whose sense of self is anchored in being perceived, being needed, being relevant—this is profoundly disturbing. Not frightening. Worse. *Diminishing.*

**What to do:**

Before your visit, spend time with this idea. Sit with it. You are going to a place that is complete without you. You are going to witness a system that has been running longer than you have been alive, that was not made for you, that will not remember you. This is not hostility. It is the simple fact of a world—a small, enclosed, self-sustaining world—that has no slot for a human observer. You are not the protagonist of the Cyst Works. You are not even the audience. You are a temporary thermal anomaly that the root system's moisture dynamics will adjust for and then forget.

If you can find peace in that—genuine peace, not performed equanimity—then the Cyst Works will be one of the most extraordinary experiences available in the multiverse. If you cannot, the visit will be an endurance test, and you should shorten it accordingly.

### The Pattern Problem

The human nervous system is a pattern-detection machine, and the Cyst Works is full of almost-patterns. The pump's interval is almost regular. The gauge needles twitch in almost-rhythms. The condensation gathers and releases in almost-cycles. The root system's branching follows almost-fractal geometries. The vine's path through the space almost traces a recognizable shape.

Your brain will try to complete these patterns. It will try to find the rhythm in the pump's interval, the message in the gauge's twitching, the intention in the vine's threading. It will work very, very hard at this, because that is what brains do, and because the alternative—accepting that these are systems operating on logics that do not include legibility to human cognition—is genuinely difficult for most people to sustain.

Travelers have emerged from the Cyst Works convinced they decoded the gauge readings. Convinced the pump's interval encodes a mathematical sequence. Convinced the vine is growing in the shape of a circuit diagram, a map, a word. None of these interpretations have survived scrutiny. All of them felt absolutely certain to the person who generated them. The Cyst Works is a Rorschach test that you take with your whole body, in the hot dark, while subsonic vibrations disrupt your vestibular system and your prefrontal cortex is mildly hypoxic from the elevated CO2. Of course you see patterns. You would see patterns on a blank wall under those conditions.

**What to do:**

Notice the patterns. Write them down if you want. But hold them loosely. Do not build a theory of the Cyst Works while you are inside the Cyst Works. Your cognition is compromised in there—not dangerously, not to the point of hallucination or psychosis, but enough that the confidence you feel in your interpretations is not calibrated to their accuracy. Save your analysis for after you leave, after you've cooled down, rehydrated, slept, and let your brain recalibrate to an environment that is not a resonant ovoid filled with subsonic vibration and bioluminescent fungus.

If you find yourself becoming very certain about something in the Cyst Works—certain that the pump is communicating, certain that the vine is aware of you, certain that the system is a single organism with a purpose you've just now grasped—treat that certainty as a symptom, not a revelation. Check your watch. Check your hydration. Take a breath. Consider leaving. The Cyst Works will still be there tomorrow. The insight, if it's real, will survive the walk back to the surface. If it doesn't survive, it wasn't real, and you've saved yourself the embarrassment of joining the long list of travelers who came back with grand theories and a look in their eyes that their friends found concerning.

### The Boundary Problem

This is the subtle one, and the hardest to prepare for.

The Cyst Works dissolves categories. Not aggressively, not through any mechanism you can point to, but simply by being what it is: a space where the distinction between organic and mechanical has been rendered meaningless through long cohabitation. The root grows through the pump. The vine enters the gauge. The generator hums and the stone resonates and the fungus glows and the water gathers and somewhere in all of this the line between "alive" and "not alive," between "built" and "grown," between "system" and "organism," has been not so much erased as revealed to have been, all along, a convenience rather than a fact.

Most visitors find this intellectually fascinating and physically uncomfortable. The discomfort is somatic. It manifests as a creeping uncertainty about your own boundaries—where your body ends and the environment begins. This is partly literal: you are wet, the air is wet, the surfaces are wet, and when you lean against a root trunk to rest and feel its slow creak transmitted through your ribcage, the haptic distinction between your body and its body becomes genuinely ambiguous. It is partly metaphorical: the Cyst Works is a system that has incorporated everything within it into itself, and you are within it, and the question of whether you are being incorporated is unanswerable in the moment.

You are not being incorporated. You are a visitor. The vine will not grow into you in the span of a ninety-minute visit. The root system does not have a mechanism for absorbing mammals. The pump does not want your bones. But the *feeling* of boundary-dissolution is real, and it is the thing that keeps some travelers from ever returning and that draws other travelers back again and again, and the difference between those two responses is not a difference of courage or weakness but of what you find tolerable in the space where your sense of self meets the world.

**What to do:**

Know yourself. Know whether you are someone who finds ego-dissolution experiences enriching or terrifying. If you have had positive experiences with meditation, sensory deprivation, certain carefully managed psychedelic experiences—if you know what it feels like to let the boundaries of self soften and have found that feeling valuable—you are well-suited to the Cyst Works. If you have found those experiences destabilizing, if you need a firm sense of where you end and the world begins in order to feel safe, the Cyst Works will test you in ways that are not fun and not useful.

There is a middle ground. Many visitors report that the boundary-dissolution feeling comes in waves—intense for a few minutes, then receding as you move or shift your attention. Having an anchor helps. The watch is an anchor. The notebook is an anchor. The act of language—naming what you see, even silently—is an anchor, because language is a categorization system and categories are boundaries and the maintenance of boundaries is exactly what you need when the Cyst Works is softening them. "That is a root. That is a pump. I am a person. I am visiting. I will leave." These sentences are more useful than they sound.

## The Approach

I haven't talked about how to get there. This is deliberate. The approach varies depending on which multiverse branch you're entering from, and the specifics of the route—which basement, which forgotten stairwell, which passage through the substrate of which city—are covered in detail in navigational guides that are better resourced for that purpose than this document. What I can tell you is this:

The descent takes time. Not enormous time—you're not spelunking through kilometers of cave system—but enough that by the time you reach the cavity you have been in enclosed, warm, damp, progressively darker space for long enough that your senses have already begun to shift. Use the descent as preparation. Slow down. Let your eyes adapt. Let your skin acclimate to the humidity. Let your ears begin to pick up the sounds that will, as you get closer, resolve into the drone.

You will know you are close when you feel the pump. Not hear it—feel it. The thunk travels through stone efficiently, and you will sense it in your feet and your chest before your ears register it as sound. When you feel it, stop. Stand still. Let it happen two or three times. Get a sense of the interval. This is your introduction to the Cyst Works' timekeeper, such as it is, and arriving already familiar with its rhythm will help you orient once you're inside.

The entrance—or at least the entrance used by most travelers, which may or may not be the entrance used by whoever installed the machines—is a narrow squeeze through a gap where the tuff has been shaped by water into a kind of valve, an opening that widens as you push through and then narrows behind you. It is not tight enough to be dangerous, but it is tight enough to be memorable. Most travelers report that the moment of entering the cavity—of pushing through the gap and feeling the space open around them, the heat hit their face, the drone suddenly become three-dimensional and enveloping—is the most intense sensory moment of the entire visit. Be ready for it. Breathe. Let it wash over you. Don't try to see everything at once.

## While You're Inside

### First Fifteen Minutes

Don't move. Seriously. Find a stable spot near the entrance—there's a relatively flat area where a root trunk meets the wall at a convenient angle, and most travelers end up there—and just be still. Let your eyes adapt. Let your ears learn the soundscape. Let the heat settle into you. The temptation to explore immediately is strong, and the space is small enough that you can see most of it from near the entrance, which makes the temptation worse. Resist it. The Cyst Works rewards stillness more than motion. The details—the translucent stone shelves, the bioluminescent frills, the vine's threading, the gauge needles' tiny arcs—are things that reveal themselves over minutes, not the things you see on a first scan.

During this time, check in with your body. How is the heat? How is your breathing? How is your emotional state? If anything is significantly wrong—real difficulty breathing, chest tightness that isn't just the subsonic vibration, panic that controlled breathing isn't managing—leave now. The entrance is right behind you. There is no penalty for a short visit. The Cyst Works does not judge.

### Exploration

When you move, move slowly. Partly for safety—the floor is uneven, the light is low, the root trunks create obstacles at every height—and partly because fast movement disrupts the condensation dynamics and causes the droplets on the ceiling to fall in cascades rather than their natural one-at-a-time rhythm, which is both a loss aesthetically and, arguably, an interference with the system's processes that you should minimize.

The pump is worth visiting. Stand near it—within a meter—and wait for its cycle. The thunk, at close range, is a full-body experience. You will feel it in your teeth. The hiss that follows is wet and complex; up close, you can hear harmonics in it that the distance smooths away. Look at the root that has grown through the impeller chamber. If your light is dim enough, you can see the root's surface pulsing faintly—not with a heartbeat, not with any animal rhythm, but with the slow hydraulic pressure of sap moving in response to the pump's mechanical action, or the pump's mechanical action responding to the sap's hydraulic pressure, or both, in a feedback loop that has been running long enough to have found its own equilibrium.

The gauges are worth examining. Bring your white light for this, briefly. The two moving needles—the ones in the uncracked gauges—are fascinating in their small motion, but the third needle, the one in the gauge that the vine has entered, is the one that people remember. Its motion is different. Where the other needles twitch in arcs that look like noise—random, small, purposeless—this needle sweeps. Slowly. Through about a thirty-degree range, with what might be pauses at certain positions, or might be your pattern-seeking brain inventing pauses. Travelers have spent hours watching this needle. I recommend watching it for five or ten minutes and then moving on, unless you brought enough water for a longer vigil and set your timer accordingly.

The far wall—the wall behind the generator—has the thickest concentration of fungal shelves and the most dramatic mineral formations. If you turn off your light entirely and stand here, facing the wall, the bioluminescence becomes genuinely visible: a faint, watery green glow emanating from the frilled edges of the fungal shelves, reflected and refracted by the condensation droplets on the stone, creating a dim galaxy of green points that shift and move as the droplets themselves shift and move. It is breathtakingly beautiful in a way that no one who has seen it has managed to adequately describe, and in a way that no photograph has captured. This is the image you will carry home from the Cyst Works, if you let yourself have it.

### What Not to Do

Do not touch the yellow vine. It is not toxic—nothing in the Cyst Works is toxic—but it is *responsive* in ways that are poorly understood. Travelers who have handled it report that the sections they touched grew faster in subsequent days, sending new tendrils toward the areas where contact was made, as if following a chemical trace. Whether this is tropism toward the salts in human sweat, a wound-healing response, or something else is unclear. In any case, introducing yourself as a chemical signal into a system this finely balanced seems unwise. Look. Don't touch.

Do not attempt to take samples. Not of the vine, not of the fungus, not of the moss, not of the mineral water, not of the calcite deposits. This is not a rule imposed by any authority—there is no authority here—but a practical recommendation. Every component of the Cyst Works exists in relationship to every other component, and a sample removed from that context is a sample removed from its meaning. Mycologists who have taken fungal samples report that the organisms do not survive outside the cavity; they are adapted to conditions—temperature, humidity, mineral content, vibrational frequency—that cannot be replicated. The vine samples die within hours. Even the mineral water, removed from the pool, drops its dissolved load and becomes ordinary within a day. The Cyst Works is not portable. It is not reproducible. It is only itself, only here.

Do not leave anything behind. I said this before, in the equipment section, but it bears repeating in the context of actually being there, because the impulse to leave a marker—a coin, a note, a small offering—is surprisingly strong and has been reported by travelers with no prior inclination toward ritual or superstition. The Cyst Works evokes something in people. Some deep, old part of the brain that recognizes a sacred space, or a living space, or a space that is doing something important and deserves acknowledgment. Honor that impulse if you feel it. But honor it by witnessing, not by leaving litter for the vine to grow through.

## After You Leave

You will be dehydrated. Drink immediately, before the ascent. Drink more than you think you need. The heat exposure will continue to affect you for hours after you leave—you may feel lightheaded, flushed, slightly dissociated. This is normal. It is not the Cyst Works doing something to you. It is your body recovering from prolonged heat stress.

You will want to talk about what you saw. Be cautious about who you talk to. The Cyst Works inspires two kinds of discourse, and both are traps. The first is the mystical interpretation: the Cyst Works is alive, the Cyst Works is conscious, the system is an intelligence, the pump's rhythm is a heartbeat, the vine is a nervous system, the gauge is reading the organism's vital signs. This interpretation feels true. It is not supported by evidence, and the feeling of its truth is a residue of the boundary-dissolution experience combined with post-heat-stress cognitive looseness. It will fade over the following days, and you will be embarrassed by anything you committed to in its grip.

The second is the reductive interpretation: it's just roots in a cave with some old machines. The roots found moisture and mineral nutrients and grew. The machines were abandoned by a monitoring project. The vine is an opportunistic climber. The fungus is ordinary bioluminescent mycology. The pump's rhythm is a mechanical failure mode. There is nothing strange here. This interpretation is defensible on every individual point and yet misses the thing entirely—the *thingness* of the thing, the way the individual components have, over time, composed something that is irreducible to its parts. If you find yourself retreating to this interpretation, it is probably because the boundary-dissolution experience frightened you and you are re-establishing categories as a defense. That's fine. That's healthy. But don't mistake the defense for the truth.

The truth, insofar as the Cyst Works has one, is somewhere the language can't quite reach. It is a system. It is alive in some senses and not in others. It is mechanical in some senses and not in others. It has been running for a long time and shows no signs of stopping. It is beneath a city that has forgotten it. It was named by people who are gone. It is monitored by gauges that report to no one. It grows, it hums, it thunks, it glows, it drips. It is the Cyst Works, Station 7, and whatever that means, it means it in the hot dark with absolute conviction, and your visit does not add to or subtract from that meaning by a single degree.

Write down what you remember. Do it within twenty-four hours, while the sensory memory is still vivid—the heat, the sound, the green glow, the moment the condensation droplet fell and the space seemed to listen. These memories degrade faster than most. Not because the Cyst Works does something to your memory, but because the experience is so predominantly somatic—so rooted in temperature, vibration, humidity, the body's own internal landscape—that the hippocampus, which prefers narrative and image, struggles to encode it. What you remember in a week will be a story about the Cyst Works. What you remember tonight is the Cyst Works itself, or as close to it as you'll get without going back.

## The Question of Return Visits

Many travelers go back. The Cyst Works is, for a certain kind of person, the most important place in the multiverse—not the most beautiful, not the most awe-inspiring, not the most dangerous, but the most *real,* in some way that the word "real" was always trying to point to and never quite reaching.

If you go back, you will notice changes. The root system will have grown, minutely. The vine will have threaded into new places. The pump's interval may be slightly different. The calcite ridges on the concrete pad will be fractionally taller. You will be able to perceive these changes because you were there before, and this perception—of being a witness to a process that operates on a timescale longer than your attention span but shorter than your lifespan—is a strange and valuable thing. You become, in a small way, one of the gauges: a measuring instrument, reporting to no one, calibrated to a system that did not ask to be measured.

Some travelers make the Cyst Works a regular practice. Annually, or more often. They keep notebooks. They track the pump's interval, the gauge needles' ranges, the vine's progress. They are building a dataset that no one has asked for and that may never be analyzed, and they are doing it in the hot dark with pencils on waterproof paper, and they are, in their own way, becoming part of the system—not incorporated, not absorbed, but adjacent. Measuring. Witnessing. Continuing.

Whether you become one of these people is not a decision you make during preparation. It is a decision the Cyst Works makes for you, or that you make in response to it, or that the distinction between those two things fails to capture. Go once. See what happens. Bring water, bring a red headlamp, bring a watch, bring a notebook, bring your capacity to sit with irrelevance and ambiguity and beauty that is not for you.

And when the pump thunks and the space holds its breath and the condensation trembles on the ceiling in its tiny inverted constellation—

Be there for it. That's all. Just be there for it.
