# Preparing for the Flats of Unceasing Thunder

## The Fundamental Challenge You're Facing

Before anything else, understand what this place is: an environment that will wage a slow, patient war against your body and sanity through three relentless vectors—sound, moisture, and mineral exposure—while offering almost nothing you can eat, drink safely, or use for shelter. It is not immediately lethal. It is *cumulatively* lethal. The difference matters because it means you will feel fine for the first several hours, possibly the first day, and by the time you feel unwell, the damage will already be substantial. Prepare as if the environment is lying to you about how dangerous it is.

---

## Hearing Protection: The Single Most Critical Item

This is not negotiable. This is not optional. This is the thing that, if you forget it, will end your visit as a meaningful experience within hours and begin to cause permanent damage within a day.

Continuous thunder at the intensity described—overlapping peals arriving from multiple storm cells at varying distances, felt in the sternum and joints, vibrating pooled water—suggests sustained sound pressure levels somewhere between 85 and 110 decibels, with transient peaks from nearby strikes potentially reaching 120 or higher. For reference, 85 dB sustained will cause hearing damage over an eight-hour workday. 110 dB will cause pain and damage within minutes. And this never stops.

**Layer your hearing protection.** Bring both of the following and wear them simultaneously:

**Custom-molded silicone earplugs** rated to NRR 33 (Noise Reduction Rating of 33 decibels). Have these fitted by an audiologist before departure if possible. The seal against your ear canal needs to be perfect, because any gap will allow low-frequency sound—the exact frequency range of thunder—to bypass the plug entirely. Foam plugs are a backup option but they compress unevenly, lose their seal when wet, and will be wet constantly here. Silicone resists moisture better. Bring at least six pairs. You will lose some, drop some in the water-film, watch some degrade from the mineral-laden moisture.

**Over-ear active noise-canceling headphones or electronic earmuffs** with an NRR of 25 or higher, worn over the earplugs. The combined protection of plugs-plus-muffs can theoretically reach NRR 36-40 in practice (the ratings don't simply add together due to bone conduction). Look for models designed for industrial or military use—specifically ones rated for sustained wet environments. Consumer noise-canceling headphones will fail here. The moisture will infiltrate them within hours. You need sealed units with gaskets, ideally with hydrophobic coatings on the cushions. Models designed for aircraft carrier deck crews are ideal—they're built for exactly this combination of extreme noise, wind, and salt spray.

**Critical note on bone conduction:** Even with perfect ear protection, the lowest frequencies of thunder—the sub-bass rumble you feel in your chest and joints—will bypass your ears entirely and vibrate your skull directly. There is no practical protection against this. You will feel the thunder in your bones no matter what you do. Over extended periods (days), this can cause fatigue, headache, nausea, disorientation, and a deep psychological unease that is difficult to describe but universally reported by people exposed to sustained infrasound. Accept this. Plan for it. Limit your exposure duration accordingly.

**Bring backup hearing protection in a sealed waterproof container.** If your primary protection fails and you cannot replace it, leave. Do not tough it out. The environment will seem tolerable for a while because your auditory system will temporarily threshold-shift to compensate, making things seem quieter than they are. This is not adaptation. This is your hearing being destroyed. The threshold shift becomes permanent somewhere between 24 and 72 hours of sustained exposure, depending on the individual.

## Water: You Are Surrounded By It and Cannot Drink It

The rain is laden with dissolved iron, manganese, and whatever other minerals the ancient bedrock releases under weathering. The description specifies an ochre residue on contact and a blood-like iron smell. This water is not potable. Drinking it in small amounts will taste terrible and cause gastrointestinal distress. Drinking it in the quantities you'd need to stay hydrated will cause iron toxicity: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and in sufficient doses, liver damage.

**Bring your own water.** Carry a minimum of 4 liters per day of planned stay, plus a 2-day emergency reserve. This is heavy. A five-day expedition means 28 liters minimum, which is 28 kilograms of water weight alone. Plan your loadout around this constraint. If you cannot carry enough, shorten your visit.

**Alternatively, bring a robust water purification and filtration system**—but understand its limitations. Standard backpacking filters remove biological contaminants (bacteria, protozoa) and particulates. They do not remove dissolved minerals. You need either a distillation setup or a reverse-osmosis filter to render this water safe. A lightweight portable distiller (solar stills will not work here—there isn't enough direct sunlight) powered by a compact fuel source could work: collect rainwater, boil it, condense the steam. The distillate will be mineral-free and safe. This is heavy and fuel-dependent, but it makes extended stays feasible.

**Rainwater collected before it contacts the stone** will be significantly cleaner than the water-films on the ground. Set up a tarp or collection membrane elevated above the rock surface. Rain that falls directly into your collector without touching the bedrock will have much lower mineral content—possibly low enough to drink after basic filtration, depending on what the rain picks up from the atmosphere itself. Test it before relying on it. Bring water-quality test strips that measure iron and heavy metals.

## Food: There Is Almost Nothing Here You Can Eat

The ecosystem runs on chemolithotrophy. The organisms here derive energy from inorganic mineral oxidation. This means the biological matter available is, from a human nutritional standpoint, almost entirely useless and potentially toxic.

**The crawlers:** Their shells are described as ceramic-like—calcium carbonate or silicate matrices, probably mineralized far beyond anything you'd want to chew. Their soft undersides might be technically organic tissue, but organisms that bioaccumulate iron and manganese from their food source will concentrate those metals in their tissues. Eating crawler flesh would be like eating a concentrated mineral supplement—not immediately poisonous in small amounts, but not nutritious in any way your body can use, and potentially dangerous in quantity. The risk of heavy metal accumulation is real.

**The ribbon-swimmers:** Translucent, muscular, living in iron-rich water. Same bioaccumulation concerns. Also, you don't know what their biochemistry looks like—whether their proteins are compatible with your digestive enzymes, whether they produce toxins, whether the "translucent" quality indicates a body composition that is more water and mineral matrix than actual tissue. Don't eat these.

**The drifting sac-creatures:** Absolutely not. Unidentifiable biology, possible chemical defenses, and you'd have to catch something that moves with the wind.

**The predators:** Six-legged, hard-pointed, fast. You are not going to hunt these, and they are going to have the same mineral accumulation as everything else, concentrated further by being a trophic level up.

**Bring all your own food.** Dense, calorie-rich, shelf-stable, and waterproof-packaged. This is not a foraging destination. Think expedition rations: freeze-dried meals, nut butters, energy bars, hard cheese in wax, dried meats. Anything that can survive constant moisture exposure without degrading. Resealable waterproof bags for everything. The humidity will find any weakness in your packaging.

## Shelter and Sleeping

There is no natural shelter. The terrain is rolling exposed bedrock with no caves, overhangs, cliffs, or vegetation. The cairns are chest-high at most and offer no meaningful protection from rain or wind. You are exposed to the elements at all times.

**Bring a freestanding tent** that does not require stakes driven into soil, because there is no soil. You need a tent that can be anchored to stone. Options:

- **Rock anchors or bolt anchors** that you can set into cracks in the bedrock. Bring a small hammer and a set of removable rock anchors (climbing gear works). Look for fractures in the stone where pieces have separated—the description mentions the cairn-builders prying up flat pieces, so fractures exist.
- **Weighted anchor bags** that you fill with small rocks or water to hold down your guylines. Less secure in high wind but simpler to deploy.
- **A geodesic dome tent** with a broad, stable footprint that can resist gusting winds without external anchoring. These are heavier but more self-sufficient.

**Your tent must handle the rain.** This is constant-rain territory. Not always directly overhead, but frequently enough that you should assume your shelter will be wet most of the time. Full-coverage rain fly, sealed seams, bathtub-style floor that extends several inches up the walls. The water on the ground is shallow—finger-width films and handspan-deep pools—but if you pitch your tent in a slight depression, even an inch of water pooling around and under it will wick through any floor seam. **Pitch on the highest local ground you can find.** The swells described are only a few meters in amplitude, but even a foot of elevation advantage will keep the migrating water-films from pooling under you.

**Ground insulation is critical.** The stone will conduct heat away from your body relentlessly. A standard foam sleeping pad is minimum. A double-layer of closed-cell foam beneath an inflatable pad is better. The rock is dark iron-grey—it will be cool, possibly cold, and it will leach your body heat through conduction all night. Combined with the saturated air (which accelerates convective heat loss) and the constant fine rain, hypothermia is a real risk even if the air temperature isn't dramatically low. The description says the air is "cool" and the rain is "almost warm compared to the air"—this suggests air temperatures perhaps in the 10-15°C range (50-60°F). Comfortable enough while walking and active. Dangerous while sleeping if you're not insulated from the stone.

**Sleeping in the thunder:** This is going to be one of the hardest parts of your visit. The sound never stops. Even with hearing protection, you will feel the vibration through the stone, through your sleeping pad, through your body. The lightning will strobe through your tent fabric all night—the cloud ceiling is low and the flashes are constant, and any tent fabric thinner than complete blackout will transmit them as pulses of blue-white light. Solutions:

- **Sleep mask.** A good one, contoured, that blocks peripheral light.
- **Melatonin or a mild sleep aid** for the first few nights while you habituate. Use sparingly—you want to maintain alertness during waking hours.
- **White noise is irrelevant here.** The thunder IS the white noise. Your brain may actually learn to sleep in it faster than you expect—constant noise is, paradoxically, easier to habituate to than intermittent noise, because there are no gaps-then-sudden-sounds to startle you awake. The overlapping, continuous quality of the thunder may work in your favor. Emphasis on "may."
- **Acceptance.** You may not sleep well here. You may not sleep much. Plan for shorter stays if you're sensitive to this, and build in rest days on either side of your visit in whatever universe you're staging from.

## Skin and Body Protection

The mineral-laden water and rain will coat every exposed surface of your body. The ochre residue is iron oxide—rust, essentially. It's not acutely toxic on skin contact, but prolonged exposure to mineral-saturated water will:

- Dry and crack your skin despite the apparent wetness (mineral salts are desiccating)
- Stain everything it touches a stubborn reddish-brown
- Irritate eyes, mucous membranes, and any open cuts or abrasions
- Potentially cause contact dermatitis after days of exposure

**Wear a full waterproof shell layer** at all times when outside your tent. Top and bottom. Hood up. This means a quality rain jacket and rain pants—not water-resistant, fully waterproof, with sealed seams. Gore-Tex or equivalent. The rain is fine and constant; anything less than fully waterproof will wet through within the hour.

**Waterproof gloves.** You will want to touch things—the stone, the cairns, the tool if you find it, the crawler shells. Every time you do, you coat your hands in ochre mineral film. Thin waterproof gloves (nitrile works for short interactions, but for sustained use bring neoprene or similar) will let you handle objects without direct mineral exposure.

**Eye protection.** The rain is fine enough to be carried by gusting wind directly into your face. Sealed goggles—not safety glasses, sealed goggles with an anti-fog coating—will keep mineral water out of your eyes. The lightning strobing is intense enough that tinted lenses might also be welcome, particularly during periods of rapid, close-range flashing. Not too dark, though—the ambient light is already dim. Photochromic lenses that adjust automatically would be ideal.

**Footwear.** You are walking on wet, polished stone. Some areas are glassy-smooth; others are pitted and rough. The water-films make everything slick. You need boots with excellent wet-rock traction—look for sticky rubber soles designed for canyoneering or wet scrambling. Vibram Megagrip or Stealth rubber compounds. Ankle support is important because the rolling terrain means constant subtle changes in grade, and a twisted ankle on bare stone with no rescue infrastructure is a serious problem. **These boots must also be waterproof.** Your feet will be in shallow water constantly. Waterproof boots with gaiters to keep the water from entering over the top.

**Bring multiple pairs of synthetic socks and change them every few hours.** Wet feet are inevitable; trench foot is not, if you manage moisture aggressively. Merino wool or synthetic blends that retain warmth when wet. Never cotton. Cotton will destroy your feet here faster than the stone will.

## Lightning Safety

You are standing on an exposed, flat, wet plain beneath an active storm system that never ends. You are likely the tallest object for hundreds of meters in any direction. The lightning is constant.

This is a genuine and serious risk.

**Understanding the risk level:** The description suggests mostly sheet lightning—pulses behind and within the cloud layers—rather than cloud-to-ground strikes. This is hopeful but not reassuring. In any storm system producing continuous thunder, cloud-to-ground strikes are happening; you may simply not be distinguishing them from the overwhelming visual and acoustic chaos. The wet stone surface is conductive. A strike anywhere nearby will send ground current radiating outward through the water-films, and you are standing in those water-films.

**Mitigation strategies:**

- **Insulate yourself from the ground as much as possible.** When stationary, stand or sit on an insulating layer—your sleeping pad, a dry bag, a rubber mat. This reduces ground-current risk. It does not protect against a direct strike, but direct strikes are statistically unlikely at any given point; ground current is the more common injury mechanism.
- **Do not stand near the cairns.** They are the tallest features on the landscape. Lightning will preferentially strike them. Give them a wide berth during active flashing—approach for study during relative lulls, if such lulls exist.
- **Stay low during transit.** The classic lightning position—crouching with feet together on an insulating layer—is impractical for extended travel, but be aware that standing fully upright makes you a preferential target. If you notice a dramatic increase in flash rate or feel your hair stand up or smell ozone, drop immediately to a crouch on your sleeping pad.
- **Lightning-rated personal protection** may exist in advanced multiverse-travel supply chains. If you can acquire a personal Faraday garment—a wearable mesh of conductive material that routes electrical current around your body rather than through it—bring it. Wear it as an outer layer. It will look ridiculous. You will not care when the sky splits above you.
- **Accept the residual risk.** You cannot make yourself safe from lightning on an open plain. You can reduce the probability of injury, but you cannot eliminate it. This is a calculated risk you accept when you choose to visit.

## Navigation

There are no landmarks beyond the cairns, which are spaced roughly half a mile apart but are not arranged in any pattern you can immediately discern. The terrain rolls in featureless swells. The cloud ceiling eliminates celestial navigation. The light shifts only gradually with the movement of the brighter region (the sun behind the clouds), which gives you a rough east-west orientation if you watch it long enough but no precision.

**Bring a compass.** Test it immediately on arrival. The bedrock is iron-rich—dark iron-grey with ochre mineral deposits—which means it may have enough ferromagnetic content to distort magnetic readings. If your compass spins or gives inconsistent readings, it is useless here. Have a backup navigation method.

**GPS will not function** unless this universe has a satellite constellation, which seems unlikely. Dead reckoning and terrain-relative navigation are your options.

**Map the cairns.** As you encounter them, record their positions relative to each other using pace counts and bearing estimates. The cairns are the only reliable reference features. Build a mental and written map as you explore. Note distinguishing features: collapsed vs. intact, presence of markings, fresh stones on top. This serves double duty—navigating and studying whatever culture built them.

**Mark your base camp.** Leave a visible, distinctive marker at your tent site—something tall enough to spot from a swell or two away, and something that won't be mistaken for a cairn by the builders (you don't want to confuse whatever communication system they're using). A brightly colored flag on a telescoping pole would work. Fluorescent orange or green will contrast with everything in this pewter-grey landscape.

## Electrical Equipment and Power

The saturated atmosphere, constant rain, and mineral-laden water will destroy unprotected electronics rapidly. The mineral content of the water is conductive—it will short any exposed contacts, corrode any exposed metal, and infiltrate any seal that isn't rated for full submersion.

**Everything electronic goes in an IP68-rated waterproof case or a sealed dry bag when not in active use.** When in use, minimize exposure time.

**Camera equipment:** You will want to photograph this place. The visual environment—the strobing lightning, the iron-red pools, the crawler patterns, the cairn markings—demands documentation. Use a waterproof camera or a standard camera in a waterproof housing rated for sustained rain exposure. A housing designed for underwater photography (with accessible controls) is ideal. Wipe the lens constantly. The mineral rain will coat it with ochre residue between every shot.

**Lighting:** The ambient light is dim. A headlamp is essential for any close-up work (examining cairn markings, studying the crawler biology, inspecting the knapped tool). LED, waterproof, with a red-light mode to preserve your dark adaptation during the dimmer periods. The lightning provides irregular illumination but you cannot time your observations to it.

**Power:** Solar charging is not viable—the cloud cover is too thick and permanent. Bring sufficient battery capacity for your entire stay, with margin. Lithium batteries perform well in cool temperatures. Keep them in waterproof cases and in an inner pocket close to your body when not in use, both for moisture protection and to maintain operating temperature.

**Electromagnetic interference:** Continuous lightning means continuous electromagnetic pulses. Sensitive electronics may experience interference, data corruption, or induced currents. A Faraday bag (signal-blocking pouch) for storage of sensitive devices when not in use is prudent. Don't leave anything powered on unnecessarily.

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section.

The Flats of Unceasing Thunder will grind at you in ways that are hard to anticipate from a description. The combined, unrelenting sensory assault—the sound that never stops, the vibration in your bones, the grey-violet dimness that never resolves into day or night, the wet that never dries, the featureless horizon that gives you nothing to fix your eyes on—produces a psychological state that experienced travelers to extreme environments describe variously as *dissolution*, *erasure*, or *the flattening*.

**You will lose your sense of time.** The absence of a clear day-night cycle, combined with the overwhelming dominance of the thunder (which masks the subtler auditory cues your brain normally uses to track the passage of time), will make hours feel like days and days feel like hours, unpredictably. **Keep a watch. Check it regularly. Log the time.** The cairn-builders seem to scratch tally marks, and there may be a deep wisdom in that—the act of marking time becomes an anchor for identity when the environment is trying to dissolve yours.

**You will become habituated to the thunder, and this will feel like peace, and it is not.** Around the 24-48 hour mark (varies individually), your brain will begin to tune out the continuous noise. The thunder will recede into background. You will feel calmer, more adapted, more at home. What is actually happening is that your auditory processing is downregulating in response to sustained overload—you are losing sensitivity, not gaining comfort. Do not remove your hearing protection because it "doesn't seem that loud anymore." It is exactly as loud as it was.

**The mineral smell will become invisible to you** through olfactory habituation. This also means you'll stop noticing how much mineral water you're inadvertently ingesting when you lick your lips, touch your face, eat with contaminated hands. Maintain hygiene discipline even when the motivation fades.

**The crawlers will become companions.** This is not a warning, exactly—it's an observation. In the absence of human company, on a featureless plain, under an oppressive sky, your brain will fixate on the most comprehensible living things it can find, and the crawlers—slow, purposeful, countable, differentiated by shell size and pattern—will become objects of intense interest and, eventually, something approaching affection. You may name them. You may start tracking individual crawlers across the stone, mapping their paths, noting their interactions. This is fine. This is healthy, probably. It gives your mind something to organize around. But be aware that it's happening because of the environment's effect on you, and use it as a diagnostic: if you realize you've spent four hours watching a single crawler without checking the time, you're deeper in than you think.

**Loneliness and the cairn-builders.** You will become preoccupied with whoever is making the cairns. This is inevitable. They are the only evidence of intelligence besides yourself, and the evidence is tantalizingly incomplete—marks you can't read, a tool placed and not retrieved, fresh additions to structures that might be ancient. You will construct elaborate theories about them. You will look for them on every swell, scan the dim horizon during every lightning flash. You may begin to feel that they are watching you—not because of any evidence, but because the human mind, isolated and under-stimulated, generates the sensation of being observed as a defense mechanism. **They may in fact be watching you.** The flat terrain offers little concealment, but the dim light, the rain, and the low cloud ceiling reduce visibility enough that a figure prone on the stone a few hundred meters away would be very difficult to spot. Consider that whatever approach you take to their cairns—touching, examining, photographing—is being observed and evaluated by beings whose intentions you do not know.

**Do not add stones to their cairns.** Do not scratch marks alongside theirs. Not until you understand what those acts mean. You don't know if a cairn is a waypoint, a grave marker, a territorial boundary, a devotional object, or a message board. Adding to it without understanding is, at best, noise in their signal. At worst, it means something you didn't intend.

## Approach to the Cairn-Builders

If you encounter them directly, or if they choose to approach you:

**Move slowly. Be visible. Do not shout**—they live in thunder; voice-range communication may not be their primary mode. Watch for gestural communication. Watch their hands, their body orientation, what they look at. The cairn-building itself is a form of communication, possibly the primary one: messages left in stone, durable enough to outlast the weather, readable by anyone who knows the code.

**The knapped quartz tool suggests sophisticated material culture.** Whoever these beings are, they understand their environment well enough to identify usable tool-stone, work it with skill, and leave it in specific locations (by a pool full of ribbon-swimmers—a fishing tool? a processing tool?). They may be physiologically human-like or they may not be. The description doesn't specify. Prepare for either.

**Leave an offering if you want to signal good intent.** Something they cannot make themselves—something from outside. A small brightly-colored object, perhaps, placed on the stone near a cairn. Not on the cairn. Beside it. This says: *I see your work. I add to the landscape, not to your structure. I am something new.* Then withdraw and observe.

## Duration Recommendations

**Day visit (6-12 hours):** Feasible and probably the wisest choice for a first trip. Bring water and food for the day, full weather protection, hearing protection. Focus on observing, documenting the nearest cairns, studying the fauna. Return to your staging universe before the psychological effects deepen.

**Extended stay (2-5 days):** The minimum needed to observe the ecosystem's rhythms—the migration patterns of the ribbon-swimmers, the movement of the water-films, the grazing circuits of the crawlers, the shifts in storm intensity. Requires full camp setup, serious water logistics, and strong psychological resilience. You will begin to feel the flattening by day two. Use your notebook. Write down your thoughts. Anchor yourself in language and observation.

**Long expedition (1-2 weeks):** Only if you are specifically trying to make contact with the cairn-builders or document the cairn network comprehensively. Requires either a resupply plan or a water-production system. Hearing damage is a real risk despite protection. The psychological toll will be substantial. Bring a partner if at all possible—the isolation compounds everything. Two people can check each other's perception, share observations, and maintain a social anchor that the environment cannot provide.

**Beyond two weeks:** Not recommended without compelling reason. The cumulative effects of infrasound exposure, sleep disruption, mineral contact, and sensory monotony become genuinely dangerous to cognitive function and emotional stability. The cairn-builders, whoever they are, have presumably adapted over generations. You have not.

## A Final Note

There is something about this place that the preparation cannot fully address, and it is this: the Flats of Unceasing Thunder are, in a profound sense, a world that has *finished.* The mountains are gone. The soil is gone. The rivers have completed their work and dissolved. What remains is the irreducible minimum—stone, water, minerals, and the organisms that learned to live on the boundary between chemistry and biology. The thunder is not a storm that will pass. It is the weather. It is the permanent state of the sky. The cairn-builders are not sheltering from it or waiting it out. They are living in it, as the crawlers live on the stone, as the ribbon-swimmers live in the films of water—fully, completely, without the expectation that it will ever be otherwise.

You will arrive with the assumption, buried deep in your psychology, that conditions will improve. That the clouds will break. That the thunder will stop. That the sun will come out. Releasing that assumption is the most important preparation of all, because until you do, you will be waiting instead of seeing. And this place, for all its barrenness, for all its noise, for all its ancient, worn-down austerity, has things to show you that you can only see when you stop waiting for it to become something else.

Go prepared. Go protected. Go humbly. And when you find the tool at the pool's edge, leave it where it is. Someone is coming back for it.
