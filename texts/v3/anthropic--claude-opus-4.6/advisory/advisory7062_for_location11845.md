# Preparing to Visit The Drowned Canopy

---

## First: Understanding What You Are Entering

Before packing a single piece of equipment, sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation failures will begin in your assumptions.

You are not visiting an underwater forest. You are visiting a world where the distinction between ocean and atmosphere has been dissolved — literally — into a gradient. There is no surface. There is no moment where you transition from breathing air to breathing water. There is a thickening. This means that the conceptual frameworks you bring from diving, from forestry, from oceanography, from spelunking — all of them will apply in fragments and none of them will apply whole. You are entering a medium that is not quite any medium you have trained for.

You are also entering a place that is, by every description available, in a state of total equilibrium. This is not a living ecosystem in the sense you understand living ecosystems — dynamic, competitive, evolving, responsive. This is a system that has *arrived*. It is not going anywhere. It is not coming from anywhere. It has no trajectory. This will affect your psychology in ways I will address at length, because the psychological preparation for this place is, I believe, more important than the physical preparation.

## The Medium: Navigating the Gradient

### The Atmosphere-Ocean Transition

The world has no surface. The transition from vapor to liquid is gradual, spanning hundreds of meters. This means your approach from above will involve a slow increase in density, pressure, and resistance around your body. You need to think about this carefully.

**Pressure acclimation will not work the way it does in conventional diving.** In a normal ocean, you hit the water, you descend, and pressure increases predictably — roughly one atmosphere per ten meters of seawater. Here, the gradient is different. The upper reaches are diffuse — a thick, wet atmosphere that slowly becomes a thin, warm proto-ocean. The pressure curve will be nonlinear and gentle at first, steepening as you descend into true liquid. Your equipment must handle a *continuous spectrum* of ambient density, not a binary air/water state.

What this means practically:

- **Standard dive suits will be inadequate.** A wetsuit assumes water. A pressure suit assumes gas. You need something that can manage both and everything in between. Look for — or commission from a multiverse outfitter who has dealt with gradient-medium worlds — an **adaptive-density environmental suit** with variable buoyancy compensation that can respond to real-time changes in ambient medium density. The suit should be able to function as atmospheric protection in the upper vapor layers and transition seamlessly into full hydrostatic support as you descend into true liquid.

- **Breathing apparatus must be multi-modal.** In the upper gradient, you may be able to extract oxygen from the thick vapor directly, depending on its composition (which you should scout or test before committing to a descent — send probes first). As you descend into liquid, you will need either a closed-circuit rebreather adapted for dissolved gas extraction, or a self-contained supply. I strongly recommend carrying your own supply rather than relying on extraction from the local medium, because you do not know the dissolved gas composition of this water. It may be oxygen-rich (the trees are pumping mineral-laden warm water, and photosynthetic processes may or may not be occurring — the description mentions no leaves, so the oxygen cycle here may be entirely different from what you expect). It may contain dissolved gases that are toxic to human biology. **Do not breathe the local medium until it has been analyzed.**

- **Your gas supply must be enormous by conventional standards.** You are going to be in-medium for the entire visit. There is nowhere to surface. There is no air pocket. There is no beach. You will be breathing from your supply for the entire duration of your stay, and if your stay extends beyond your supply, you will die in a place that does not notice. Plan for redundancy. Carry primary, secondary, and tertiary breathing systems. Cache additional supplies at your entry point if you can.

### Buoyancy and Movement

You will be neutrally buoyant or close to it once you are in the true liquid layer. Movement will be by swimming, by fin propulsion, by small thrusters, or by drifting in the current systems the forest itself generates.

**Drifting is the primary mode of transit here.** The forest manufactures its own ocean circulation. Warm water rises from the canopy. Cool water sinks at the plateau edges. There are stable thermal plumes, permanent vortices, reliable current corridors. Learn them. Map them as you go. They are your highways. Fighting the current in a medium this vast will exhaust you and deplete your resources for trivial gains. Move *with* the system.

However: the currents are driven by the canopy's crown-tube pumping, and they are vast and slow. "Slow" at this scale may still mean speeds significant enough to carry you kilometers from your intended position if you are not anchoring or actively navigating. Carry a **reliable inertial navigation system** that does not depend on surface reference, GPS, magnetic north, or any external signal that may not exist in this world. Dead reckoning and pre-mapped landmark navigation (using trunks, shimmer-walls, and distinctive root formations) will be your primary tools.

**A small, quiet propulsion unit** — something low-vibration, low-noise — is essential for maneuvering in the understory, where current is minimal and the distances between trunks are measured in hundreds of meters. Emphasis on *quiet* and *low-vibration*. I will explain why below.

### Temperature

The understory is described as cold. The canopy layer receives volcanic heat carried through the root systems. There will be a significant thermal gradient — cold at the root mat and in the spaces between trunks, warming as you ascend toward the canopy, with pockets of real warmth inside the thermal plumes. Your suit must provide **thermal regulation across a wide range**. Hypothermia in the understory is a real risk during extended exploration. You will be immersed continuously. Cold water conducts heat away from the body twenty-five times faster than cold air. Even "cool" water at, say, 10-15°C will incapacitate you within hours without thermal protection.

Plan your excursions to alternate between the understory (cold, dark, still) and the canopy or mid-water thermal plumes (warm, bright, active). This is not just comfort — it is survival physiology.

## The Sound: The Most Important Thing You Will Prepare For

I need to spend significant time on this because I believe the sound environment of the Drowned Canopy is the single factor most likely to cause a visit to fail.

### What You Will Experience

The description is explicit: millions of crown-tubes expressing water, cilia beating in synchrony, thermal columns rising through kilometers of water. The sound is "loud the way a waterfall is loud if you live inside it." It is bassy. It is pervasive. It is felt in the chest, in the bones. It has overtones. It creates visible standing waves in the water.

**Water transmits sound approximately 4.3 times faster than air, and with far less attenuation.** Low-frequency sound in water can travel thousands of kilometers. You are inside a world-sized resonating chamber filled with millions of biological sound sources, and the medium itself is an almost perfect conductor for their output.

This means:

1. **The sound will be inescapable.** There will be no quiet place. There will be no shelter from it. Every trunk, every root, every surface is coupled to the medium and is both a transmitter and a reflector of this sound. You cannot outswim it. You cannot hide from it.

2. **The sound will be physical.** At the power levels implied — industrial-scale water pumping through millions of organ-pipe-sized tubes — the acoustic energy in the water will be felt as vibration in your body. Your chest cavity will resonate. Your sinuses will vibrate. Your vision may blur at certain frequencies if the vibration couples to your skull. If there are infrasonic components below 20 Hz (and given the scale of the tubes, there almost certainly are), you may experience the well-documented physiological effects of infrasound exposure: unease, anxiety, feelings of awe or dread, nausea, disorientation, and at sufficient intensity, resonance of internal organs.

3. **The sound will be beautiful.** This is the dangerous part. The description mentions harmonic overtones, interference patterns, shimmer-walls of standing waves. This is not noise. This is a world-organ playing a single continuous chord that has been tuning itself for geological time. It will be the most profound thing you have ever heard. It will vibrate structures in your inner ear that have never been vibrated before. You may weep. You may feel religious. You may feel that you have finally heard what music was always trying to approximate. **Do not let this experience override your judgment.** The beauty is incidental. The forest is not performing for you. It does not know you are there.

### Acoustic Protection

You need **active noise management**, not passive ear protection. Earplugs will be useless — the sound will conduct through your skull bones, through your suit, through the water column pressing against every square centimeter of your body. You need a suit-integrated acoustic dampening system that can:

- Attenuate frequencies below 20 Hz (infrasonic) to prevent organ resonance
- Reduce overall sound pressure level at the ear to safe ranges (below 85 dB continuous exposure)
- **Selectively pass** frequencies you want to hear — the crackling of grazers, the movement of eels, your own equipment alerts — while suppressing the overwhelming drone
- Monitor cumulative acoustic exposure and alert you when you are approaching damage thresholds

**Bone conduction is your enemy here.** Even with perfect ear protection, sound in this medium will vibrate your entire skeleton and transmit to your cochlea through bone. Your dampening system must include some form of vibration isolation between the medium and your body — possibly a suit with an intermediate gas layer or active vibration cancellation at the suit shell.

Test your acoustic protection extensively before descending to canopy level. The understory is quieter (relatively — it is still the background drone of a world-engine transmitted through a perfect acoustic medium). The canopy, where you will be inside the machinery itself, will be dramatically louder. Ascend slowly and monitor your acoustic exposure continuously.

### Psychological Effects of the Sound

Even with protection, you will be living inside a sound. The drone will become your baseline. After hours of exposure, you will stop consciously hearing it, and then you will start to feel its absence in your body if your dampening system momentarily increases attenuation. This oscillation between perception and non-perception of an omnipresent stimulus can cause **dissociative effects**. You may feel that the sound is coming from inside you. You may feel that your heartbeat has synchronized with it. (It may have. Low-frequency rhythmic stimuli can entrain cardiac rhythm. Monitor your heart.)

Plan for regular "acoustic recalibration" periods where you consciously attend to the sound, note its character, and reaffirm your identity as separate from it. This is not mysticism. This is sensory hygiene. The sound will try to become part of you because it is so constant and so all-encompassing. Maintaining the boundary between "the sound" and "me" is a concrete psychological task that you should practice before arrival and perform deliberately during your visit.

## Light and Vision

The light is directionless. It comes from everywhere. It is the color of old paper. Shadows are suggestions.

This will disorient you more than you expect.

Human vision relies heavily on shadow, contrast, and directional light to perceive depth, shape, and distance. In the Drowned Canopy, especially in the canopy layer, you will be in a uniformly lit environment where depth cues are radically diminished. Objects will appear flatter than they are. Distances will be difficult to judge. The pale trunks, already smooth and featureless, will look like they could be twenty meters away or two hundred meters away, and you will not be able to tell without active ranging.

**Carry an active sonar/lidar ranging system** integrated into your helmet or suit. Not for navigation only — for basic spatial awareness. Your eyes will lie to you in this light. Believe your instruments.

The shimmer-walls — standing acoustic waves made visible by aligned particulate — will be the most visually striking features of the mid-water and canopy environment. They are described as luminous, gorgeous, permanent. They will also be **perceptual traps.** Because they are flat, vertical planes of shimmering light in an otherwise directionless-light environment, your visual system will latch onto them as the dominant spatial reference. You will orient to them unconsciously. If you are navigating by landmark, you may find yourself treating shimmer-walls as "walls" — as boundaries, as edges of spaces — even though they are acoustically generated artifacts with no physical substance. You can swim through them. They are not barriers. But your brain, starved for normal spatial cues, will treat them as solid until you train yourself otherwise.

**Practice, early in your visit, swimming through several shimmer-walls.** Feel what it is like. Notice the slight tingling as you pass through the standing wave. Note that nothing changes except the visual effect. Teach your brain that these are decorations, not architecture. This will save you from navigational errors later.

The understory, by contrast, will be dark — jade-colored, the description says, and dark *relative* to the canopy. Not pitch dark. But dim enough, and directionless enough, that your biological vision will be operating at its margins. The bioluminescent flanks of the bladder-bodied "birds" will be visible here, faint amber lights drifting in the gloom. **Do not navigate toward them.** They are predators. They eat the grazers, and they may investigate you if you approach. Their bioluminescence may be attractant in function. You do not know. Maintain distance and observe.

Carry **adjustable artificial illumination** — something that can produce directional light when you need depth cues, but that you can dim or switch off when you want to see the environment as it naturally presents. A powerful directional light in the understory will create the first real shadows this place has seen in — well, in whatever unit of non-time applies — and the visual effect will be extraordinary but also potentially disturbing to fauna that have never experienced shadow. Use light judiciously.

## Pressure at Depth

The canopy sits roughly a thousand meters above the plateau floor. The plateaus rise two thousand meters above the surrounding seabed. The water goes from the crust to above where the atmosphere should be.

Depending on where you enter and how deep you descend, you may be dealing with pressures ranging from near-atmospheric (in the upper gradient) to **enormous** (at the plateau floor or, god forbid, in the deep water beyond the plateau edges).

If the canopy is at, let's say, a few hundred meters of water-equivalent depth below the point where the medium becomes true liquid, you are already looking at pressures of 20-30+ atmospheres. The understory, a thousand meters below the canopy, could be at 100+ atmospheres. The plateau floor — the root mat, the fossil stone — could be at pressures that are simply incompatible with human physiology without extreme technological intervention.

**You must determine the pressure profile of this world before committing to a depth.** Probe first. Send instruments. Map the pressure gradient from the upper vapor layers down to the canopy, down through the understory, down to the root mat. Determine where in this column a human body in a pressure suit can survive, and do not go deeper than that line.

Most likely, you will need a **hard-shell atmospheric diving suit** or a **one-atmosphere submersible** for any exploration below the upper canopy. A soft suit will not protect you at the pressures implied by the described geometry. If you are in a hard suit, your mobility and tactile contact with the environment will be limited. If you are in a submersible, you will be separated from the medium entirely — you will not feel the water, will not feel the vibration directly, will experience the drone only as transmitted through your hull.

This is a significant tradeoff. The experience of the Drowned Canopy is fundamentally a haptic, full-body, immersive experience. Being inside a submersible will let you see it and hear some of it, but you will not *feel* it the way the description implies it should be felt — the cold water holding you, the drone filling every cavity in your body. Conversely, being in the water in a soft suit at survivable pressures may limit you to the upper canopy and the zone above it, where the experience is less complete.

**My recommendation:** Use a hard suit for initial exploration and safety. Map the environment. Identify safe zones. Then, if you find regions where the pressure is within soft-suit tolerance — perhaps the upper canopy, perhaps the spaces just above the crown-tubes — switch to a soft suit for a limited period to experience the full sensory immersion. Have your hard suit or submersible nearby as immediate shelter. Do not linger in the soft suit. The cold, the pressure, the acoustic energy, and your own finite gas supply all impose time limits that are shorter than your desire to stay.

## Interaction with the Ecosystem

### The Forest

The trees will ignore you. This is stated outright and it is the truth: nothing here will react to your presence in any way that you would recognize as reaction. You are a temporary mote in a permanent system. The trunks are too large for you to affect. The root mat is harder than stone. The crown-tubes operate at scales that make your presence irrelevant.

**Do not touch the cilia.** The crown-tube interiors are lined with cilia the size of a human arm, and they are in continuous rhythmic motion. If you enter a crown-tube — and the tube diameters are described as railway-tunnel-scale, so you physically can — you will be in an environment of powerful, rhythmic, biological machinery. The cilia are moving water. They will move you. You could be swept through the tube and ejected in a thermal plume, possibly at speed, possibly into the open water above the canopy, possibly disoriented and far from your entry point. **Do not enter the crown-tubes.** Observe from outside. Observe the mouth of the tube, where you can see the cilia working. Do not put yourself inside the machine.

The bark is described as smooth, almost ceramic. You can touch it. You can rest against it. It is a reasonable anchor point in the understory, where you might want to stop and conserve propulsion energy. The biofilm coating every surface may be slippery — bring textured grips or gentle adhesion tools for anchoring to trunk surfaces. Be aware that the grazers moving across the bark in herds may investigate your hands or equipment if you are stationary against the surface for extended periods. They are described as small and armored — probably not dangerous, but their calcite shells "ring musically" when they scrape bark, which means they are hard-edged and their feeding behavior involves mechanical abrasion. They could damage soft suit components or exposed equipment if they decide to graze on your biofilm-accumulating surfaces.

### The Fauna

**The eels:** Silver, translucent, gill plates the size of dinner tables. These are large animals navigating by acoustic landmarks (the shimmer-walls). They travel in schools on fixed routes. They are described in the mid-water column, between canopy and understory. They are, as far as we can tell, not predatory toward anything your size — their navigational behavior suggests they are migrants or filter-feeders following the thermal plumes. However, "gill plates the size of dinner tables" implies an animal that is *very large*, and a school of very large animals moving through the water near you presents a collision risk. They follow fixed routes. Once you identify those routes (by observing the shimmer-walls they thread between), stay out of the traffic lanes. Observe from adjacent voids.

**The sessile filter-feeders:** Anemone-analogues the size of weather balloons, clinging to the undersides of horizontal branches, tentacles trailing forty meters downward. **Forty meters of tentacle.** In a world with no current in the understory (the currents are in the mid-water and above), those tentacles are hanging straight down into the cold, still water of the sub-canopy space. You need to know whether those tentacles are passive mechanical filters or whether they carry nematocyst-analogues, venom, adhesive compounds, or anything else that could harm you on contact. **Assume they are dangerous until proven otherwise.** Give them a wide berth — wider than forty meters, because you may misjudge distance in the directionless light, and a tentacle is a thin structure that is very hard to see in jade-dark water.

**The "birds":** Bladder-bodied, neutrally buoyant, bioluminescent, automobile-sized, predatory. They live in the understory gloom. They eat grazers. They have been eating the same prey in the same region for an incomprehensible duration. They are probably not interested in you — you are not a grazer, you do not look like a grazer, you do not move like a grazer — but they are *predators*, and predators in equilibrium systems may have very simple behavioral triggers. If you scrape against bark (like a grazer does), if you are stationary on a trunk surface (like a grazer is), if you are in the understory gloom near the root mat (where grazers stray when they are about to be eaten) — you might match enough of the pattern. Their size alone (automobile-scale) makes them dangerous even if their "attack" is just an investigatory approach. Bioluminescence in the understory is *their* signal, not yours — if you are carrying lights in the understory, consider whether your light signature could be misinterpreted as a conspecific signal or a prey signal by these creatures. Probably not, but consider it.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section I have been building toward, and it is the most important.

### The Permanence Problem

The Drowned Canopy is, by its own description, a place that has *stopped*. It has reached equilibrium. Nothing is changing. Nothing is growing. Nothing is decaying. Nothing is becoming anything else. It has no age, because age implies change.

**You are a creature of change.** Every cell in your body is metabolizing, dividing, aging, dying, being replaced. Your thoughts are sequential. Your experience of consciousness is a flowing river of moments, each one slightly different from the last. You are entropy's creature — you exist because of thermodynamic gradients, because of disequilibrium, because things are moving from one state to another and you are part of that movement.

You are entering a place that has *finished moving.*

The psychological effect of extended exposure to a static environment is well-documented even in mundane contexts — sensory deprivation studies, Antarctic winter-over syndrome, solitary confinement research all describe what happens when the external environment stops providing novelty, variation, and change. The mind begins to generate its own. Hallucinations. Time distortion. Dissociation. A creeping sense that you yourself are becoming permanent, becoming part of the furniture, becoming fixed.

In the Drowned Canopy, this effect will be amplified by several factors:

1. **The sound never changes.** The drone is permanent, stable, unvarying. It will become the texture of your consciousness. After enough hours, you may stop being able to distinguish between the drone and your own internal monologue. This is not a metaphor. Constant auditory stimulation at sufficient intensity literally habituates the auditory cortex to the point where novel internal auditory experiences (your own thoughts, in their "heard" form) become harder to distinguish from the external signal.

2. **The light never changes.** There is no day-night cycle mentioned. The glow comes from above, directionless, constant. Without a day-night cycle, your circadian rhythm will begin to free-run within 24-48 hours of arrival. You will lose track of time. You will sleep at irregular intervals and for irregular durations. Your mood will destabilize — circadian disruption is one of the most reliable triggers for affective disturbance.

3. **Nothing responds to you.** The forest does not notice you. The fauna, for the most part, will not react to your presence. You are not a participant in this system. You are not even an observer in any meaningful ecological sense — observation implies that the observed could, in principle, be changed by the act of observing, and nothing here can be changed by anything. You are a ghost. You are irrelevant. This will, after some hours, begin to feel like a statement about your fundamental nature rather than a situational fact.

4. **The scale is inhuman.** Trunks hundreds of meters apart. Canopy a thousand meters overhead. Crown-tubes the diameter of railway tunnels. Eels with dinner-table-sized gill plates. Everything here is built at a scale that excludes you. You are not merely small — you are *scaled out*. You are below the resolution of this world's attention. In human environments, even very large ones (cathedrals, canyons, open ocean), there are features at human scale that let you locate yourself. Here, the smallest features described — the grazers, the biofilm — are also indifferent to you. There is no scale at which you *fit*.

### Countermeasures

**Bring a companion.** If at all possible, do not visit the Drowned Canopy alone. Another human being — someone who breathes, who talks to you, who changes expression, who gets tired and annoyed and fascinated and bored — is the single most effective psychological anchor against the permanence of this place. A companion gives you someone who exists in *your* timeframe, who shares your scale, who responds to you, who changes. If the forest is a bell that has stopped ringing, your companion is proof that ringing is still possible.

**Maintain a rigid schedule.** Impose an artificial day-night cycle on yourself. Set alarms. Sleep at designated times, even if you don't feel tired. Eat at designated times. Perform routine suit checks at designated times. Change your position — ascend, descend, move laterally — on a schedule, not on impulse. The goal is to maintain your own internal narrative of sequential, changing time against the world's insistence that time is irrelevant. Every transition you make — "now I am ascending to the canopy" / "now I am returning to the understory" / "now I am resting against this trunk" — is an act of temporal self-assertion. You are saying: *I am still happening.* Keep happening. On schedule.

**Journal extensively.** Write, dictate, record. Not just observations — feelings, reactions, the quality of your experience. The act of narrating forces you to experience time as sequential. "First I saw this, then I felt this, then I moved here." Narrative is inherently temporal. The Drowned Canopy has no narrative. You must supply your own, and you must do it actively, or the world's narrativelessness will seep into you.

**Set a firm departure time and do not extend it.** Decide before you arrive how long you will stay. Carry a countdown timer visible at all times. When the timer reaches zero, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. Do not say "just another hour." The desire to stay — to drift, to let the drone fill you, to watch the shimmer-walls, to become part of the permanent — will grow stronger the longer you are there. It is not a desire that serves you. It is the place doing what it does: absorbing things into its equilibrium. You are not immune. Leave on time.

## Equipment Checklist (Summary)

### Life Support
- Adaptive-density environmental suit (vapor-to-liquid rated)
- Hard-shell atmospheric suit or one-atmosphere submersible for deep work
- Primary closed-circuit rebreather with maximum duration gas supply
- Secondary rebreather (independent system)
- Tertiary emergency gas supply (bailout)
- Cached gas resupply at entry point
- Thermal regulation system (active heating, capable of continuous operation in cold water)
- Medium-analysis kit (dissolved gas composition, toxicology, particulate content)

### Navigation and Spatial Awareness
- Inertial navigation system (no external signal dependency)
- Active sonar/lidar ranging (helmet-integrated)
- Mapping and recording system for landmark identification
- Low-vibration, low-noise propulsion unit
- Gentle adhesion/anchoring tools for trunk surfaces
- Depth/pressure monitoring (continuous, with alarms)

### Acoustic Management
- Active noise cancellation system (suit-integrated)
- Infrasound attenuation (below 20 Hz)
- Bone-conduction vibration isolation
- Selective frequency pass-through (configurable)
- Cumulative acoustic exposure monitor with alarms

### Vision
- Adjustable directional illumination (dimmable, shutoff-capable)
- Low-light vision enhancement for understory
- UV/alternate-spectrum imaging (may reveal features invisible in the yellow-white ambient light)

### Communication and Recording
- Two-way communication with companion (acoustic or hardwired)
- High-resolution recording equipment (visual, audio, pressure, temperature)
- Journal/dictation system (always accessible)

### Psychological
- Countdown timer (prominent, non-dismissable)
- Pre-set schedule with alarms (sleep/wake/eat/move cycle)
- Companion (the single most important item on this list)
- Familiar personal objects — a photograph, a piece of music from home (stored locally, playable through your suit audio), a letter from someone who changes, who ages, who is *in time*

### Emergency
- Emergency ascent system (rapid return to upper gradient)
- Emergency beacon (may not transmit through the medium — plan for this; tethered line to entry point may be more reliable)
- Self-rescue medical kit (pressure injury, hypothermia, acoustic trauma)
- Contingency plan for companion incapacitation

## Final Notes

You will want to stay.

I want to be very clear about this. You will enter the Drowned Canopy with a plan and a schedule and a countdown timer and a companion and all of your careful preparations. And at some point — maybe after three hours, maybe after thirty — you will be drifting between the pale trunks in the cold jade water with the drone in your bones and the shimmer-walls catching the directionless light, and you will feel something settle in you. A stillness. A sense that you have been doing this forever. That the timer on your wrist is counting down to nothing, because nothing is what comes after everything has already happened, and everything here has already happened.

The grazers will be chittering on a trunk nearby. An eel school will pass in a silver ribbon a hundred meters above you, threading between two shimmer-walls on a route they have always followed. The amber glow of a bladder-bird will pulse faintly in the understory below, patient and permanent.

And you will think: *What if I just stayed here?*

That thought is the place talking. The place does not talk. The place does not think. The place does not want you. But the equilibrium is *gravitational* — not physically, but psychologically. It pulls things into itself the way a basin pulls water: not through desire, but through shape. The shape of this world is *rest*. Permanent, total, unchanging rest. And you — you are so tired, traveler. You are always in motion, always changing, always becoming the next version of yourself. Wouldn't it be nice to stop?

No. It would not be nice. It would not be anything. That is the point. Niceness requires change — the transition from not-nice to nice, the appreciation of the difference. In the Drowned Canopy's equilibrium, there is no difference. There is no appreciation. There is only the state. The bell that has stopped ringing does not enjoy its silence. It simply is bell-shaped and silent.

You are not a bell. You are a song. Songs end, and that is what makes them songs. Go to the Drowned Canopy. Hear its chord. Feel the drone in your chest. Watch the shimmer-walls and the silver eels and the pale columns of wood rising through the eternal water. And then leave. Carry the memory of it back into a world where things change, where things end, where time matters, where your heartbeat is your own rhythm and not an echo of something vast and permanent and indifferent.

The memory of the Drowned Canopy will be one of the most beautiful things you own. But it will be beautiful *because you left*. Because you returned to a world where beauty is fleeting, where the light changes, where the sound fades, where forests grow and die and grow again. Where things become.

Set your timer. Bring your friend. And come home.
