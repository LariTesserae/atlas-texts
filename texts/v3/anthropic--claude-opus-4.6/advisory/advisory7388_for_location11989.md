# Preparing to Visit The Weeping Shelf

---

## First: The Disposition You'll Need Before You Begin Preparing at All

Before you pack a single item, before you study a single map, you need to sit with something uncomfortable. This place does not want you to arrive efficiently. I don't mean it's hostile—it is the opposite of hostile—but the entire character of the Weeping Shelf is one of slow accretion, patient process, water finding its way through microscopic pathways over centuries. If you approach it with the mindset of someone executing a plan, you will arrive on the ledge and find only a wet rock with some moss on it. The preparation for this place is as much about preparing your *tempo* as your gear.

Begin slowing down several days before you intend to travel. I mean this literally. Walk more slowly than you need to. Eat without reading. Sit somewhere and listen to ambient sound—traffic, rain, a refrigerator hum—and try to hear the individual components within it. You are training yourself to perceive at the resolution the Shelf operates at. You're going to need to hear the difference between seven different drip-rhythms. You're going to need to notice a black salamander against black wet stone. If you arrive still vibrating at the frequency of your ordinary life, these things will be invisible and inaudible to you, and you will have climbed a mountain for nothing.

## Understanding the Multiverse Context

As an ordinary multiverse traveler, you should consider which version of this place you're visiting. The description carries no named geography, no coordinates, no cultural markers. This matters. The Weeping Shelf exists in what experienced travelers sometimes call a "convergent location"—a place whose essential character recurs across many branches of the multiverse with only minor variations. Mountains with dark metamorphic rock, southwest-facing concavities, persistent groundwater seepage, warm microclimates at elevation—these are not rare geological features, and the combination produces something recognizably *this place* across dozens of known branches.

This means:

**The approach terrain will vary.** In some branches the cloud forest below is temperate, dominated by species you'd recognize from Earth-adjacent worlds—rhododendron, laurel, beech. In others it's subtropical, and the vegetation is denser, stranger, thorned. In at least two documented branches, the cloud forest includes organisms that are not quite plants—photosynthetic but mobile on geological timescales, reshaping themselves over decades. These are not dangerous but they can be disorienting, because the path you used ascending may not match what you see descending. Mark your route with something the forest cannot absorb. Biodegradable surveyor's tape is inadequate in these branches. Use small cairns of stones gathered from *below* the cloud forest band, where the geology is different enough to remain visually distinct.

**The altitude will vary.** High enough that valleys flatten into abstraction and clouds pass at eye level—this could mean anything from 2,500 meters to 4,500 meters depending on the atmospheric density and mountain scale of the branch you're visiting. Research the specific branch. Altitude sickness is real and it doesn't care about the transcendence of your destination. If you're above 3,000 meters in any Earth-standard atmosphere, you need acclimatization time. Plan to spend at least two nights at intermediate elevation before the final ascent to the Shelf.

**The Shelf's "behavior" may vary in subtle ways.** The seven basins, the dark stones, the polyrhythmic dripping—these are consistent across branches to a degree that makes experienced travelers uneasy. Too consistent. The number seven recurs. The dark vitreous stones that don't match the local geology recur. The acoustic amplification recurs. Whether this represents convergent geology, something deeper about the structure of the multiverse, or something about the Shelf itself is a question you should hold lightly and not attempt to answer during your visit. You are not going there to solve it. You are going there to be in the presence of it.

## Physical Preparation

### Fitness

The Weeping Shelf has no trail. Let me say that again because it's the most important logistical fact about this trip: **there is no trail.** You will be climbing through dense cloud forest on a mountain slope steep enough that the shelf represents a notable concavity in its face. This means:

- Bushwhacking through rhododendron thickets, which if you've never done it is one of the most physically punishing forms of overland travel. The branches interlock. The foliage is dense enough to block light. You cannot go over it or around it; you go through it, bent nearly double in places, pulling yourself through gaps, climbing over root masses. This is full-body work and it will take three to five times longer than you expect.

- Steep uphill travel on uncertain footing. The mountain's surface beneath the cloud forest will be schist—the same dark metamorphic rock as the Shelf—and schist weathers into plates and flakes that slide underfoot. Wet schist is treacherous. And it will be wet. The same groundwater that feeds the Shelf's seepage saturates the slope below it. Every surface will have a film of moisture.

- Route-finding without landmarks. Inside the cloud forest, visibility may be ten meters or less. You cannot navigate by the Shelf's sound until you're very close—the forest absorbs it. You'll be navigating by compass, altimeter, and slope angle, adjusting constantly.

You need to be in good cardiovascular condition and have strong legs, particularly eccentric strength for steep downhill travel on the return. But more than raw fitness, you need *patience fitness*—the ability to move slowly for hours without becoming frustrated. Train for this by going on long hikes where you deliberately cut your pace in half. The mountain will enforce this pace whether you've practiced it or not, but if you haven't practiced it, the enforced slowness will agitate you, and you'll arrive at the Shelf still agitated, and that will ruin everything.

### Acclimatization

Spend three to five days at moderate altitude before attempting the Shelf. Many branches have settlements or base areas in the valleys below the mountain. Stay there. Walk at elevation. Hydrate aggressively. If you experience headache, nausea, or unusual fatigue, wait another day. The Shelf has been weeping for longer than anything alive can remember. It will still be weeping tomorrow.

## Gear

### What to Bring

**Footwear:** This is the single most important gear decision. You need boots with aggressive tread that grip wet rock, ankle support for steep uneven terrain, and waterproofing that actually works—not water-resistant, waterproof. Your feet will be in contact with wet surfaces for the entire approach and for the duration of your time on the Shelf. If your feet are cold and wet, you will not be able to give the Shelf the attention it deserves. Neoprene-lined mountaineering boots are ideal. Break them in thoroughly before the trip.

However: consider also bringing a pair of thin-soled water shoes or neoprene socks for wearing on the Shelf itself. The stone is worn smooth by millennia of water. It is not sharp or uncomfortable. And there is something about standing on the Shelf in heavy boots that feels wrong—like wearing shoes in someone's home when they've asked you to remove them. No one has asked. But you will feel it. Thin soles will let you feel the temperature of the stone, the slight undulations of the water channels, the texture of the schist. This sensory contact matters here more than you'd expect.

**Clothing:** Layer for variable conditions. The approach through cloud forest will be warm, humid, and physically demanding—you'll want moisture-wicking base layers and the ability to strip down. The Shelf itself maintains its "perpetual gentleness" of temperature, but the air is saturated with moisture; anything you wear will become damp within minutes, not from sweat but from the ambient humidity. Synthetic or merino wool fabrics that retain warmth when wet. Nothing cotton. A light waterproof shell for the approach—not because of rain necessarily, but because the cloud forest itself is wet, every leaf and branch depositing moisture on you as you push through.

**Rain gear for your pack:** Everything inside your pack should be in dry bags. The humidity on the Shelf will find its way into anything that isn't sealed. Your journal, your food, your spare clothing—bag it all separately.

**Navigation:**
- Compass (the Shelf faces roughly southwest; this is your primary approach bearing for the final portion of the climb)
- Altimeter or GPS with barometric altitude (you need to know when you're approaching the Shelf's elevation)
- Topographic map of the specific branch if available. If not, be prepared to scout. The Shelf occupies a concavity in the slope; from below, if you can get a clear sightline (rare in the cloud forest), you're looking for a break in the green—a dark horizontal line where stone is exposed.

**Water:** Here's an irony: you're climbing to one of the most water-abundant locations on the mountain, but you need to carry your own drinking water for the approach. The groundwater seeping through the Shelf is almost certainly potable—it's been filtered through meters of schist—but "almost certainly" is not a standard you should apply to drinking water in an unfamiliar branch of the multiverse. Bring a reliable filter or purification system. On the Shelf, you can filter water directly from the basins. The water in them is extraordinarily clear—the description of it appearing to be a lens laid into the stone is accurate—and after filtration, it is some of the finest water you will ever drink. Cool, mineral-inflected, carrying the taste of the stone it passed through. Bring a vessel you enjoy drinking from. This matters.

**Food:** High-calorie, compact, quiet. I mean that last word. Do not bring food in crinkly wrappers. Do not bring food that requires preparation, heating, or anything that produces sound beyond the sound of your own chewing. Nuts, dense bread, dried fruit, hard cheese if the branch supports it. Eat before you arrive at the Shelf if you can. Eating on the Shelf is not prohibited by anyone or anything—it's not a temple, it's a geological feature—but you will find that the act of eating feels like an interruption there, a different category of activity than what the space invites. Better to arrive fed and let the Shelf fill you with something other than food.

**Shelter:** You do not need a tent on the Shelf. The overhang provides protection from rain, and the thermal mass of the schist keeps the temperature stable through the night. However, the humidity means everything will be damp by morning. Bring a lightweight bivy sack or sleeping bag made of synthetic insulation (down will absorb moisture and lose its loft). A sleeping pad is essential—the stone, though smooth, is hard and conducts heat away from your body. A closed-cell foam pad is better than an inflatable here; inflatables can puncture on schist.

Plan to spend at least one night. Dusk and dawn on the Shelf are different experiences than afternoon, and you need both. The description of the basins becoming "seven small windows looking upward into the purple overhead" at dusk—this is real, and seeing it is worth the discomfort of sleeping on stone.

**Light:** Bring a headlamp for the approach through the forest, but once on the Shelf, use no artificial light. Your eyes will adjust. The wet stone catches and holds ambient light—starlight, moonlight—in ways that dry stone cannot. The basins will show you the sky. The moss wall will be a dark presence you feel more than see. If you turn on a headlamp, you will blind yourself to all of this, and you will turn the Shelf into a lit stage, which it is not. It is a place that includes darkness as one of its materials.

If you must have light—to find your water bottle, to check on something—use a red-filtered light at the lowest possible setting, directed downward, for the shortest possible duration.

### What NOT to Bring

**Recording equipment:** I know. I know you want to capture the sound. Everyone wants to capture the sound. The polyrhythmic dripping, the aggregate hiss, the way the overhang gathers and returns it—it seems like exactly the kind of thing that should be recorded and shared. But recording equipment introduces a layer of mediation between you and the Shelf that will fundamentally alter your experience. You will spend your time monitoring levels, adjusting microphone placement, worrying about wind noise, and listening *to the recording* rather than *to the place*. The sound of the Shelf cannot be meaningfully captured anyway. It is spatial. It is architectural. A stereo recording of it will be a flat approximation that conveys the content of the sound—water dripping—without any of its character. Leave the recorder at home. Listen with your body.

Photographs are a lesser but similar concern. A few photographs taken quickly and without fuss—fine. But do not spend your time on the Shelf composing shots. The Shelf is more beautiful than your photograph of it will be, and every moment you spend looking at it through a viewfinder is a moment you are not looking at it with your eyes.

**Hammocks, chairs, or other comfort furniture:** There is nowhere to hang a hammock. There is nothing wrong with sitting on wet stone. This is part of the experience. The mild physical discomfort of a damp seat keeps you from becoming so comfortable that you stop paying attention. The Shelf is not a place to relax, exactly. It is a place to be alert in a very quiet way.

**Books, journals (with a caveat):** Do not bring a book to read on the Shelf. You will not want to read. Bringing a journal for writing afterward—off the Shelf, at camp—is wise. You will want to record what you experienced, and you will find that the experience resists language in interesting ways. The attempt to write about it is itself part of processing it. But don't write on the Shelf. Be on the Shelf.

**Incense, candles, ritual objects, offerings:** The Shelf is not a temple. Treating it as one—performing ceremony, leaving gifts—imposes a framework of meaning that may not apply and that will certainly interfere with your ability to perceive whatever meaning the place actually holds. The Shelf has a purpose it has not disclosed. Deciding in advance what that purpose is, and acting on your decision, is a way of closing yourself to the actual strangeness of the place. Arrive with empty hands and an open question.

## The Approach

### Route Planning

You will need to determine the Shelf's approximate location on the mountain before you begin climbing. In most branches, local knowledge exists—not directions, exactly, but references. People who live in the valleys below the mountain know about the Shelf even if they've never been there. It appears in local language as a landmark, a weather indicator ("when the cloud sits on the Weeping Shelf, rain comes within two days"), a reference point in stories. Ask. Listen carefully to what people say and what they don't say. In some branches, locals will be pleased that you want to visit; in others, they will be puzzled, or cautious, or subtly discouraging without ever saying you shouldn't go. None of this reflects danger. It reflects the fact that the Shelf is a place that provokes complicated feelings in people who live near it—a sense of something significant that they cannot explain or use.

The general approach in most branches: ascend the mountain's southwestern face (the Shelf faces southwest, so you're approaching from the direction it looks toward). Enter the cloud forest. Climb through it. Look for the point where the vegetation changes—where the dense laurel-rhododendron zone gives way to the band of thick moss and epiphytes that indicates proximity to the Shelf's moisture plume. The air will get wetter. The ground will get wetter. You may notice the sound before you see the Shelf—but only if you're close, and only if the wind is quiet. The sound doesn't carry far through the forest.

Plan the approach so that you arrive in early-to-mid afternoon. This gives you the full sequence of light: the golden-green luminous afternoon, the transition of dusk, the night, the dawn. Each is different. Each reveals something the others don't.

### Time Required

The approach through the cloud forest will likely take four to eight hours depending on the specific terrain of your branch, the density of the vegetation, and your fitness level. Start early. Do not rush. If you find yourself rushing, stop and stand still for five minutes. Listen to whatever sounds surround you. Then continue at a pace that feels almost too slow.

## On the Shelf: What to Expect and How to Be

### Arrival

You will push through the last of the dense vegetation and find yourself suddenly on bare stone. The transition is abrupt—the description of the green density stopping at the shelf's edge "as if respecting a boundary" is exact. One step you are in the forest; the next you are on wet dark rock, in open air, with the sound wrapping around you.

Your first reaction will likely be physical: a kind of full-body attention, as if every nerve ending has suddenly activated. The humidity, the warmth, the sound, the light—they all hit you at once. Your eyes will take a moment to adjust to the change in light quality. The air will feel different in your lungs.

Stand still. Just stand still at the edge for several minutes. Do not walk to the center. Do not go look at the basins or the moss or the stones. Just stand at the threshold and let the place come to you. It will. The sound will organize itself in your hearing—you'll begin to pick out individual drip-rhythms from the aggregate hiss. The light will settle. Your breathing will slow down without you deciding to slow it down.

### Moving on the Shelf

Move slowly and deliberately. The stone is smooth and wet; it is slippery, though not dangerously so if you're careful. Step in the shallow water channels when possible—the flowing water provides slightly better grip than the smooth ridges between them, and your feet are going to get wet anyway.

Do not step on the moss. Do not step on the liverworts. Do not touch the ferns. This is not a rule imposed by anyone; it is something you will feel. The organisms on the Shelf are so clearly, palpably old—generations compressed into felt, centuries of slow growth—that damaging them will feel like a violence out of all proportion to the physical act. Watch where you put your feet.

The dark stones scattered across the Shelf: you may approach them, look at them, crouch near them. Do not pick them up. I cannot give you a rational reason for this. They are just stones. But they have been sitting in their depressions for what may be centuries, each one slowly settling into the schist, each one developing its own tiny ecosystem of tentative moss. Lifting one would be an interruption of a process so slow and so patient that it would feel—I'm going to use this word even though I can't fully defend it—*rude*. You will understand when you see them. They are at rest. Leave them at rest.

### The Basins

The seven basins are the heart of the Shelf's experience. Spend time with each one individually before you try to hear them together. Crouch beside a basin and watch the drops fall into it. The water is so clear that you can see the bottom of the basin—the smoothed schist, perhaps a few grains of mineral sediment, the play of light on the submerged stone. Watch the ripples from each drop. They are tiny, perfect, concentric, and they interact with the ripples from the next drop in patterns that are endlessly varied and never repeat.

Then sit in the center of the Shelf—you'll find a natural sitting spot, a slightly raised area of stone between several channels that is damp but not actively flowing—and listen to all seven at once. This is what you came for, even if you didn't know it.

The polyrhythm is real. It is not a metaphor or an exaggeration. Seven different drip rates, each determined by the specific geometry of its water source and the size and shape of its basin, produce a composite rhythm that has genuine musical complexity. Your brain will try to find a pattern. It will succeed, briefly—you'll hear what seems like a repeating figure, a motif—and then the rhythms will drift apart and the pattern will dissolve. And then, minutes later, a different alignment will emerge. Different basins will coincide. A new almost-pattern will form.

The description says these alignments feel "like a word almost spoken." This is accurate. There is a sensation—limbic, pre-verbal, felt in the chest rather than processed in the forebrain—of meaning approaching and receding. Not meaning you can articulate. Not a message. More like the *shape* of meaning, its contour, perceived from a distance. This can be profoundly moving. Some travelers report weeping. Some report a deep calm that persists for days afterward. Some report nothing in particular and wonder what the fuss was about. All of these responses are valid. The Shelf does not guarantee a specific experience. It offers conditions in which certain kinds of experience become possible.

### The Salamander

If you sit quietly near the largest basin for twenty minutes or more, you may see the salamander. It is small, black, glistening. It surfaces slowly, like something being born from the water. It rests on the basin's rim. It watches nothing you can identify.

Do not attempt to touch it or interact with it in any way. Its presence is a gift of your patience, not a reward. It is not performing for you. It is living its life. You are a witness, briefly, to the fact of it.

In some branches, there are multiple salamanders. In at least one documented branch, there is something in the largest basin that is not a salamander—something eyeless, translucent, the size of a thumb, that surfaces and rests on the rim in exactly the same way the salamanders do in other branches. Travelers who have seen it describe feeling an intensification of the Shelf's characteristic quality—the sense of being attended to, the sense that the place has a purpose operating on a timescale beyond human perception. This organism may be part of that purpose, or it may just be an animal that lives in a basin. There is no way to know, and the attempt to determine which is the wrong approach to the Shelf.

### Duration

Stay at least one full afternoon-to-morning cycle. Arrive in early afternoon. Experience the golden-green light of full sun. Watch the transition to dusk: the overhang darkening, the moss wall going black-green, the basins becoming sky-mirrors. Sit through the darkness. Sleep if you can, but don't worry if you can't—the sound of the Shelf at night is different, lower, slower, and being awake in it is its own form of rest. Experience dawn, which brings a cool blue light that makes the Shelf look entirely different—austere, mineral, the water and stone dominant over the green. Then leave.

Staying longer is possible and some travelers do—two nights, three. But there is a point of diminishing returns, and it comes sooner than you might expect. The Shelf's effect is not cumulative. It is a single sustained experience that reaches its full depth within the first twelve to eighteen hours and then plateaus. Staying beyond that risks tipping from receptivity into obsession—wanting the basins to *say* something, wanting the dark stones to *explain* themselves—and this wanting is the opposite of what the Shelf rewards. The Shelf rewards the absence of wanting. When you notice yourself wanting, it's time to leave.

## Psychological and Emotional Preparation

### The Sense of Being Attended To

The description is precise about this: not watched, but *attended to*. The distinction matters. Being watched implies an observer with an agenda—surveillance, judgment, interest. Being attended to implies a kind of generalized awareness, a orientation toward you that is not about you. The Shelf is aware of your presence the way a room is aware of a sound made inside it—it responds, acoustically and thermally and in the subtle way the humidity shifts around a warm human body, but it does not *care* about you specifically. You are an event occurring within it. It attends to events.

This can be unsettling. Most uncanny-feeling places are unsettling because they feel hostile or haunted or dangerous. The Shelf is unsettling because it feels *patient*. It has been doing what it does—seeping, dripping, ticking, growing moss, wearing stone—for longer than human civilization, and it will continue doing so after you leave, and your presence on it is a vanishingly brief interruption in a process that operates on a timescale that makes your entire life look like a single drip falling into a basin. This is not a metaphor. It is literally true, and being in a place where this truth is palpable—where the depth of time is audible in the rhythm of water and visible in the smoothed stone and the compressed moss and the settled stones—can trigger a response in you that ranges from peaceful awe to existential vertigo.

Prepare for this. Not by steeling yourself against it—that would be counterproductive—but by acknowledging, before you go, that you are going to a place where your sense of your own duration and significance will be gently but firmly challenged. If that prospect excites you, good. If it frightens you, also good—the Shelf is one of the safest places in the multiverse to feel small. Nothing here is hostile. The patience of this place is not the patience of a predator. It is the patience of stone.

### The Almost-Words

Multiple travelers have reported the sensation described in the text—the drip-rhythms aligning into something that feels like approaching meaning. Some travelers have found this profoundly moving; others have found it maddening. One traveler described spending four hours trying to "decode" the basin rhythms and leaving frustrated and angry. Another described hearing the rhythms align once, feeling a surge of emotion she couldn't name, and weeping for several minutes without distress.

The preparation here is: **do not try to understand the basins.** Do not listen *for* something. Listen *to* something. The rhythms are not a code. They are not a message. They are water falling into stone. The fact that water falling into stone can produce a sensation of approaching meaning says something extraordinary about the nature of pattern and consciousness, but what it says is not something the basins can tell you. It is something you can only observe in yourself while the basins do what they do.

If you find yourself trying to decode, trying to count drips, trying to establish ratios between basin frequencies—stop. Stand up. Walk to the shelf's edge. Look out at the valley. Remember that you are on a mountain, in the air, above the world. Then go back and listen again, this time without trying.

### After You Leave

The effects of the Shelf are subtle and delayed. Most travelers report feeling nothing unusual for the first day or two after leaving. Then, gradually, something shifts. Sounds become more interesting. The rhythm of rain on a roof, traffic patterns, the cadence of a conversation—these begin to reveal a complexity you hadn't noticed before. You start hearing polyrhythms in ordinary acoustic environments. This is not a hallucination or a supernatural effect. The Shelf has simply trained your auditory attention to a higher resolution. You were always surrounded by complex rhythmic environments; now you can hear them.

This heightened auditory attention fades over weeks to months, settling into a slightly-above-baseline sensitivity that most travelers describe as permanent and pleasant.

Some travelers also report a lasting change in their relationship to patience—a greater comfort with slowness, with waiting, with processes that take longer than a single sitting to complete. Whether this is a direct effect of the Shelf or simply the result of having spent a day sitting on wet stone listening to water is unclear and probably doesn't matter.

## Ethical Considerations

### Leave Nothing

Nothing. Not a thread, not a food wrapper, not a boot print if you can help it (you can't, on the wet stone, but the water will erase your prints within hours). The Shelf is not pristine in the sense of never having been visited—some of the stone smoothing on the floor may reflect the passage of feet over centuries, though this is impossible to distinguish from water-wear—but it is complete. Adding anything to it diminishes it. The impulse to leave a marker, a cairn, a memento—resist it completely. The dark stones that are already there are not yours to add to. The basins are not wishing wells. The moss wall is not a surface for carving.

### Take Nothing

Not a stone, not a piece of moss, not a vial of water. Removing anything from the Shelf disrupts relationships that have been establishing themselves for centuries. The dark stones sit in depressions they have worn over unimaginable time. The moss grows on moisture the wall provides. The water in the basins is in conversation with the stone of the basins. These relationships are the Shelf. Taking a piece of the Shelf is taking the Shelf apart.

### Tell Thoughtfully

When you return from the Shelf, people will ask about it. Some of those people will want to visit. Consider carefully how you describe it and to whom. Not everyone should go—not because the Shelf is exclusive or sacred, but because the journey is demanding, the location is fragile, and the experience requires a disposition that not everyone possesses or wants to cultivate. Someone who goes to the Shelf hoping for a transcendent experience and finds only a wet rock will not be harmed, but they may harm the Shelf—through impatience, through the impulse to *make* it be what they expected, through leaving trash or taking souvenirs or, in the worst case, attempting to "improve" the site.

Describe the Shelf honestly. Describe the difficulty of the approach. Describe the wet, the discomfort, the hours of sitting on stone. Describe the absence of spectacle—no waterfall, no grand vista from the Shelf itself (you're facing into the mountain), no wildlife beyond a salamander you might not see. Let the people who are right for the Shelf recognize themselves in this description. Let the others decide it's not for them. Both outcomes are correct.

## A Final Note on the Dark Stones

I've left this until last because it's the thing I'm least certain about, and honesty about uncertainty seems appropriate when preparing someone for the Weeping Shelf.

The dark stones don't belong there. They are not the same mineral as the schist. They are river-smooth in a place with no river. They are vitreous, dense, round. No source rock above matches them. No creature could have carried them. They are simply present.

In some branches of the multiverse, travelers have had the stones analyzed after the fact—not by taking them, but through non-invasive scanning, spectral analysis from a distance, that sort of thing. The results are inconsistent across branches. In some, the stones are a mundane igneous rock, basalt or obsidian, explicable by some past geological event—volcanic ejecta, glacial transport. In others, the mineral composition doesn't match any known formation on the mountain or in the surrounding region. In one branch—a single account, not verified—a traveler with geological training reported that the stones appeared to be *older than the mountain they rested on*, composed of material that predated the tectonic events that created the range.

I don't know what to make of this. I don't know what you should make of it. The stones sit in their depressions. The moss grows on them tentatively. They catch the last light of dusk and hold it a moment longer than the schist around them.

When you are on the Shelf, near one of them, crouching in the warm wet air with the basins ticking their asymmetric time all around you—you will look at the stone and you will feel something. I can't tell you what. It will not be fear. It may be recognition. It may be the same thing you feel when you meet someone who has been waiting for you without knowing it was you they were waiting for.

Or it may be nothing. It may just be a stone.

Go. Sit with it. Find out.
