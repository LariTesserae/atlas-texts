# Preparing for the Plateau of Living Engines

## A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## First and Most Important: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, before you check a single piece of gear, you need to sit with something uncomfortable. This is not a destination in the way you are accustomed to thinking about destinations. You are not visiting a place where life happens to exist. You are proposing to insert yourself — a discrete, bounded, individual organism with personal boundaries and a strong sense of where *you* end and *not-you* begins — into a system that has abolished that distinction entirely. The Plateau of Living Engines has no empty space. It has no unused surface. It has no room for you. You will not be welcomed, because the concept of a welcome implies a threshold, an inside and an outside, and the Plateau is all inside. Everything is already touching everything else.

This means your preparation is not primarily about survival gear, though survival gear matters enormously. Your preparation is primarily *psychological and philosophical*. You need to arrive with your mind in a particular state, or this place will break something in you that gear cannot fix.

---

## Psychological Preparation

### Months Before Departure

**Claustrophobia inoculation.** Begin working with confined spaces well in advance. Not the dramatic kind — not caves or coffins — but the subtle, pervasive kind. Spend time in dense forests where the undergrowth presses against you from all sides. Visit cities during their most crowded festivals and practice standing still while bodies press past you continuously. The Plateau's claustrophobia is not about walls closing in. It is about *fullness*. Every cubic centimeter is claimed. You will not find a clear patch of ground to sit on. You will not find a gap between the towers wide enough to stand in without touching them. You will push through biological material constantly, and it will close behind you, and there will be no direction you can look that is not filled with living structure. If tight spaces trigger panic in you, this must be addressed. Thoroughly.

**Sensory overwhelm training.** The drone alone can incapacitate an unprepared visitor. Descriptions of the sound environment make it seem manageable — a hum here, a whisper there, a periodic call. But you must understand scale. A trillion mite-carapaces sliding against each other is not a whisper. It is a whisper multiplied until it becomes a roar that registers as a whisper only because no single source is loud. The subsonic grinding of the grub-colonies will vibrate your internal organs. The web-pulses will hit you as physical impacts, brief shudders in the structure you are leaning against or pushing through. All of this, simultaneously, continuously, with no pause and no silence and no escape. Practice spending extended periods — hours, then days — in environments of constant, layered noise. Industrial spaces. Ship engine rooms. Standing beside waterfalls. Learn to let the sound become background. Learn to think inside it. Learn to sleep inside it, because you will have to.

**Scale comprehension exercises.** The Plateau is continent-sized. Your traverse of it — assuming you are crossing any meaningful fraction — will take weeks at minimum, possibly months, pushing through packed biological material at what may amount to a few hundred meters per hour. You need to be at peace with this. You need to have made your peace with the possibility that you will spend many consecutive days making what feels like no progress at all, surrounded by a vista that does not change because it is uniform in every direction, under a sky that does not change because it is featureless white at all times. This is not a trek with landmarks and milestones and scenic overlooks. This is immersion in a living medium. If you need external variety to maintain psychological stability, develop internal resources before you go. Meditative practices. Memory palaces. Mathematical games. Narrative construction. Something that lets your mind roam while your body inches forward through an unchanging world.

**Identity reinforcement.** This is the strangest piece of preparation, and perhaps the most important. The Plateau is a superorganism, or something very like one. Everything in it is part of something larger. The mites are part of the moss. The moss is part of the tower base. The towers are part of the web network. The web network is part of a communication and structural system that spans the entire landmass. There is no individual creature on the Plateau that exists in isolation. You will be the only discrete, self-contained entity for thousands of kilometers. This will produce a feeling — subtle at first, growing over days — that you are *wrong*. That your separateness is a deficiency. That you should be connected, should be part of, should be contributing, should be integrated. Travelers to hive-environments and superorganism-spaces report this consistently: a mounting sense of loneliness that is not social loneliness but something deeper, a *structural* loneliness, the feeling of being the only unattached node in a fully connected network. Some travelers describe it as a pull, an almost gravitational attraction toward dissolution. You must have a strong sense of who you are, where your boundaries are, and why they matter. Journal. Carry photographs. Carry objects that are meaningful to you alone, that have no function in any system, that exist purely because they are *yours*. You will need anchors.

### In the Days Before Arrival

**Rest.** Truly rest. This is a place that will tax every system you have — respiratory, cardiovascular, thermoregulatory, psychological, perceptual. You should arrive as close to peak condition as you can manage. Sleep long nights. Eat well. Hydrate aggressively (you will need the reserves). Do not arrive already depleted from transit. If your multiverse crossing method is itself exhausting, build in recovery time on the other side before you step onto the Plateau.

**Altitude acclimatization.** If your transit point allows it, spend at least a week at high altitude before arrival. The Plateau's atmosphere is thin — the description mentions lungs aching, air sparse and biting. This is consistent with altitudes of 4,000 meters or above, possibly well above. Altitude sickness on the Plateau would be catastrophic. There is no easy evacuation. There is no "going downhill." You are on a tableland that stretches in every direction. If pulmonary edema or cerebral edema begins, you cannot descend. You can only retreat to your entry point, pushing through dense biological growth, in thin air, while your lungs fill with fluid. Acclimatize beforehand. If pharmaceutical altitude aids are available to you (acetazolamide, dexamethasone), bring them, but do not rely on them as your primary strategy. Your body needs to have made the physiological adjustments before you arrive.

## Physical Gear and Equipment

### Breathing

**Supplemental oxygen.** Non-negotiable. Even if you have acclimatized, you will be exerting yourself continuously — pushing, climbing, shouldering through dense biological structure. Your oxygen demand will be high and the supply will be low. Carry supplemental oxygen with the highest capacity-to-weight ratio available to you. Closed-circuit rebreather systems are ideal if you can source them, as they recycle exhaled gas and dramatically extend your supply. Open-circuit systems burn through compressed gas too fast for a multi-week traverse.

**Air filtration.** The atmosphere is saturated with biological particulate. A trillion mite-carapaces in constant motion means chitin dust. The moss layer likely sheds microscopic fragments. The grub-colonies' mineral processing probably produces calcium-ite particulate. The condensation cycle means all of this is alternately wet and dry, creating ideal conditions for it to be inhaled and to adhere to mucous membranes. A high-quality particulate filter over your mouth and nose at all times. Not a simple cloth mask — a properly sealed respirator with replaceable filters. Carry many replacement filters. Budget at minimum one per day, possibly more if the particulate load is as dense as the biological density suggests.

**Humidity management.** The air is cold and moisture cycles strangely here — exhalations from below, condensation, freezing, melting. Your respiratory tract will be under assault from dry cold air at altitude and intermittent high humidity near the stone surface. A heat-moisture exchanger (HME) in your breathing system can help moderate this.

### Thermal Protection

This is a cold environment and you will be in it constantly. There is no shelter on the Plateau in any conventional sense — the biological structures are not hollow spaces you can crawl into (the towers are inhabited by their builders; the web spaces are structural, not sheltering). You will sleep in the cold, work in the cold, eat in the cold.

**Layering system.** Base layers of moisture-wicking material are essential. The condensation cycle means you will be intermittently damp from environmental moisture, and your own exertion will produce sweat. Wet cold kills faster than dry cold. Wick moisture away from skin aggressively.

Mid-layers of insulating material. Choose synthetic over down — down loses its insulating properties when wet, and you *will* be wet. Synthetic insulation maintains loft even when damp.

Outer shell must be both windproof (for the rare air pressure shifts that can produce brief wind) and water-resistant (for the condensation that will coat every surface including you). But it must also be durable against abrasion. You are pushing through a world of sharp basalt edges preserved in outline under moss, of bone-hard tower surfaces, of tensioned web fibers. Your outer layer will be in constant contact with rough, hard, sometimes sharp biological and geological structure. Ripstop is insufficient. You need something closer to mountaineering-grade abrasion resistance. Canvas-weight synthetic, reinforced at the shoulders, forearms, hips, and knees.

**Hand protection.** Critical. Your hands will be your primary tools for moving through the tower forest — pushing, gripping, breaking. The tower surfaces are described as ridged like cartilage and the material is harder than the basalt beneath. This will shred unprotected skin. Heavy gloves with reinforced palms, flexible enough to maintain dexterity but thick enough to protect against abrasion and cold. Carry multiple pairs. They will wear through.

**Foot protection.** Rigid-soled boots with ankle support. The substrate is uneven basalt under a layer of living moss that conceals the true topography. The moss gives when stepped on (because it is full of mites, because it has understructure) which means your footing is perpetually uncertain — you are stepping on a living surface that deforms unpredictably under your weight. This is an ankle injury waiting to happen. High, stiff boots. And gaiters — the moss-mite layer will try to colonize your footwear. More on this below.

**Sleep system.** A sleeping bag rated for extreme cold, with a bivy sack for moisture protection. You will not be able to pitch a tent — there is no clear ground, and driving stakes through the moss-mite layer into basalt would require specialized equipment and would also mean destroying part of the living system (with unpredictable consequences — does the web network register damage? Do the web-tenders respond? More on this below). A hammock is theoretically possible, slung between tower structures, but the towers are inhabited and the vibration of your weight might disrupt grub-colony work, and you would be within the web network's structural matrix, which is to say *in the nervous system of the Plateau*. Your movements would be transmitted. Things would respond. Better to sleep on the moss layer itself, in your bivy, accepting the mites, accepting the cold, accepting the subsonic hum through your body all night. Earplugs help. Vibration dampening under your sleeping pad helps more — closed-cell foam, thick, to insulate you both thermally and mechanically from the living substrate.

### Nutrition and Hydration

**Do not eat anything on the Plateau.** This cannot be stressed strongly enough. The biological systems here are deeply integrated and entirely alien. The moss-mite layer, the grub secretions, the web fiber material — none of it has been tested against your biochemistry. The mineral processing that the organisms perform may concentrate elements that are toxic to you. The biological material itself may contain proteins or chemical compounds that provoke severe immune responses. And beyond the biochemical danger, there is a systems-level concern: everything on the Plateau is part of the Plateau. Consuming any of it means consuming part of an integrated superorganism. You do not know how that system monitors itself. You do not know if the removal of material — even the tiny amount a human would eat — is registered and responded to. You are a foreign body in a living system. Do not give that system a chemical reason to notice you.

**Carry all food.** Calorie-dense, lightweight, requiring no preparation. You cannot make fire here (what would you burn? everything is either stone or part of the living system, and the air may not support combustion well at this altitude anyway). Cold-consumable rations. Energy-dense bars, nuts, dried proteins, glucose gels. Budget generously — the exertion of pushing through the tower forest in thin cold air will be immense. You may need 5,000-6,000 calories per day, possibly more. This is one of the primary constraints on your traverse duration. You can carry only so much food. Plan accordingly.

**Water.** The condensation cycle provides water, technically. Moisture beads on cold surfaces, freezes, melts, trickles in cracks. You could collect it. But see the warning above about not consuming anything from the Plateau's systems. The condensation rises from deep vents and has passed through the entire biological layer — it has been in contact with moss, mites, grub secretions, mineral films. It is not pure water. It is a solution of whatever the Plateau's biology puts into it. If you must use environmental water (and on a long traverse, you may have to — carrying weeks of water is physically impossible), you need serious purification. Not just filtration, not just UV treatment — both of those, plus chemical treatment, plus distillation if you can manage it. A portable distillation setup that runs on body heat or chemical heat packs would be ideal. Collect condensation from surfaces, distill it, test it if you have the capability. Accept that there is residual risk. This is the most dangerous logistical challenge of the traverse: you cannot carry enough water, and the available water may not be safe at any level of treatment.

Consider also: **a closed-loop hydration system.** Some advanced multiverse travelers carry urine recycling systems adapted from spacecraft technology. Your own body's waste water is at least biochemically familiar. It is not pleasant. It may be necessary.

### Navigation

There are no landmarks. The terrain is uniform. The sky is featureless white. There is no sun to track, no stars to navigate by, no magnetic field has been confirmed. You cannot navigate by sight.

**Inertial navigation.** A high-quality inertial measurement unit that tracks your movement through accelerometers and gyroscopes, independent of any external reference. This is your primary navigation tool. Calibrate it before entry. Check it frequently. Carry a backup.

**Distance marking.** Carry a supply of small, inert, non-biological markers — metal tags, ceramic beads — and drop them at regular intervals. If you need to retrace your steps, they are your breadcrumb trail. Make them distinctive enough that you can find them in the moss layer. Make them heavy enough that the moss-mite layer does not bury them too quickly.

**Mapping.** A simple compass-and-stride-count system as backup to your inertial unit. Count your steps. Note your direction. Record everything. If all electronic systems fail (and on some multiverse destinations, they do), dead reckoning by pace count is ancient and reliable.

**Know your entry and exit points.** Before you arrive, have a clear plan for how you are leaving. If your transit method deposits you at a fixed point, you must be able to return to that point. If it can retrieve you from anywhere on the Plateau, that changes your calculus enormously — it means you can traverse freely without the constraint of return navigation. Confirm your extraction method before you commit to the traverse. Getting lost on the Plateau is potentially a death sentence. There is no high ground to climb for perspective (it is already high ground, all of it). There is no horizon to orient toward (the white sky has no features). There is no one to find you.

### Tools for Movement

**A machete or heavy cutting tool.** You will need to cut through web fibers and break tower stalks to make passage in the densest areas. The tower material is harder than basalt — a standard steel blade may dull quickly. Consider carbide-edged or ceramic tools. Carry sharpening equipment.

But use this tool *judiciously*. Every cut, every broken tower, every severed web fiber is damage to the system. The web-tenders patrol and repair. If you leave a trail of damage, web-tenders will converge on your path. These are room-sized organisms with multiple limbs and they have purposes you do not fully understand. They may ignore you as irrelevant foreign matter. They may attempt to repair you — to incorporate you into the web, to seal you in fiber, to treat you as damage that needs structural correction. We do not know. Minimize your impact. Go around when you can. Go through only when you must.

**Climbing gear.** The basalt ridges are sharp and steep. The tower lattices can be climbed but require trust in alien structural engineering — is that branch load-bearing? It looks load-bearing. It was built to channel moisture and provide acoustic resonance. Will it hold a human's weight? Maybe. Maybe not. Light climbing gear — a harness, some cord, a few carabiners — gives you options. Do not use pitons on the tower structures (you would be hammering into an inhabited building, essentially). Use friction and the natural architecture.

### Protection and Safety

**Physical barriers against the biology.** The Plateau's organisms will attempt to colonize you. Not maliciously — they colonize everything. You are a surface. You are a substrate. The moss-mite layer will attempt to establish on your boots, your clothing, the underside of your pack where it rests on the ground. The mossy black-green layer will begin forming on any surface that stays in contact with the Plateau for more than a few hours. The mites will explore you. They are rice-grain-sized or smaller, and they are everywhere, and they will find the gaps in your clothing and they will investigate.

Seal your clothing as completely as you can. Gaiter your boots. Tuck your pants into your socks, your shirt into your pants. Wear gloves continuously. Check yourself at every rest stop — look for moss establishment on your gear, mite intrusion into your clothing layers, any sign that the biology is attempting to integrate your equipment into the local system. Brush off, scrape off, remove. Do this consistently and without fail. The organisms are patient. You must be more patient.

**A full-face shield or goggles.** The white light is intense. There is no shade except in the crevices between ridges, and those are filled with ice crystals and biology. The obsidian faces throw light sideways in blinding sheets. Reflected light from the moss's iridescent surface adds to the load. UV protection is essential — at this altitude, with this much reflective surface, UV exposure would be severe even under a normal sun. Under this sourceless white sky, the UV profile is unknown but should be assumed dangerous. Full UV-blocking eye protection, worn at all times. Snow blindness — or its equivalent here — would be disabling.

**Skin protection.** Same UV concern applies. Cover all skin. Use physical sunblock (zinc oxide or equivalent) on any exposed areas, particularly around the edges of goggles and masks where clothing gaps exist.

**First aid.** Oriented toward the specific risks. Altitude sickness medications. Treatments for respiratory irritation (the particulate issue). Wound care supplies, especially for abrasions — you will be scraped and scratched constantly by the environment. Infection risk from alien biology is unknown; carry broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals but understand they may be useless against organisms from a different evolutionary context. Clean and cover any broken skin immediately. You are surrounded by biology that colonizes every surface. An open wound is a surface.

**Hearing protection.** The constant drone. The subsonic vibration. The web-pulses that arrive as physical shudders. Prolonged exposure to low-frequency sound at high intensity can cause nausea, disorientation, organ damage. High-quality earplugs reduce the audible component but do nothing for the subsonic — that enters through your body, not your ears. There is no complete protection against it. Limit your exposure if symptoms develop (disorientation, nausea, a sense of your heartbeat becoming irregular or synchronizing with external rhythms — *especially* the last one, which is a sign that the Plateau's vibrational environment is beginning to entrain your autonomic nervous system. Break the entrainment immediately. Move to a different area. Change your activity. Sing, talk aloud, create your own sound. Do not let the Plateau's rhythm become your rhythm.)

## Behavioral Guidelines on the Plateau

### Movement

Move slowly. Not just because the terrain demands it (it does), but because rapid movement is anomalous here. Everything on the Plateau moves at geological pace. The grazers cover meters per day. The web-tenders climb with arachnid deliberation. The mites ripple slowly. A fast-moving object is *foreign* in a way that a slow-moving object is not. You want to be as un-foreign as possible. You want to be boring. You want the system to register you as an inert object being slowly pushed through by ambient processes, not as an active agent disrupting the network.

### Interaction with Fauna

**The mites:** Unavoidable contact. They will be on you and around you at all times. Ignore them as much as possible. Brush them off when they accumulate. Do not crush them if you can avoid it — dead organisms release chemical signals, and you do not know what signal a crushed mite sends to the network.

**The grub-colonies:** Do not disturb the towers more than necessary. If you must break a tower to pass, break it near the base where the wall is thickest and the interior colony may be smaller or absent. Do not reach into the hollow cores. The grubs are soft-bodied and unlikely to harm you, but their chemical secretions — the mineral-processing compounds they use to convert basalt into building material — may be caustic.

**The web-tenders:** Give them space. When you see one — and you will, they are room-sized and climb the lattices above you — stop moving. Let it pass or let it work. Do not touch the web fibers in its immediate vicinity. Do not cut web fibers while a web-tender is within visual range. Observe how it responds to your presence. If it ignores you, proceed cautiously. If it orients toward you, freezes, or begins moving in your direction, retreat. It may be investigating you as damage. It may be investigating you as a structural element that needs to be connected to the network. Neither investigation ends well for you.

**The grazers:** These are the safest large fauna, in the sense that they are slow, they are not predatory (nothing here is predatory in any recognizable sense — there is no predation, only processing), and they are so large that you are beneath their notice. But do not get in front of one. They do not stop. They do not steer around obstacles. They process forward through the tower forest at their fixed pace, and their mass is that of a large vehicle. If you are in the path, you will be walked over, pushed down, and potentially churned through the moss-mite layer under their ventral processing surfaces. Give them a very wide berth. Listen for them — their movement produces a distinctive low creaking as they push through tower structures, and their calls through the web network can help you triangulate their positions.

**The web-tenders' calls:** When you feel a web-pulse arrive — a shudder in the ground or in nearby structures — note it. Multiple pulses from the same direction may indicate a web-tender working nearby. Pulses from multiple directions converging on your location may indicate that your presence has been noted and multiple web-tenders are responding. This is the Plateau's immune response, if it has one. If you notice convergent web activity, move. Change your location significantly. Create distance.

### Rest and Sleep

You will need to sleep on the Plateau, and this is one of the most psychologically challenging aspects of the visit. There is no safe space. There is no enclosed shelter. You will lie down in your bivy sack on the living, rippling, humming, cold moss-mite layer, surrounded on all sides by the tower forest, under the unchanging white sky, and you will try to sleep while the entire world vibrates and whispers and processes around you.

Practical advice: Find a spot between tower structures where the moss layer is thick and relatively flat. A shallow basalt basin, moss-lined, is ideal — the basin walls give you a slight sense of enclosure and may muffle the drone fractionally. Lay down your pad (thick, closed-cell foam). Lay down your bivy. Get in, seal it. Wear your earplugs. Wear your eye mask (the light never fully dims; what passes for night is merely a *slightly-less-bright* period). Accept the vibration. Accept the cold. Accept the mites, which will explore the outside of your bivy with the mindless patience of organisms that have colonized an entire continent of bare stone. They will not get through the bivy fabric if it is intact. Check it for tears before each sleep.

Set a maximum sleep duration and alarm yourself awake. Extended immobility on the Plateau surface gives the biology more time to begin colonization of your gear. More than six or seven hours in one position and you may find moss beginning to establish on the underside of your bivy. Eight hours and the mites may have built understructure incorporating your pad's edges. Do not sleep so long that you become part of the landscape.

### Duration of Visit

Be honest with yourself about your limits. A day-visit to the Plateau's edge — stepping in, experiencing the density, the sound, the cold, the light, and stepping back out — is a profound experience and for most travelers, sufficient. A multi-day traverse into the interior is an expedition of the most serious kind, comparable to polar travel or deep cave exploration, with the added dimension of alien biology. A full crossing of the Plateau has, to this writer's knowledge, never been documented. It may not be possible for a single human on foot, carrying supplies. The distances are too great, the pace too slow, the caloric demands too high.

Recommended: **Three to seven days, penetrating no more than ten to fifteen kilometers from your entry point.** This gives you deep immersion in the system without committing you to logistical extremes. You will see the full range of fauna. You will hear the calls propagating. You will feel the subsonic grinding in your bones. You will watch the slow crystalline growth of ice in a shaded crevice. You will experience the perceptual shift that comes after the third or fourth day, when the boundary between you and the environment begins to feel negotiable, when the drone starts to feel like it has always been there, when the ripple of the moss-mite layer begins to look like breathing and you begin to feel that you are inside something alive. That shift is the experience. That shift is what you came for. Let it happen. Then leave. Leave before the shift becomes permanent. Leave before you stop wanting to leave.

## What to Bring Home (and What Not To)

**Do not bring biological samples.** I understand the temptation. The organisms here are extraordinary. The mineral-processing biochemistry alone would revolutionize materials science. The web-fiber material has structural properties that are clearly remarkable. The moss-mite symbiosis (or identity) is scientifically fascinating. Do not take samples.

First: you do not know what you are taking. A piece of moss is also a colony of mites, is also a chemical film of mineral exudate, is also a node in a network that extends for thousands of kilometers. You cannot isolate a piece. You can only sever a piece, and the severed piece may behave unpredictably outside its context — it may die, it may attempt to colonize, it may emit chemical distress signals that affect unrelated biology in other multiverse locations.

Second: the Plateau is a superorganism. Taking a piece of it is not like taking a rock from a mountain. It is like taking a piece of a living body. The ethical dimensions of this are not trivial.

**Bring recordings.** Audio recordings of the drone layers are profoundly moving and scientifically valuable. Capture the full frequency range if your equipment allows, including subsonic — the web-pulse patterns may contain information structure that is not apparent in real-time but becomes visible in spectral analysis.

**Bring photographs and video.** The visual environment is extraordinary and your memory will struggle to retain it accurately. The uniformity of the landscape means your brain will compress the experience — "it was all the same" — when in fact the subtle variations are immense and important. Photograph the ice crystals. Photograph the web-membranes. Photograph the grub-colony towers at high magnification, where the crystalline layering is visible. Photograph a web-tender if you can do so from a safe distance.

**Bring your journal.** Write in it every day. Record not just what you see but how you feel. Record the moments when your sense of self wavers. Record the moments when the drone changes character — when it thickens, when it thins, when a web-pulse passes through and the whole acoustic environment shifts for a moment before settling back. These subjective records are valuable. The Plateau may be conscious in some diffuse way. Your consciousness interacting with it — one discrete mind touching an oceanic one — is the experiment. Record the results.

## Final Note: On Coming Back

Travelers returning from the Plateau report a consistent re-entry experience. The first thing you will notice is silence. Even in a noisy transit point — even in a crowded market, a busy station — the acoustic environment will feel *empty* compared to the Plateau's all-encompassing drone. You will feel a phantom vibration in your bones for days, sometimes weeks. Your body became accustomed to the subsonic field and will continue to expect it.

The second thing you will notice is *space*. The gaps between objects. The air between buildings. The emptiness in a room. After the Plateau's absolute density, any ordinary environment will feel shockingly, almost painfully sparse. You may feel exposed. You may feel lonely in a way that has nothing to do with other people. You may find yourself pressing against walls, standing in corners, seeking the comfort of surfaces on all sides.

The third thing — and this fades last — is the sense that you left something behind. Not an object. Not a piece of yourself in the poetic sense. A connection. While you were on the Plateau, you were — despite your separateness, despite your sealed clothing and your respirator and your carefully maintained boundaries — *in contact with the network*. The web-pulses passed through you. The mites explored your outline. The drone shaped your cardiac rhythm. You were not integrated, but you were *touched*. You were known, dimly, by something vast. And now you are not.

This passes. It takes time, but it passes. Talk to other travelers who have been. They understand. They all felt it. The Plateau remembers everyone who visits, in the way that an ocean remembers every ship that crosses it — which is to say, not at all, and completely.

Go well. Dress warmly. Breathe carefully. Move slowly. And when the moss starts to look like it is breathing, when the drone starts to feel like a heartbeat, when you catch yourself standing still for too long, staring at the slow, slow growth of an ice crystal in a shaded crevice, feeling that you could stand here forever, feeling that forever would be fine — 

That is when you leave.
