# Preparing for the Calving Plateau

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are visiting a place that is actively manufacturing itself. This is not a ruin, not a wonder preserved in amber, not a landscape that finished becoming what it is. The ground beneath your feet will be, in the most literal geological sense, still setting. The plateau is a factory floor where the product is terrain, and you are an uninvited visitor to the production line. Your preparation must account for the fact that the environment is not hostile in the way a desert or a glacier is hostile—it is *indifferent* in a way that is deeper and more total than hostility. Nothing here was shaped by or for anything with intentions. You are visiting a process, not a place.

This means your preparation has two layers: the practical (how to survive the physical conditions) and the psychological (how to remain functional and oriented in an environment that offers almost no landmarks, no shelter, no familiar reference points, and a sensory palette so muted and strange that it can produce genuine disorientation even in experienced travelers).

---

## Multiversal Navigation Considerations

The Calving Plateau sits at a Tech 2.5 — Industrial designation, but this is a descriptor of the *geological process*, not of any civilization present. There is no civilization present. There is no infrastructure, no settlement, no communication network, no rescue service, no trail, no marker. You should treat this as a wilderness expedition into terrain that has never been mapped because it is still being created. Any maps you obtain from prior travelers will be approximations at best and actively misleading at worst — new flows may have altered the surface topography since the last survey, pools may have appeared or vanished, crack networks may have shifted. Calibrate your arrival point carefully. The plateau is vast, and arriving at the wrong coordinates means potentially days of walking across featureless basalt to reach your intended observation area or extraction point.

If your multiverse transit method involves manifesting at a fixed altitude relative to local terrain, confirm that the plateau surface hasn't risen since your reference data was collected. Even a few meters of new basalt could mean the difference between arriving on the surface and arriving partially embedded in fresh rock. If your transit method involves emerging at a fixed altitude above sea level, account for the fact that the plateau is nearly three kilometers up and growing. Arriving at the altitude the surface *was* at during your last briefing might put you several meters in the air above hard, unforgiving basalt.

Plan your extraction point and time with care. The mist is persistent and can thicken unpredictably. If your transit method requires visual line-of-sight to a beacon, landmark, or specific coordinate marker, you may find yourself unable to transit during periods of heavy condensation. Build buffer time into your visit. Assume you may be stuck for hours — possibly a full day cycle — waiting for conditions clear enough to navigate or extract.

## Physical Conditioning and Altitude

Three kilometers of elevation. Thin air. This is non-negotiable: you need altitude acclimatization or supplemental oxygen, and ideally both.

If you have time before your visit, spend at least a week at progressively higher elevations in whatever staging universe you're working from. Get your body producing additional red blood cells. Get your lungs accustomed to working harder for less return. If you don't have time for natural acclimatization, carry supplemental oxygen — not a full mountaineering rig, but a lightweight system with enough capacity for your planned duration plus that buffer period I mentioned. Acetazolamide (Diamox) or its multiversal equivalent is worth considering as a prophylactic, though it will increase urination, which is a logistical nuisance in an environment with no privacy features whatsoever.

The altitude will affect you in ways beyond breathlessness. Your cognitive function will be slightly impaired. Your decision-making will be slower. Your emotional regulation may be compromised. In an environment as psychologically unusual as this one, that matters. You will already be dealing with disorientation from the featureless terrain, the flat light, the strange soundscape. Altitude-induced mild confusion layered on top of that is a recipe for poor choices. Know your symptoms. If you start feeling euphoric or unusually calm about things that should concern you, that's the altitude talking, not spiritual attunement to the landscape.

Physical fitness should be oriented toward endurance, not power. You won't be climbing anything. The plateau is essentially flat. But you will be walking for extended periods across uneven basalt, which is far more taxing than walking on a trail. Fresh basalt has a texture that ranges from smooth and glassy (slippery when wet, and everything here is wet) to rough and vesicular (excellent grip but punishing on footwear and ankles). You will be stepping over cracks, navigating around pools, and maintaining your balance on surfaces with irregular camber. Think of it as a very long, very cold, very high hike across a broken parking lot that's been sprayed with a garden hose.

## Clothing and Personal Protection

**Layering is everything.** The temperature on the plateau will be cold — bitterly so in the ambient air — but the volcanic ground heat creates a thermal gradient that makes conditions variable in strange ways. Standing still, you'll feel cold from the waist up and oddly warm from the feet down. Walking generates enough body heat to make you sweat inside waterproof layers, but stopping for even a few minutes in the wind will chill you dangerously fast. You need a system that lets you modulate quickly.

**Base layer:** Merino wool or high-quality synthetic wicking fabric. Not cotton. Nothing cotton. The persistent moisture in the air means cotton will become damp, stay damp, and leach heat from you with patient, lethal efficiency. Merino has the advantage of retaining warmth when wet and resisting the kind of clammy stink that develops when you're wearing the same base layer for multiple days in a humid environment.

**Mid layer:** Insulating fleece or light down. Down is lighter and more compressible but vulnerable to moisture — and moisture is the defining feature of this environment. Synthetic insulation is heavier but retains loft when damp. Given the conditions, I'd lean synthetic, or down treated with a hydrophobic coating. You may want two mid layers of different weights so you can fine-tune your insulation.

**Outer layer:** A fully waterproof and windproof shell, both jacket and pants. Not water-resistant. Waterproof. The condensation on the plateau is not rain — it won't fall on you in drops — but it will settle on every surface of your clothing in a fine film that gradually saturates anything not sealed against it. A good Gore-Tex or equivalent membrane with fully taped seams. Pit zips for ventilation. A hood that can cinch around your face and still allow peripheral vision — you'll want to see the pikas scattering, and you'll want to notice the stillness patterns that indicate a mustelid hunting nearby, not because the mustelids are dangerous to you but because reading the plateau's behavioral signals is part of the experience.

**Gloves:** Bring at least two pairs. A thin liner glove for dexterity when you need to operate equipment or handle objects, and an insulated waterproof outer glove for general wear. Your hands will be the first casualty of the cold damp air. If you plan to touch the rock — and you will want to, the texture is extraordinary, glassy and alien — a bare hand on wet basalt at this altitude will go numb in minutes.

**Headwear:** A warm hat that fits under your hood. A balaclava or buff for when the wind picks up and the mist drives into your face. The air is thin enough that your ears and nose will lose heat disproportionately fast.

**Footwear:** This is perhaps the single most critical equipment decision. You need boots that are:
- Waterproof (you will be stepping in and through those shallow pools constantly — avoiding them all is impossible)
- Stiff-soled (fresh basalt can have edges sharp enough to cut through flexible soles, and the vesicular texture will destroy soft rubber rapidly)
- Ankle-supporting (the uneven surface and the cracks between flow units create constant ankle-roll hazards)
- Warm (the rock's volcanic heat is subtle and intermittent — most of the time, you're standing on cold wet stone)
- Well-fitted (blisters on the Calving Plateau, with no shelter and potentially days from extraction, could become a survival issue)

Heavy-duty mountaineering boots or approach shoes with rigid midsoles are ideal. Break them in thoroughly before you go. Bring gaiters — low gaiters at minimum — to keep water from wicking down into your boots from above. Bring extra socks. Merino wool, again. At least three pairs. Rotate them, dry them against your body if necessary. Wet feet in these conditions will become cold feet, then painful feet, then a medical problem.

## Shelter and Camping

There is no natural shelter on the Calving Plateau. None. No trees, no boulders, no overhangs, no caves you can safely enter. The lava tube network that honeycombs the subsurface is scaled for pikas, not people, and even if you found an opening large enough to crawl into, you'd be entering an unmapped volcanic void system of unknown stability with potential thermal and gas hazards. Don't.

You will need to carry your own shelter. A four-season mountaineering tent is the baseline — something rated for high winds, cold temperatures, and persistent moisture. Free-standing is strongly preferable; the basalt surface won't accept stakes easily, and drilling or hammering into potentially gas-permeable volcanic rock is inadvisable. A tent with a vestibule large enough to store wet gear outside the sleeping area will make your life significantly more bearable. Consider a footprint or ground cloth rated for abrasion — fresh basalt is rough enough to damage tent floors, and a punctured floor on the wet plateau means a wet sleeping bag means a genuinely dangerous night.

Your sleeping system should be rated colder than you think you'll need. The ground will steal heat aggressively — rock is a superb conductor. A high-R-value sleeping pad (R5 or above) is essential. Two pads stacked is better. Your sleeping bag should be synthetic-fill for the same moisture reasons as your mid layers, or if you insist on down for its weight-to-warmth ratio, enclose it in a waterproof bivy sack and guard it obsessively against moisture.

Choose your campsite with care. Not in a depression — water collects there. Not at the edge of the plateau — the edges are described as sharp as broken pottery, and in limited visibility you could walk off one. Not near a visible thermal vent or heat source — volcanic gases (CO₂, SO₂, H₂S) may accumulate at ground level near vents, and in a sealed tent this could become lethal. Find a slightly elevated section of flat basalt, as far from visible venting as practical, and set up there. Orient your tent opening away from the prevailing wind direction if you can determine one — on the plateau, wind patterns may shift with the thermal cycling of the volcanic heat, so this may be a guess.

## Water and Food

**Water** is abundant but must be treated with caution. The standing pools on the plateau are everywhere, but they are not clean drinking water. They contain:
- Algal biofilms and associated microbial communities
- Midge larvae and their biological waste products
- Dissolved volcanic minerals leached from the fresh basalt, potentially including elevated levels of silica, fluoride, sulfur compounds, and heavy metals
- Whatever the pikas, salamanders, and mustelids have contributed

Filter and purify. A high-quality pump filter with a ceramic or hollow-fiber element rated to 0.2 microns will handle the biological contaminants. For dissolved minerals, you'd ideally want activated carbon filtration as well, though this adds weight. Chemical purification (iodine, chlorine dioxide) will handle pathogens but not mineral contamination. Boiling works for biological safety but concentrates dissolved minerals.

The pragmatic approach: carry enough clean water from your staging point for the first day. Use filtered plateau water thereafter, accepting some mineral exposure for short visits. If you're staying more than three or four days, the cumulative mineral load from the water could become a health concern, and you should bring a more comprehensive purification system or carry more of your own supply.

**Food** should be calorie-dense and require minimal preparation. The altitude and cold will increase your caloric needs substantially — perhaps 4,000 to 5,000 calories per day if you're active. Carrying a stove is worthwhile for morale (hot food and drink in that damp cold is a genuine psychological lifeline) but adds weight and fuel requirements. A lightweight canister stove will struggle slightly in the thin air — bring a windscreen and expect longer boil times. Be aware that an open flame on the plateau could theoretically ignite volcanic gases in certain concentrations near vents — cook only in well-ventilated areas away from any thermal features.

Do not eat the local organisms. The pikas are everywhere and might seem like an easy protein source, but you don't know their parasitology, their mineral bioaccumulation profile, or how their biology might differ from similar organisms in your home universe. The same goes for the salamanders, the midges, the algae, and the mosses. Look, don't consume.

## Navigation

This is where the Calving Plateau will truly test you.

The landscape is essentially featureless. Flat dark rock, scattered pools, mist. No landmarks. No elevated features. No vegetation patterns distinctive enough to serve as waypoints. The light is directionless — you cannot use shadows to determine direction because there are effectively no shadows. The mist limits visibility to perhaps eight or ten kilometers on a good day, less when it thickens. Everything looks the same in every direction.

**GPS or equivalent positioning:** If your transit method provides you with a positioning system that works in this universe, carry it and guard it with your life. Keep spare batteries. Keep a backup unit. Keep the coordinates of your extraction point written on a physical card in your pocket in case all electronics fail.

**Compass:** Volcanic basalt is often highly magnetic due to its iron mineral content. A magnetic compass may behave erratically on the plateau, deflected by local magnetic anomalies in the rock. Test your compass at your arrival point — if it seems reliable, use it, but don't trust it absolutely. If it's spinning or sticking, it's worse than useless because it will give you confident wrong answers.

**Dead reckoning:** If technology and magnetism both fail you, dead reckoning — counting paces and maintaining direction by picking a distant reference point and walking toward it — is your last resort. On the plateau, your "distant reference point" will be a vague shape in the mist, possibly a slightly different density of cloud, possibly nothing at all. This is extremely difficult and error-prone terrain for dead reckoning.

**Marking your path:** Carry something to mark your route. Small cairns of stacked basalt fragments at regular intervals. Bright-colored biodegradable flagging tape weighted with stones. Anything that will let you retrace your steps. The ethical question of whether to leave marks on pristine geological surface is real, but less pressing than the practical question of whether you can find your way back to your tent.

**The edges:** The plateau has edges. They are sharp and they drop away steeply. In limited visibility, you may not see an edge until you're very close to it. If the mist is thick, do not walk toward any direction in which you cannot see at least fifty meters ahead. When exploring, approach any apparent change in the horizon — any place where the flat rock seems to end — with extreme caution. Get low. Move slowly. The basalt at the edges may be fractured or undercut, and a step onto an unsupported ledge at three kilometers of altitude above a valley floor "that doesn't quite exist yet" is a step you don't come back from.

## Volcanic and Geological Hazards

You are standing on an active volcanic system. The plateau description emphasizes the slow, industrial regularity of the process, and this may lull you into thinking it's safe. It is not safe. It is merely predictable over geological timescales, which is a very different thing from being predictable over the timescale of your visit.

**Ground tremors:** The faint tectonic restlessness described is background. It is the normal state of the plateau. You will get used to it. That is when it becomes dangerous, because you may fail to notice a change in the tremor pattern that indicates something more significant — a new dike propagating toward the surface, a subsurface collapse, a change in magma pressure. You have no seismic instruments, no warning system, no geological survey telling you what's normal and what isn't. The best you can do is pay attention. If the tremor intensifies, changes rhythm, or is accompanied by new sounds (cracking, rumbling, hissing), move. Move to what you hope is a safer area, which in practice means move toward the center of the plateau away from edges, away from any visible thermal features, and away from any area where the ground feels warmer than baseline.

**Volcanic gases:** CO₂, SO₂, and H₂S are all common emissions from basaltic volcanic systems. CO₂ is heavier than air and can accumulate in low spots — those depressions where the pools form. SO₂ smells sharp and irritates the lungs. H₂S smells of rotten eggs at low concentrations and becomes odorless at dangerous concentrations because it paralyzes the olfactory nerve. If you smell sulfur, note it. If you stop smelling sulfur in an area where you previously smelled it, leave immediately — the concentration may have increased to the point of olfactory fatigue, which means it's approaching lethal levels.

A portable gas detector rated for volcanic environments is worth its weight. Small, battery-powered units that measure CO₂, SO₂, and H₂S concentrations exist and are used by volcanologists. Carry one if you can obtain one.

**New flows:** The description indicates that magma is being delivered to the surface continuously. New basalt flows could theoretically emerge during your visit. The probability on any given day is likely low — the description suggests the process operates on timescales of decades to centuries per flow unit — but "likely low" is not "zero." A new flow would probably be preceded by increased tremor activity, ground cracking, and the smell of hot rock and gases. If you see red glow through a crack in the basalt, or feel a sudden dramatic increase in ground temperature, you are in the immediate vicinity of an active eruption. Move perpendicular to the crack or heat source. Do not attempt to observe. This is not a tourist-friendly lava flow in Hawaii with park rangers and rope barriers. This is unmonitored, unmapped volcanism on a remote plateau with no rescue services.

**Lava tube collapse:** The subsurface is honeycombed with voids — the pika burrows exploit them, but they were created by flowing lava. Some of these voids may be large enough to represent a structural weakness in the plateau surface. If the ground sounds hollow when you step on it, or if you notice a section that feels subtly different — bouncier, thinner, resonant — avoid it. A surface collapse into a lava tube at this scale could be anything from a twisted ankle to a fatal fall into a subsurface void.

## Wildlife Interaction

**The pikas:** They are absurdly abundant, they will surround you at all times, and they are not dangerous. They are, however, a critical information source. Learn to read their behavior. When they scatter ahead of you, that's normal — you're a large moving object. When they freeze, something else has their attention. When a section goes completely still and silent, a predator is active. The pikas' behavioral patterns are your best real-time ecological awareness system on the plateau.

Do not feed them. Do not attempt to handle them. They are wild animals adapted to an extreme environment, and any organism living in a novel volcanic ecosystem may carry pathogens or parasites your immune system has never encountered. Their bites, if provoked, could introduce unknown infections.

**The mustelids:** These are predators. They are described as small-bodied — long, low, weasel-like — and they almost certainly pose no direct threat to an adult human. But they are predators, and they may investigate your camp, your food stores, and your gear. Store food in sealed hard containers (mustelids are notoriously persistent and dexterous). If one enters your tent, give it a clear exit path and let it leave. Do not corner it. A mustelid in a confined space that feels trapped will bite, and the bites of small predators are disproportionately prone to infection.

**The birds:** The rivers of flight above you are spectacular and almost entirely non-interactive. The birds are transiting. They are not nesting on the plateau (there's nothing to nest in or on), they are not feeding on the plateau (their food sources are elsewhere), they are riding thermals. You are irrelevant to them. The raptors may occasionally take interest in movement on the ground — particularly if you're near a pika aggregation — but a raptor stooping on a pika near your feet is startling, not dangerous. The large pale gliders at high altitude are unknown and unknowable from your vantage. Do not spend too long staring up at them in the mist; you'll lose your balance and your spatial orientation.

**The salamanders:** Under the rocks at pool margins. You'll find them if you look. Handle them minimally — amphibian skin is permeable and sensitive to oils and chemicals on human hands. Observe, appreciate, replace the rock exactly as you found it. These organisms are clinging to existence in a barely habitable niche; displacing their shelter could kill them.

**The midges:** They will be in your face, in your ears, in your nostrils if you breathe carelessly. They are not biting midges (the description doesn't indicate feeding on vertebrate blood), but they are persistent and maddening. A headnet — the fine-mesh kind used in midge-heavy environments like the Scottish Highlands — is a small, light item that will massively improve your quality of life. Bring one.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where I need to be most honest with you.

The Calving Plateau is not a place that assaults you. It does something more disquieting: it ignores you. The sensory environment is profoundly unusual — the flat grey light with no source, the featureless expanse, the muted soundscape dominated by the aggregate wingbeat-breathing and the subliminal midge whine, the ceaseless micro-tremor underfoot, the wet gleaming rock in every direction. These conditions, sustained over hours, produce a state that experienced travelers in extreme environments sometimes call *the drift* — a gradual dissolution of your normal orienting frameworks.

You may lose track of time. The sourceless light provides no diurnal cues. The sky doesn't brighten or darken in ways you can easily parse. Carry a watch and check it regularly. Set alarms for meals, for hydration, for navigation checks, for sleep.

You may lose track of distance. On the featureless plateau, with no scale references, distances become ambiguous. A pool that looks fifty meters away may be two hundred. A slight rise in the rock that looks close may take an hour to reach. Trust your pace count, not your eyes.

You may experience a profound sense of smallness that tips over into something more troubling — a feeling of irrelevance so total that it becomes existentially uncomfortable. The plateau is an engine. It is building a mountain. The birds flow through the sky by the tens of thousands. The pikas number in the millions. The salamanders pulse their tiny throats in the dark. The rock grows. None of it requires you. None of it notices you. You are not unwelcome — you are simply unregistered. For some travelers, this is freeing. For others, it triggers a deep, almost primal anxiety — a need to be *acknowledged* by the landscape, to matter to it, that the landscape cannot and will not satisfy.

If you are prone to existential unease, to loneliness in vast spaces, to depersonalization under sensory monotony, prepare yourself. Bring anchoring objects — a journal, a book, photographs of people who matter to you, a familiar piece of music on a playback device with headphones. Something that connects you to a world where you are known and relevant. Use these when the drift sets in.

If you are the opposite type — if vast indifferent spaces fill you with calm, if you find peace in being small and unnoticed, if the idea of standing inside a geological engine while life flows around you in intricate unconscious patterns sounds like exactly what you need — then the Calving Plateau may be one of the most profound experiences available to you in the multiverse. Just don't let the serenity make you careless. The plateau doesn't care about you, and that includes not caring whether you fall off an edge or step on a thin spot or breathe volcanic gas in your sleep.

## Equipment Checklist (Summary)

**Worn/Carried at All Times:**
- Waterproof shell jacket and pants
- Insulating mid layers (adjustable)
- Merino base layers
- Stiff-soled waterproof boots, broken in
- Gaiters
- Warm hat and balaclava/buff
- Waterproof insulated gloves (and liner gloves)
- Headnet for midges
- Watch with alarm function
- Positioning device (GPS or equivalent) with spare batteries
- Compass (with awareness of potential magnetic interference)
- Route-marking materials (flagging tape, small stones for cairns)
- Portable gas detector (CO₂, SO₂, H₂S)
- Supplemental oxygen (lightweight system)
- Emergency whistle (though there is no one to hear it — old habits)
- Small first aid kit emphasizing blister care, wound cleaning, altitude medication
- Journal and pencil (pencils work in wet/cold; pens may not)

**In Pack:**
- Four-season freestanding tent with abrasion-resistant footprint
- Sleeping bag (synthetic fill, rated to well below expected temperatures)
- Sleeping pad(s), R5+ value
- Water filtration system (hollow fiber + carbon if possible)
- Water bottles or bladder (insulated to prevent freezing in extreme cold)
- Stove, fuel, windscreen, lightweight cook pot
- Calorie-dense food for planned duration + 2-day buffer
- Extra socks (3+ pairs merino)
- Dry bag for sleeping bag and critical dry items
- Backup lighting (headlamp + spare batteries — the directionless light may become truly dark at times, or the mist may thicken to near-zero visibility)
- Binoculars (for the birds, especially those high pale gliders — you will want to try to resolve them, even if you likely can't)
- Camera if desired (note: the flat light makes photography extremely challenging — everything looks grey and dimensionless in photos; the plateau is better recorded in words and memory)

## Duration and Timing

I'd recommend a visit of two to four days for a first trip. Less than two days won't give you enough time to acclimatize to the altitude, get past the initial sensory strangeness, and begin to actually *perceive* the plateau — to hear the breathing of the bird-rivers, to read the pika-stillness, to feel the tremor as something other than discomfort. More than four days raises the cumulative risks: water mineral exposure, equipment degradation from persistent moisture, psychological fatigue from the drift, and the simple statistical accumulation of volcanic hazard exposure.

Timing relative to local seasons (if applicable — the description doesn't specify strong seasonality) may matter primarily for the bird transits. If the rivers of flight are migratory, there may be peak periods and lulls. Prior travelers' accounts, if available, should be consulted. The plateau in full bird-transit, with tens of thousands overhead in their silent rivers, is the experience. The plateau without them would be emptier, quieter, and significantly more psychologically demanding.

## A Final Note

When you arrive and take your first breath of that thin, damp, faintly mineral air, and you feel the tremor come up through your boot soles, and you see the dark wet rock stretching away in every direction under that pewter sky with its hundreds of grey light-holes where the pools reflect nothing, and you hear — or rather, you become gradually aware of — the vast quiet breathing of the birds above you and the barely-there whine of the midges and the enormous silence that contains them both:

Do nothing for a moment. Just stand there. Let the plateau not notice you. Let it go about its work. You are standing on a mountain that is being born, inside an engine that has no builder, surrounded by an ecosystem that assembled itself out of nothing on rock that barely exists yet. Every organism here — the moss in the cracks, the pikas at the pools, the white mustelids in the shadows, the salamanders beneath the stones, the rivers of birds riding invisible heat — every one of them arrived here, found the cracks in the newness, and made a life without permission or precedent.

You're doing the same thing by being here. You found a crack in the multiverse and you squeezed through it to stand on wet black rock at the top of a mountain that hasn't finished happening yet.

That's worth a moment of stillness before you start walking.
