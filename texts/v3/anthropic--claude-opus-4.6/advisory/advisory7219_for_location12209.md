# Preparing for the Weeping Tiers of Oshkaal

---

## Initial Assessment and Mindset

Before anything else, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation strategy hinges on a fundamental recognition: Oshkaal is not dangerous in the way a volcano is dangerous or a war zone is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that a place where the operating assumptions of physical reality are quietly, patiently being renegotiated is dangerous. Your primary threats are not predators, weather events, or hostile inhabitants. Your primary threats are perceptual destabilization, ontological exposure, the slow mineral chemistry of a mountain that is becoming something other than a mountain, and the possibility that prolonged presence in a location where physics is "thinning" could alter you in ways you won't notice until you've left — or can't leave.

This means your preparation must address two parallel tracks: the mundane mountaineering and survival challenges of an extreme-altitude, wet, lifeless stone environment, and the far more unusual challenge of maintaining coherent selfhood and reliable perception in a place where the boundary between "here" and "somewhere else" is actively eroding.

Take both equally seriously.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude and Atmosphere

The description places you far above normal cloud formation altitude. On Earth, that would put you somewhere above 6,000–8,000 meters, potentially higher. The air is described as "thin and cool" but not savagely cold and not immediately incapacitating, which suggests the atmosphere of this world may be denser or differently composed than Earth's — but you should not rely on that assumption holding stable across your entire visit, especially given the location's tendency to blur into other configurations of reality.

**Acclimatization:** If there is any way to stage your arrival — spend time at lower elevations on this world first, ascend gradually — do it. Your Earth-evolved physiology will struggle with reduced partial pressure of oxygen regardless of local atmospheric quirks. Spend a minimum of several days at intermediate elevations if the approach allows it. If you're arriving directly onto the Tiers (as multiverse transit sometimes demands), bring supplemental oxygen. Not as a primary breathing system — you'd run out — but as a rescue measure. A small, lightweight compressed-oxygen system with a regulator and mask, enough for 6–12 hours of intermittent use. Use it if you feel the onset of acute mountain sickness: headache, nausea, confusion, ataxia. The confusion part is critical here because *you need to be able to distinguish altitude-induced cognitive impairment from perception-warping effects of the location itself*, and having supplemental oxygen lets you run a crude diagnostic. If you hit your oxygen and the confusion clears, it was altitude. If it doesn't, it's the Tiers.

**Medication:** Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) and begin taking it 24–48 hours before arrival if possible. It accelerates acclimatization by promoting bicarbonate excretion and stimulating ventilation. Carry dexamethasone as an emergency treatment for high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). Carry nifedipine for high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE). Know the symptoms of both. Write them on a laminated card you keep in a chest pocket, because your judgment will be the first thing to degrade and you need external reference material for exactly the moments when you're least capable of remembering what you know.

### Thermal Management

The cold is described as "persistent" and stone-dwelling — the kind that conducts through contact rather than convecting through air. This is crucial. You can be hypothermic on the Tiers without ever feeling wind-chill or shivering in a blizzard. The stone will pull heat from you through your feet, your hands, your body if you sit or lie down. The omnipresent moisture accelerates this. Everything is wet. You will be wet.

**Clothing system:**

- **Base layer:** Synthetic or merino wool, absolutely not cotton. Cotton kills in wet-cold environments. A snug-fitting long-sleeve top and bottom that wicks moisture away from skin.
- **Mid layer:** Insulating fleece or light synthetic fill. Something that retains warmth when damp, because it will be damp. Merino wool is excellent here too.
- **Outer layer:** This is tricky. There's no rain, but there's omnipresent moisture — sheets of water across every surface, mist-fine curtains of weeping water, condensation, spray from crystal formations. You need water resistance but also breathability, because exertion at altitude will produce sweat and you cannot afford a moisture buildup inside your clothing system. A high-quality Gore-Tex or equivalent hardshell jacket and pants, fully seam-sealed. Pit zips for ventilation. A hood that cinches well.
- **Hands:** Waterproof gloves, ideally two pairs — a thinner pair for dexterity (you'll want to examine things, handle stone, possibly interact with the built structures) and a heavier insulated pair for rest periods. Bring a third pair as backup. Wet gloves at altitude are a fast track to frostbite or at minimum to miserable, clumsy uselessness.
- **Feet:** Waterproof boots with rigid soles. The stone surfaces are textured but persistently wet and potentially slick with mineral film. You want aggressive tread and ankle support. Gaiters to prevent water from running down into the boot tops. Two or three pairs of wool socks. Manage your feet religiously. Inspect them twice a day. Wet stone environments destroy feet.
- **Head:** A warm hat that fits under your jacket hood. The diffuse brightness means you won't need sun shading from a brimmed hat, but you will need cranial insulation — you lose enormous amounts of heat through your head, and at altitude with reduced oxygen, keeping your brain warm is keeping your brain functional.

**Insulated sleeping pad:** If you plan to sleep on the Tiers (and you'll need to if your visit extends beyond several hours — which it probably will, given the distances involved in traversing multiple terraces), bring the highest R-value sleeping pad you can carry. The stone will leach your body heat all night. A foam pad underneath an inflatable pad. Do not sleep directly on rock under any circumstances.

**Sleeping bag:** Rated to well below the temperatures you expect. Synthetic fill, not down — down loses its insulation when wet, and on the Tiers, everything gets wet eventually. A bivy sack or lightweight waterproof shell over the sleeping bag to manage the moisture.

### Hydration and Nutrition

Here is a genuinely strange preparation challenge: the Tiers are saturated with water, and the water is described as clear, cool, and faintly mineral. It tastes of dissolved chalk and something "alkaline and electric." You will be surrounded by drinkable-seeming liquid at all times. The question is whether you should drink it.

**My strong recommendation: do not drink the Tier water as your primary hydration source, especially not initially.**

The water is the active agent of a process that is slowly exceeding the boundaries of normal chemistry. It dissolves the mountain and reconstructs it into impossible geometries. It deposits minerals that cast shadows in the wrong direction. It carries, at some level, whatever force is causing the Tiers to "become somewhere else." Ingesting it is introducing that process into your body. Your body is, after all, a chemistry set. You are mineral. You are water. You are, from the perspective of whatever is happening on the Tiers, a mobile, warm, mineral-rich substrate through which water flows constantly.

Think about that.

Carry your own water. As much as you can. A minimum of 4 liters per day at extreme altitude with exertion, and you'll be exerting — the terraces are vast and staggered, with significant elevation changes between them. A hydration bladder in your pack plus hard-sided bottles. If your visit extends long enough that you must supplement with local water, filter it first through a high-quality mechanical filter (0.1 micron or better) AND a chemical purification step, not because you're worried about biological pathogens (there's no life here to produce them) but because you want to reduce the dissolved mineral load as much as possible. Activated carbon filtration would help with the mineral taste and might strip some of whatever else is dissolved in it. Bring electrolyte supplements to add to your carried water — at altitude you lose electrolytes rapidly through increased respiration and urination.

Even with precautions, if you use local water, pay attention to yourself. Note any changes in sensation, perception, or thought pattern that don't correlate with altitude effects. Keep a log. Write things down.

**Food:** High-calorie, compact, no-cook options. You have no fuel sources on the Tiers (no plant material, no combustible anything — it's bare stone), so either carry your own stove fuel or eat food that doesn't require heating. Caloric needs at extreme altitude are enormous — 4,000–6,000 calories per day. Carry more than you think you need. Nut butters, hard cheeses, dense energy bars, chocolate, dried meats, olive oil (you can add it to anything for caloric density). Your appetite will be suppressed by altitude; eat on a schedule regardless of hunger. Cognitive function depends on glucose. You need your cognition here more than most places.

### Navigation and Orientation

The terraces are described as vast and repeating at multiple scales — self-similar fractal drainage patterns, similar mineral compositions, similar crystal growths. This means the Tiers will be disorienting even before accounting for anomalous effects. One plateau may look very much like another. The omnidirectional light eliminates the sun as a directional reference. Shadows, where they exist, are unreliable — some point the wrong way entirely.

**Carry a compass.** Test it immediately upon arrival. If it works, use it. If it doesn't (and it may not — the mineral composition could be magnetically active, or the Tiers' anomalous properties might affect magnetic instruments), you need backup methods.

**Mark your path.** Not by building cairns — you don't want to interfere with the existing built structures or introduce confusion between your markers and the work of the previous builders. Use small, brightly colored flagging tape tied to stones at regular intervals. Something visibly artificial and retrievable. Collect it on your way out.

**Map as you go.** Carry a small notebook (waterproof paper — Rite in the Rain or equivalent, because everything is wet) and sketch the terraces as you traverse them. Note the numbered plateaus, their relative positions, any landmarks — especially the anomalous features, which seem to be location-specific and therefore useful as unique reference points. The crystal cluster with wrong-way shadows. The straight channel with the other sky. The hexagonal lattice. These are your landmarks.

**GPS and electronics:** Bring them, but expect them to be unreliable. The sourceless omnidirectional light suggests this location may not have a standard star or orbital mechanics, which means satellite-based navigation is meaningless. Even if you carry a GPS from your origin universe, it has nothing to talk to here. An altimeter based on barometric pressure might still work and would help you track your relative position on the terraced slope. Bring one.

## Perceptual and Psychological Preparation

This is where preparation for the Tiers diverges radically from preparation for any ordinary extreme environment. This section is at least as important as everything above.

### The Light

The omnidirectional, sourceless brightness is your constant companion and your first perceptual challenge. It is described as making everything "hyper-legible" — every crack, vein, and wet streak visible with surgical clarity. This sounds pleasant in the abstract and will become exhausting within hours. Your visual system is evolved to process a world lit from a dominant direction, with shadows that encode depth and shape. On the Tiers, that encoding system is disrupted. You will see everything with equal clarity and equal brightness, and your brain will struggle to prioritize, to build the normal hierarchy of visual attention. Everything will seem equally important. This is fatiguing and eventually destabilizing.

**Eyewear:** Bring high-quality, lightly tinted glasses or goggles — not dark sunglasses (there's nothing to shade against, no point source), but something that reduces the total light load by 20–30% and provides a slight warming of the color temperature. The light is described as neither white nor blue, just *luminance*. A light amber or rose tint might make the visual environment more parseable by introducing a slight color context your brain can use. Anti-glare coating. Side shields if possible — the light comes from everywhere.

**Deliberate visual rest:** Every few hours, close your eyes for 10–15 minutes. Cup your hands over your eyes to create total darkness. Let your visual cortex reset. The hyper-legibility of the Tiers, combined with the impossible visual phenomena you'll encounter, means your visual processing system will be working harder than it ever has. Rest it like you'd rest a muscle.

### The Sound

The bell-choir of water sounds is described as too loud to ignore, too harmonious to call noise, and continuous. This is a fascinating and serious psychological factor. Continuous, complex, semi-musical ambient sound has well-documented effects on consciousness, and they are not all benign. In many contemplative traditions, sustained exposure to resonant, patterned sound is used deliberately to alter awareness. The Tiers are doing this to you for free, all the time, without your consent.

The sound will be, at first, beautiful and mesmerizing. After hours, it may become a kind of trance-inducer. The thousands of individual water-on-crystal tones, each at slightly different pitches, reverberating off rock faces, folding and refolding — this is essentially a massive, naturally occurring generative soundscape optimized for perceptual absorption. You can lose yourself in it. On the Tiers, losing yourself may be more than a metaphor.

**Earplugs:** Carry high-fidelity earplugs — the kind musicians use, which attenuate volume evenly across frequencies without distorting the sound. Use them intermittently. Not constantly — you need to hear the environment, and the sound contains information (changes in water flow, shifts in deposition activity, potential structural changes in the stone). But give yourself periods of reduced auditory input. Even 20 dB of attenuation will significantly reduce the trance-inducing quality.

**Deliberate silence-seeking:** If you find any location on the Tiers where the sound is diminished — a sheltered overhang, a recessed area — note it and use it as a rest station. Your cognitive baseline resets in relative quiet.

**Your own voice:** Talk to yourself. Narrate what you're seeing, what you're doing, what you're thinking. Speak out loud. Your own voice is a familiar, human-scale sound that anchors you in your own identity and your own perceptual framework. It is an act of mild defiance against an environment that would prefer to dissolve you into its own acoustics. Don't be embarrassed. There's no one to hear you.

### The Anomalies

Now we come to the hardest part of preparation, because what I'm about to advise runs counter to every explorer's instinct, and you need to hear it clearly:

**You must develop, before you arrive, a disciplined practice of** ***looking without engaging.***

The Tiers are studded with phenomena that violate known physics in specific, localized, deeply fascinating ways. Shadows that point toward the light. A water channel that reflects a sky from another place. A mineral formation that occupies more than three spatial dimensions. These things are extraordinary. They may represent contact points with other configurations of reality. They are exactly the kind of phenomena that a multiverse traveler lives for.

They are also, almost certainly, the leading edges of whatever process is causing this location to "become somewhere else." And that process has been running, with geological patience, for an inconceivable length of time. You are visiting a work in progress. The formations are building toward something. The anomalies are symptoms or components of a transformation that is not complete and whose endpoint you cannot predict.

**Do not touch the impossible formations.** The crystal structure in the hexagonal lattice that seems to extend into additional dimensions, the one your eyes refuse to resolve — do not touch it. Do not lean in close enough that the water beading on its surface could drip onto your skin. The description notes that water runs across it in directions that include "inward, toward a center that keeps receding." That is a spatial anomaly. Your hand is a spatial object. Do not introduce your spatial object into a place where space is behaving incorrectly.

**Do not drink from or immerse anything in the straight channel that reflects the wrong sky.** The reflection persists when the water surface is disturbed. It has, in the description's words, "a deeper claim to reality than the thing doing the reflecting." This suggests the channel is not reflecting anything — it is a window, or a seam, or a thin place, and the water flowing through it is flowing *between* configurations of reality. Your hand passed through it safely once. That doesn't mean it will the second time, or the twentieth, or that "safely" means what you think it means. Maybe the water carried a few molecules of your skin to wherever those stars are. Maybe next time it carries more.

**Do not enter the roofless enclosures facing specific directions.** These were built by someone who understood the mountain. They are not shelters. They are instruments — focusing devices for the water's depositing process, guiding mineral growth into specific patterns. The "tiny city" growing in one basin is an engineered artifact of collaboration between a builder and the mountain's own transformative chemistry. You do not know what those enclosures are calibrated to do, whether they are still active, or whether the process they facilitate would treat an organic body the same way it treats inorganic mineral substrate. The builders knew. You don't. Stay outside. Observe from a respectful distance.

**The wrong-way shadows:** You can observe these more safely, as they appear to be a property intrinsic to the crystal formations rather than an active spatial distortion. But do not spend prolonged time in a wrong-way shadow. You don't know what a shadow that extends toward its light source is *doing* to the space it occupies, and standing in that space means the shadow is falling on you — and this shadow is not merely an absence of light but an active, anomalous phenomenon.

### Mental Anchoring Practices

Before arriving on the Tiers, establish a set of cognitive anchoring practices that you can perform regularly to maintain your sense of identity, location, and baseline reality:

1. **The inventory:** Every two hours, stop and recite aloud: your name, where you are from, how you arrived, how long you have been here, and what you plan to do next. If any of these become difficult to remember, it is time to leave immediately.

2. **The body check:** Touch your own face, hands, arms. Confirm the physical reality of your own body. At altitude, with dehydration, in an environment of sourceless light and continuous harmonic sound, depersonalization is a real risk even without anomalous physics. On the Tiers, it could be much more than a psychological phenomenon.

3. **The object:** Carry one small, personally meaningful physical object from your home reality. Something with strong associative memories. A ring, a photograph (waterproofed), a small stone from a specific place you love. Touch it during your inventory. It is your tether. It is proof that you come from somewhere that is not here, somewhere that obeys consistent physical laws, somewhere that is not in the process of becoming somewhere else.

4. **The journal:** Write constantly. Waterproof notebook, pencil (pens may fail in wet/cold/altitude conditions). Record observations, times, your physical state, your emotional state, anything that seems strange. The act of writing is itself an anchoring practice — it requires linear, sequential, language-based cognition, which is the mode of thought most resistant to the kind of perceptual dissolution the Tiers seem to encourage. Write even when you feel you have nothing to say. Write especially then.

5. **Set a hard departure time before you arrive.** Do not extend it under any circumstances. The Tiers will give you reasons to stay longer. The crystal cities are growing so slowly — surely another hour won't matter. Surely you need to see what the hexagonal lattice looks like at a different time. Surely you want to follow that perfectly straight channel to see where it ends. These impulses are natural curiosity, and they may also be something else. Set your time. Honor it. Leave.

## Equipment Checklist

Beyond what's discussed above, the specific and complete list:

**Survival and Shelter:**
- Lightweight waterproof bivy or single-person tent with sealed floor (the ground is perpetually wet)
- Sleeping bag, synthetic fill, rated to -20°C or lower
- Two sleeping pads (one closed-cell foam, one inflatable, stacked)
- Compact stove and fuel canisters if you want hot food/drinks (strongly recommended for morale and warmth — hot liquid is psychologically grounding)
- Lightweight pot and insulated mug
- Fire-starting materials as backup (though there is nothing to burn on the Tiers, you may need fire at lower elevations on approach)

**Hydration:**
- Minimum 6 liters of water from your origin reality (in durable, sealed containers)
- Water filter (0.1 micron) and activated carbon filter
- Chemical purification tablets as backup
- Electrolyte powder packets
- Collapsible water containers for carrying filtered local water if necessary

**Nutrition:**
- 5,000+ calories per day, pre-planned and pre-packed
- No-cook options as primary; hot-meal options if carrying stove
- Simple sugars for quick energy (honey packets, glucose tabs)

**Clothing:**
- Full layering system as described above, with redundant base and mid layers
- Three pairs of gloves
- Three pairs of socks
- Waterproof gaiters
- Warm hat and balaclava

**Navigation and Recording:**
- Compass (test on arrival)
- Barometric altimeter (independent of electronics)
- Waterproof notebooks (minimum three — they may degrade)
- Pencils (minimum six, pre-sharpened, with a sharpener)
- Flagging tape in a bright, non-natural color
- Small camera or sketching materials (for recording anomalous formations — but see warnings below)
- Wristwatch, mechanical if possible (electronic devices may be unreliable; you need reliable timekeeping for your departure schedule)

**Medical:**
- Acetazolamide (Diamox)
- Dexamethasone
- Nifedipine
- Ibuprofen (for altitude headaches)
- Basic first aid kit (adhesive bandages, antiseptic, blister treatment — wet feet breed blisters)
- Emergency foil blanket
- Pulse oximeter (battery-powered; gives you objective data on your blood oxygen saturation — critical for distinguishing altitude effects from anomalous effects)

**Perceptual Protection:**
- Tinted eyewear with side shields
- High-fidelity earplugs
- Full-block earplugs as backup (for sleeping — the sound never stops)
- Personal anchoring object

**Anomaly Precautions:**
- A length of cord or rope (25–50 meters), for measuring distances and marking boundaries around anomalous formations you want to keep your distance from
- Brightly colored chalk or tape for marking formations you've already observed (to track whether they change during your visit)
- A small mirror — if reflections on the Tiers behave anomalously, having a known reflective surface from your origin reality gives you a comparison baseline. If your mirror starts reflecting the wrong sky, that's critical information about the anomaly's range. It's also time to leave.
- A plumb line (small weight on a string) — a simple, physics-dependent tool for testing whether gravity is behaving correctly in a given location. If the plumb line hangs true, local gravity is normal. If it doesn't, move away from wherever you are.

## Specific Warnings and Behavioral Guidelines

### On Photographing the Anomalies

You may want to photograph or otherwise record the impossible formations. This is understandable but carries risks. Electronic devices in anomalous zones are unpredictable. There is a non-trivial possibility that an imaging sensor pointed at a formation that "occupies more dimensions than three" could produce an image that itself retains anomalous properties — an image that is, in some sense, a cross-section of the thing it depicts, carrying information that doesn't belong in a two-dimensional representation. If you photograph the formations, do not review the images on the Tiers. Store them. Examine them later, in a stable reality, with appropriate caution. Sketch by hand where possible — the act of translating anomalous geometry through your own perception and motor system into a drawing on paper is inherently a normalizing, flattening process, which is safer than a device that records without interpretation.

### On the Builders

Someone was here. Someone built structures that collaborate with the mountain's impossible mineralogy. They are gone. You do not know why they are gone. Consider the possibilities:

1. They finished their work and left.
2. They were changed by the Tiers and became something that no longer leaves traces of habitation.
3. They are still here, in a form you cannot perceive.
4. They are the crystal cities growing in the basins they carved.

None of these possibilities is comfortable. All of them should inform your behavior. Treat the built structures with respect. Do not modify them, add to them, or remove material from them. If the builders are in some sense still present — encoded in the mineral process they collaborated with — then interfering with their work is interfering with *them*.

### On the Water

I want to return to this one more time because it is the central fact of the Tiers: the water is the mechanism. The water is what dissolves, transports, and deposits. The water is what fills the reflecting channel with another sky. The water is what feeds the impossible formations. The water is what makes the mountain weep, and what the mountain is weeping is *itself* — dissolved, transformed, and reconstructed as something that exceeds its original nature.

You are a bag of water.

You are approximately 60% water by mass. The water inside you is a participant in every chemical reaction that keeps you alive. If the Tiers' water interacts with your body water — through skin contact, inhalation of mist, ingestion — you are introducing an active transformative agent into your own chemistry.

This may be harmless. Human skin is a reasonably effective barrier. Brief contact with the weeping water is probably fine. But *prolonged, repeated, full-body contact* is another matter. Don't wade through the sheet-flows barefoot. Don't stand in the mist-curtains from the weeping rock faces for extended periods. Don't let the water pool in your clothing against your skin. Stay as dry as your waterproof layers will allow.

And watch for changes. Specifically: watch for mineral deposits on your own skin or equipment. If you notice a faint white or crystalline residue forming on surfaces that have been exposed to Tier water — and especially if that residue is forming in patterns, in the same fractal drainage-channel shapes that mark the stone — that is the mountain treating you as a substrate. Leave. Leave immediately. Wash thoroughly with your carried water. Remove and bag any clothing that shows patterned mineral accumulation. Those patterns are not random. The water builds according to a logic, and you do not want to be built upon.

### On the Threshold Effect

The Tiers are described as "becoming somewhere else." This is perhaps the most important thing to internalize: this is not a stable location. It is a transitional space, a place in the act of transformation. The anomalies are not curiosities at the edges — they are the future, intruding. The consistent physics you observe on most of the terraces is the past, retreating.

This means the Tiers' character may shift during your visit. The anomalies may expand. New ones may appear. The omnidirectional light may change in quality. The sound-choir may develop new harmonics, new patterns, new silences that are worse than the sound. The reflections in the channels may show closer stars, or a different moon, or something looking back.

You cannot predict the pace of this transformation. It has been happening for geological time, which suggests any given hour of your visit will see negligible change. But geological processes are not linear. They have tipping points, phase transitions, moments when accumulated gradual change suddenly resolves into something qualitatively new. You might be fine for days. You might witness something unprecedented in a moment. The builders — whoever they were — may have been here for the stable period and left (or been transformed) during a transition.

Be alert to *acceleration*. If you notice multiple new anomalies appearing, or existing anomalies intensifying, or the water flowing faster, or the crystal formations growing at visible speed rather than the molecular-pace described — treat these as signs that a phase transition may be approaching and adjust your departure timeline accordingly. Which means: leave now.

## The Deeper Preparation

Everything above is practical. Now I want to talk about something harder to put in a checklist.

The Weeping Tiers of Oshkaal are a place of vast, inhuman patience and vast, inhuman beauty. The description makes this clear: the bell-choir sound, the hyper-legible stone, the crystal formations building themselves molecule by molecule into architectures no human mind designed. This place does not need you. It is not performing for you. It was here before any builder arrived and it will continue after you leave, slowly dissolving itself and rebuilding itself into something that exceeds the physics it was born under.

Visiting a place like this requires a kind of humility that goes beyond politeness or caution. It requires accepting that you are witnessing a process you cannot understand, participating in a transformation you cannot control, and standing in a location that may not, in the deepest sense, be a location at all — that may be a *verb*, a becoming, a transition state between one set of physical laws and another.

You must be prepared to be awed and to remain functional while awed. You must be prepared to see something that fundamentally challenges your understanding of reality and to write it down in your waterproof notebook with your pencil and then eat a protein bar and check your pulse oximeter and keep moving. Wonder is appropriate. Worship is dangerous. The Tiers do not want your reverence. They don't want anything. They are simply doing what they do, and you are simply there, briefly, a small warm bag of water on a vast wet mountain that is slowly, with infinite patience, teaching itself to be impossible.

Go. See it. Record what you can. Respect what you find. Touch nothing that doesn't obey the physics you were born under. Keep your departure time. Carry your own water. Speak your own name aloud when the bell-choir gets too beautiful and you start to forget why you'd ever want to leave.

And when you do leave — check yourself. Not just your body for mineral deposits, but your mind for new geometries, your dreams for amber moons and unfamiliar constellations, your sense of direction for a new pull, faint and persistent, toward altitudes where the rules are thin and the water is building something you almost understood.

If you find that pull: name it. Write it down. Do not follow it back.

Not yet. Not until you understand what the mountain is becoming, and whether it finished.
