# Preparing for a Visit to the Plateau of Settled Bones

---

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

This is not a hostile place. I want to begin there, because so much of multiverse travel preparation is oriented around danger, and if you approach the Plateau of Settled Bones expecting danger you will over-prepare in the wrong directions and under-prepare in the ones that matter. The Plateau will not try to kill you. It is not indifferent to you in the way a blizzard is indifferent. It is indifferent to you in the way a mountain is indifferent to a beetle walking across it — which is to say, it is aware of you at some scale you don't fully have access to, and it has decided you are neither threat nor nutrient, and that is that.

The real preparation is for something harder to equip against: the psychological and physiological experience of being a fast, loud, temporary thing on the surface of something slow, quiet, and functionally eternal. People who've visited comparable locations — the deep time ecosystems, the living geological formations, the places where the boundary between organism and landscape has dissolved — report that the challenge is not survival. The challenge is *staying coherent*. Not in a mystical sense. In a practical one. Your sense of time will stretch. Your urgency will drain. You will need to remember why you came and when you intend to leave, because the Plateau will not remind you.

So prepare for that. Prepare to be deeply, quietly altered by a place that isn't trying to alter you.

## Physical Preparation

### Cardiovascular and Respiratory Conditioning

The Plateau is high. The description suggests altitude consistent with something in the range of 3,500 to 4,500 meters — enough to produce noticeable breathlessness on exertion but not enough to be immediately dangerous to a healthy person. The "gentle thinning" language is key: this is not the gasping crisis of extreme altitude. It is the slow tax of moderate altitude sustained over time.

**Begin acclimatization training six to eight weeks before departure.** If you have access to altitude simulation masks or hypoxic tents, use them. If not, do your cardio at a pace that keeps you slightly breathless — the threshold where you can still speak in short sentences but not comfortably hold a conversation. This trains your body to operate efficiently on less oxygen, which is exactly the demand the Plateau will place on you.

More importantly: **train yourself to move slowly.** This sounds trivial. It is not. The Plateau rewards slow movement and punishes fast movement — not through any mechanism of threat, but because moving quickly at altitude drains you, and because moving quickly across the Plateau means stepping on things. The shelled creatures are everywhere, camouflaged to near-invisibility, and while they are presumably hardy enough to survive being occasionally stepped on (they've survived everything else), you don't want to be the clumsy interloper crushing the residents. Practice walking at half your normal pace. Then halve it again. The pace you want is the pace of someone browsing in a museum — deliberate, observant, with frequent stops.

**Do not neglect leg and ankle strength.** The stone surface is described as smooth and worn, but smooth does not mean flat. These are the undulations of a former mountain range compressed into gentle waves. You will be walking on curved surfaces, on domes, along shallow runnels, across stone that has been polished by millennia of shelled-creature passage into something potentially quite slick. Ankle rolls are a real risk, and a twisted ankle on a twenty-mile plateau at altitude with no infrastructure is a serious problem. Strengthen your stabilizer muscles. Practice walking on uneven river stones if you can find them. Get comfortable with surfaces that shift subtly underfoot.

### Hydration and Nutrition

This is your most critical logistical concern. **There is effectively no reliable water source on the Plateau.**

The rain is described as so infrequent it feels like a memory. The carved basins may or may not hold water when you arrive. The moisture that sustains the ecosystem comes primarily from the warm exhalations of the deep channels — and that moisture is almost certainly laden with minerals, biologics, and compounds you have no way of identifying or filtering with confidence. Do not drink from the channels. Do not drink condensation collected near the channel openings. You have no idea what you'd be ingesting, and given that the entire surface ecology appears to be a dependent outgrowth of whatever lives below, "nutritive" for lichen and felt-birds may be "deeply inadvisable" for human biochemistry.

**Carry all your water.** Calculate your needs based on moderate exertion at altitude in warm, dry air. A minimum of three liters per day, and honestly four is safer. If you plan to stay for multiple days — and you should, for reasons I'll discuss — you need a resupply plan or you need to carry a punishing amount of weight. Consider a lightweight collapsible water bladder system. Every gram matters when you're also carrying everything else and walking on worn stone at altitude.

For food: **calorie-dense, low-preparation meals.** You will not be cooking on the Plateau. I'll address fire below, but the short version is: don't. Bring nut butters, dense grain bars, dried fruit, jerky or its equivalent, hard cheeses if the temperature permits, anything that delivers maximum calories per gram without requiring water to prepare. Your appetite may diminish at altitude and under the Plateau's strange temporal influence — people report forgetting to eat, not out of anorexia but out of a kind of absorptive distraction — so set alarms. Eat on schedule. Your body needs fuel whether your mind remembers to ask for it or not.

### Skin and Eye Protection

The light is described as plain and diffused, without glare, which is a mercy — but do not be deceived. Overcast skies at altitude still deliver significant UV, and diffused light means the UV comes from every direction rather than from a single point source, making shade largely irrelevant. **Wear broad-spectrum sunscreen, SPF 50 minimum, reapplied every two hours.** Bring more than you think you need. The warm, dry air will desiccate your skin aggressively, so also bring a heavy moisturizer and apply it every evening.

For your eyes: a good pair of UV-filtering sunglasses, but not the darkest tint you can find. You need to see the ground clearly. You need to distinguish shelled creatures from cobbles. A medium amber or rose tint will cut UV while preserving contrast on the grey-brown stone. Bring a clear pair as well for the moments when the light dims further, or for evening observation.

**Lip balm with SPF.** I cannot stress this enough. Dry air at altitude will crack your lips within a day, and once they're cracked, every subsequent day becomes measurably worse. Bring three tubes. Put one in your pocket, one in your pack, one in your shelter kit. Use them constantly.

## Gear and Equipment

### Footwear

This is the single most important gear decision you will make for the Plateau, and the answer is less obvious than you might think.

You do not want heavy hiking boots. The terrain does not demand ankle support in the traditional sense — there are no steep scrambles, no loose scree, no trails cut into hillsides. What you need is **a shoe with exceptional grip on smooth stone, a sole stiff enough to protect against sustained walking on hard surfaces, and enough tactile sensitivity that you can feel what you're about to step on.**

An approach shoe — the kind designed for the transition between trail and rock face — is ideal. Look for one with a sticky rubber sole, moderate stiffness, a low-cut profile for freedom of ankle movement, and a snug fit that gives you proprioceptive feedback from the ground. You want to feel the subtle curvature of the worn stone through the sole. You want to feel the slight give of a lichen crust that might indicate a shelled creature underneath.

**Break these shoes in thoroughly before you arrive.** Walk in them on smooth concrete, on river rock, on any worn stone surface you can find. You should be so comfortable in them that you forget you're wearing them, because on the Plateau, your attention needs to be on the ground and the life covering it, not on your feet.

Bring a second pair. Not as a backup for the same conditions, but as a camp shoe — something soft-soled, lightweight, for wearing around your resting area in the evenings. Your feet will need relief from hard stone, and your camp shoe should have the softest sole you can find to minimize impact on any creatures near your rest site.

### Shelter

You will need shelter, and this is where things get philosophically interesting.

There are no trees. There are no natural overhangs described — the erosion has removed all protrusions, all features that might create a sheltered space. The ancient walls are knee-high and open. You cannot use them as shelter in any structural sense, and you probably shouldn't — they are ecologically active, colonized by creatures and lichen, serving as gathering points for the felt-birds. Camping in or against them feels like setting up your tent in the middle of a congregation.

**Bring a freestanding tent or bivouac shelter that requires no stakes.** You cannot drive stakes into this stone. Even if you could, you shouldn't — you don't know what's beneath the surface, and given the network of deep channels and the something-large that breathes down there, puncturing the stone feels deeply inadvisable. A geodesic tent with a weighted base, or a bivvy bag with a self-supporting hoop frame, is what you want.

Place your shelter **away from the channel openings.** This is important for multiple reasons. The warm, humid exhalations will create condensation on your shelter, and that moisture may carry compounds you don't want to be breathing all night. The shelled creatures cluster near the openings, and you don't want to be in their path. The felt-birds orient toward the channels, and placing yourself between them and their apparent focus of attention seems like the kind of rudeness that, while it might not provoke a visible response, sits wrong in the gut. Find a spot on the relatively bare stone between lichen crusts, away from visible runnels and channels, where your footprint — literal and ecological — is minimized.

Your shelter should be **muted in color.** Not for camouflage in any tactical sense, but because the Plateau's palette is grey, brown, ochre, verdigris, pale rose. A bright orange tent pitched in the middle of this would be an aesthetic violence that I think would actually affect your experience. Choose grey, tan, olive — something that settles into the landscape rather than announcing itself. This is not vanity. It is respect, and it is also practical: the felt-birds are described as watchful and interested rather than fearful, and a garish shelter might alter their behavior around your campsite in ways that change what you're able to observe.

### Navigation

**Distances deceive on the Plateau.** The description is explicit about this, and you should take it seriously.

The combination of featureless terrain, haze, altitude, uniform lighting, and the absence of familiar reference points (no trees, no buildings, no trails) creates conditions ripe for spatial disorientation. Twenty miles of this, and every acre looks nearly identical to every other acre, distinguished only by subtle variations in lichen coverage and the density of shelled creatures.

Bring a compass. Bring a GPS device with waypoint capability and **mark your arrival point immediately.** Mark your shelter location. Mark any channel opening you investigate. Mark the ancient walls you encounter. Build a waypoint map as you explore, because your memory will not serve you as well as you expect — the sameness of the terrain actively interferes with spatial memory formation. You'll swear you've been walking east for an hour and discover you've curved south without noticing, because there was nothing to mark the curve against.

**Do not rely on the sun for direction.** It is diffused, locationless, a general brightening. It will not give you east and west with any confidence.

Consider also: a simple pedometer or step counter. Knowing how far you've walked is grounding in a way that matters more here than in most places. The Plateau's temporal strangeness — its tendency to make time feel stretched and elastic — extends to distance. A walk that felt like a mile may have been three. A walk that felt like three may have been one. Concrete data anchors you.

### Tools and Instruments

This depends on your purpose, but I'll assume you are visiting as an observer — someone who wants to understand the Plateau rather than extract from it.

**A good hand lens or jeweler's loupe, 10x magnification.** The ecology here operates at scales you'll miss with the naked eye. The lichen crusts, the translucent mites that feed on the lichen, the texture of the shelled creatures' encrusted dorsal surfaces, the fibrous integument of the felt-birds — all of this rewards close inspection. A loupe lets you enter the miniature world without disturbing it.

**A thermometer, ideally one that measures both air and surface temperature.** You'll want to document the temperature differential between ambient air and the exhalations from the deep channels. This data will help you understand the metabolic scale of whatever lives below. If the exhalations are significantly warmer than ambient — ten degrees, twenty degrees — that tells you something about the size and metabolic rate of the subterranean organism. Bring an infrared thermometer if you can; it lets you measure surface temperatures without contact.

**A small, sensitive anemometer or even a strip of silk thread on a stick.** The exhalations from the channels create air currents you can feel on your ankles, but measuring them — timing their intervals, gauging their strength — gives you data about the respiratory rhythm of the deep presence. An eight-to-twelve-second cycle, the description suggests. Time it precisely. Observe whether it varies with time of day, with weather, with your proximity. This is the closest you will come to direct interaction with the Plateau's deepest resident.

**A notebook. Physical, paper, bound.** Not a tablet, not a phone. This is not Luddism; it's practical. Electronics are fine for data logging, but the act of writing by hand on the Plateau serves a cognitive function that typing does not: it forces you to slow down, to choose words carefully, to process your observations through the deliberate mechanism of handwriting rather than the automatic mechanism of keyboarding. On a place that operates at the speed of lichen growth, you want your recording method to have some friction to it. You want to sit with what you've seen before you commit it to the page. Also, paper doesn't run out of battery, and there is absolutely no infrastructure on this plateau to charge anything.

Bring pencils, not pens. Pens can dry out in warm dry air. Pencils are permanent enough for field notes and will work regardless of temperature or humidity.

**Do not bring a drone.** I feel strongly about this. The felt-birds don't fly much, but they are fliers, and the airspace above the Plateau is theirs. A buzzing mechanical intruder in that space — in the immense quiet of this place — would be a desecration. It would also almost certainly alter the behavior of every creature in visual and auditory range. Observe from the ground. Accept the limitations of your ground-level perspective. The Plateau has depths you cannot access and that's part of the experience.

## Behavioral Preparation

This section matters more than the gear section. Gear keeps you alive. Behavior determines whether the visit is meaningful.

### Slowness as Practice

You need to learn to be slow before you arrive. Not merely to move slowly — though that is essential — but to *perceive* slowly. The Plateau operates on timescales that range from the geological (the erosion of the mountain range) to the barely-perceptible (the movement of the shelled creatures) to the rhythmic-but-unhurried (the breathing of the deep channels). If you arrive with your normal human tempo still running — the tempo of conversation, of task completion, of checking things off a list — you will see almost nothing. You will walk across an alien landscape and register it as a barren, hazy, weirdly crowded rock field, and you will miss everything that matters.

**In the weeks before your visit, practice stillness.** Not meditation in the formal sense, unless that works for you, but the specific skill of sitting in one place and watching a very small area for a very long time. Go to a park. Find a square meter of ground. Watch it for an hour. Notice the insects. Notice the way the light changes. Notice how your perception sharpens as your restlessness fades. This is the skill you need on the Plateau. This is the only way to see the shelled creatures move, to watch the crawlers flow through crevices, to catch the moment a felt-bird opens its triangular mouth to taste the air.

**Practice walking meditation.** Not the spiritual kind specifically — though if that's your tradition, by all means — but the physical discipline of walking so slowly that each step is a deliberate decision. Place your foot. Feel the ground. Transfer your weight. Pause. Place the next foot. On the Plateau, this pace will protect the creatures underfoot, conserve your energy at altitude, and keep you in sensory contact with the surface you're crossing. It will also, over hours, shift your temporal perception in ways that bring you closer to the Plateau's own rhythm.

### The Discipline of Non-Interference

**Do not touch the shelled creatures.** I know you'll want to. They're right there, they're everywhere, they look like stones and the urge to pick one up and turn it over to see its underside will be powerful. Resist it. You don't know what disruption a human touch introduces to a system this old and this finely balanced. The lichen on their shells may be symbiotic. Their position in the runnels may be the result of a movement pattern that's been optimizing for longer than human civilization has existed. Picking one up and putting it down again — even in the same spot — interrupts something you can't see.

**Do not put anything into the deep channels.** No probes, no cameras, no thermometers lowered on strings. You do not know what lives down there, and more importantly, you do not know the relationship between what lives down there and what lives on the surface. The warm exhalations may be respiration. They may be communication. They may be a form of tending, as the description suggests. Introducing foreign objects into that system is the equivalent of sticking your hand into someone's mouth while they're breathing: even if it doesn't cause harm, it's a violation of something that should be left alone.

**Do not leave anything behind.** This is standard multiverse travel ethics, but it's especially critical here. The Plateau's ecology is so tightly looped, so efficient in its recycling — "everything feeding on everything else's leavings in tight, efficient loops that waste nothing" — that any foreign material you introduce becomes a contaminant. A dropped granola bar wrapper. A lost button. A splash of spilled water from your canteen, with its non-native mineral composition. In most environments, these things are trivial. On the Plateau, where the food web is compressed to the point of intimacy, they could propagate effects you can't predict.

Carry a designated waste bag. Put everything in it — food scraps, used tissues, medical waste, everything. Pack it all out. Leave the Plateau exactly as you found it, minus your footprints, which the shelled creatures will polish away in time.

### Managing the Psychological Experience

This is the preparation most travelers underestimate, and it's the one I want to spend the most time on, because the Plateau of Settled Bones is not a dangerous place but it is a *disorienting* one, and the disorientation is subtle enough to become advanced before you recognize it.

**The Plateau will make you feel temporary.** Not in the way that a dangerous place makes you feel mortal — that's adrenaline, that's immediate, that's actually easy to process. The Plateau makes you feel temporary the way looking at a very old fossil makes you feel temporary, except the fossil is alive, and it's everywhere, and it's breathing, and you are walking on it. This feeling accumulates. It doesn't spike; it deepens. By the second or third day, travelers to deep-time locations commonly report a strange lassitude — not depression, not despair, but a draining of urgency that can become a draining of motivation. Why walk farther? The next acre looks the same. Why take notes? Who are they for? Why leave? What's out there that isn't here?

**This is the Plateau settling into you.** It is not malicious. It is what happens when a human nervous system, evolved for a world of rapid change and immediate consequence, is immersed in an environment where change happens at the speed of erosion and consequence is measured in millennia. Your brain begins to downregulate urgency because there's nothing to be urgent about. This is, in some ways, a gift — a rest from the relentless tick of human time. But it can become dangerous if it progresses to the point where you stop eating, stop hydrating, stop checking your GPS, stop remembering that you are a visitor and not a resident.

**Set external anchors before you arrive:**

- **A fixed departure time.** Not flexible, not "when I feel ready." A date and time, written in your notebook, committed to before you set foot on the stone. When that time comes, leave. Even if you don't want to. Especially if you don't want to.

- **A daily routine.** Wake at a set time (use an alarm — the unchanging light won't give you reliable morning cues). Eat. Hydrate. Check your gear. Record your observations. Walk for a set number of hours. Return to camp. Eat again. Record again. Sleep. This structure is your scaffolding. Without it, the days will dissolve into an undifferentiated continuum of sitting and watching and breathing with the Plateau, and you will lose track of how long you've been there.

- **A communication check.** If your multiverse transit method allows for any form of external communication, schedule regular check-ins with someone who is not on the Plateau. Hearing a human voice, or even composing a message to one, reactivates the parts of your cognition that the Plateau's deep stillness tends to quiet. If communication isn't possible, write letters. You don't have to send them. The act of addressing another person — "Dear [name], today I watched a felt-bird for three hours and I think I understand why they face the channels" — keeps your social cognition from going dormant.

- **Bring a personal object that is *aggressively* temporary.** A fresh flower — something that will wilt and brown and die over the course of your visit. Keep it in your shelter. Watch it change. In an environment where nothing seems to change at a human timescale, this small, familiar arc of bloom-to-decay will remind your nervous system that you belong to a different timestream. You are a thing that changes quickly. That is not a flaw. That is your nature, and it is what you will eventually return to.

### Encountering the Deep Presence

Let me address this directly, because it will be the defining experience of your visit and you need to be ready for it.

You will, at some point, find yourself standing near a channel opening, feeling the warm exhalation on your skin, smelling the chalk-and-copper smell, and you will know — not believe, not suspect, but *know* with a certainty that bypasses intellect entirely — that something is down there. Something alive. Something vast. Something that has been breathing since before the mountain range was a mountain range.

This knowing will not feel like an inference. It will feel like recognition. As if you've always known this thing was here and are only now remembering.

**This is the moment you are most likely to do something inadvisable.** People respond to this recognition in different ways. Some want to call down into the channel — to shout, to speak, to make contact. Some want to climb in. Some want to run. Some want to lie down on the stone and press their ear to it and listen. Some want to weep. All of these are understandable responses to the sudden, visceral apprehension of a living presence that operates on a scale your brain was never designed to process.

**Here is my guidance: do nothing.** Stand near the channel. Feel the warmth. Smell the smell. Let the exhalation wash over your ankles. Watch the shelled creatures cluster. Watch the felt-birds orient. Breathe. That's all. You are being allowed to witness something — not invited to participate, not challenged to respond, just allowed to be present. The appropriate response to that allowance is presence. Just be there. Just breathe. Notice that your breathing and the channel's breathing are not synchronized, and do not try to synchronize them. You are a different kind of thing, on a different kind of time. That difference is okay. The Plateau has room for you exactly as you are.

If you feel the urge to weep, weep. There is nothing wrong with tears in the presence of deep time. They are, if anything, the most honest response a temporary creature can offer to a permanent one: *I am here, I know I won't be here long, and I am moved.*

But do not shout. Do not climb in. Do not attempt to feed anything into the channel, or record the sound from inside it, or lower any instrument into the darkness. Whatever is down there has not asked for your attention. You are giving it freely, and you should give it freely, and that should be enough.

## What to Wear

The Plateau's environment is warm and dry with thin air and diffused UV. Your clothing should address all of these simultaneously.

**Lightweight, long-sleeved, UPF-rated shirts and pants.** Cover your skin. The sun is mild but constant, and at altitude the UV is stronger than it feels. Choose light colors that approximate the Plateau's palette — grey, tan, warm brown. Not for camouflage but for thermal regulation (light colors reflect heat) and for the same reason I recommended a muted shelter: you are a guest in an ancient place, and you should dress like one.

**A wide-brimmed hat.** The light comes from everywhere, so the brim matters less for shade than for reducing your overall UV exposure and keeping the diffused brightness from fatiguing your eyes. A hat with a chin cord, because even the rare wind on the Plateau can lift an unsecured hat, and chasing it across the stone means stepping on shelled creatures.

**Layers for evening.** The Plateau is warm during the day, but high, dry places radiate heat quickly once the light fades. Nights may be significantly cooler. Bring a lightweight insulating layer — fleece or synthetic fill — and a wind-resistant outer shell. The wind, when it comes, is described as rare and low, but a sustained light breeze at altitude on cooling stone can chill you faster than you expect.

**Gaiters or high socks.** The warm exhalations from the channels reach ankle height, and while they're not described as harmful, prolonged exposure of bare skin to air of unknown biological composition seems unwise. Also, the crawlers — the segmented creatures the color of dried blood — flow through crevices at ground level, and while there's no indication they bite or sting, having a layer of fabric between their many legs and your skin is basic prudence.

**Gloves.** Light, thin, tactile. Not for warmth but for when you're examining anything up close, turning your hand lens on lichen crusts or inspecting the ancient walls. You don't want to touch surfaces directly. Your skin oils, your sweat, your dead cells — all foreign inputs into the Plateau's closed system.

## Medical Preparation

### Altitude-Related Issues

At the Plateau's likely altitude, acute mountain sickness (AMS) is possible, especially in the first 24–48 hours. Symptoms include headache, nausea, fatigue, and dizziness. **Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) as a prophylactic if your physician approves.** Begin taking it 24 hours before arrival. Stay hydrated — dehydration exacerbates altitude sickness. If symptoms become severe (confusion, ataxia, severe headache unresponsive to analgesics), descend immediately. The Plateau's gradual western slope means descent is physically possible, though slow.

### Respiratory Concerns

The warm air from the channels carries an organic smell described as not unpleasant, but "not unpleasant" is not the same as "safe to breathe." If you plan to spend significant time near channel openings — and you will — consider carrying a lightweight particulate mask. Not for constant use, but to have available if you begin to notice any respiratory irritation: coughing, tightness, unusual mucus production. The compounds in that exhalation are unknown, and your lungs have no evolved defense against them.

### Skin and Wound Care

Any break in the skin on the Plateau — a scrape from a fall on the stone, a crack in your dry lips, a blister from your shoes — should be cleaned and covered immediately. You are in an environment saturated with unfamiliar microbiota. The lichen, the mites, the organic compounds in the air — any of these could colonize an open wound. Carry a comprehensive first aid kit with antiseptic, sterile bandages, antibiotic ointment, and blister care supplies. Inspect your skin every evening.

### Mental Health

I've addressed the psychological effects above, but from a medical standpoint: if you have a history of depressive episodes, dissociative states, or derealization, consult with a mental health professional before visiting the Plateau. The combination of altitude (which can affect mood), sensory uniformity (which can trigger dissociation), temporal distortion (which can exacerbate derealization), and the deep-time confrontation with your own impermanence (which can trigger existential crisis in vulnerable individuals) makes this a location that requires psychological resilience. This is not a place to visit while you're fragile. It is, conversely, a place that can be profoundly restorative for someone who is psychologically stable but emotionally exhausted — the stillness, the quiet, the sense of being cradled on the surface of something vast and patient. But know yourself. Be honest about where you are.

## Duration of Visit

**Three to five days is ideal.**

Less than three days and you won't adjust. You'll spend the first day dealing with logistics — setting up shelter, acclimating to altitude, learning to walk without stepping on the shelled creatures. The second day your perceptions will begin to shift. By the third day, you'll be seeing the Plateau on something approaching its own terms. That third day is when the real visit begins. The fourth and fifth days are for deepening — for sitting by a channel opening and timing the breath, for following a single shelled creature's path over the course of an afternoon, for watching the felt-birds and beginning to discern individual differences in their behavior.

More than five days and you risk the motivational erosion I described above. The Plateau is patient and it will wait for you, but you are not patient in the same way, and pretending you are — staying longer than your human timestream can sustain — leads to the lassitude, the lost days, the forgetting to eat. Five days. Mark your departure. Honor it.

## Ethical Considerations

You are visiting something that may be a single organism. The entire Plateau — the subterranean breather, the lichen, the shelled creatures, the crawlers, the felt-birds, the ancient walls — may constitute one living system. If it does, then your visit is not analogous to hiking through a national park. It is analogous to walking across the skin of a sleeping giant. The ethical framework shifts accordingly.

**You are not entitled to this experience.** The Plateau has not invited you. It has not refused you either — it appears to tolerate visitors with the same unhurried indifference it applies to everything — but tolerance is not invitation. Be grateful for the tolerance. Do not test its limits.

**Take nothing.** No stone samples, no lichen scrapings, no shell fragments, no felt-bird plumage found snagged on the ancient walls. This is a closed system. Removing material from it is removing a piece of a body.

**Publish with care.** If you write about your visit — and I hope you will, because the Plateau deserves witnesses — be thoughtful about what you include. Precise location coordinates could invite a flood of visitors that the ecosystem cannot absorb. The Plateau has survived erosion, time, and the dissolution of mountain ranges; it should not have to survive tourism. Be descriptive enough to convey the experience. Be vague enough to protect the place.

**Do not name the deep presence.** You will want to. The human instinct to name things is powerful, and an unnamed living presence feels unfinished, feels like it's waiting for you to complete it by giving it a word. Resist this. It has existed without your name for longer than naming has existed. To name it is to claim a relationship with it that you haven't earned and that it hasn't offered. Let it remain what it is: something down there, breathing, warm, unnameable.

## What to Expect When You Leave

You will walk west, toward the gradual descent, and the Plateau will not change behind you. The felt-birds will watch you go. The shelled creatures will not notice. Below, something will exhale.

You will feel, for some time afterward — days, possibly weeks — as though you are moving too fast. The ordinary pace of human life will feel frantic. Conversations will seem rushed. The urgency of daily tasks will strike you as absurd. This is normal. This is the residue of having briefly entrained to a slower rhythm. It fades. Let it fade at its own pace. Do not try to hold onto it — that way lies a nostalgia that can curdle into a permanent dissatisfaction with the tempo of your own life.

What you can keep — what is worth keeping — is the knowledge that the Plateau exists. That somewhere, in some fold of the multiverse, a mountain range finished eroding and something moved in and breathed, and it is still breathing, and it will still be breathing after you and everyone you know have blown away like the last flake of stone in the wind.

That knowledge does not make your life smaller. It makes it more precise. You are a specific thing, in a specific time, moving at a specific speed, and you were there, briefly, on the back of something ancient, and you were witnessed by creatures with triangular mouths and forward-facing eyes, and you left no mark, and that was enough.

It was enough.
