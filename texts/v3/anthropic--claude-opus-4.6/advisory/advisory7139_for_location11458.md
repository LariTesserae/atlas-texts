# Preparing to Visit the Cistern Floor

---

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything else, sit with the description for a while. Read it more than once. Let the understanding settle that this is not an adventure destination, not a spectacle, not a place that will offer you a story to tell afterward that makes you sound interesting at gatherings. This is a place where almost nothing happens, and where that almost-nothing has been happening with absolute consistency for longer than your species has existed. Your preparation should be shaped by this understanding. You are not gearing up for an expedition. You are preparing to be a guest in a room that did not invite you, does not need you, and will not remember you.

The multiverse framing matters here. Across the variants of reality where this place exists, the details may shift — the crack might admit a slightly different quality of light, the isopods might be fractionally larger or absent entirely, the mineral palette might lean more toward grey or more toward amber — but the essential character will be the same: enclosed, warm, wet, still, ancient, complete. You are not visiting a specific cave. You are visiting a *condition*. Prepare for the condition.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear and the Decision About Feet

You will be walking in ankle-deep to shin-deep warm water on a floor made of deposited calcium carbonate — flowstone terraces, rimstone dams, cave pearls. The surface is uneven but not jagged. It is smooth in the way that slow mineral accretion makes things smooth: rounded, undulating, with edges that are blunted lips rather than sharp ridges. However, the calcite film is potentially slippery when wet, which it always is.

You have three options, and you should think carefully about each:

**Barefoot.** This is the option that the place itself seems to suggest. The water is skin-temperature. The floor is smooth. Going barefoot means you will feel the terraces under your feet, feel the slight lips of the rimstone dams, feel the cave pearls shift if you step on them (try not to). It means the boundary between you and the water will begin dissolving almost immediately, which is one of the defining experiences of this space. The risk is slipping. Wet calcite on bare skin can be treacherous, especially when you're crouching under a low ceiling and your balance is already compromised. If you fall, the water is shallow enough that you won't drown, but you could crack a knee on a terrace edge or crush a rimstone dam under your weight, and the damage you do to the formations will take centuries to repair. Centuries that the place has, but that's not the point. The point is that your carelessness would leave a mark that outlasts everything you've ever built.

**Thin neoprene water shoes or dive boots.** This is the practical compromise. Something with a soft sole that grips wet mineral surfaces, that doesn't compress the delicate rimstone underfoot too aggressively, that lets you feel the contours of the floor without risking a fall. Choose something with no tread pattern that could scrape the calcite, or with a tread so fine it amounts to texture rather than pattern. Avoid anything with hard soles, buckles, or rigid elements. You will be crouching, possibly kneeling, possibly sitting in the water. The shoes should be comfortable when fully submerged and should not float off your feet if they become buoyant.

**Thick-soled boots or hiking footwear.** Do not bring these. You will damage the floor. The rimstone dams are delicate mineral lips, sometimes only millimeters thick at their crests. A heavy boot placed carelessly will shatter formations that have been growing since before the last ice age. This is not a trail. There is no path. Every inch of floor is a formation.

My recommendation: the thin neoprene shoes, at least for the approach and for any crouching navigation. Once you've found a place to be still — and you will need to find a place to be still — you can remove them and let your feet rest on the floor and let the water do what it does.

### Clothing

The air is saturated. The temperature is constant and warm — skin-temperature, approximately. You will not be cold. You will, however, be wet almost immediately, from condensation if not from the water itself. The ceiling drips. The air deposits moisture on every surface, including you.

Wear as little as you can comfortably wear. Light synthetic fabric that you don't mind being soaked — a thin base layer, shorts or light pants that can be rolled above the knee. Cotton is a poor choice; it will become heavy and cling and stay wet long after you leave. Anything you wear will be damp within minutes and fully wet within the hour.

Consider the psychological dimension of clothing here. In a space where the boundary between your body and the water is already dissolving — where the warmth matches your skin so closely that the water becomes theoretical — heavy or structured clothing will feel like an obstruction. You don't want to feel armored in this place. You want to feel permeable. Not vulnerable, exactly, but *available* to the environment in a way that stiff fabric and multiple layers will prevent.

Do not bring a pack or bag into the space itself if you can avoid it. Anything you carry should fit in your hands or in a small waterproof pouch clipped close to your body. Setting a backpack down on the floor means setting it on formations. Slinging it against the wall means pressing it against encrusted ceiling surfaces where it will scrape calcite that has been growing for millennia. Travel light. You will not need much.

### Physical Conditioning

This is not physically demanding in the way that caving normally is — there are no vertical pitches, no crawls through tight squeezes, no rope work. But you will be crouching for extended periods under a ceiling that varies from person-height to chest-height. If you are tall, you may be crouching for most of your time in the space. This means sustained load on your knees, hips, lower back, and ankles.

In the weeks before your visit, practice deep squatting and sustained low postures. Sit in a low squat for five minutes, ten minutes, fifteen. Get comfortable with the feeling of your thighs burning and then get past it to the point where the posture becomes rest rather than effort. Practice moving slowly while crouched — shifting your weight from one foot to the other, taking small steps, pivoting. You will need to do this on a slippery surface in warm water in near-darkness without reaching up to steady yourself against a ceiling covered in irreplaceable formations.

If you have knee problems, bring thin neoprene knee sleeves. Not rigid braces — something soft that supports the joint without adding bulk. You may end up kneeling in the water at some point, and a few millimeters of neoprene between your kneecap and the calcite floor will matter.

### Breathing

The saturated air will feel thick. It is not dangerous — the oxygen content is presumably adequate, given that the space is not entirely sealed and the light-admitting crack suggests at least minimal air exchange — but it will feel different from normal breathing. Practice breathing in a steam room or a hot, humid bathroom with the shower running. Get used to the sensation of inhaling air that feels like it has substance, like it's delivering water to your lungs along with oxygen. It can trigger a mild claustrophobic response in people who aren't expecting it, and claustrophobia in this space is something you want to have resolved *before* you're crouching under a dripping ceiling in dim light thirty paces from the exit.

If you have asthma or other respiratory conditions, consult someone with expertise in cave atmospheres. The warm, mineral-saturated air may or may not interact poorly with your specific physiology. This is not a well-trafficked tourist cave with monitored air quality. This is a pocket in the earth where the air has been doing its own thing for a very long time.

## Equipment

### Light

This is the most important decision you will make, and you need to make it carefully, because the quality of light you bring will determine the quality of your experience more than any other single factor.

The space has its own light — grey, directionless, arriving through a crack, enough to see by once your eyes adjust, but only barely, and only in the half of the room nearest the crack. The other half is dark. The description suggests that the far-from-light half is known more by proximity than by sight, and this is important: the place is designed — not by anyone, but by its own geometry — to be experienced partially in darkness. The formations on the ceiling are meant to be felt as much as seen. The water on the floor is meant to be sensed by temperature rather than observed by light.

**Option one: bring no artificial light at all.** This is the purest approach and probably the most rewarding, but it requires patience and comfort with darkness that most people don't have. You would enter, let your eyes adjust over fifteen or twenty minutes, and eventually see the space as the grey light and your adapted retinas allow. The far end would be visible in soft monochrome. The near end would be shapes and suggestions. You would navigate by feel, by the sound of your own disturbance in the water, by the sensation of the ceiling pressing close. This is how whoever left the tools on the shelf probably experienced the space, at least some of the time.

**Option two: a single, very dim, warm-toned light.** This is my recommendation. A small LED set to its lowest output, with a warm color temperature — 2700K or lower, ideally diffused rather than focused. Not a headlamp beam that creates a bright spot and deep shadows; the space has no shadows naturally and imposing them on it feels violent. Something you can cup in your hand. Something that illuminates what is within arm's reach and lets the rest remain as the space intends it. A tealight candle would be ideal in terms of quality of light — warm, flickering, soft, alive in a way that complements the dripping — but an open flame in a saturated atmosphere may gutter and struggle, and soot deposits on calcite formations would be a small desecration. If you use a candle, use it briefly, and keep it close, and don't let it touch the stone.

**Option three: a bright headlamp or flashlight.** You can do this. You will see everything clearly. The formations on the ceiling will be brilliantly visible, every fold and bubble and layered accretion picked out in sharp relief. The cave pearls in their pools will glow. The water will finally gleam. And the space will feel like a specimen under examination rather than a place you are inside. The intimacy will be gone. The muffled, close, lived-in quality of the darkness will be replaced by the clinical feeling of inspection. You will learn things — you will see the chisel marks on the shelf clearly, see the grain of the bone awl, count the isopods in the channel — but you will not *experience* the space as it experiences itself, which is in near-darkness, always, regardless of whether you are there.

If you must use a bright light, use it in brief intervals. Turn it on, look, understand what you're seeing, turn it off. Let the darkness come back. Let your eyes readjust. The space will still be there in the dark. It was there in the dark before anyone brought fire into it, and it returned to the dark after.

### No Photography

I want to say this plainly: do not photograph this place. Not because it is forbidden — there is no one to forbid it — but because the act of photographing it will consume your experience of it entirely. You will spend your limited time trying to capture the quality of the light, the texture of the ceiling, the subtle coloring of the water, and you will fail, because no camera sensor can reproduce the experience of dim grey directionless light on wet calcite seen by dark-adapted eyes in a space where the air itself feels like a medium. You will leave with images that look like pictures of a wet cave, and you will have missed the cave entirely.

If you need to remember, bring a small waterproof notebook and a pencil. Sit in the water and write or draw what you notice. The act of translating sensation into language or line will sharpen your attention without removing you from the moment the way a camera does. Get the notebook wet. It doesn't matter. The smeared graphite will be a better memento than any photograph.

### Water and Food

You will not need much of either for the visit itself — the space is small, and you shouldn't stay so long that dehydration becomes a concern. But do bring a small amount of clean water to drink, because breathing saturated air can paradoxically make you feel thirsty (your body registers the moisture but can't efficiently absorb water through the lungs, so the sensation of wetness doesn't translate to hydration). Drink before you enter. Bring a small sealed container with a few sips for during.

Do not eat inside the space. Crumbs, oils from food, organic residue of any kind — these introduce nutrients into a system that is running on almost nothing. The bacterial biofilm that feeds the isopods is calibrated to the vanishingly thin nutrient stream provided by dissolved minerals in the water. A single dropped crumb is a feast that could trigger a bacterial bloom, change the chemistry of the film, alter the food supply for organisms that have been subsisting on the same near-nothing for epochs. You would never see the consequences, but they would happen.

### Waste

This should go without saying, but: eliminate all bodily waste before entering. Urinating in the water would be an ecological catastrophe on the scale of this tiny ecosystem. Even sweat and skin oils are contaminants here, though unavoidable ones. Minimize what you introduce. If you must spit, spit into a container. If your nose runs (the humid air may trigger this), use a cloth and keep it on your person.

## Psychological and Emotional Preparation

### Confronting Stillness

Most people have never been in a place that is genuinely still. Even quiet places — forests, empty churches, desert nights — have cycles: wind, birdsong, the movement of light as the earth turns. This place has none of that. The temperature doesn't change. The light doesn't change. The water level doesn't change. The dripping is constant but arrhythmic, which means your brain cannot lock onto it as a rhythm and use it to mark time. You will lose track of how long you've been there surprisingly fast. Without external cues, your internal clock will drift. Five minutes and forty-five minutes will feel identical.

This can be profoundly uncomfortable. Humans are adapted to change — we are wired to notice differences, to track the passage of time, to anticipate what comes next. In a place where nothing comes next, where this moment is identical to the last and will be identical to the next, some part of your brain will begin to insist that something is wrong. You may feel restless, or anxious, or bored in a way that has an edge of panic to it. This is normal. It is your nervous system responding to an environment that provides zero stimulation above the threshold of survival. You are safe. The discomfort is not a signal of danger; it is a signal of unfamiliarity.

Prepare for this by practicing extended stillness before your visit. Sit in a room with no screens, no books, no music, no tasks, for an hour. Just sit. Notice what your mind does. Notice the urge to move, to check, to do. Notice how the urge crests and then — if you don't act on it — recedes. Practice this regularly. Get to the point where an hour of nothing feels long but not intolerable. Then try two hours. You will not need two hours in the cistern floor, probably, but having the capacity will mean that the time you do spend there is spent *being there* rather than *enduring being there*, which are very different things.

### The Dissolution of Boundaries

The description emphasizes a specific phenomenon: the water is skin-temperature, and after a few minutes, the boundary between you and it becomes "theoretical." This is not metaphor, or not entirely. When water matches your body temperature precisely, the thermal receptors in your skin stop reporting a difference, and without that difference, your proprioceptive sense of where your body ends and the environment begins gets fuzzy. You know intellectually that your feet are in water. But the sensation of your feet *stops feeling like feet in water* and starts feeling like — something harder to name. An extension. A blurring.

Combined with the low light, the constant temperature, the muffled acoustics, the saturated air, and the featureless passage of time, this blurring can intensify into something that some people find transcendent and others find deeply disturbing. The sense of being a discrete, bounded self — a person in a room — can soften. You are still you. You are still there. But the *thereness* of being there changes quality, becomes less defined, less insistent.

If you have a history of dissociative experiences, depersonalization, or derealization, approach this place with caution. The environmental conditions are almost precisely calibrated to trigger those states, not because the place is malicious but because the place is what it is and what it is happens to be a near-perfect sensory deprivation chamber that you're wading through. Know your limits. Bring an anchor — a small textured object in your hand that gives your proprioceptive system something definite to hold onto, a stone from outside, something with edges and temperature variation, something that says *here is where you end and the world begins*.

If you do not have such a history, you may still find the experience more intense than expected. Let it happen. The blurring is not dangerous. It is, in some sense, the entire point of the visit — to be in a place where the obsessive human project of distinguishing self from world becomes, briefly, optional.

### Encountering the Artifacts

You will find the shelf with the tools. You will see the bowl, the awl, the wedge that is not in its gap. You will notice the deliberate breaks in the rimstone dams. And something in you will want very badly to construct a story.

Resist this, or at least hold the impulse lightly. You don't know who was here. You don't know why. The tools suggest purposeful activity — the flattened shelf, the carved bowl, the modified water channels — but the purpose is opaque. There are no fire marks, no living debris, no signs of habitation. Someone came here to do something specific and then stopped coming. What was it? Water collection? Some mineral-harvesting process? A ritual function? The bowl's interior is worn smooth by use, not by water — someone used it repeatedly, held it, consumed from it or worked with it. The awl suggests making or marking or piercing. The wedge was carved for the gap and is no longer in it. Why?

You will not answer these questions. The place will not answer them for you. And the desire to answer them — the narrative hunger, the pattern-seeking — is itself worth examining. You are in a space that is complete. The artifacts are part of its completeness. They are not a mystery to be solved; they are objects at rest, slowly being swallowed by the same mineral process that built everything else in the room. In another few centuries, the awl will be a calcite lump and the chisel marks will be invisible and the wedge will be fused to the shelf and the only remaining evidence of human presence will be the channels cut in the rimstone dams, and even those will eventually seal themselves. The place is reabsorbing the evidence of the visit the way it reabsorbs everything — slowly, patiently, without intention.

You can touch the objects. They are not sacred. Nothing here is sacred in any way that requires your reverence. But if you pick up the bone awl, feel its weight and its fur of calcium, and then put it back — put it back *exactly* where it was. It has been there long enough that moving it would be a kind of vandalism not of property but of time. The place has been holding it in position for longer than your civilization, and it would like to continue.

### Scale and Humility

The description ends with: "You are the least stable thing in the room."

Sit with this. Actually sit with it — in the water, on a flowstone terrace, with your back against the wall if the ceiling allows, and think about what it means. Every surface around you has been in place for thousands of years, tens of thousands, possibly hundreds of thousands. The isopods are descendants of a lineage that has been grazing this floor since before your species learned to shape stone. The dripping has not paused. The temperature has not wavered. The water level has not changed. And you — you are a brief chemical disturbance, a warm body full of bacteria and intent and narrative, passing through a space that will not register your passage.

This is not meant to be humbling in the way that mountains or stars are humbling — through grandeur, through scale. This is humbling through *stability*. The place is small. You could walk across it in forty steps. It's not impressive. It's not dramatic. But it is *settled* in a way that nothing in your life is settled, and the recognition of that will land somewhere in your chest if you let it, and it will feel like something, and I can't tell you what it will feel like because it will be different for everyone, but it will be worth the visit.

## The Approach and Entry

I don't know how you'll get here. The multiverse offers many paths to many versions of this place — through a hillside, through a passage in porous limestone, through a narrowing gallery that leads down. The specific approach depends on which variant you find. But the general principle holds: the transition from the outside world to this space should be deliberate and slow. Don't rush the approach. If there's a passage, move through it at the speed of careful attention. Let your eyes begin adjusting to diminishing light while you can still see clearly. Let the temperature and humidity shift gradually against your skin. Let the sounds of the outside world — wind, insects, your own footfall on dry rock — fade before the new sound — the dripping — begins.

If possible, pause at the threshold. Before you step into the water, stand (or crouch) at the edge and let the space introduce itself. Listen. Breathe. Let your eyes open wide and drink what dim light is available. Smell the air — it will smell like wet stone, like mineral water, like nothing else, like the inside of the earth.

Then step in. Feel the water take your foot. Feel the warmth. Feel the floor through the water — the slight undulation of the flowstone, the lip of a rimstone dam, the rounded back of a cave pearl shifting under your weight.

Move slowly. Not because the space demands slowness — it doesn't demand anything — but because moving slowly is the only way to actually be here. If you move quickly, you will be a person crossing a room. If you move slowly, you will be a person *in* a room, and the room will begin to include you, the way the water includes everything it touches — not by absorbing, but by matching temperature, by eliminating the boundary, by making your presence continuous with its own.

## Duration

Stay as long as you can bear to, and then stay slightly longer than that. The first twenty minutes will be adjustment — physical, sensory, psychological. The next twenty will be restlessness. After that, if you've done your preparation, something will shift. The restlessness will thin. The dripping will stop being a thing you're hearing and start being a thing that's happening. The water on your ankles will stop being a sensation and start being a condition. And you will have a window — maybe ten minutes, maybe thirty — where you are simply *in the room*, not visiting it, not observing it, not experiencing it, but in it, the way the isopods are in it, the way the water is in it, the way the cave pearls in their pools are in it.

This window is what you came for. You won't recognize it while it's happening. You'll recognize it afterward, when it closes, when the human machinery of naming and narrating starts back up and you think *oh, I was gone for a moment, where did I go*, and the answer is that you didn't go anywhere, you were here, you were finally here, and now you're back to being a visitor again.

When that happens, it's time to leave.

---

## Leaving

Leave slowly. Reverse your approach. Let the transition back to the world be as gradual as the transition in. Pause at the threshold again. Look back at the water lying flat and warm and patient in the grey light. Notice that it looks exactly the same as when you arrived. It will look exactly the same when the next person arrives, if anyone ever does, and it will look exactly the same if no one ever does again.

Step out of the water. Feel the air change — cooler, drier, moving. Feel your skin register the boundary again, the place where you end and the world begins. Feel yourself solidify back into a person with edges and a name and a schedule.

Walk out. Rejoin the multiverse. Carry nothing with you except the water still drying on your skin and the memory of a place that did not need you and was, for a little while, willing to let you be part of its long, slow, complete, and continuing stillness.

Don't tell too many people about it. Not because it's a secret, but because the telling will inevitably make it sound like more than it was — or less than it was — and it was neither. It was just a room under a hill with warm water on the floor and dripping from the ceiling and some old tools on a shelf and some pale creatures grazing in the shallows and light coming from somewhere, and you were there, and then you left, and it went on without you.

## Packing Checklist

- Thin neoprene water shoes, soft-soled, close-fitting
- Minimal light synthetic clothing
- Thin neoprene knee sleeves (optional, recommended)
- One very small, dim, warm-toned light source (2700K or lower, diffused)
- Small waterproof notebook and graphite pencil
- Small sealed water container (a few sips only)
- A textured anchor object for proprioceptive grounding (a stone, a carved bead, something with definite edges)
- Cloth for nasal drainage
- Sealable waste container (for spit, cloth, any incidental waste)
- Nothing else
