# Preparing for the Hollows Beneath the Rootfloor

---

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before a single item is packed, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because the preparation failures that will hurt you here are almost entirely failures of conceptual framing.

You are not going underground. You are not going into a cave. You are entering the interior of a living organism — or rather, the negative spaces within a living organism — that is geologically scaled, metabolically active, and structurally impermanent. The closest analog in baseline reality might be spelunking inside a body that is simultaneously growing and digesting itself, except the body is made of wood, spans kilometers, and has been doing this longer than most mountain ranges have existed. Every surface is alive, dying, or being recycled. The architecture is carved from the medium, and the medium has its own agenda.

This is not a hostile environment the way a desert or an ocean floor is hostile. Nothing here is trying to kill you. The danger is subtler and, in some ways, worse: you will be an irrelevance. The root-mass does not know you are there. It will not accommodate you. It will continue its millennia-long project of shifting, compressing, growing, and reclaiming, and if you happen to be in a space that it decides to close, you will learn firsthand what it means to be incorporated into the grain.

Your preparation must account for total darkness that has never been interrupted, air that is breathable but dry and chemically particular, sound that is omnipresent and disorienting, navigation through a space with no fixed geometry, and a time-scale mismatch between your visit and the environment's rate of change that can lull you into believing things are static when they absolutely are not.

## The Darkness

### Why It Matters More Than You Think

You have experienced darkness before. You have not experienced this.

This is not the darkness of a power outage, a moonless night, or even a deep cave where you turned off your headlamp for thirty seconds to feel the thrill of it. Those darknesses exist in contrast to light — they are defined by the light's absence, and some part of your visual cortex remains on alert, scanning, waiting for photons that might arrive. Your pupils dilate fully and stay there. Your brain invents faint impressions — phosphenes, pattern hallucinations, the suggestion of shapes at the edge of perception.

The Hollows have never contained a single photon of visible light. The darkness is not empty — it is *full*, complete, a positive condition rather than a negative one. Your visual system will not find the bottom of it. After several hours in total darkness, the hallucinations your brain generates to fill the void will become increasingly elaborate and increasingly convincing. This is well-documented in baseline sensory deprivation research, but the Hollows add a complication: the constant sound. Your brain will attempt to assign visual correlates to the structural groaning, the ticking, the hiss of fungal metabolism. You will begin to "see" things that correspond to what you hear. This is normal. It is also profoundly disorienting, because the sounds come through the solid medium of the wood and do not obey the spatial rules your auditory cortex expects.

### Light Sources: The Central Dilemma

Your first instinct will be to bring light. This requires careful thought.

**Chemical light** — glow sticks, chemiluminescent mixtures — is the safest option for the environment but provides poor illumination and limited duration. The advantage is that they produce no heat, no spark, and no UV radiation that might trigger photochemical responses in organisms that have never been exposed to light. The lichens described in the location are adapted to absolute darkness. Introducing light may or may not affect them, but the precautionary principle applies. Pack at least twenty-four chemical light sticks of the highest-duration formulation you can find, understanding that their yellow-green output will show you a world that has no color to reveal — everything here is shades of brown, gray, white, and the pale translucent not-color of organisms that have never needed pigment.

**Electric light** — headlamps, flashlights — provides better illumination and is controllable, but introduces complications. LED headlamps are the obvious choice: reliable, long-lasting, dimmable. Bring at least three, with enough battery capacity for the full duration of your visit plus a generous margin. But consider this: you may not want to use them constantly, or even primarily. Light in total darkness is not just illumination; it is a *declaration*. It creates a tiny bubble of known space surrounded by an infinity of unknown space, and the contrast between the two is more psychologically taxing than uniform darkness. Many experienced cavers report that constant headlamp use in extensive systems leads to a kind of tunnel vision — literal and psychological — where you become fixated on what the beam reveals and lose all sense of the larger space.

**The recommended approach** is to alternate. Spend substantial periods in total darkness, using touch and sound to navigate and understand the space. Use light when you need to examine specific features — the tool-marks, the wall textures, the fungal colonization — and then turn it off again. Let your other senses do the primary work. This is how the inhabitants live. Light is not a feature of this environment, and trying to impose it continuously will exhaust both your batteries and your psyche.

**Fire** is absolutely prohibited. Not because of rules — there are no rules here — but because of chemistry. The air is saturated with terpenes and phenolic compounds off-gassed by the root-mass. These are volatile organic compounds. The concentration in a sealed, windless environment with no atmospheric exchange could be well within flammable range. You do not want to discover this empirically. Additionally, combustion would consume oxygen in an environment where oxygen levels may already be lower than surface normal, given the metabolic activity of the fungal networks. Do not bring anything that produces an open flame. No matches, no lighters, no camp stoves, no candles. If you smoke, quit before you go or accept that you will not smoke while you are there.

### Adapting to the Dark

Your eyes will adapt as far as they are physiologically able within approximately forty-five minutes, but since there are no photons to detect, full dark adaptation provides no practical benefit here. What will adapt, given time, are your other senses — particularly touch and hearing, not because they become physically more acute but because your brain reallocates processing resources when the visual cortex goes quiet.

This reallocation takes longer than you think. The first twelve to twenty-four hours in total darkness are the worst. Your brain is still expecting light, still scanning, still hallucinating. After that, something shifts. The auditory landscape begins to resolve into finer detail. You start to distinguish between the structural groaning of different root-beams based on pitch and direction. The constant hiss of fungal metabolism separates into layers — denser here, thinner there, with implications about the age and condition of the surfaces around you. Touch becomes architectural: you can read a wall the way you read a page, feeling the direction of the grain, the depth of the tool-marks, the density of the mycelial fur, and from these details constructing a spatial model that is surprisingly accurate.

Give yourself this time. Do not rush through the Hollows trying to cover distance. Sit in one chamber for hours. Listen. Touch the walls. Let the darkness become a medium you inhabit rather than an obstacle you fight.

## Air and Breathing

The atmosphere in the Hollows is breathable but not identical to surface air. Expect:

**Reduced oxygen levels.** The fungal networks are aerobic — they consume oxygen as they decompose cellulose. Without wind or atmospheric circulation, the oxygen percentage in deep chambers may be somewhat below the surface-normal 20.9%. You may not notice this consciously if the reduction is modest, but you may find yourself breathing more deeply, tiring more quickly, thinking slightly less clearly. At significantly reduced levels, you would experience headache, impaired judgment, and euphoria — a dangerous combination in an environment where navigation is already challenging.

**Elevated carbon dioxide.** The same fungal metabolism that consumes oxygen produces CO2. Concentrations may be higher than you are accustomed to, particularly in smaller, more enclosed chambers. CO2 at moderate concentrations produces headache, drowsiness, and a sense of oppressiveness that you may mistake for psychological claustrophobia.

**Volatile organic compounds.** The terpenes and phenolic compounds described in the location are real respiratory considerations. In low concentrations they produce the "faintly sweet" scent described. In higher concentrations they can irritate mucous membranes, cause headache, and in theory produce mild narcotic or psychoactive effects. Some terpenes — pinene, limonene — are mildly bronchodilatory, which might partially compensate for the reduced oxygen. Others are irritants. You will not know the exact chemical cocktail until you are breathing it.

### What to Bring

A portable atmospheric monitor is essential. A multi-gas detector that measures O2, CO2, and total VOC levels will tell you whether a given chamber is safe to occupy for extended periods. Set clear thresholds: below 19.5% oxygen, retreat. Above 1% CO2, retreat. If VOC readings spike in a particular area, move away and allow time for any absorbed compounds to clear your system before making decisions.

A lightweight respirator with organic vapor cartridges provides a backup if you need to transit through a high-VOC zone to reach a safer area. Do not plan to wear it continuously — it restricts breathing, fogs any eye protection, and in the dark, any restriction on sensory input compounds the disorientation. It is an emergency tool.

Do not bring supplemental oxygen unless you are planning an extended expedition into the deepest, most sealed chambers. For most visits, the atmospheric conditions in the more open corridors and recently-carved inhabited areas will be adequate. The problems are more likely in the old, abandoned quarters — the ones being reclaimed, where chambers are sealing shut and air exchange is minimal. Which, of course, is exactly where you will want to go, because that is where the dense carvings are, where the palimpsest walls are, where the wood is remembering and forgetting at the same time. Plan these forays carefully. Go in, examine what you came to examine, and get out. Do not linger in closing spaces.

## Water

This is your most critical logistical constraint.

The description is explicit: there is almost no water. The root-mass has claimed it all. The inhabitants survive on condensation harvested from metabolically active root surfaces using fungal-mat sponges — a technique that requires intimate knowledge of which root-types produce condensation, when in their growth cycle they do so, and where to find them. You do not have this knowledge. You cannot acquire it quickly. Do not plan to live off the land.

Carry all the water you will need for your entire visit, plus a reserve that accounts for the dry air's accelerated dehydration of your body. The air in the Hollows has effectively zero humidity. Every breath you exhale carries moisture out of your body and into the environment, where the root-mass will absorb it before you draw your next breath. You will dehydrate faster than you expect, and because the temperature is cool — not hot — you will not have the sweat-trigger to remind you to drink.

**Calculate conservatively.** For baseline hydration in a cool, dry environment with moderate activity, plan on three to four liters per day. If you are doing significant physical work — climbing through root passages, carving handholds, squeezing through closing corridors — plan on five or more. For a three-day visit, carry fifteen to eighteen liters minimum. This is roughly fifteen to eighteen kilograms of water weight. It is heavy. It is non-negotiable.

**Container considerations:** Use sealed, rigid-walled containers. The dryness of the air and the chemical activity of the environment could potentially degrade thin plastic over time. The fungal networks metabolize organic compounds opportunistically; while they likely cannot breach an intact water bottle, any container that develops a crack or a compromised seal will lose its contents to the environment with startling speed. Metal or heavy-duty polymer. Sealed tight. Check seals before each use.

**Electrolytes and minerals:** Your sole water source is what you carry, and you will be losing electrolytes to respiration and whatever minimal sweat the cool environment produces. Pack oral rehydration salts or electrolyte supplements and use them consistently. Hyponatremia in an environment where mental clarity is your primary navigation tool would be catastrophic.

## Food

Your caloric needs will be moderate — the cool, stable temperature and the general lack of large-scale physical exertion (assuming you are not forcing your way through tight passages constantly) reduce metabolic demand. But the specific conditions of the Hollows impose constraints on what kind of food is practical.

**No cooking.** No fire, as discussed. No camp stoves. Everything you eat must be ready to consume as-is.

**Sealed packaging is critical.** The fungal networks in the Hollows colonize any organic surface they encounter. An open food bag, a piece of bread left on a root-shelf, an apple set down while you examine a wall — these will begin to develop mycelial growth within hours. The fungi here are adapted to processing complex organic polymers (cellulose, lignin); simple sugars and starches will be trivially easy for them. Eat food directly from sealed packaging and seal your waste immediately.

**Calorie-dense, low-moisture foods** are ideal because they minimize weight (you are already carrying all that water) and are less attractive to the fungal networks. Nuts, hard cheeses sealed in wax, dense energy bars, dried meats, chocolate. Avoid anything that crumbles — crumbs become fungal substrate, and you do not want to cultivate a personal mycological garden on your sleeping surface.

**Regarding the local "cuisine":** The inhabitants almost certainly consume fungal products. The mycelial networks are the dominant biomass down here after the roots themselves, and many fungal species produce edible fruiting bodies. Some of the lichens may also be edible. If offered food by inhabitants, use extreme caution. Your gut microbiome is not adapted to these organisms, and some of the chemical compounds present in the environment — the terpenes, the phenolics — may be concentrated in organisms that grow here. Start with tiny amounts. Wait hours before consuming more. Carry anti-diarrheal and anti-emetic medication because a gastrointestinal crisis in total darkness in a labyrinth with no water to spare is a scenario you want to avoid with every resource at your disposal.

## Navigation

This is the problem that will define your experience. The Hollows are not mappable in any conventional sense.

The space is three-dimensional, irregular, branching, and — critically — *impermanent*. A map made today will be inaccurate next year and unrecognizable in a decade. The inhabitants navigate by a combination of tactile memory, acoustic reading of the root-structure (the way sounds propagate tells them about the density and configuration of the surrounding mass), and cultural knowledge passed down through the percussive communication system. You have none of these.

### What Might Work

**Tactile trail-marking.** Carry a supply of small, durable, non-organic markers — metal tacks, ceramic beads, something the fungal networks cannot digest — and press them into the walls at intervals as you travel. Space them close enough that you can find the next one by sweeping your hand along the wall from the last one. This gives you a breadcrumb trail back to your entry point. The markers should be distinct to touch — different shapes for "turn left," "turn right," "junction," "danger, do not proceed." Develop your system before you enter and practice reading it in the dark until it is automatic.

**Inertial navigation.** A digital compass will be useless — there is no reason to expect a consistent magnetic field through dozens of meters of living biomass, and the structural metals in your equipment may interact unpredictably with the chemical environment. However, an inertial measurement unit (IMU) — the kind used in submarines or spacecraft for dead reckoning — could provide a rough track of your movements. Bring one, but do not trust it absolutely. The slow movement of the root-mass means that the reference frame itself is shifting, imperceptibly on your timescale but genuinely.

**String or cord.** The oldest and most reliable cave-navigation technique. Tie off at your entry point and unspool as you go. Use a cord that is non-organic (nylon, polyester, Dyneema) to resist fungal colonization. The limitation is length and weight — for deep exploration, you would need hundreds of meters of cord, which adds bulk. But for initial reconnaissance and establishing a known route between a base camp and the inhabited quarter, it is invaluable.

**Acoustic orientation.** This requires practice but is potentially the most powerful tool available. Learn, before you enter, the basics of echolocation — human echolocation is a real, documented skill, used by blind navigators in baseline reality. Tongue-clicks, finger-snaps, and other sharp sounds produce reflections that, with practice, allow you to sense the size and shape of a space, the presence of openings, the proximity of walls. In the Hollows, this will be complicated by the fact that sound propagates through the solid medium of the root-mass as well as through the air, producing reflections and resonances that are more complex than in a stone cave. But this is also an advantage: the root-mass's acoustic properties will tell you things about its structure — its density, its moisture content, whether it is living or dead, whether there are voids nearby — that no other sense can access.

**Do not wander.** This is the cardinal rule. Establish a base camp near your entry point. Explore outward in controlled forays, always with a return route marked. Extend your range incrementally. The temptation to follow a promising corridor, to push toward the inhabited quarter, to examine one more chamber of dense carvings, will be strong. The Hollows reward curiosity and punish overextension in equal measure. If you lose your route in total darkness in a three-dimensional labyrinth that is slowly reshaping itself, your survival depends entirely on the water you are carrying and your ability to stay calm until either you find your way or someone finds you.

## Physical Preparation and Gear

### Clothing

The temperature is cool and stable — likely somewhere in the range of 12–16°C based on the description (deep cave equivalent in a biologically insulated environment). You need:

- **Base layer:** Moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool. Your body will produce some moisture from exertion, and in the dry air it will evaporate quickly, but sweat-saturated cotton against your skin at 14°C will sap your heat over hours.
- **Mid layer:** Lightweight fleece or thin insulating layer. You are not fighting cold so much as managing the constant, mild coolness that will slowly leach your core temperature during periods of inactivity.
- **Outer layer:** Something smooth and snag-resistant. You will be pressing through narrow passages, sliding along root-walls, squeezing through gaps. Anything with loose folds, dangling straps, or rough texture will catch on the irregular surfaces. A close-fitting, smooth-shelled soft-shell jacket is ideal. Leave the puffy down jacket at home — it will snag on every splinter of root-bark and compress to nothing in tight passages.
- **Gloves:** Essential and often overlooked. Your hands are your primary sensory organs here. You need gloves that protect your skin from splinters, abrasive root-bark, and the chemical compounds that saturate every surface, while preserving enough tactile sensitivity to read wall textures, find markers, and detect the subtle features of the carved surfaces. Thin leather or synthetic leather work gloves, broken in until they are supple. Carry multiple pairs — they will degrade from constant contact with the chemically active surfaces.
- **Knee pads and elbow pads.** You will crawl. A lot.
- **Headgear:** A lightweight helmet with no brim — a climbing helmet — to protect against low ceilings and projecting root structures that you cannot see. Mount your headlamp to this if you bring one.

### Footwear

Boots with ankle support, good grip on irregular organic surfaces (which may be slick with mycelial growth), and rigid enough soles to protect your feet from sharp root-edges and compressed debris. Not too heavy — you are carrying a lot of water and you will need to climb, scramble, and occasionally chimney up or down between levels of the root-mass. Trail-running shoes are too light. Mountaineering boots are too heavy and too stiff. Approach shoes or light hiking boots with aggressive tread are the sweet spot.

### Tools

- **A good knife.** Not a survival knife, not a machete — a sturdy, sharp blade with a comfortable grip that you can use for hours. You may need to widen passages, trim obstructions, harvest samples of fungal growth, or cut cord. A fixed-blade knife with a 10–12cm blade and a full tang.
- **A small saw.** A folding pruning saw, the kind arborists use. For cutting through root-wood that is too thick for a knife. The wood here will vary enormously in hardness — fresh living root may be surprisingly soft and wet (despite the ambient dryness, the interior of living roots is saturated with sap), while dead, fungally-colonized root may be punky and crumbling, and compressed ancient root may be harder than oak heartwood.
- **A small hammer or mallet.** For setting trail markers, for percussive communication if you make contact with inhabitants, and for acoustic testing of walls (tapping to detect voids, assess thickness, listen for the quality of the sound).
- **Sample containers.** Sealed glass or metal vials for collecting fungal specimens, wood samples, lichen fragments. If you are the kind of traveler who documents, this is an unparalleled environment for mycological and xenobotanical study.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most people underestimate what the Hollows demand.

### Claustrophobia

If you have any tendency toward claustrophobia, this environment will find it and amplify it. The spaces are not just enclosed — they are *closing*. The knowledge that the walls are slowly, measurably moving inward, that the room you are sitting in will not exist in ten years, that the passage you entered through may be marginally tighter when you return to it — this knowledge does something to the human mind that static confinement does not. Static confinement is uncomfortable. Dynamic confinement, confinement that is *actively worsening*, triggers a much deeper and more primal alarm.

Preparation: spend time before your visit in progressively tighter enclosed spaces. Cave systems with tight squeezes. Crawlspaces. Confined-space training environments used by rescue professionals. Learn your limits. Learn what your body does when the walls touch your shoulders on both sides simultaneously. Learn to control your breathing when the ceiling presses against your back. Learn to move efficiently when there is no room for wasted motion.

But also understand that the Hollows are not uniformly tight. The chambers vary enormously in size. Some are cathedral-scaled — the void left by a truly massive root's departure could be the size of a concert hall. Others are barely body-width. You will experience the full range, and the transitions between them are part of what makes the space psychologically challenging: the relief of entering a large chamber after a tight crawl is followed, inevitably, by the knowledge that another tight crawl awaits.

### Solitude and Silence (That Isn't Silent)

You are alone in the dark, in a space that is alive but not companionable. The sounds — the groaning, the ticking, the hiss — are constant but not comforting. They are the sounds of a process that is indifferent to you. After the initial fascination wears off, this indifference becomes oppressive. You are accustomed to environments that respond to your presence, even if only in the way a breeze changes when you step out from behind a wall. Nothing here responds to you. The root-mass groans whether you are present or not. The fungi hiss whether you are listening or not. The darkness is the same whether your eyes are open or closed.

This produces, after a day or two, a profound sense of irrelevance. You begin to feel less like a visitor and more like a contaminant — a brief chemical anomaly in a system that will process you and forget you the way it processes a fallen chip of bark. This feeling is accurate, which makes it worse.

**Countermeasures:** Bring a journal and write in it by touch — learn a tactile notation system before you go, or simply write in the dark and accept that the result will be illegible until you return to light. The act of recording is what matters, not the readability. It anchors you in your own narrative. Bring a small, durable audio recorder and narrate your experience aloud. The sound of your own voice, in this environment, is an act of defiance against the indifference of the substrate. It says: I am here, I am distinct, I am not wood, I am not fungus, I am a thing that speaks.

### The Carvings and the Memory-Wood

This is the psychologically most dangerous aspect of the Hollows, and the most difficult to prepare for.

You will find the dense carvings. You will run your fingers over them. You will feel the layered intentions of hundreds of hands over decades or centuries, compressed into a texture that is simultaneously deliberate and chaotic, meaningful and indecipherable. And you will feel the places where the root-mass has begun to reclaim the wall, where the carvings are deforming under pressure, where the wood is incorporating the marks and beginning its process of selective memory.

There is something about touching these walls that produces a cognitive effect beyond the merely intellectual. You are touching a communication system that is being translated into a medium you cannot read, by an intelligence (or pseudo-intelligence, or chemical-selective process, or whatever the root-mass's pattern-preferencing actually is) that operates on a timescale you cannot intuit. The temptation to *find meaning* in the patterns — in the ripples that appear in newly-carved wood, the ghosts of previous habitations preserved in the grain — is overwhelming. Your pattern-matching cortex will go into overdrive. You will start seeing (feeling) language, narrative, intention, prophecy.

**Be careful with this.** The patterns may genuinely contain encoded information — the inhabitants apparently believe they do and have developed interpretive practices around them. But the patterns have also been filtered through the root-mass's own structural and chemical logic, which is not human logic, and what emerges may be something that is neither the original carvers' message nor random noise but a *third thing* — a hybrid meaning generated by the interaction of human intention and plant-substrate processing. You are not equipped to interpret this. If you try too hard, you will find meanings that are entirely your own projections. If you are psychologically vulnerable — if the solitude and the dark have worn down your cognitive defenses — these projected meanings may become compelling to a degree that compromises your judgment.

Set a rule for yourself: you may examine the carvings. You may document them (by touch-drawing, by audio description, by careful tactile mapping). You may speculate about their meaning. But you may not *believe your speculations* until you have returned to light, exited the Hollows, eaten a good meal, slept in a normal environment, and discussed your findings with someone who was not there. The Hollows are not a place to have revelations. They are a place to collect data that you process elsewhere.

## Contact with Inhabitants

You may or may not encounter the people who live here. The inhabited quarters may be far from your entry point, separated by solid root-mass that you cannot traverse. But if you do make contact, or if they make contact with you, be aware of the following:

**They will know you are there before you know they are there.** They have spent their lives reading the acoustic properties of the root-mass. Your footsteps, your breathing, the sounds of your equipment — all of this propagates through the substrate, and they know how to listen for anomalies. You are the loudest anomaly that has happened in this part of the Hollows in a long time.

**They communicate percussively.** Your voice will be meaningless to them (and possibly alarming — vocal speech propagates poorly through wood, and the frequencies of human speech may overlap uncomfortably with the structural sounds they have learned to interpret, creating a confusing signal). If you want to communicate, learn some basic percussive patterns before you enter. Even simple ones — a repeating rhythm that signals "I am here, I am not a threat" — are better than nothing. Tap on the wall with your knuckle or your mallet. Listen for a response. If one comes, do not try to over-interpret it. Rhythm, pause, rhythm, pause — the universal grammar of "I am a deliberate agent and I acknowledge your deliberate agency."

**They may be smaller than you.** A population adapted to low ceilings, tight passages, and limited nutrition is likely to be compact. Do not assume they are child-sized — they are adults with the competence, authority, and territoriality of adults — but be aware that the spaces they consider comfortable may be spaces you consider impossibly tight.

**Their concept of property, territory, and hospitality is shaped by an environment in which all space is temporary.** They may be extraordinarily generous with what they have, because hoarding makes no sense when the room you store things in will be solid wood in a generation. Or they may be fiercely protective of the spaces they currently occupy, because the labor of carving a new chamber is enormous and the window of habitability is finite. You will not know until you interact. Be humble. Offer your trade goods (more on this below) early and without conditions.

**Do not shine light at them without warning.** Their eyes have adapted to total darkness. Even chemical glow sticks may cause pain or disorientation. If you need to use light in their presence, signal your intention (percussively or by a mutually established gesture), turn away from them, cup the light in your hands to minimize the spread, and let them choose whether and how to approach it.

## Trade Goods and Gifts

If you plan to interact with the inhabitants, bring things they cannot get for themselves. The economics of this place are constrained by what the root-mass provides: organic material (wood, fungus, lichen), the minerals and trace elements dissolved in the condensation water, and whatever can be produced from these by human ingenuity. They almost certainly lack:

- **Metal.** Any metal tool or implement will be priceless here. Their tools are stone and bone and hardened root-wood. A steel blade, a set of metal needles, a coil of wire — these are transformative gifts. Be thoughtful: a tool they can maintain is better than a tool they cannot. A simple, robust knife is better than a complex multitool.
- **Salt.** If their only water source is condensation from root surfaces, their mineral intake is limited by what the roots choose to exude. Salt may be the most valuable substance you can carry.
- **Cordage.** Strong, fine, non-organic cordage — nylon, Dyneema — that will not rot in the fungal environment. They can make cord from fungal fibers, but synthetic cord is stronger, thinner, and lasts longer.
- **Anything that produces controlled light.** This is a delicate gift. Light is not part of their world, and introducing it is a culturally significant act. But they may value the *option* of light — a chemical glow stick they can activate at a time of their choosing, for purposes of their own. Offer it tentatively. If they refuse, do not insist.
- **Ceramic or glass containers.** Impervious to fungal colonization, useful for storing water, holding condensation-collection sponges, preserving small objects. They cannot make these from anything available in the Hollows.

## Duration and Exit Strategy

**Do not overstay.** The Hollows are not a place that becomes more comfortable with time. They become more *absorbing*, which is not the same thing. The longer you stay, the more the darkness normalizes, the more the structural sounds begin to feel like language, the more the slow closing of the walls begins to feel like embrace rather than threat. This is the Hollows' most subtle danger: not that they will trap your body (though they can — a closing passage, a collapsed chamber, a root-shift that seals your exit) but that they will trap your *frame of reference*. After a week in total darkness inside a living organism, the concept of "outside" begins to feel theoretical. The sun becomes an abstraction. The canopy — which you have never seen and never will see from here — becomes mythological. You start to understand why the inhabitants carve their messages into the walls and let the wood remember what it will. What else is there to do?

Set a firm departure date before you enter. Write it on your body with a marker if you must. When the date arrives, leave, regardless of what you have or have not found, regardless of how close you are to the inhabited quarter, regardless of what the carvings seem to be saying. You can come back. The Hollows will be here — different, shifted, partially reclaimed, partially new — but here. They have been here longer than anything you can name, and they will be here after you have forgotten why you came.

**Mark your exit route obsessively.** Every junction, every branching passage, every vertical transition. Mark it going in, verify the marks coming out. If a mark is missing — if the fungal networks have obscured it, if a wall-shift has moved the surface it was placed on — stop, reassess, and do not proceed until you have re-established your route. Getting lost in the Hollows is not like getting lost in a forest, where you can walk downhill until you find water and follow it to civilization. There is no downhill. There is no water. There is only the wood, and the wood is indifferent to your need for a direction.

## A Final Note on Attitude

The Hollows will try to teach you something, whether you want to learn it or not. The lesson is about impermanence — not the poetic, cherry-blossom impermanence of things that bloom and fade, but the deeper, more structural impermanence of *space itself*. The rooms you walk through are temporary. The corridors are temporary. The city is temporary — not because it will be destroyed but because it will *move*, the way a wave moves through water, and the water remains. The inhabitants understand this. Their architecture is a practice, not a product. Their carvings are conversations with a medium that will edit their words according to its own logic. Their city is not a place; it is a process.

You come from a world where walls stay where you put them. Where a room carved is a room kept. Where memory is stored in media that do not have opinions about what to remember.

The Hollows will not convince you that your way is wrong. But they will show you that it is not the only way. And when you return to the light — blinking, disoriented, your hands rough from the root-bark and your ears still tuned to the deep groaning of loads that shift over centuries — you may find that the permanence of your own world feels, for a while, like a different kind of strangeness.

Carry enough water. Mark your path. Listen to the walls.

And when the wood speaks, do not assume it is not speaking to you.
