# Preparing for the Pump Station at Depth

---

## Understanding What You Are Going To

Before anything else, you need to sit with a fundamental problem: you are preparing to visit a place that does not want you, does not need you, and has no concept of a visitor. This is not hostile territory — hostility implies awareness. This is indifferent territory, and the indifference is so total that it constitutes a kind of environment in itself. You will be forty meters below a cold northern sea, in a place where the only purposeful activity is the movement of fluid through a pipe, managed by machines that have no sensory apparatus oriented toward anything but their own operational parameters. You are not expected. You are not a variable the system accounts for.

This changes how you prepare. You are not preparing the way you would for a wilderness — there is no ecosystem to leave intact, no trail to follow, no summit. You are not preparing for a ruin or an abandoned place — the station is active, functional, maintained on a schedule. You are not preparing for danger exactly, though you could die here easily enough. You are preparing to exist, for a short time, in a place where your existence is completely extraneous to everything happening around you. This is a surprisingly difficult thing to prepare for, and most of the preparation is not physical.

---

## The Multiverse Consideration

As a multiverse traveler, you must consider which variant of this place you are entering. The description is specific enough — a subsea pump station on a pipeline at forty meters in a cold northern sea — that it narrows the range, but not to a single point. Across the accessible branches, the differences may be trivial: the pipeline carries gas condensate instead of crude, the station was installed in 1998 instead of 2003, the zinc anodes are slightly larger. Or the differences may be significant in ways that matter to your survival: the anti-fouling coating uses a compound that is mildly neurotoxic to surface-dwelling primates, the water temperature is two degrees colder (which at this depth and exposure time changes your thermal budget meaningfully), the pipeline suffered a micro-fracture six months ago and there is a faint plume of hydrocarbons leaching from a flange joint on the station's northwest face.

You cannot know in advance. What you can do is prepare for the envelope — the range of conditions that a place like this represents across likely branches — and then make your final assessments on arrival. This is standard practice for multiverse travel, but it bears repeating because the blandness of this destination may lull you into thinking there is nothing to assess. There is. The blandness is, in a way, the assessment. You are trying to confirm that the place you have arrived in is as featureless and inert as it should be, and any deviation from that baseline is information.

## Physical Preparation

### Diving Competence

You need to be a competent diver. Not an extraordinary one. Forty meters is within the range of recreational deep diving on air, though it is at the boundary, and most responsible training agencies would tell you that forty meters on air is the absolute limit and not a place to linger. You will be narced. Nitrogen narcosis at forty meters is not theoretical — it is happening to you, metabolically, the entire time you are at depth. Its effects vary by individual and by day: a slight euphoria, a slowing of thought, a detachment that feels like clarity but is its opposite. On a good day it is manageable. On a bad day it will make you do something stupid, and at forty meters in a cold sea next to an industrial installation, stupid is a very short walk from dead.

If you are diving on air, you should have logged at least fifty dives, with at least ten below thirty meters, before attempting this. You should be comfortable — genuinely comfortable, not merely certified — with deep stops, gas management, and the particular quality of mental discipline required to monitor yourself for narcosis while narced. You should know what your narcosis feels like, which means you should have experienced it in controlled settings and learned to recognize its onset in your own cognition.

The better option is to dive on a nitrox mix or a trimix that reduces or eliminates the nitrogen loading at depth. A normoxic trimix — something like 21/35 (21% oxygen, 35% helium, balance nitrogen) — would give you a clear head at forty meters and extend your bottom time meaningfully. This requires additional training and equipment. The helium will make you cold faster, which matters here. You are trading one problem for another, and you need to decide which trade you prefer.

Regardless of breathing gas, your bottom time is limited. On air at forty meters, you have perhaps ten to twelve minutes before mandatory decompression stops become necessary. On trimix you have more, but not unlimited time. Plan your dive. Know your turnaround pressure. Know your ascent rate. Know your stop depths and durations. Write them on a slate and clip the slate to your harness. You will think you can remember them. At forty meters, you may not be able to remember them. The slate is not optional.

### Thermal Protection

The water is cold. The description says "cold northern sea," which in the branches where this kind of installation typically exists means the North Sea, the Norwegian Sea, or something comparable — water temperatures at forty meters between 4°C and 8°C depending on season, with limited seasonal variation at depth. You will be in this water for the duration of your dive, including decompression stops on ascent. Total immersion time could be thirty to sixty minutes.

A wetsuit is inadequate. I want to be clear about this because many divers, especially those who have mostly dived in temperate water, will tell you that a thick wetsuit — 7mm, maybe a semi-dry — is fine for forty meters in cold water for a short bottom time. And they are correct in the narrow sense that you will probably survive. But you will be cold. You will be cold in a way that erodes your decision-making, your dexterity, and your ability to enjoy or even perceive anything about the place you have come to visit. The cold will become the dominant experience. It will crowd everything else out. You will spend your bottom time thinking about how cold you are and how much you want to ascend, and you will miss the pump station entirely.

Wear a drysuit. A neoprene drysuit or a trilaminate shell suit with appropriate undergarments. The undergarment matters more than the suit — choose a weight appropriate for the water temperature and your personal metabolism. Err on the side of warmth. You can vent excess heat; you cannot generate it at depth. Dry gloves, not wet gloves. A hood that seals properly. Boot-integrated suit or separate drysuit boots with gaiters.

If you are diving trimix, the helium will conduct heat away from your body faster than nitrogen would. Increase your thermal protection accordingly. Some divers in this scenario use argon inflation in the suit instead of air, since argon is a better insulator. This adds another gas system to manage. Decide if the complexity is worth the warmth. In water this cold, it probably is.

### Equipment Configuration

Your equipment should be clean, simple, and redundant where it matters. A backmount twin-cylinder configuration with an isolation manifold, or a single cylinder with a pony bottle as a bailout. Stage bottles if you are using trimix and need a different gas for decompression. A primary and backup light — not because it is dark at forty meters (it is not dark, exactly) but because the light quality is so flat and low-contrast that a focused beam becomes essential for seeing detail on the station's surfaces. Without a light, everything will look the same shade of blue-grey and you will not be able to distinguish the zinc anodes from the valve housings from the patches of algae. You will see the station as a shape, not as an object.

Carry a reel and a surface marker buoy. Carry a cutting device. Carry a slate and a pencil. Carry a compass — the featurelessness of the seabed means that if you lose the pipeline, you lose your reference. The pipeline is your navigation. Follow it to the station. Follow it back. Do not leave it.

Do not carry unnecessary equipment. Every additional item is something that can snag on the station's flanges and valve spurs, which protrude from the housing at various angles. Industrial subsea installations are not designed with diver navigation in mind. They are designed for ROV access, where snagging is a minor inconvenience resolved by reversing a thruster. For you, a snagged hose or reel line on a flange at forty meters in cold water with narcosis building is a potentially fatal entanglement. Streamline everything. Clip everything close. Move slowly near the structure.

## Navigating the Approach

You will descend — from a boat, presumably, anchored or holding station above the pipeline's known coordinates. The descent is through forty meters of water that the description tells you is "slightly turbid." This means you will not see the bottom from the surface. You will descend through a featureless blue-green column of water that brightens above you and darkens below you, and at some point — perhaps at thirty meters, perhaps at thirty-five — the bottom will appear, and it will appear all at once, a flat grey plain resolving out of nothing, and you will feel a strange sense of arrival that is disproportionate to what you have arrived at.

The silt is your first concern. It is "fine grey silt, packed firm," but packed firm is a relative term. Your fin kicks near the bottom will lift it. Once lifted, it hangs in the water and does not settle quickly — at this depth, with minimal current, suspended sediment can reduce visibility to zero in seconds and take minutes or hours to clear. Move with small, precise fin strokes. Use a frog kick or a modified flutter that directs thrust backward and upward, away from the bottom. Do not touch the bottom. Do not kneel on the bottom. Do not set anything on the bottom. The silt is patient and firm until you disturb it, and then it is a blinding cloud that erases your entire visual field. This is not theoretical. This is the single most common way divers get into trouble in environments like this one.

Find the pipeline. It should be within your descent area if your surface coordinates were good. If it is not immediately visible, navigate in expanding squares at a constant depth until you find it. The pipeline is a meter in diameter and slightly raised above the silt on its berms — it is the most prominent feature in a landscape with no other features. You will find it.

Follow it. The pumping station is on it. You will know the direction from your briefing — northwest to southeast, station at a known point. If you do not know the direction, pick one and follow the pipe. If you reach the station, good. If you reach the limit of your planned bottom time without finding it, turn around and go back. Do not extend your dive to search. The station is not going anywhere. You can come back.

When the station appears — and it will appear suddenly, the way everything appears at twelve meters of visibility, with no warning, fully formed — you will have a moment of cognitive adjustment. The thing you have been told about, the thing you have imagined, the thing you have traveled across branches to visit, is simply there, sitting on the silt, a yellow-brown steel box with pipes coming out of it. It is smaller than you expected. Or perhaps larger. It is hard to say, because there is nothing to compare it to, no human-scale reference except yourself, and at forty meters with narcosis pulling at the edges of your perception, your own scale is not entirely reliable.

## What You Will Experience

### The Hum

Get close. Within a meter of the housing. Place your hand on it if the anti-fouling coating permits — check your gloves afterward for any residue or irritation, because you do not know exactly what compound was used in this branch, and some anti-fouling agents are not things you want on your skin.

You will feel the hum. It will come through your hand and into your arm and you will feel it in your teeth. It is not a dramatic vibration — it is a low, steady, purposeful tremor, the kind of vibration that speaks of rotational machinery operating within normal parameters. It is the most alive thing at this depth. It is, in fact, the only thing at this depth that is *doing* something, and the contrast between its purposeful activity and the total inertia of everything around it is one of the central experiences of this visit.

Hold still. Listen with your body. The hum is the sound of a turbine on a platform kilometers away, burning gas that was pulled from beneath the seafloor, converting it to electricity, sending that electricity down a cable buried in the silt beneath your fins, arriving here as rotational force in a motor you cannot see, pushing fluid through a pipe, maintaining pressure across distance. You are touching the outer surface of an industrial process that stretches across hundreds of kilometers of infrastructure, and this is the one point where you can feel it. Everywhere else, the process is invisible — buried cables, enclosed pipes, distant platforms. Here, under your hand, you can feel the machine thinking. Not thinking. Working. Doing the one thing it was made to do.

Step back three meters. The hum is gone. The silence is absolute. This transition — from faint mechanical presence to total absence in three steps — is the most disorienting thing about this place. Not frightening. Disorienting. The silence at five meters from the station is so complete that it retroactively makes you doubt you felt anything at all. You will want to go back and touch the housing again to confirm. This is normal. Go back. Touch it. Confirm. The hum is there. Step away. It is gone. The place exists in two states — the station's immediate skin of vibration, and everything else's absolute stillness — and the boundary between them is sharp enough to feel like a threshold.

### The Light

Look up. The surface is forty meters above you, and on a bright day you will see it as a faint, diffuse brightening of the water column overhead, without detail, without texture, just a slightly lighter ceiling to the world. It does not look like the surface. It does not look like a place you could go. It looks abstract, like a concept — the idea of "up" made visible as a gradient.

Look down. The silt. Look at it carefully. Under your dive light it may show more detail than the ambient light reveals — faint ripple marks from some ancient or very occasional current, tiny burrow holes from organisms too small or too hidden to be visible, the texture of the sediment itself, which up close is not uniform grey but a mosaic of mineral grains in subtly different shades. Or it may show nothing. It may be exactly as featureless up close as it is from a distance. Both outcomes are consistent with this place.

Turn off your light. Let your eyes adjust. The ambient light at forty meters is enough to see by, but only just, and only in the blue-grey register that the water permits. Under this light, the station looks less like an industrial object and more like a geological one — a dark, angular outcrop that happens to have geometric edges. The yellow paint is invisible in this light; it reads as dark brown or dark olive. The pipeline is a ridge in the silt. The visual compression — the way everything flattens into the same narrow tonal band — is a real perceptual phenomenon, and it does something to your sense of depth and distance. Things that are five meters away look like they could be fifteen. The edge of visibility looks like it could be the edge of the world. In a sense, here, it is.

### The Absence

You will notice the absence of life. The description told you about it, so you are expecting it, but expectation does not prepare you for the experience of looking at a hard-surfaced structure that has been sitting in the sea for years and seeing nothing living on it. In any other marine environment — tropical, temperate, even cold — a structure like this would be colonized. Hard substrate in the ocean is rare and valuable, and marine organisms find it and grow on it with an eagerness that makes artificial reefs out of shipwrecks in a matter of months. Here, nothing. A few thin patches of brown algae on the pipe, and that is all.

Swim around the station. Check every face. Look at the flanges and the valve housings, the places where geometry creates sheltered nooks — under any other circumstance, these would be prime real estate for encrusting organisms, for small fish seeking shelter, for the whole cascade of life that builds itself around structure in the ocean. There is nothing. The zinc anodes, half-dissolved, sit in their brackets with surfaces that are clean in a way that submerged metal is almost never clean. No biofilm. No fouling. Just the slow electrochemical dissolution of zinc in seawater, converting one metal into ions to protect another metal from the same fate.

This absence will affect you. It is not dramatic — there is no corpse, no evidence of catastrophe, no sign that life was here and left. It is simply that life never arrived, or arrived and found nothing worth staying for, and the result is a silence that is not just acoustic but biological. The station exists in a zone of non-life, and as you float beside it, you are the only living thing within your field of vision in every direction. You may have experienced this before in other environments — deep caves, certain desert landscapes, sterile volcanic terrain. But those places do not hum. Those places are not actively doing something. The combination of active machinery and absent life is specific to places like this, and it creates a feeling that is difficult to name. It is not loneliness. It is something more like irrelevance. You are alive, and this place does not care, not because it is hostile to life but because life is simply not a category it operates in.

### The Time

You will lose track of time. Not in the figurative sense of being so absorbed that hours pass like minutes — you do not have hours; you have ten or fifteen minutes of bottom time, and your computer or your dive plan will enforce this. You will lose track of time in the perceptual sense. The light does not change. The hum does not change. The silt does not move. Nothing happens, and in the absence of happening, your internal clock — which relies on change, on event, on contrast to establish duration — will malfunction. A minute at the station will feel like five minutes or like thirty seconds, and you will not be able to tell which. Check your computer. Check it often. This is not a place to lose track of minutes, because at forty meters, minutes are the unit of currency that keeps you alive.

## The Ascent and After

When your plan tells you to go, go. Do not linger. The station will not reward lingering because it does not change with extended observation. What you see in the first two minutes is what you would see in two hours, because nothing here is in the process of doing anything visible. The changes that are occurring — the silt creeping over the mattress edge, the anodes dissolving, the algae growing in imperceptible increments — happen on timescales of months and years. You cannot observe them. You can only know they are occurring and hold that knowledge in your mind as you look at something that appears perfectly, immutably still.

Ascend along the pipeline if possible — it gives you a visual reference for your depth, and in the featureless water column above the seabed, a visual reference prevents the vertigo that can come from ascending through empty blue with no orientation cues. Do your safety stops. Breathe. Decompress. Watch the light change above you as you rise through the water column — this is the reverse of your descent, but experienced differently because now you are coming from somewhere, and the somewhere you are coming from was a very specific kind of nowhere.

When you surface, the world will seem noisy. Not because the surface is particularly loud — it may be a quiet day, a calm sea — but because your auditory system has just spent thirty minutes in a silence so total that even the faint hiss of your regulator was the loudest thing in the environment. The sound of waves, of wind, of a boat engine, will seem almost aggressive. Your eyes will ache in the brightness. Not painfully, but noticeably — the full spectrum of surface light after the compressed blue-grey of forty meters is a sensory expansion that feels physical, like stepping from a small room into an open field.

You will want to talk about what you saw. This is where you will encounter a problem, because what you saw was a steel box on a grey seabed in blue water, and when you describe it, the person listening will wait for the rest of the story, and there is no rest of the story. The pump station at depth is exactly and only what it is, and the experience of visiting it is the experience of being in the presence of something that is exactly and only what it is, and that turns out to be a surprisingly profound thing to experience and a nearly impossible thing to convey.

## What to Bring Back

Nothing physical. There is nothing to take and you should not take anything — the station is a maintained industrial asset and interfering with it, even trivially, could trigger a maintenance response that costs someone a great deal of money and accomplishes nothing.

Bring back the hum. The memory of it in your hand. Bring back the transition from hum to silence in three steps. Bring back the grey silt and the absence of life and the way the pipeline vanished into twelve meters of visibility like a sentence that just stops. Bring back the knowledge that somewhere, right now, in whatever branch you visited, the station is still there, still running, still humming at a frequency that nothing can hear, pushing fluid through a pipe in the dark, doing its work with a patience that is not patience because patience implies waiting, and the station is not waiting for anything. It is just running. It will run until it is told to stop or until it breaks, and either of those events is years away and managed by a spreadsheet in an office building that you will never visit in a city you may never see.

Bring back the equanimity of the place. That is what it offers, if it offers anything: a model of existence in which purpose and function are so perfectly aligned that there is no residue, no excess, no longing. The pump station does not aspire. It does not decay romantically. It does not symbolize anything. It is infrastructure, performing infrastructure's fundamental act of being invisible and essential and utterly without narrative, and you went forty meters below a cold sea to see it, and you saw it, and it was there, and now you are here, and it is still there, and that is the whole of it.

## Packing List

- Drysuit, properly fitted, recently serviced, with appropriate thermal undergarments
- Dry gloves with ring system
- Hood, 7mm or thicker
- Twin cylinders with isolation manifold, or primary cylinder with bailout pony
- Trimix fills if certified and equipped; nitrox at minimum for decompression
- Primary regulator, backup regulator on long hose
- Dive computer with gas integration, programmed for planned mix(es)
- Slate and pencil with dive plan written out
- Primary light, canister or handheld, 1000+ lumens
- Backup light, compact, clipped to harness
- Reel, 50m minimum, with surface marker buoy
- Compass
- Cutting tool, shears or line cutter, accessible with either hand
- Jon line or current hook if surface conditions require
- Adequate surface support: boat, crew, emergency oxygen, first aid, communication

And patience. Not the station's kind — it doesn't have a kind, it just runs — but yours. The human kind, which is harder, because it requires choosing to be still in a place that gives you nothing for your stillness, and finding that the nothing was, after all, enough.
