# Preparing for the Drowning Vaults

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a ruin. You are not exploring a wreck. You are entering a living structure at the bottom of a warm, dark sea — a place that was never meant for air-breathers, never designed for light, never built with the assumption that someone like you would come. The Drowning Vaults are not hostile, but they are profoundly *indifferent* to your presence. Everything there — the architecture, the water chemistry, the creatures, the slow geological metabolism of the place — operates on timescales and sensory registers that have nothing to do with you. Your preparation needs to account for this indifference, because it is the defining feature of the environment. Nothing there will try to kill you. Nothing there will try to help you. You are a temporary warm body in a place that thinks in millennia.

## Physical Preparation

### Pressure and Depth Acclimation

Two hundred meters. This is the number that should organize your physical preparation before all others.

At two hundred meters of seawater, the ambient pressure is approximately twenty-one atmospheres. This is well beyond recreational diving limits. It is beyond most technical diving limits. On Earth, this is the domain of saturation divers, deep commercial operations, and specialized military diving — all of which require either rigid atmospheric suits or carefully managed breathing gas mixtures and extended decompression protocols that can take days.

You need to determine which approach to take, and the description of the Vaults strongly favors one over the other.

**Option one: Atmospheric diving suit (ADS) or rigid submersible.** This keeps you at surface pressure inside a sealed shell. You bring your own atmosphere. The water never touches you. This is the safe option, the sane option, and it is almost certainly the wrong one for this place. The Vaults are described as intimate. Ceilings you can reach up and touch. Walls that are close. Oval apertures sized for eels. A rigid suit — the kind rated for two hundred meters — is bulky, articulated with rotary joints, and about as graceful as a refrigerator. You will not fit through the passages. You will not be able to trail your fingers along the carved basalt. You will not feel the water, its warmth, its mineral character. You will be sealed away from the very thing you came to experience. An ADS turns you into a tourist pressing your face against glass.

**Option two: Open-water deep diving with breathing gas management.** This means you are *in* the water. The water is against your skin, or at least against your exposure suit. You breathe a gas mixture — almost certainly a helium-oxygen blend (heliox) or a trimix — through a demand regulator or rebreather. You are subject to the pressure. You must manage nitrogen narcosis (eliminated by replacing nitrogen with helium), oxygen toxicity (managed by keeping the oxygen partial pressure below 1.4 ATA, ideally lower), and high-pressure nervous syndrome (HPNS, a helium-related tremor and neurological effect that begins around 150-200 meters). And critically, you must manage decompression on the way back up. A dive to two hundred meters, depending on bottom time, can require *hours* of staged decompression stops. For extended stays, you would need to be in saturation — living at pressure for the duration, breathing the appropriate gas mixture continuously, and then undergoing a single long decompression at the end.

This is the approach the Vaults demand. Not because it's safer — it isn't — but because the place is built for direct contact. The description emphasizes touch: fingers tracing obsidian inlays, hands running along walls, the brush of a shrimp's antennae on your knuckle. The Vaults are experienced *through the water*, not despite it. Your preparation should therefore be oriented toward enabling the deepest possible immersion (in every sense) while keeping you alive.

**What this means practically:**

Begin training in technical diving if you haven't already. You need certifications and experience through progressively deeper depths — 40 meters, 60, 100, eventually working toward the 150-200 meter range. This is not something you rush. It takes months to years. Your body needs to learn how it responds to helium at depth, how narcosis feels at various gas mixes, how to manage the slow discipline of decompression stops when every instinct tells you to ascend.

Train specifically with **closed-circuit rebreathers (CCR)**. A rebreather recycles your exhaled gas, scrubbing carbon dioxide and adding oxygen and diluent as needed. The advantages for the Vaults are enormous: dramatically extended bottom time compared to open-circuit, near-silent operation (no bubble noise to disturb the creatures or the silence), and efficient gas usage. The Vaults are quiet. Profoundly quiet. The loudest sound is a crab's leg clicking on stone. Open-circuit scuba would fill every chamber with the roar and bubble-cascade of your exhalations. A rebreather lets you exist there without sonically announcing yourself every four seconds.

Get a **dive medical evaluation** that goes beyond the standard recreational screening. At two hundred meters, latent cardiac issues, undiagnosed patent foramen ovale (a small hole between the heart's chambers that can cause arterial gas embolism during decompression), or pulmonary vulnerabilities become potentially fatal. You need echocardiography, pulmonary function testing, and a physician experienced in hyperbaric medicine to clear you.

**Saturation diving infrastructure.** If you plan to spend more than a brief visit — and the Vaults seem like a place that reveals itself slowly, over days, not hours — you will need a saturation system. This means a pressurized habitat, likely positioned on the seamount plateau near the Vaults, where you live at ambient pressure (21 ATA) breathing the appropriate heliox mixture. You dive from the habitat to the Vaults and back, never changing pressure, never accumulating decompression obligation beyond what you'll manage at the end. When you're finished — after days, or weeks — you undergo a controlled decompression in the habitat, ascending in pressure over the course of roughly a week, the helium slowly washing out of your tissues while you sit in a metal chamber and wait.

This is not comfortable. Your voice will sound absurd on heliox — squeaky, cartoonish, a helium-distorted parody of speech that can be corrected somewhat with an electronic unscrambler but never fully. The habitat will be small, humid, and monotonous. You will lose track of day and night because there is no light at two hundred meters and you will be living on artificial schedules. But this is how you earn *time* in the Vaults. Not minutes. Days. Enough time for the crabs to stop noticing you. Enough time for the eels to begin threading past your body as though you were another column.

### Thermal Considerations

The water is described as warm — "the temperature of a drawn bath slowly cooling." This is remarkable at two hundred meters of depth and is entirely a product of the geothermal system. Estimate somewhere between 25-35°C (77-95°F), likely varying by chamber, warmer near the central plumbing and cooler at the periphery.

This is actually a significant gift. Deep water on Earth is typically near freezing. The warmth of the Vaults means you don't need the heavy-insulated drysuits or heated undergarments that deep divers normally depend on. A **thin wetsuit or even a dive skin** may suffice for thermal protection, which dramatically improves your tactile sensitivity — important in a place where touch is the primary sense.

However, prolonged immersion in warm water presents its own problems. **Hyperthermia** is a real concern during saturation diving in warm water. Your body struggles to shed heat when surrounded by water near body temperature, especially when breathing dense gas mixtures that affect thermoregulation. Monitor your core temperature. Have cooling protocols available in the habitat. Hydrate aggressively — warm-water diving is dehydrating, counterintuitively, and dehydration is one of the leading risk factors for decompression sickness.

The mineral content of the water also warrants attention. The description mentions sulfurous warmth. Geothermally heated seawater near volcanic seamounts is often enriched with dissolved minerals — hydrogen sulfide, silica, various metal sulfides, elevated CO2. Some of these are toxic. **Hydrogen sulfide** in particular is dangerous even in low concentrations. You need to test the water chemistry before prolonged exposure. Your skin will be immersed in this for days if you're in saturation. Mineral-rich geothermal water can cause skin irritation, eye inflammation, and — if the concentrations are high enough — systemic toxicity through mucous membrane absorption. Bring water sampling equipment and chemical test kits. Bring topical barrier creams. Bring eye protection — a full-face mask rather than a standard mask-and-regulator setup, keeping the mineral water off your eyes and nasal passages.

### Physical Fitness and Movement Training

The water resists haste. The description makes this a physical law of the place. The warm, mineral-dense water is likely slightly more viscous and dense than standard seawater, and at twenty-one atmospheres, the breathing gas is dense enough that respiratory work increases significantly. You will not be able to move quickly even if you want to. Train for slow, controlled movement. Practice low-exertion finning techniques. Practice being still — truly still, neutrally buoyant, breathing slowly, hanging in the water like a piece of the architecture.

This is also a yoga-and-meditation consideration disguised as a diving one. The Vaults demand stillness. If you are someone who fidgets, who needs to be in motion, who gets anxious in dark confined spaces, you need to address that before you arrive. Not suppress it. Address it. Meditation practice. Sensory deprivation float tanks. Time spent in dark, confined, warm water on the surface until your nervous system learns that this is not a threat, that darkness and enclosure and warmth can be a cradle instead of a coffin.

## Sensory Preparation

### Navigating in Total Darkness

The Vaults are dark. Not dim. Not murky. *Dark*. Two hundred meters deep on a seamount, with no bioluminescence to speak of — only the faintest amber-red mineral glow in the central chamber, which the description says is not enough to see by. You will be functionally blind for most of your time there.

You have choices to make about light, and they are not simple.

**Bringing artificial light** is the obvious instinct and potentially the worst decision. Consider: every creature in the Vaults is adapted to total darkness. The tube worms, the crabs, the eels, the ray — they navigate by pressure, by water chemistry, by touch, by senses you don't have names for. Introducing a dive light — even a dim one — is the equivalent of setting off a flashlight in a cave full of animals that have never seen the sun. You don't know what it will do. It might send the eels into panicked flight, crashing through apertures. It might cause the tube worms to retract permanently, disrupting their feeding. It might attract creatures from the cold water outside the thermal island — things that respond to light from the deep, which are often predators, and which might not belong in the delicate ecology of the Vaults. The mineralized crabs on the columns, the slow-breathing ray, the pale translucent shrimp — you have no idea what photoreceptor remnants they retain, vestigial or otherwise, or what a sudden blast of photons would do to their behavior or physiology.

**My recommendation: learn to operate without light.**

This requires serious preparation. Spend time — weeks, if possible — practicing all diving skills blindfolded or in zero-visibility conditions. Learn to navigate by touch. Learn to read architecture with your hands — the direction of carved channels, the pattern of apertures in walls, the texture differences between basalt and obsidian and pumice. Map spaces tactilely. Practice fin-tip contact with floors, fingertip trailing along walls, the slow methodical sweep of a hand ahead of you before moving your body.

Bring a light source as **emergency backup only** — a small, red-filtered, low-intensity light clipped to your harness that you reach for only if you are lost, disoriented, or in a safety emergency. Red light is less disruptive to dark-adapted organisms than white light, though even this is a guess for creatures that may have no evolutionary relationship with surface-dwelling light responses.

Better still, consider **infrared or thermal imaging**. If the Vaults' creatures don't see visible light, they likely have no sensitivity to infrared. A helmet-mounted thermal camera feeding to a heads-up display could give you a ghostly, heat-map view of the chambers — the warm geothermal channels, the body heat of the ray, the slightly cooler periphery — without emitting any visible photons into the environment. The resolution won't be good. It won't let you read chisel marks. But it will let you orient yourself, avoid collisions, and observe the animals without disturbing them. Pack this.

### Sound Discipline

The loudest sound in the Vaults is a crab's leg clicking on stone. Your breathing, your heartbeat, the creak of your equipment, the whir of your rebreather's solenoid valve — these are all cacophonous by comparison. At depth, sound travels faster and farther through the dense medium. Every sound you make will propagate through all connected chambers.

**Minimize your acoustic footprint.** Choose equipment for silence. Insulate metal-on-metal contact points with neoprene or rubber. Breathe slowly and evenly. Move without letting your fins or equipment contact the stone. The animals here navigate partly by pressure waves and the "taste" of the water; they almost certainly navigate by sound as well, using the subtle acoustics of the chambers the way bats use echolocation in reverse — listening to the passive sounds of their environment to build a spatial map. Your noise could blind them, functionally. Be quiet the way you would be still: not as a constraint, but as a gift to the place.

### Touch as Primary Sense

You will experience the Vaults primarily through your skin. This is unusual for a sighted, hearing-dependent species, and you should train for it.

Wear the thinnest hand covering you can manage — or none at all, if the water chemistry permits. Bring **thin, flexible gloves** made of a material that transmits texture: something like nitrile surgical gloves underneath thin neoprene, offering chemical protection without deadening sensation. Or custom-fitted dive gloves with reinforced palms but bare fingertips — some free-diving and archaeological diving operations use these.

Practice haptic mapping on the surface. Get architectural stone samples — basalt, obsidian, pumice — and learn their textures blind. Learn to feel the difference between hand-worked stone and natural fracture. Learn to feel chisel marks, joint seams, the subtle proud standing of a harder inlay against a softer matrix. This will transform your experience from groping in the dark to *reading* the Vaults like braille.

### Chemoreception: The Water's Taste

The description mentions sulfurous water. You will taste and smell this through your full-face mask or around your regulator mouthpiece. Even through a mask, gas at twenty-one atmospheres will carry trace volatiles from the water. The mineral character of the Vaults' water is information — the animals navigate partly by "tasting" the water, and you can develop a crude version of this sense.

Before the dive, sample the water at various points around the seamount. Catalog the mineral signature. Note variations — the central chamber's geothermal upwelling will taste different from the peripheral chambers. Over days of saturation diving, you may begin to unconsciously associate certain water flavors with certain locations. This is not mysticism. This is your chemosensory system doing what it evolved to do, given enough exposure and enough necessity.

## Equipment

### The Essentials

- **Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR):** Fully redundant, tested to 250 meters. Carry bailout gas in small independent cylinders — enough for an emergency ascent to the saturation habitat, not enough for a surface ascent (which would kill you from decompression sickness anyway). Prefer a unit with an electronically quiet solenoid — some rebreathers click audibly when adding oxygen. Research the quietest models available.

- **Full-face mask with integrated communications:** You need to be able to talk to your support team in the habitat. The comms will sound absurd through heliox. Use an electronic voice unscrambler. Also: the full-face mask keeps mineral water away from your eyes, nose, and mouth.

- **Thermal imaging system:** Helmet-mounted IR camera with a small, low-power heads-up display inside the mask. Adjustable gain. The ability to turn it off entirely so you can experience the true darkness when you choose.

- **Tactile mapping tools:** Waterproof slate and stylus for making tactile sketches — raised-line drawings you can feel with your fingers later, since you can't see them. Alternatively, a voice recorder integrated into your comms system, so you can narrate what you're feeling as you explore.

- **Water chemistry sampling equipment:** Small collection vials, test strips for H2S, pH, dissolved minerals. A compact underwater spectrometer if your multiverse technology permits.

- **Navigation line and markers:** A thin guide line — Kevlar or equivalent, neutrally buoyant — to run from the habitat to the Vaults' entrance and through the chambers you explore. Clip-on tactile markers (different shapes for different junctions) so you can navigate the line by touch. This is your lifeline. In total darkness, in a multi-chambered stone structure underwater, disorientation is not a possibility but a certainty without reference.

- **Minimal fin profile:** Short, low-profile fins. The chambers have low ceilings and close walls. Standard long blade fins will crack against stone, generate noise, disturb sediment (if any), and potentially damage the architecture or its inhabitants. Freediving fins or modified force fins — something compact with good control at low speed.

- **Exposure suit:** Thin wetsuit, 2-3mm neoprene, with high-tactile gloves. You're in warm water; you don't need thermal protection so much as chemical protection and mechanical protection against the rough volcanic stone. Consider adding thin knee and elbow pads — you will be crawling through apertures.

### Things to Leave Behind

- **Cameras with lights or flash.** No.
- **Collecting jars or specimen tools.** Do not take anything from the Vaults. Not a stone chip, not a shell fragment, not a mineralized crab. The place is a system in equilibrium. The dead crabs becoming columns becoming substrate for living crabs — this is a cycle. Interrupting it removes something that will not be replaced on any timescale meaningful to you.
- **Excess gear.** Every piece of equipment is volume and noise and snagging potential. Strip down to the minimum. If it doesn't keep you alive or help you perceive, leave it in the habitat.
- **Expectations of speed.** Leave your schedule. Leave your itinerary. The Vaults will reveal themselves at the pace of warm water and slow crabs, or not at all.

## Psychological and Philosophical Preparation

This section is not optional. It is arguably more important than the equipment list.

### Confronting Deep Time

The Vaults are ancient in a way that erodes human psychological frameworks. The description mentions geological layers, erosion measured in epochs, repair work that is itself ancient. When you touch the walls, you are touching stone that was worked by hands — *something's* hands — so long ago that the marks have been nearly dissolved by warm water. And then other hands came, and repaired, and those repairs are also ancient.

You need to prepare for the emotional experience of deep time. This is not an intellectual exercise. It is a felt experience — the tactile knowledge, transmitted through your fingertips on softened chisel marks, that something was here before you and will be here after you and does not care about your presence because your entire life is a rounding error in its calendar.

Some people find this comforting. Some find it annihilating. Most find it both, in alternating waves.

Before you go, spend time with old things. Visit geological formations. Hold fossils. Sit with a stone that you know is a billion years old and try to *feel* the time in it. Read about deep time until the numbers stop being numbers and start being a sensation — a kind of vertigo, but downward through time instead of space. Get comfortable with that vertigo, or at least familiar with it, because in the Vaults it will come for you. When your hand finds a column where the chisel marks are ghosts and the crabs have become stone and the stone has become crabs, and you realize that this has been happening for longer than your species has existed — that vertigo will arrive. Better to have met it before.

### Confronting the Dark

Not the dark of a power outage. Not the dark of a closet. The dark of two hundred meters of water over your head, stone on all sides, and no light source within reach. This is an ancient darkness, a fundamental one — the default state of most of the universe. Light is the anomaly. The Vaults know this.

If you have any unresolved relationship with darkness — fear, anxiety, the reflexive clutching need for illumination — resolve it now. Sensory deprivation training. Extended time in complete darkness. Learn to let your other senses take over. Learn the specific flavor of panic that comes when your visual cortex has nothing to process and starts generating its own signals — phantom lights, pattern hallucinations, the sense that something is *right there* — and learn to let those signals pass through you without action. They are your brain complaining about unemployment. They are not real. In the Vaults, what is real is what you can touch, hear, taste, and feel through the water's pressure on your skin.

### Confronting the Builders

You will be in a structure made by hands. The description is careful about this: "hands or something like hands." Bone chisels. Obsidian tools. Stone fitted without mortar, underwater, in total darkness, by beings who understood geological erosion well enough to overbuild for it.

You will not meet the builders. Or if you do, you won't recognize them. They may be the crabs. They may be something the crabs evolved from. They may be something entirely separate that visits periodically — "when the time is right" — with their tools and their patient knowledge. The description suggests ongoing maintenance over immense timescales, which implies either an extraordinarily long-lived species or an extraordinarily stable culture, or both, or something else you can't easily categorize.

Prepare for the possibility that the builders are present and you don't know it. Prepare for the possibility that the builders are the animals — that the distinction between "builder" and "inhabitant" is one the Vaults dissolved long ago, the way they dissolve everything, slowly, over time. One of those dinner-plate crabs picking its way along the obsidian inlays might be a maintenance worker. The eels threading through the apertures might be following paths laid out by their ancestors who were also the architects. You don't know. You can't know. Sit with that.

Also prepare for the possibility that the builders will become aware of you. Not with alarm — nothing in the Vaults is alarmed by anything — but with the slow, patient attention of beings who have all the time in the world. A crab that sits motionless on a column while you pass might be watching you the way you watch it. The behavioral difference between a crab that is ignoring you and a crab that is studying you may be indistinguishable to your senses.

Bring humility. Not as a moral stance but as a survival strategy. You are in someone's home. You are in someone's *temple*, possibly — the central chamber with its columns and channels has the feel of a place with purpose beyond the utilitarian, though you can't be sure, and you shouldn't project your own sacred geometries onto an alien architecture. But treat it as you would a sacred space of a culture you don't understand: with deference, with care, with the assumption that everything is more meaningful than you think.

### Confronting the Slow

The greatest psychological challenge of the Vaults may be the pace. Everything is slow. The creatures are slow. The water resists haste. The architecture reveals itself slowly, through touch, over repeated visits. The erosion is slow. The repair is slow. Life and death and mineralization are slow.

You are not slow. You are a mammal. You breathe quickly, think quickly, move quickly, lose interest quickly. The Vaults will bore you before they fascinate you, unless you prepare for the transition.

Practice patience as a skill, not a virtue. Meditate, but specifically practice *open-awareness* meditation — the kind where you sit with whatever arises without grasping at it or pushing it away. This is the Vaults' native mode of consciousness. Things arrive. Things pass. A crab moves. An eel threads through a wall. The water shifts. Nothing is urgent. Nothing demands immediate attention. Everything is worth attending to, but only if you can attend at the speed it offers itself.

I would suggest spending at least a month before your visit practicing extended stillness. Sit in one place for an hour. Then two. Then four. Notice what happens when your mind runs out of entertainment and starts to actually *perceive* — the grain of the wood under your hand, the temperature gradient across your skin, the nearly inaudible sounds your environment makes when you finally stop making yours. This is the perceptual state the Vaults require. You cannot rush your way to it.

## Logistics and Planning

### Duration

Plan for at least **two weeks in saturation** to justify the decompression cost. A single brief dive to two hundred meters — down and back — would give you perhaps thirty minutes of bottom time and then hours of decompression, all for a fleeting, overwhelming, poorly understood experience. Saturation lets you live at depth. It lets you make repeated excursions into the Vaults, learning the layout over days, returning to the same chambers, noting changes (how the crabs have repositioned, whether the ray has moved, the state of the tube worm colonies). The Vaults will not make sense on a single visit. They barely make sense on a dozen visits. Give yourself time.

Your decompression at the end will take **five to seven days** in the habitat, gradually reducing pressure. Bring books. Bring music. Bring patience. The decompression itself is a kind of final lesson from the Vaults: you cannot leave quickly. You must leave slowly, the helium easing out of your tissues the way the mineral water eases the sharpness out of the stone, grain by grain, no shortcuts.

### Support Team

You cannot do this alone. You need a surface support team managing your gas supply, monitoring your decompression schedule, maintaining the habitat, and prepared for medical emergencies. At minimum: a dive supervisor, a life support technician for the saturation system, a hyperbaric medical officer, and a standby diver (also in saturation) in case you need rescue inside the Vaults.

The standby diver should be briefed on the same darkness protocols, silence protocols, and non-disturbance policies you follow. An emergency rescue conducted with blazing lights and panicked movement could damage the Vault ecosystem in ways that take centuries to heal.

### Access and Approach

The seamount's plateau drops away steeply into cold abyssal water on all sides. Your approach to the Vaults' exterior will likely be a descent from a surface vessel, down a shot line or submersible elevator to the habitat positioned on the plateau. From the habitat, you swim to the Vaults — presumably a short distance, since you'll want the habitat close but not so close that its noise and light and thermal output disturb the environment.

Scout the exterior before entering. The outer walls of the Vaults should be documented — their condition, the locations of apertures, any external fauna. Determine the primary entrance. There may be more than one; the description suggests chambers connecting in all directions. Choose an entry point and commit to it initially. Run your guideline from the habitat to this entry point. Do not explore additional entries until you have thoroughly mapped the first accessible chambers.

### Ethics and Non-Interference

I want to be explicit about this because multiverse travelers sometimes carry a collector's mentality, a sense that the extraordinary places they visit are experiences to be harvested.

The Drowning Vaults are not yours. They are not for you. Your presence is tolerated by the environment, not welcomed by it. The creatures will not flee from you, but neither do they consent to your observation. The builders — wherever and whatever they are — did not extend an invitation.

**Do not:**
- Remove any material from the Vaults, including loose stone, biological material, or water samples taken from inside the structure (sample from outside, away from the thermal island).
- Touch the tube worm colonies. Their mineral sheaths are probably fragile, and their alcoves are clearly designated spaces.
- Interfere with the crabs, including the mineralized ones. You don't know which are alive, which are dead, and which are in some intermediate state that your biology doesn't have a category for.
- Block the apertures. The eels' circulation through the walls may serve ecological functions you don't understand — distributing nutrients, carrying chemical signals, maintaining water flow.
- Disturb the central chamber's geothermal channels. This is the heart of the system. The heated water distribution is what keeps the entire thermal island alive. Dislodging a stone, shifting a column, or obstructing a channel could alter flow patterns with cascading effects.
- Leave anything behind. No markers, no plaques, no flags, no memorial stones, no "I was here." The Vaults do not need your contribution to their architecture. Your memorial would be absorbed into the stone eventually anyway, becoming just another thing the mineral water rounds and softens, but in the meantime it would be debris.

**Do:**
- Move slowly.
- Be quiet.
- Touch gently.
- Stay a long time.
- Pay attention.
- Leave the way you came.

## What to Expect Emotionally

The first few hours will be disorienting. The darkness, the pressure, the warmth, the confinement, the silence — all at once. You will want your light. You will want to go back to the habitat. Your lungs will feel the density of the gas and your brain will interpret it as difficulty breathing, even though you're getting enough oxygen. This will pass.

By the second or third day, the Vaults will start to become navigable. You'll recognize chambers by their acoustic signatures — the way sound moves differently in a tall narrow room versus a broad low one. You'll learn where the crabs congregate by the faint clicking. You'll learn the tube worm wall by its feathered texture under your fingers. You'll learn the central chamber by the warmth intensifying, by the subtle current of the geothermal channels against your skin, by the barely-there amber glow that your dark-adapted eyes will eventually, tentatively, begin to resolve.

By the end of the first week, you may begin to feel something that I can only describe as *phase-locking* — your internal rhythms starting to synchronize with the rhythms of the Vaults. Your breathing will slow. Your movements will become unconsciously economical. You will find yourself pausing, motionless, for minutes at a time without deciding to pause. The patience that the water imposes will begin to feel less like imposition and more like release. You are being freed from urgency. The Vaults have no use for urgency, and after long enough, neither do you.

By the end of the second week, you may not want to leave.

This is the most important thing to prepare for: **the difficulty of leaving.** Not because the Vaults trap you. Not because anything prevents your departure. But because the world above — with its light and noise and speed and surfaces — will feel, after two weeks in the slow dark warmth, like an assault. The decompression days in the habitat will serve as a buffer, a gradual re-introduction to the sensory intensity of the surface world. But even so, you will step out of the decompression chamber and into daylight and feel as though you've been struck. Everything will be too bright, too fast, too loud, too dry. The air on your skin will feel thin and inadequate. You will miss the pressure. You will miss the warmth. You will miss the dark.

This fades. It takes a few days, maybe a week. You recalibrate. The surface world stops feeling like an attack and starts feeling like home again.

But you will carry the Vaults with you. In quiet moments. In dark rooms. When you close your eyes in a warm bath and hold still and listen for something you can't quite hear — the tick of a crab's leg on ancient stone, the whisper of a ray's fin-edge dislodging dust, the slow pulse of a place that has been breathing since before you were born and will continue breathing long after you are gone.

*Tik.*

The vaults hold.
