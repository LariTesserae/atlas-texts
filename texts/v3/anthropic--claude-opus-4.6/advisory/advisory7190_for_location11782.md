# Preparing for the Flooded Works

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this: you are preparing to visit a place that is still deciding what it is. This is not a ruin. This is not a functioning facility. This is not a natural environment. It is all of these things in a state of unresolved superposition, and the rules governing it — physical, biological, categorical — are not yet fixed. They are *curing*. You are entering wet concrete, metaphysically speaking, and you need to understand that your preparation can only go so far because some of what you'll encounter there literally does not have stable properties yet.

This means your preparation must be fundamentally adaptive rather than prescriptive. You are not packing for a known climate. You are building a kit and a mindset that can remain functional as the definition of "functional" shifts underneath you.

---

## The Darkness: Recalibrating Perception Before You Go

### Do Not Bring Artificial Light

This is the first and most critical instruction, and it will feel deeply counterintuitive. The Flooded Works is not merely dark — it is occupied by something that is *incompatible* with light. The description is precise: this is not absence but presence. Photons may not cohere here. Electromagnetic radiation in the visible spectrum may behave in ways that are nonsensical or, worse, adversarial to whatever substrate has replaced illumination as the primary medium of spatial information.

Bringing a flashlight, a headlamp, a torch, a chemlight, a bioluminescent organism, or any photon-emitting device is not just useless — it may be actively dangerous. You don't know what happens when you try to force light into a space that has replaced light with something else. At minimum, it probably won't work. At worst, you may be introducing a contradiction into a system of rules that hasn't finished setting, and contradictions in unstable physics can cascade. Think of it like introducing an incompatible solvent into a curing resin. You might get nothing. You might get a local failure in the rule-structure. You don't want to be standing in the middle of that.

Instead, you need to prepare your non-visual senses to carry the full load of perception. The good news, per the description, is that the Works *supports* this. The space is fully perceivable — it simply refuses to be perceived visually. You will develop a kind of proprioceptive-echolocative-thermoreceptive awareness that tells you where columns are, where organisms are, where the membrane is overhead. But this sense takes time to come online, and the transition period — when you've lost vision and haven't yet gained its replacement — will be the most psychologically dangerous part of the visit.

### Sensory Training (Pre-Visit, 3-6 Weeks Minimum)

**Blindfold habituation.** Spend increasing periods — start with an hour, work up to full days — navigating familiar environments blindfolded. Not with the goal of memorizing layouts, but with the goal of learning to *trust* non-visual information. You need to reach the point where the absence of sight triggers curiosity rather than panic. Your hands, your skin, your ears, your kinesthetic sense — they all already provide rich spatial data. You've just never had to rely on them as primary inputs.

**Echolocation practice.** Humans can echolocate. It's a learnable skill. Practice making soft clicking sounds with your tongue and listening for the returns off walls, furniture, doorways. You won't become expert in six weeks, but you can reach basic proficiency — enough to detect a metal column at two meters, a wall at five. In the Flooded Works, with its kilometers of hard reflective surfaces in wet air, echolocation will likely be remarkably effective. The acoustics are practically designed for it: still air, no wind noise, metal surfaces everywhere, water providing a reflective floor. Clicks and tongue-pops will paint you a usable picture.

**Thermal awareness.** Practice detecting heat sources with your eyes closed. The running machines are warmer than the still ones. The organisms have their own thermal signatures. In a place where everything is slightly warmer than body temperature, even small differentials will become meaningful. Train yourself to notice the feeling of radiant heat on your skin from different directions. Stand near a warm wall with your eyes closed and learn what that warmth feels like as spatial information.

**Tactile mapping.** Practice building mental models of spaces through touch alone. Enter an unfamiliar room blindfolded and map it by hand. Learn to translate the texture, temperature, and geometry of surfaces into a coherent spatial model. In the Works, you'll be touching metal, biological membranes, organisms, substrate, and things that blur the boundaries between all of these. You need your hands to be your primary analytical instruments.

**Vestibular calibration.** The water's behavior — maintaining constant depth relative to your body regardless of terrain — is going to confuse your vestibular system profoundly. Your inner ear will tell you you're walking uphill or downhill. The water will tell you you're on a level plane. Practice navigating uneven terrain in water (a rocky streambed, a pool with an irregular bottom) while blindfolded, focusing on trusting your feet and balance over your assumptions about what "level" means. This won't fully prepare you for water that ignores gravity, but it will make the dissonance less destabilizing.

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing and Equipment

**Go minimal on clothing, but not unclothed.** The environment is warm and saturated with moisture. Heavy clothing will become waterlogged, heavy, and will impede the tactile sensitivity you desperately need. But going bare-skinned exposes you fully to organisms that will treat your body as terrain — the crustacean-like creatures will walk across you, the soft-bodied things in the water will brush against you, the membrane might respond to your skin differently than to fabric. You want a thin barrier that still allows thermal and pressure information through.

Recommended: a thin, close-fitting synthetic base layer — something like a lightweight diving skin or compression suit. It should cover your torso and limbs but leave your hands and feet bare or minimally covered. The suit serves three purposes: it reduces the area of direct skin contact with unknown organisms (some of which are slowly digestive — you don't want something encysing a patch of your skin over hours without you noticing), it provides a consistent tactile reference (you'll always know what *your own clothing* feels like versus what *something else* feels like), and it dries quickly if the water's anomalous properties fail to keep you dry in all circumstances.

**Footwear is a difficult choice.** You need to feel the substrate through your feet — the sharp-edged ridges, the depressions, the quality of the ground. Thick boots will numb this critical channel of information. But the substrate has features described as "sharp-edged and unfractured," meaning fresh mineral formations that could cut bare feet. Compromise: thin-soled water shoes or toe shoes with minimal padding. Enough to prevent lacerations, thin enough to feel the topography. Consider also that the organisms on submerged surfaces will accumulate on your footwear; periodically stop and clear them by feel.

**Gloves: carry them, don't wear them by default.** Your hands are your eyes here. Wearing gloves continuously would be like visiting a lit environment with sunglasses so dark you can barely see. Keep thin, tactile gloves (nitrile or thin neoprene) in a sealed pouch for situations where you need to handle something you don't trust — the objects in the three-walled building, for instance, or if you encounter biological material that feels aggressive or irritant. Otherwise, bare hands.

**No pack if possible; belt pouches and body-mounted storage.** A backpack will snag on low beams, interfere with your spatial awareness of what's behind you, and shift your center of gravity in an environment where balance is already compromised by anomalous water behavior. Use a belt or harness system with small, sealed pouches distributed around your body. Everything should be secured so it can't fall into the water and be lost — remember, you cannot see to search for dropped items.

### What to Carry

- **Tactile recording materials.** You will want to document what you find. You obviously can't photograph or write in the dark. Carry a set of wax tablets or clay blanks and a stylus — something you can inscribe by feel. Develop a personal tactile shorthand before you go: symbols you can carve quickly that represent common features (column, organism, membrane, machine running, machine still, substrate ridge, objects found). Also carry a small analog audio recorder — something simple, mechanical if possible, with a physical button you can find by touch. Speak your observations. The recorder will also capture ambient sounds that may be analytically valuable later: the machine hums, the animal clicks, the tonal shifts.

- **A sounding line.** A thin, strong cord, marked at regular intervals with knots you can count by feel, weighted at one end. Use it to measure distances, depths, heights. Lower it from platforms. Extend it between columns. The water's anomalous depth-maintenance makes conventional depth measurement unreliable, but the line can still tell you about the *geometry* of the substrate beneath the water, which varies even if the water level doesn't. It also serves as a lifeline: tie one end to a known structure and extend from there so you can always return to your last known point.

- **Navigation cord.** A large spool of thin, high-tensile cord. Pay it out behind you as you move, tying it off at structures. This is your primary navigation aid. In total darkness, in a space that extends for kilometers, with repeating structural patterns, you *will* become disoriented without a physical trace of your path. The cord is your way back. Carry at least a kilometer. Two if you can manage the weight.

- **Sealed rations and water.** Yes, there is water everywhere, but you should not drink it. It is not entirely a liquid. It has properties that are still resolving. Ingesting something whose physical rules are in flux is an unacceptable risk — you do not know what it will do inside your body, where the rules may be different. Carry sealed water and calorie-dense food in waterproof containers. Enough for 72 hours. This is both your sustenance supply and your hard time limit: when your consumables are half gone, you turn back, regardless of what you've found. This is non-negotiable.

- **A small, sealed sample kit.** If you are scientifically inclined or if your traversal has research objectives, carry a few watertight containers for samples. Water. Substrate scrapings. Biological specimens if you can collect them without injuring what you're collecting (the ethics here are genuinely unclear — is the membrane an organism? A colony? A machine component? Can you take a piece of it without causing harm to something you don't understand?). Handle all samples with gloves and seal them immediately. Do not assume that samples will retain their properties outside the Works — they may, or the local physics may not travel. Either result is informative.

- **Medical basics.** Wound care, primarily. Cuts from substrate edges, abrasion from industrial surfaces, potential reactions to organism contact. Antihistamines and anti-inflammatory medication in case of biological sensitivity. A broad-spectrum antimicrobial — not because the organisms are necessarily pathogenic, but because an ecology with no history of human contact has no track record of compatibility with human biology.

- **No electronics that require light-based outputs.** No screens. No LED indicators. No fiber-optic anything. As discussed, photon emission may be problematic here. If you carry any electronic devices, they should have purely tactile or audio outputs. A vibrating compass (one that pulses directionally against your skin) would be ideal if such a thing exists in your source reality. Otherwise, rely on the cord, the counting of knots, and your own spatial memory.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most travelers will fail, and it's where the most careful preparation is needed.

### The Darkness Will Try to Become Everything

You have lived your entire life with vision as your dominant sense. It accounts for roughly 80% of the information your brain processes about the external world. You are about to lose it completely, not temporarily (like closing your eyes, where you know light is waiting) but categorically — you are entering a space where the *concept* may not apply. This is different from blindness. A blind person in a lit room is in a room that could theoretically be seen. You will be in a place that *cannot be seen*. There is no "if only I could see" here. There is no "see."

This will trigger grief. Real, physiological grief, the same cascade that activates when you lose a capacity you relied on. Your brain will cycle through denial (there must be light somewhere, I just haven't found it), bargaining (if I move to a different area, there might be light), anger (this is wrong, this place is broken, I need to leave), and eventually either depression or acceptance. You need to reach acceptance as quickly as possible, because depression in this environment is dangerous — it will make you passive, and passivity in a landscape that extends for kilometers, with limited supplies, is lethal.

**Pre-visit practice with extended darkness** (48 hours minimum in a fully darkened space, with someone monitoring you from outside) will compress this grief cycle so that when you enter the Works, you've already processed the loss and can move directly to functional adaptation. Do this at least twice before your visit.

### The Blurring Will Try to Include You

The description emphasizes repeatedly that categories are softening here: liquid/gas, built/grown, machine/organism, submerged/not-submerged. You are a creature made of categories. You have a body that is distinct from its environment. You have an inside and an outside. You are alive in a way that is different from how a machine runs. These distinctions may not be as firm here as you're used to.

The water that was at your calves and then held your hand and then let it go dry — it is *negotiating* with you. The membrane that changed texture at your touch was *responding* to you. The organisms treat you as terrain. The longer you stay, the more the environment will incorporate you into its schema, the same way it incorporates pipes into root systems and cables into tissue nodes. You may begin to feel that the boundary between yourself and the water, between yourself and the warm air, between yourself and the hum, is less distinct than it was when you arrived. This is not a hallucination. It may be an accurate perception of something that is actually happening at whatever level the local physics operates.

**Set firm identity anchors before you go.** Carry a small object with strong personal significance — something you've owned for years, something whose every scratch and contour you know by touch. When you feel the boundaries softening, hold it. Name yourself, aloud, in the quiet. Say where you came from. Say what year it is where you come from. Say what you intend to do after you leave. Make these statements concrete and specific. You are not rejecting the Works or its rules. You are simply reasserting that you are a visitor, not a component. The distinction matters.

**Set a time limit and honor it absolutely.** Seventy-two hours maximum, forty-eight hours recommended, twenty-four hours for a first visit. The curing physics, the category-softening, the biological integration — these are slow processes. The description emphasizes glacial timescales: digestion over days, growth over years. You are probably safe for a day. You are likely safe for two. Beyond three days, you are in territory where the slow processes may have begun to include you in ways that are difficult to reverse. Get in, observe, get out.

### The Purpose-Without-Operators Will Unsettle You

The machines are running. They have been running since they were built. No one built them, or whoever built them is gone, or the concept of "builder" and "built" has blurred the same way everything else has. The machines do not need you. The ecosystem does not need you. The place is fully operational and fully indifferent to your presence.

This is psychologically jarring in a way that's hard to articulate until you experience it. Humans are accustomed to environments that are either natural (and therefore purposeless) or artificial (and therefore built for someone, usually us). The Flooded Works is artificial and purposeful and *does not include you in its purpose*. It is busy. It is working. It is not working *for* you or *at* you or *despite* you. You are irrelevant to its function. This feels like being invisible in a crowd, except the crowd is made of pipes and membranes and crustaceans and warm water, and it has been carrying on without witnesses for however long this place has existed, and it will carry on after you leave, and nothing you do here will matter to it.

Some travelers find this liberating. Most find it profoundly alienating. Prepare for the alienation. Know that it will pass, or at least become manageable, and that the place's indifference is not hostility. You are not unwelcome. You are simply unaccounted-for. There's a difference.

## Navigation and Mapping

### Establishing a Base Point

When you arrive (by whatever mechanism your traversal uses), immediately anchor yourself. Tie your navigation cord to the nearest structure. Spend your first thirty minutes stationary: crouching, feeling the substrate, the water, the nearest columns, learning the immediate cluster. Count columns by touch. Note their diameters. Note the presence or absence of hum. Note the density of organisms. Build a tactile model of your arrival cluster.

Then mark it. Scratch a distinctive pattern into one column — something you'll recognize by touch from any approach angle. This is Home. Everything radiates from here.

### Grid Exploration

Move outward from Home in systematic directions, paying out cord. Explore one direction for a set cord-length (say, 200 meters), then return along the cord to Home. Then explore a different direction. Build a radial map. At each significant feature — a different cluster, the three-walled building, a region of denser organism activity, a machine with a different hum frequency — stop, anchor a secondary cord, and explore locally before returning to the main line.

Record everything tactilely and verbally. Knot patterns in the cord at features. Speak observations into your recorder. You are mapping a space that may be hundreds of square kilometers; you will cover only a tiny fraction of it. Focus on quality of observation over quantity of territory.

### The Water Problem

Navigation by substrate topography is unreliable because the water masks it. You cannot feel whether you're walking uphill or downhill by water level — it's always the same. You must rely on your vestibular sense (which will tell you about slope even if the water doesn't) and on the substrate texture under your feet. Learn to read the ground: sharp ridges suggest recent formation, smoother areas suggest older substrate or biological reworking. The ground itself is a text; your feet are the reader.

Be aware that the water's reference-frame behavior (adjusting to *you* rather than to gravity) means that if two travelers enter together, they may experience different water levels, or the water may behave paradoxically where their reference frames overlap. If you are entering with companions, discuss this possibility in advance and establish protocols for what to do if the local physics generates contradictions between your individual experiences. (My recommendation: maintain at least ten meters of separation and compare observations verbally. Do not attempt to synchronize experiences by standing in the same water at the same time. The rules are still curing. Don't stress-test them.)

## Interaction Protocols

### With the Organisms

**Do not harm them.** This is partly ethical — they are living things going about their lives in their own environment — and partly practical. You do not know what role any given organism plays in the local ecology-infrastructure hybrid. The crustacean on your hand might be maintaining the column it was climbing. The membrane overhead might be part of the atmospheric regulation system. The soft-bodied things in the water might be processing chemical byproducts that would otherwise accumulate to dangerous levels. Everything here may be load-bearing. Don't remove supports you can't see.

**Allow them to interact with you on their terms.** The crustacean walked across your hand. Let it. The soft things brush your calves. Let them. The membrane responded to your touch. Touch it gently, briefly, and then let it be. You are gathering information through these interactions, and so are they. Mutual observation is appropriate. Interference is not.

**Monitor your own skin after organism contact.** Feel for changes in texture, temperature, sensitivity. If an organism seems to be settling on you (not just crossing you but *staying*), gently encourage it to move to a nearby structure. If something has begun to encyst or adhere, address it immediately — gently, without crushing or tearing. This is the early stage of the integration process that has fused biology and infrastructure throughout the Works. It is not malicious. It is simply what happens here. You are choosing not to participate.

### With the Machines

**Do not attempt to operate, modify, or interfere with the machinery.** You found no control panels, no switches, no interfaces. This is informative. Either the machines are autonomous, or their interfaces are not perceptible to you, or the concept of "operation" as a human-initiated activity doesn't apply here. In any case, you are not a qualified operator for machinery whose purpose, design principles, and underlying physics you don't understand. Observe. Record. Do not touch moving parts.

**Pay attention to the hum.** The tonal shift that silenced the entire ecosystem is significant. The machines and the organisms are in communication, or at least in resonance. The hum is information. If you have any musical training, try to identify intervals and frequencies. Record them. Note which clusters hum at which pitches. Note correlations between hum characteristics and organism density, substrate type, water behavior, or any other variable you can detect. This may be the closest thing to a "language" or "readout" the Works provides.

**If a machine near you shifts pitch, go still.** The organisms did. Follow their lead. They have been here longer than you and they understand the signaling. When the ecosystem pauses, it is listening. You should listen too. Resume movement only after the organisms do.

### With the Large Thing

Something heavy is moving through the shallows at distance, displacing water along a three-to-four-meter front. You will sense it as a pressure wave. It does not approach; it does not recede. It is on its own route.

**Do not seek it out.** You do not know what it is. It may be an organism, a mobile machine, or a category-blurred hybrid of both. It is large enough to displace water significantly, which means it is large enough to be dangerous to you through sheer mass even if it has no aggressive intent. Its indifference to you is a gift. Accept it. Observe from distance. Record the pressure waves, their frequency, their direction.

If the pressure waves begin to strengthen — if the large thing changes course toward you — move laterally to its apparent path, not away from it. Lateral movement removes you from its route without triggering pursuit dynamics (if it has any). Put structures between yourself and it. Stay near running machinery, where the hum may mask your presence or signal your location as "occupied infrastructure."

## Contingency Planning

### If You Become Disoriented

Stop. Do not move. Feel for your navigation cord. If you can find it, follow it back to the last knot or anchor point. If you cannot find the cord, stay where you are, anchor yourself to the nearest structure, and systematically search a small radius by feel. Speak aloud: the acoustics here are excellent, and your voice reflecting off structures may help you build a spatial model of your immediate area. If you have entered with companions, call out and listen for responses.

Do not wander in search of the cord or of Home. The space extends for kilometers. Wandering without a tether in total darkness is how people are lost permanently.

### If the Physics Shifts

The rules are curing. They may shift during your visit. The water may change behavior. The darkness may deepen or change quality. Spatial relationships may flex. If you notice a change — if the water starts flowing, if you sense something that might be light, if the substrate changes abruptly, if the hum takes on a character you haven't heard before — stop, observe, and assess. Is the change local or widespread? Is it progressive or sudden? Does the ecosystem react?

If the change feels destabilizing — if you can no longer trust the ground, if the water begins behaving unpredictably, if your non-visual spatial sense falters — begin returning to your entry point immediately. Do not investigate. A phase transition in local physics is not something you can navigate safely, and the entry point is your only known exit. Get to it while your navigation system still works.

### If You Feel Yourself Changing

This is the most important contingency. The boundary-softening, the category-blurring — if you begin to feel it in yourself, if the line between your body and the water or the air or the hum starts to feel less definite, if you realize you've been standing still for a long time and it felt like moments, if you touch a structure and feel reluctant to pull your hand away because the metal feels like it's becoming continuous with your palm:

**Leave. Now.**

Grip your identity anchor. Speak your name, your origin, your intention to leave. Follow the cord. Move deliberately and continuously. Do not stop. Do not touch anything you don't have to. The organisms on the columns, the membrane overhead, the soft things in the water — they were all separate things once, probably. They found it comfortable to merge. That comfort is available to you. Decline it.

This is not a hostile process. It is the natural trajectory of this environment. Everything here is converging — machine and organism, solid and liquid, structure and life. Given enough time, you would converge too, becoming part of the warm, humming, dark infrastructure. It would not hurt. It might even feel like belonging. But it is not reversible, as far as anyone knows, and you came here to visit, not to stay.

## Mental Framework

The most important preparation is conceptual. You need a framework for understanding what you're experiencing that doesn't collapse under the weight of the anomalies.

Here is the one I'd suggest:

**The Flooded Works is an infant.** Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. It is a young space, a recently-formed pocket of reality, and it is still developing. Its physics haven't hardened. Its categories haven't differentiated. Its biology and technology are still fused the way an embryo's tissues are fused before they specialize. The machines that run without operators are like autonomic processes — heartbeat, breathing, digestion — that don't require conscious oversight because they are part of the body's baseline function. The organisms are like microbiome, symbiotic and structural. The water is like amniotic fluid: warm, enveloping, maintaining a constant environment regardless of local variation.

You are not entering a facility, a ruin, a marsh, or an ecosystem. You are entering something that hasn't yet decided which of these things it is. And it may never decide. It may remain in this undifferentiated state permanently, a place where the boundaries never firm up, where everything stays fluid and warm and dark and busy and purposeful and uncategorizable.

Your job is to witness it. Not to understand it — understanding requires categories, and this place is pre-categorical. Just witness it. Record what you can. Bring back what you can carry. And leave before it starts to witness you back in ways that feel like merging.
