# Preparing for the Plateau of Leng-Katal

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before addressing any practical matter, you need to sit with something uncomfortable: Leng-Katal is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is not indifferent in the way a blizzard is indifferent. It is *complete* in a way that has no use for you. You are not entering a dangerous environment so much as an irrelevant one — a place where your presence is a grammatical error in a finished text. This distinction matters because it will shape every psychological and practical challenge you face. The plateau will not try to kill you. It will simply have no mechanism by which to acknowledge you exist, and over time, that will become its own kind of danger, arguably worse than something actively trying to harm you.

Prepare accordingly. This is not an expedition into peril. It is an expedition into *completion*, and you are the only incomplete thing there.

---

## Atmospheric Preparation

### Breathing

The description is precise and deeply concerning: each breath delivers approximately nine-tenths of what you need. This is not immediately dangerous. It is *cumulatively* dangerous in a way that will erode your judgment before it erodes your body.

At roughly 90% of required oxygen delivery per breath, you are functioning in conditions analogous to an altitude of somewhere between 2,500 and 3,500 meters on a standard Earth-like world — except without the normal compensatory mechanisms that altitude acclimatization provides, because this is not altitude-related hypoxia. The air is simply *thin*. Your body will attempt to compensate by increasing respiration rate and heart rate, but if the partial pressure of oxygen is the limiting factor rather than total atmospheric pressure, there may be limits to how effectively your physiology can adapt.

**What to bring:**

- **Supplemental oxygen**, obviously. But consider the duration of your visit carefully. Bottled oxygen is heavy and finite. For any stay beyond a few hours, you need a concentrator — a device that can extract and compress available oxygen from the ambient atmosphere. Since the air *does* contain oxygen (it's breathable, just inadequately so), a portable oxygen concentrator set to deliver a modest supplemental flow of 1-2 liters per minute should bridge the gap between what the atmosphere provides and what your body requires. Bring two units. Redundancy is not optional in a place where the nearest alternative atmosphere is a multiverse transit away.

- **A pulse oximeter.** Wear it continuously. The insidious quality of mild chronic hypoxia is that it degrades cognitive function — attention, judgment, emotional regulation, motivation — before it produces obvious physical symptoms. You will feel slightly dreamy, slightly slow, slightly detached. In a place like Leng-Katal, where the environment itself induces a kind of contemplative stupor, hypoxia-related cognitive dulling will blend seamlessly with the psychological effects of the location. You will not notice yourself getting stupid. The oximeter will. Set an alarm for anything below 90% SpO2. Below 85%, you leave, regardless of what you think you're in the middle of investigating.

- **Acetazolamide (Diamox)** or equivalent, if your physiology supports it. It won't solve the underlying issue, but it may help your body optimize what oxygen is available by altering blood pH to improve hemoglobin's oxygen-carrying behavior. Start taking it 24-48 hours before arrival.

- **Awareness of your exertion level.** Every physical effort on the plateau — walking, climbing into structures, manipulating equipment — costs more oxygen than it would in a full atmosphere. Budget your energy as though you are perpetually at moderate altitude. Move slowly. Rest more than feels necessary. The plateau rewards slowness anyway; nothing there is in a hurry, and neither should you be.

### Air Quality

The alkite powder. It lies in undisturbed sheets. *You* will disturb it. Every footstep will be the first disturbance that powder has experienced in centuries or longer. It will rise around your feet in small clouds and then — critically — it will hang there, because there is no wind to disperse it and the atmosphere is still enough that settling will take a very long time.

You do not know what alkite powder does to lung tissue. Nobody does, because nobody has breathed it. It is described as fine, which means it is almost certainly respirable — small enough to penetrate deep into the bronchial tree and alveoli. Fine mineral dusts on most worlds cause silicosis,ite asbestosis,ite various pneumoconioses, none of which you want any part of.

**What to bring:**

- **A high-quality respirator mask with P100/HEPA filtration**, rated for fine mineral particulates. Not a surgical mask. Not a cloth bandana. A proper sealed respirator with replaceable filter cartridges. Bring many replacement cartridges — far more than you think you'll need. In a still atmosphere where disturbed dust hangs indefinitely, your filters will accumulate load faster than in environments with air movement, because you'll be walking through your own dust clouds repeatedly.

- **Sealed eye protection.** Not sunglasses. Sealed goggles with anti-fog coating. The alkite powder will find its way onto every exposed mucous membrane. Your eyes are vulnerable. Given the already-dim conditions, choose goggles with clear or very lightly tinted lenses — do not further reduce your available light.

- **Consider the interaction between your respirator and your supplemental oxygen delivery.** These systems need to work together. A nasal cannula under a sealed face mask is uncomfortable and may compromise the mask's seal. Investigate integrated systems — respirator masks with oxygen supply ports — before departure. Test them. Wear them for hours in advance to identify pressure points, fogging issues, and psychological discomfort. You will be wearing this apparatus for the entire duration of your visit.

## Thermal Considerations

The plateau radiates geothermal warmth — described as analogous to a mild summer evening, stable, neither increasing nor decreasing. This is one of the more benign aspects of the environment, but it has subtleties.

**Stable warmth with no convection.** There is no wind. There is no weather. This means there is no convective cooling on your skin. Your body's evaporative cooling will work (you will sweat, and sweat will eventually evaporate even in still air), but inefficiently. The risk is not heat stroke — the ambient temperature is not that high — but rather a slow, persistent mild thermal discomfort that you stop noticing consciously while it continues to dehydrate you.

**What to bring:**

- Lightweight, breathable clothing that wicks moisture. Long sleeves and long pants to minimize alkite contact with skin. Light colors to maximize your visibility to yourself in the dim environment (this sounds absurd; it matters — see the section on psychology). Avoid dark maroon, rust, or ochre clothing. You do not want to look down and be unable to distinguish your own body from the ground.

- **Water.** A great deal of water. There is no water on the plateau. There is no evidence water has ever been on the plateau. There is no atmospheric moisture to collect, no condensation on cold surfaces (there are no cold surfaces), no hidden springs, no ice. Every molecule of water you need must come with you. For a multi-day visit, this is a significant logistical challenge. Calculate ruthlessly: in a warm, dry, still environment with increased respiration rates (due to mild hypoxia), you should budget at minimum 4-5 liters per day, more if you are exerting yourself. Bring 150% of what you calculate. There is no margin for error.

- A reliable method of **water purification/recycling** if staying more than 2-3 days. A closed-loop system that can reclaim moisture from exhaled air and perspiration is ideal. This is the kind of equipment used in hostile desert environments and spacecraft. It is heavy and complex. Weigh this against simply bringing more water.

## Light and Vision

The sourceless, nearly useless dim glow. Enough to distinguish shape from emptiness, edge from open ground, but never enough to cast a shadow.

This is a profoundly disorienting lighting condition. Your visual system relies on shadows, contrast gradients, and directional light cues to construct three-dimensional spatial awareness. Leng-Katal provides almost none of these. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere — diffused through the static dust ceiling, reflected off the ochre-maroon surfaces uniformly. Depth perception will be compromised. Distance estimation will be unreliable. The edges of structures will appear to bleed into the background. The low, whale-backed ridges on the horizon will be nearly invisible against the sky because the sky and the ground are almost the same color and luminosity.

**What to bring:**

- **A high-quality headlamp and multiple handheld lights**, with extensive battery supplies. LED lights with adjustable beam width and intensity. However — and this is important — *use them judiciously*. A bright light in this environment will create the shadows the natural light denies, which will dramatically improve your spatial awareness in your immediate vicinity, but it will simultaneously destroy your dark adaptation and render everything beyond the light's reach completely invisible. You will create a bubble of readable space surrounded by absolute murk. In an environment with no wind to bring threats toward you but also no visual range to assess your broader surroundings, this tradeoff must be managed carefully.

- **Recommendation:** Use a low-intensity red or amber light for general navigation, preserving dark adaptation, and switch to a brighter white light only when examining specific structures, machines, or details up close. The amber is preferable to red here because the environment is already red-shifted (maroon stone, ochre sky); red light will provide almost no contrast against the natural coloring of everything. A cool blue-white light will provide maximum contrast against the warm-toned environment, making details pop. But it will also make the environment look alien in a way it doesn't look under its native illumination — it will look like a crime scene, all wrong colors and hard edges, and this will compound the psychological strangeness. Choose your light color with both practical and psychological awareness.

- **Night vision or light amplification devices.** These could be transformative. If there is ambient light — and there is, that sourceless dim glow — then a good image intensifier will amplify it dramatically, potentially giving you functional vision across significant distances without the shadow-destroying effect of active illumination. Bring the best generation of night vision monocular or binocular you can acquire. Test it extensively before arrival. One eye with amplified vision, one eye dark-adapted for peripheral awareness — this is a proven technique.

- **A rangefinder or laser distance measurer.** Since your depth perception will be compromised by the shadowless lighting, a technological substitute for spatial judgment is invaluable. When approaching structures, when estimating distances across the flat expanses between buildings, when trying to gauge how far away the ridgeline is — have a device that can give you hard numbers. You cannot trust your eyes here.

## Navigation

There are no landmarks in the conventional sense. The ridges are too low and too smoothed to serve as distinct reference points. The buildings, while numerous, are rectangular and similar in form. The rail lines provide the most reliable navigation infrastructure — they are linear, they connect known points, they run in identifiable patterns — but following rail lines means you can only go where the rail lines go, and the rail lines were not designed for your purposes.

**What to bring:**

- **A GPS-equivalent positioning system** appropriate to this world. If multiverse transit technology provides any kind of absolute positioning, use it. If not, bring a high-precision inertial navigation unit — a device that tracks your movement from a known starting point through accelerometers and gyroscopes, maintaining a running estimate of your position. These drift over time, so calibrate them against fixed reference points (the transit point where you arrived; specific identifiable structures or switching yards you've mapped).

- **Surveyor's flags or markers.** Small, brightly colored (choose a color that contrasts with maroon/ochre — electric blue, bright green, vivid white), driven into the alkite powder at regular intervals along your path. In an environment with no wind, these will remain exactly where you place them indefinitely. They will be your breadcrumb trail. The alkite powder will also record your footprints — remember, nothing has disturbed it in ages — so your own tracks will be visible as long as you don't cross your own path too many times. But don't rely solely on footprints; in the dim light, they're hard to see, and if you're using an active light source, the shadowless conditions may render them invisible even when directly underfoot.

- **Detailed mapping effort from your arrival point.** Before going anywhere, spend time at a high vantage (even the gentle ridgelines provide some elevation) with your light amplification gear and rangefinder, mapping the layout of structures, rail lines, and terrain features as thoroughly as possible. Create a reference map. Update it continuously. Leng-Katal is not large by city standards — twelve square kilometers — but twelve square kilometers of sameness in bad light with no landmarks is a labyrinth.

- **Mark your transit point with something unmistakable.** A battery-powered beacon with a long life. A tall pole with a bright flag. Something you can find from a distance, in dim light, while cognitively impaired by mild hypoxia and psychological disturbance. If you cannot find your transit point, you cannot leave. There is nothing else on this plateau for you.

## Equipment for Investigation

You are presumably visiting Leng-Katal to understand it — to investigate the machines, the rail system, the sealed northern district, the hum. Bring tools for this.

### General

- **Cameras with extreme low-light capability.** Shoot everything. Your memory will not be reliable — not because of any supernatural effect, but because mild hypoxia plus an environment of extreme sensory uniformity equals poor episodic memory formation. Things will blur together. Was it the third warehouse from the switching yard or the fourth? Did the symbol on that signal drum have one radial line or two? You will not remember. The camera will.

- **Audio recording equipment** with high sensitivity and low noise floor. The hum needs to be recorded and analyzed. Its frequency, its harmonics, any sub-audible components below human hearing, any ultrasonic components above it. Bring a spectrum analyzer or recording equipment with sufficient bandwidth to capture from infrasound (below 20 Hz) through ultrasound (above 20 kHz). The hum is described as barely above the threshold of hearing and deep in pitch — this suggests a fundamental frequency in the range of 20-60 Hz, possibly with significant content below 20 Hz that you're perceiving through bone conduction and visceral vibration rather than hearing. Infrasound in the range of 7-19 Hz has known physiological and psychological effects on humans: unease, feelings of presence, visual disturbance, anxiety, awe. If the hum has a significant infrasonic component, it may be affecting your mental state without your awareness. You need to know this.

- **Vibration sensors / seismometers.** Place them on the ground at various distances from the northern district. Map the hum's propagation through the bedrock. Determine if it's a point source or distributed. Determine if it's mechanical vibration or geological resonance. The description says it becomes indistinguishable from a property of the stone — find out if that's poetic or literal.

- **Geiger counter / radiation detector.** Unknown machines with unknown power sources that have been running for geological timescales. Nuclear processes are an obvious candidate. Check for ionizing radiation before approaching the northern district. Check for it everywhere, actually. The faint luminescence of the dust-sky, sourceless and in no identifiable spectrum — that also deserves a spectral analysis and radiation check.

- **Spectrometer** (portable). Analyze the alkite powder. Analyze the building stone. Analyze the rail alloy. Analyze everything. You are in a place built from materials that don't quite match known categories ("not quite iron, not quite anything identifiable"). Understanding the material science is fundamental to understanding the place.

- **Gas analyzer.** What, exactly, is in the atmosphere? Nitrogen-oxygen mix? What proportions? Any trace gases? Any volatile compounds near the sealed buildings? Carbon dioxide levels? (With no photosynthesis and no wind, is CO2 accumulating in low-lying areas? Could you walk into a basin and find an invisible pool of CO2-rich air that pushes your already-marginal oxygen supply into critical territory?)

### The Northern District

The sealed buildings that hum. Pressure-rated mineral shutters bolted from the outside.

**Do not open them.**

Not on your first visit. Possibly not ever. Something inside has been running for longer than erosion can account for. The shutters are bolted from the outside, which means they were sealed by whatever built this city, deliberately, as part of the design. They are pressure-rated, which means whatever is inside may be at a different pressure than the ambient atmosphere. Opening a pressure-rated seal in a thin atmosphere could result in a rapid outgassing event — and you have no idea what gases might be involved.

Investigate the northern district through non-invasive means first:

- Vibration and acoustic monitoring from outside
- Thermal imaging through the walls (bring a FLIR or equivalent thermal camera — if the machines inside are running, they are likely generating heat, and the thermal signature through the walls will tell you something about their nature and distribution)
- Radiation monitoring around the perimeter
- Atmospheric sampling near any seams or joints in the shutters for trace outgassing
- Ground-penetrating radar to image the interior structure through the walls and floors

If you eventually decide to attempt entry, do so only with full protective equipment including a sealed environment suit with independent air supply, and have an immediate evacuation plan to your transit point. But frankly, I would advise against it on a first visit. Understand the outside completely before you consider breaching the inside. The city has been waiting for geological ages. It can wait through a few of your visits.

### The Machines

The processing machines in the open (unsealed) warehouses are described as complete and potentially still operational. The temptation to try activating one will be significant. Resist it until you have thoroughly documented and understood the system.

Map the flow. The rail lines connect everything — follow them. Determine the intended industrial process: where does raw material enter the system? Where does it get processed? What are the intermediate stages? Where does the output go? The rail switching yards are the key — they are the routing logic of the entire operation, a physical program written in track geometry. Map every switch position. Record every signal drum symbol. Reconstruct the operational logic.

Bring materials for making rubbings or impressions of the signal drum symbols and any other notation you encounter. Photograph them, yes, but also make physical copies. If the symbols constitute a notation system — and they do — you'll want to study them at length after departure, and physical rubbings capture dimensional details (depth of carving, width of channels) that photographs may not.

### The Threadwights

The semi-animate entities inside the warehouse structures. They move slowly, they avoid open spaces, they orient toward mineral dust in machine seams.

- Do not touch them.
- Do not obstruct their movement.
- Do not shine bright lights directly on them. (You don't know if they're photosensitive, but if they've spent geological time in near-darkness, intense illumination could constitute an assault on whatever sensory apparatus they possess.)
- Observe them with passive light amplification.
- If possible, observe one for an extended continuous period (hours). How do they move? How fast? Do they have preferred paths? Do they interact with each other? Do they interact with the machines in any way beyond feeding on dust in the seams? Could they be *maintaining* the machines — a geological-timescale custodial organism that keeps the mechanisms clean and functional by consuming accumulating mineral deposits from critical joints and surfaces?

That last possibility is worth serious consideration. If the machines have remained operational-in-principle for geological ages without degradation, something is preventing the normal entropic processes of corrosion, mineral deposition, and mechanical fouling. The threadwights might be that something. This would make them a crucial part of the system — not inhabitants of the city but *components* of it, biological (or quasi-biological) maintenance elements integrated into an otherwise purely mechanical infrastructure.

If this is the case, disturbing them or reducing their population could begin a degradation cascade in the machinery. Tread carefully.

## Food and Sustenance

There is nothing to eat on the plateau. Nothing biological exists except you and the threadwights (and the threadwights are not food — they may not even be biological, and eating something that feeds on mineral dust from ancient alien machinery is a decision so poor it hardly needs arguing against).

Bring all your food. High-calorie, low-weight, low-preparation. You will not have water to spare for food preparation, so avoid anything that requires rehydration. Calorie-dense bars, nuts, dried provisions. Bring more than you think you need. The mild hypoxia will suppress your appetite (this is a known effect of altitude-equivalent conditions), so you may not feel hungry even when you need to eat. Set scheduled eating times and adhere to them regardless of appetite.

**Eat inside your shelter, mask off, in a sealed environment.** You do not want alkite powder in your food. You do not want to remove your respirator in open air any more than absolutely necessary.

## Shelter

You need a sealed, enclosed shelter for sleeping and resting periods. The open environment is warm enough but offers no protection from alkite dust, provides no psychological enclosure (see psychology section), and gives you no space where you can remove your respirator safely.

Bring a **sealed tent or habitat module** with the following properties:

- Airtight closure with a simple airlock or vestibule system where you can brush off alkite powder before entering the clean interior
- Internal atmosphere management — at minimum, filtration of incoming air; ideally, supplemental oxygen enrichment so you can breathe comfortably while sleeping without wearing a mask
- Some form of interior lighting — warm-spectrum, adjustable, capable of being bright enough to counteract the oppressive dimness of the plateau

Place the shelter in an open area between structures, not inside a warehouse. The threadwights are inside the warehouses. You do not want to sleep near the threadwights. Not because they are necessarily dangerous, but because sharing a dark enclosed space with slowly moving quasi-mineral entities while you are asleep and psychologically vulnerable is a recipe for panic responses if you wake disoriented.

Also avoid placing your shelter within the northern district or close to it. The hum. If the hum has infrasonic components (and it almost certainly does), sleeping in close proximity to it will disrupt your sleep architecture, potentially severely. Infrasound exposure during sleep causes nightmares, arousal disturbances, and a persistent sense of dread. You will have enough psychological challenges without adding involuntary infrasonic bombardment during your only rest periods.

Place your shelter at the periphery of the city, near but not at your transit point, in a location from which you can see your transit beacon.

## Psychology

This is the most important section.

Leng-Katal will do things to your mind. Not through any supernatural agency — through the intersection of its physical properties with human cognitive architecture. Understand these effects in advance so you can recognize them when they begin.

### Sensory Deprivation Effects

The plateau offers almost no sensory input. No wind on skin. No variation in light. No variation in temperature. No sounds except the hum, the rare thermal tick, and your own breathing and footfalls (muffled by alkite). No biological activity. No movement except the threadwights, which move so slowly they barely register. No color variation — everything is maroon, ochre, rust, dull alloy gray.

Your brain requires sensory input to maintain a stable model of reality. Deprived of it, the brain begins to *generate* its own. Expect:

- **Visual disturbances.** After extended time on the plateau, you may begin seeing movement in your peripheral vision where none exists. Shapes may appear to shift or breathe. The signal drum symbols may seem to change when you're not looking directly at them. The buildings may appear closer or farther than they are. These are normal perceptual artifacts of a low-stimulus environment, not evidence of anything anomalous. Probably. Note them, record them, but do not trust them.

- **Auditory artifacts.** You may begin hearing patterns in the hum — rhythms, melodies, voices. The human auditory system is a pattern-completion engine; given a sustained featureless sound, it will impose patterns on it. The hum does not modulate. If you hear it modulating, that is your brain, not the hum. Record it with your equipment and check.

- **Temporal distortion.** Without day/night cycles, without weather changes, without any environmental rhythm to anchor your sense of time, your internal clock will drift rapidly. Hours will feel like minutes or days. You will lose track of how long you have been standing in one place, how long you have been walking, how long you have been on the plateau. **Bring multiple reliable timekeeping devices. Set alarms for regular intervals. When the alarm sounds, log your position, your oximeter reading, your water consumption, and your subjective state.** This is not optional. This is survival protocol.

### The Completeness Problem

The description ends with a remarkable passage about *completeness* — the sense that Leng-Katal is not waiting, not dead, but finished. A complete sentence in a language that never needed a speaker. This is the deepest psychological hazard of the location.

Human beings derive meaning from incompleteness. We are motivated by needs, gaps, problems, questions. A place that is complete — that has no need, no gap, no question — offers the human mind nothing to attach to. You cannot *help* here. You cannot *fix* anything. You cannot even understand the city as something that *wants* to be understood, because it shows no evidence of wanting anything. It is what it is, fully and without remainder.

Exposure to this kind of ontological completeness can induce a state that resembles depression but is not exactly depression. It is more like a profound motivational collapse — a sudden inability to remember why you are doing anything, why investigation matters, why you would want to return home, why home is different from here. The warmth rising through the stone, the hum filling your bones, the signal drum saying *ready* — they become sufficient. You can sit down on the alkite powder, the first person to ever sit in this spot, and feel that sitting here is the most appropriate thing you have ever done, because the plateau is complete and now, sitting here, you are part of the completeness.

**This is the moment you are in danger.**

Countermeasures:

- **Before departure, record a video of yourself.** In it, speak directly to your future self. Describe your home. Describe the people you love. Describe what you plan to do after the visit. Describe something unfinished in your life — a project, a conversation, a book you haven't read. Describe *wanting*. Make the video long and specific and full of the kind of messy, incomplete, yearning human content that Leng-Katal is the total absence of. Load it on a device you carry with you at all times. Set a recurring alarm — every four hours — to watch it. Not skim it. Watch it, and listen, and remember that you are an incomplete being, and that incompleteness is not a deficiency; it is the engine of your life.

- **Bring something from home that is imperfect.** A letter with a coffee stain. A child's drawing. A stone from a river. Something handmade, something that shows the marks of its making, something that is the opposite of the plateau's ancient, featureless, finished surfaces. Keep it in your pocket. Touch it often.

- **Do not stay longer than you planned.** Set a departure time before you arrive and honor it. The city will still be here. It has been here for geological ages. It will endure your absence. But you might not endure an extended stay with your motivation and identity intact.

- **Talk to yourself out loud.** Your voice is imperfect, biological, present-tense. It introduces chaos into the equilibrium. The sound of human speech — even your own, even muttering observations or reading equipment values aloud — disrupts the completeness of the soundscape enough to keep you anchored in your own living, speaking, *needing* self. You will feel absurd doing this. Do it anyway.

### The Hum

A special note about the hum. It is described as deep, tonal, unvarying, transmitted through bedrock. You will feel it before you hear it. It will become the new baseline of your experience, the floor under the silence.

Prolonged exposure to low-frequency tonal sound without variation has specific neurological effects. It induces a meditative or trance-like state. It can synchronize brainwave patterns downward toward theta rhythms associated with drowsiness, hypnagogia, and susceptibility to suggestion. Combined with the sensory deprivation, the mild hypoxia, and the ontological completeness effect, the hum could accelerate the motivational collapse described above.

Use earplugs intermittently to break your exposure to the hum. High-quality earplugs with good low-frequency attenuation. Alternate — thirty minutes with earplugs, thirty minutes without — to prevent adaptation to either the hum or the silence. When the earplugs come out, the hum will be noticeable again, which is what you want; it's when you stop noticing it that it's doing its most insidious work.

## Ethical Considerations

You are visiting a place that, by all evidence, was never designed for visitors. It was designed as a self-contained system. Your presence introduces contamination — biological contamination (skin cells, exhaled moisture, shed bacteria), physical contamination (footprints in the alkite, disturbance of dust deposits), and potentially electromagnetic or chemical contamination from your equipment.

Should you be here? The question is genuine. The city has maintained its pristine completeness for geological ages. You are the first disturbance in eons. Even your footprints are an alteration — and in a windless environment, your footprints will persist for as long as the plateau persists. You are writing yourself onto a surface that has been blank since before your species existed.

Minimize your footprint (literally and metaphorically). Stay on the rail lines where possible — walking on the rails and between them disturbs less alkite than walking on open ground. Do not take samples unless you have compelling reason and a clear plan for what you will learn from them. Do not attempt to operate any machinery. Do not remove anything. Do not leave anything behind except the unavoidable (footprints, exhaled CO2, shed cells).

The threadwights in particular deserve caution. If they are part of the system's maintenance architecture, disturbing them could initiate a cascade failure in machinery that has been operational for longer than your civilization has existed. Your curiosity does not outweigh the integrity of something this old and this complete.

## Departure Preparation

When it is time to leave, you may find it difficult. Not physically — there is presumably nothing preventing you from returning to your transit point — but psychologically. The completeness of Leng-Katal creates a strange gravity. Leaving feels like introducing an incompleteness into something that was complete, like getting up in the middle of a chord that doesn't need to resolve because it was never dissonant.

This is the hypoxia talking. This is the sensory deprivation talking. This is the hum talking. Watch your video. Touch your imperfect object. Read your oximeter. Note the time. Walk to your beacon. Leave.

And then — this is crucial — **debrief extensively within 24 hours of departure.** Talk to someone. Talk to them about what you saw, what you felt, what you thought while standing in the alkite dust between the warehouse rows with the hum in your bones and the signal drum saying *ready*. Describe it in words, messy human words that do not capture it, that reduce it, that get it slightly wrong. Get it wrong. Getting it wrong is how you prove to yourself that you are a living, interpreting, imperfect being who *can* get things wrong, because the plateau cannot get anything wrong, because the plateau is not trying, because the plateau is not anything except what it is.

You will want to go back. That wanting — note it — is the most human thing about the experience. Leng-Katal wants nothing. You want. Hold onto that. Examine it later, when you are rested and oxygenated and surrounded by noise and weather and imperfection. Then decide if you will return.
