# Preparing to Visit The Evacuated Works

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description and let it reorganize your assumptions. This is not a ruin in the romantic sense — not something photogenic and melancholy that you visit to feel the passage of time. This is an active ecosystem housed in industrial infrastructure, a place where the human chapter closed long enough ago that everything has settled into a stable post-human equilibrium. The buildings are not falling down. The animals are not scavenging. The whole system — drainage, shelter, water collection, territorial boundaries — is functioning, just under new management.

You are not exploring an abandoned city. You are entering an occupied one. The occupants are not human, they do not recognize your authority, and they have been there long enough to consider every corner of the place theirs. This distinction should inform every single preparation you make.

---

## Multiverse Positioning and Variant Considerations

The Evacuated Works reads as a place that exists across a range of possible variants. The fundamental parameters — alkaline hardpan, industrial settlement, chemical contamination sufficient to prevent plant growth, large-bodied megafauna plus colonial rodents plus massing birds — these are specific enough to identify the location but loose enough that the details may shift between crossings. The animals described don't map cleanly onto any terrestrial species, which means you're likely dealing with a parallel evolutionary lineage or a far-future divergence.

Before crossing, establish with whatever navigational tools you use:

**Atmospheric composition.** The description mentions chemical sharpness, ammonia from bird droppings, and mineral-laden water. This could range from mildly irritating to genuinely toxic depending on the variant. Some versions of this place may have higher concentrations of whatever industrial byproduct killed the soil. You need to know if the air is breathable for extended periods or if you're on a clock.

**Water toxicity.** The amber-tinted water supports no life — no algae, no biofilm, nothing. The animals drink it, which means it's not acutely lethal to them, but their physiology is not yours. In some variants, the water may contain heavy metals, dissolved industrial solvents, or alkaline concentrations that would damage your kidneys over days of exposure. Assume you cannot drink any water you find on-site until proven otherwise.

**Animal behavior baselines.** The description suggests the large animals are tolerant of observation — they don't flee, they don't charge, they exist in a state of comfortable dominion. But tolerance is not indifference, and it is certainly not invitation. Different variants may have different thresholds. Some crossings may land you in a version where the animals are more territorial, more reactive, or more curious about novel intruders. You need a feel for the local behavioral norms within the first hour, and you need to be prepared to retreat if the norms are hostile.

**Temporal positioning.** How long ago was the evacuation? The description suggests decades at minimum — long enough for rubble to stabilize, for animal populations to reach carrying capacity, for territorial patterns to become entrenched. But some variants might be earlier in the process, with less stable animal hierarchies and more active structural collapse. Others might be further along, with the buildings beginning their final dissolution and the animal communities in a different configuration. Know where you are in the timeline.

## Physical Preparation

### Respiratory Protection

This is non-negotiable. The air carries:

- Fine alkaline mineral dust from the hardpan, blown in and resuspended by movement
- Ammonia and particulate matter from massive bird roosts — "inches thick" accumulations of droppings means serious off-gassing, especially in the enclosed upper stories of the factory buildings
- Whatever residual industrial chemistry is in the soil, the water, the rust, the concrete dust
- Possible fungal or bacterial spores from the bird guano accumulations, which in many biological contexts harbor histoplasma or analogous organisms

Bring a quality half-face respirator with combination particulate/organic vapor cartridges. Not a dust mask. Not a bandana. A proper respirator with P100 particulate filtration and an activated carbon layer for organic vapors and ammonia. Carry spare cartridges — the ammonia will saturate them faster than you expect if you spend any time in or near the factory buildings.

For the upper stories of the factories, where you'd be walking through concentrated bird roost areas, consider a full-face respirator. The ammonia concentration in enclosed spaces with inches of accumulated droppings could be eye-watering in the literal sense — corneal burns from ammonia exposure are a real concern in heavy accumulations.

### Eye Protection

Sealed safety goggles, not just glasses. The alkaline dust is an irritant, and if the wind shifts or you disturb a surface, you'll get a faceful. In the bird roost zones, the ammonia issue applies to eyes as well. Goggles that seal against your face and are compatible with your respirator.

### Skin Coverage

Full-coverage clothing. Long sleeves, long pants, gloves, boots. Not because the animals are dangerous to skin contact — though their waste products might be — but because:

- The alkaline dust and mineral water are skin irritants over prolonged exposure. Calcium hydroxide in solution is caustic. You don't know the pH of that amber water, but the fact that nothing grows in it is telling.
- The bird guano is a biohazard in any biological context. Dried guano particulate on skin is a vector for infection.
- The rust, the corroded metal, the broken concrete — this is a tetanus landscape, a laceration landscape. Every surface is rough, oxidized, or sharp.
- You want a barrier between yourself and whatever industrial residue coats every horizontal surface in this place.

Wear something you can decontaminate or discard. Tyvek coveralls over your base layer are not overkill. If that feels excessive, at minimum wear tightly woven long-sleeved work clothing that you can wash aggressively or throw away after the visit. Tuck pants into boots, tape the junction if you're going into the drainage channels where the rodent-columns travel — you do not want small fast colonial rodents running up your pant legs if you surprise a column.

### Footwear

Heavy-soled boots with ankle support, full waterproof. You will be walking on:

- Cracked mineral-cruite hardpan (uneven, sharp edges)
- Compacted alkaline silt (slippery when wet, abrasive when dry)
- Broken concrete with exposed rebar
- Rusted metal grating (the elevated walkway — you're testing each step, the description says, which means some steps will fail)
- Standing water of unknown chemical composition up to a foot deep

Steel-toed or composite-toed if possible. The large animals weigh two hundred pounds plus and move through narrow streets — if one steps on your foot in passing, you want that protection. Bring gaiters if you plan to wade. Ideally boots with chemical-resistant soles — some industrial solvents will degrade rubber or standard EVA foam over prolonged contact.

### Water and Food

**Bring all your water.** Every drop. Bring more than you think you need — the heat is described as close and pre-breathed, the kind of humidity that saps you without the relief of evaporative cooling. You'll drink more than you expect. Figure a minimum of four liters per day for moderate activity in high heat and humidity, more if you're climbing through factory buildings or covering significant distance.

Do not drink the local water. Do not filter the local water and drink it. Standard filtration removes biological contaminants but not dissolved heavy metals, not industrial solvents, not alkaline mineral loads. If you had a portable reverse-osmosis system and a water testing kit and confirmed the output was safe, maybe. But the simpler answer is: bring your water, carry your water, drink your water, leave.

**Bring calorie-dense food that doesn't require preparation.** You won't be cooking here — any fire would be conspicuous and potentially alarming to the animal populations, and the surfaces are contaminated enough that you don't want to handle food more than necessary. Sealed ration bars, dried provisions, things you can eat with gloved hands or after a quick glove change. Keep food sealed against the dust and the ammonia.

### Navigation and Orientation

The city is gridded but the grid is not even, and the haze limits visibility. The description makes clear that you can't judge distances — the hardpan dissolves into haze at an indeterminate range. Inside the settlement, the narrow residential streets, the uniformity of the workers' housing, and the directionless light will conspire to disorient you.

Bring a compass. Bring a GPS if it functions in the variant (don't assume it will — satellite infrastructure requires a civilization to maintain it). Bring chalk or flagging tape to mark your route, though be aware that marking territory in a place where animals mark territory may communicate something you don't intend. A hand-drawn map, built as you go, is your most reliable tool. The layout is comprehensible — residential blocks south and east, factory complex north and west, processing yard with the lake as the central landmark, the wide avenue as the dividing line. Orient yourself to this schema early and keep it in your head.

### Lighting

The ambient light is low and diffuse — pewter sky, no hard shadows, the kind of illumination that flattens depth perception and makes distances ambiguous. Inside buildings, especially the deep interiors of the factory complex, it will be dark. The residential buildings with narrow windows will be dim. The drainage channels will be shadowed.

Bring a headlamp with a red-light mode. White light will serve you for navigation and structural assessment, but red light is less likely to provoke a response from the animals — many large-bodied animals are less reactive to red wavelengths. Bring spare batteries. Bring a backup handheld light. If you're going into the upper factory stories among the birds, be aware that sudden bright light in a space containing tens of thousands of roosting birds can trigger a panic flush, and ten thousand stocky birds launching simultaneously in an enclosed space is a physical danger to you — the buffeting alone could knock you off compromised flooring.

## Understanding the Animals

### The Large Animals (The Dominant Residents)

Two hundred pounds plus. Hyena-pig architecture — heavy shoulders, sloped backs, coarse brown fur. They live in territorial groups claiming specific residential blocks. They use communal latrines at intersections. They have worn smooth paths through the silt and polished building corners at shoulder height. They vocalize rarely and at low volume. Juveniles spar briefly and stop. Adults pass each other in narrow streets with brief rumbling exchanges.

This behavioral profile tells you several things:

**They are socially complex.** Territorial groups with communal latrines, designated paths, low-volume communication — this is not a dumb herd animal. This is something with social structure, with negotiated boundaries, with behavioral protocols for encounter and conflict resolution. Respect that complexity. You are not smarter than this social system just because you have hands and language.

**They are habituated to their environment but not to you.** They have never seen a human, possibly not in their entire generational memory. You are a novel stimulus. Novel stimuli in established territorial animals can provoke a range of responses: curiosity, avoidance, investigation, defensive aggression. The initial response you get will tell you a lot, and you need to be ready for any of these.

**Do not block paths.** The worn trails through the silt are their roads. Standing in the middle of one is the equivalent of parking your car across a highway. If you see a smooth-packed trail, step to the side. If you hear a large body shifting its weight around a corner, stop, give ground, let it pass.

**Do not approach latrines.** Communal latrine sites at intersections are territorial markers. Their chemistry "probably contributes to the sterility of the ground," meaning they're potent. These are signposts and boundary declarations. Standing at or near one may read as a territorial challenge. Give them wide berth.

**Do not approach juveniles.** In virtually every social mammalian system, approaching young animals triggers protective aggression from adults. The juveniles are lighter, leaner, faster, and more curious — they may approach you. Do not reciprocate. Let them investigate if they choose, keep your movements slow and non-threatening, and be aware that a parent or dominant adult is watching.

**Watch for the rearing behavior.** The description of juveniles rearing on hind legs to spar gives you a threat-display template. If an adult rears in your presence, it is escalating. Back away slowly. Do not make sudden movements. Do not run — a two-hundred-pound animal built like a hyena is faster than you on flat ground and knows the terrain infinitely better.

**General conduct:** Move slowly. Move predictably. Make yourself smaller rather than larger. Speak in a low, steady voice if you need to alert an animal to your presence — sudden silence followed by sudden appearance is more startling than a continuous low-level sound that allows them to track your approach. Do not make eye contact for prolonged periods; in many social mammals, sustained direct stare is a challenge. Look at them with peripheral vision. Observe obliquely.

### The Rodent-Columns (The Channel Network)

Grey, long-tailed, traveling in coordinated groups of forty to fifty that move as a single fluid organism through the drainage channels. They appear and vanish. They drink at the processing yard outfall and withdraw.

**Do not enter the drainage channels.** These are their primary transit routes, and a column of forty to fifty rodent-sized animals pouring past or over you in a confined concrete trench is at minimum deeply unpleasant and at maximum a bite-risk if they feel trapped. Observe from above. If you need to cross a drainage channel, look both ways — literally — and cross quickly at a point where you have clear sight lines in both directions.

**They are fast and they are coordinated.** The description of their movement as resembling a single organism suggests either eusocial behavior or extremely tight flocking/schooling instincts. Either way, the group acts as a unit. If one reacts to you, fifty react to you. The reaction might be to scatter, or it might be to mob. Don't find out.

**Timing matters.** They seem to move in pulses — appearing at the outfall to drink, then withdrawing. If you want to observe the processing yard from ground level, note their rhythm. Go when they've just withdrawn, not when they're due.

### The Birds (The Upper City)

Stocky, dark-winged, silent except for a low clicking. They roost in enormous numbers — thousands, tens of thousands — on the steel beams and overhead cranes of the upper factory stories. Inches-thick droppings below the roosts.

**Do not enter their roost spaces.** I cannot stress this enough. The biohazard alone — ammonia, histoplasma-analog potential, particulate guano dust — makes the upper factory interiors the most dangerous environment in the settlement from a health perspective. But beyond that, disturbing a roost of tens of thousands of colonial birds in an enclosed space risks:

- Panic flush causing structural damage to already-compromised upper elements
- Physical injury from bird strikes in an enclosed stampede
- Disorientation in sudden darkness as thousands of bodies block what little light comes through broken windows
- Structural failure underfoot if the grating or flooring is compromised and you can't see where you're stepping

Observe the birds from outside and below. The elevated walkway gives you a vantage point over the processing yard — use it for the yard, but do not follow it into or through the factory buildings where the roosts are.

**The murmuration tells you something.** The description of birds lifting from one building and resettling on another in four seconds is characteristic of highly synchronized colonial roosters. They're not agitated — this is normal relocation behavior. But it tells you they move as a mass, quickly, and the triggering stimulus may not be apparent to you. If you hear a sudden intensification of clicking from a factory building, move away from the exits — you don't want to be standing in a doorway when ten thousand birds decide to relocate through it.

## Structural Safety

The buildings have "finished falling apart" — they've reached a kind of equilibrium. But equilibrium does not mean safety for a bipedal creature weighing 150-200 pounds walking in places the structural members weren't designed for in their current degraded state.

### General Principles

**Test before you trust.** The elevated walkway — rusted grating, "still solid enough to hold your weight if you test each step." This is the model for all movement through the settlement. Every step on a raised surface, every floor in a building, every staircase — test it. Shift your weight gradually. Listen for the sound of stressed metal, cracking concrete, the groan of a connection giving way. If it doesn't feel right, back up.

**Stay out of basements.** Flooded, dark, likely containing the highest concentrations of whatever chemical residues leached out of the works. The water down there may be more concentrated than the surface water. The structural risk of a floor collapse above you is the most dangerous kind — you fall into a hole filled with toxic water in the dark.

**Be cautious of upper floors.** Every floor above ground level is a floor that can collapse under you. The ground floor of most buildings is your safest elevation for interior exploration. If you go up, go carefully, and always maintain a retreat route.

**Watch for corroded connections.** In steel-frame industrial buildings, the structure stands or falls by its bolted and welded connections. Corrosion attacks connections preferentially — the joints where dissimilar metals meet, where water sits, where stress concentrates. A beam can look solid while its connection to the column has rusted to tissue paper. Don't trust apparent structural integrity at face value.

**The narrow-gauge rail tracks** are a tripping hazard. They emerge from the silt unpredictably. Walk carefully in areas where you spot them.

### The Elevated Walkway

This is your best vantage point and also one of your highest-risk locations. Thirty feet up on rusted grating connecting two factory buildings. The description acknowledges you must test each step.

- Stay close to the structural supports (the edges where the grating connects to the building frames), not in the center of the span where deflection is greatest
- Do not stop and stand in one place for extended periods — sustained load on a single point of corroded grating is more dangerous than moving load distributed across multiple points
- Be aware that the walkway may connect into the factory buildings at the bird-roost level — do not enter
- If the grating feels spongy, flexible, or makes any sound of distress, retreat to the last solid point

## Chemical and Contamination Safety

This place is contaminated. The absence of all plant life — "not a weed, not a lichen, not a thread of moss" — in an environment with standing water and humid air tells you the contamination is severe and broad-spectrum. Whatever the works produced or processed has poisoned the soil and water comprehensively enough that nothing photosynthetic can establish.

### What Might Be In the Environment

Without knowing what the works produced, you're working from inference:

- **Heavy metals** (lead, cadmium, chromium, mercury, arsenic) — common in industrial processing of all kinds, persistent in soil and water, bioaccumulative
- **Alkaline/causite compounds** — the white-crusted hardpan, the calcium deposits, the mineral character of everything suggests alkaline industrial processes (cement production, mineral processing, chemical manufacturing)
- **Solvents and organics** — less likely to persist at the surface given the elapsed time, but may be present in enclosed spaces, basements, sealed containers
- **Asbestos** — industrial buildings of this type frequently used asbestos insulation, which remains dangerous indefinitely and becomes more dangerous as buildings deteriorate and release fibers

### Protective Measures

- **Respirator** (already covered above — this is dual-purpose, biological and chemical)
- **Do not touch your face** while in the settlement. Gloves on at all times. If you need to eat or drink, move to a clean area (ideally outside the settlement on the hardpan, upwind), remove gloves, wash hands with carried water, handle food, then re-glove before re-entering.
- **Do not sit on or lean against surfaces** unnecessarily. The rust-colored mineral residue coating everything is a potential exposure vector.
- **Decontaminate before leaving.** At minimum, remove and bag outer clothing and boot covers before returning to your transit point. Wipe down all equipment. Wash exposed skin thoroughly with clean water. If you used Tyvek coveralls, dispose of them.
- **Monitor for symptoms** in the days following your visit: metallic taste, unusual fatigue, skin irritation, respiratory changes, gastrointestinal disturbance. Heavy metal exposure symptoms can be delayed.

## Psychological Preparation

This is important and often neglected.

The Evacuated Works is not a scary place in the conventional sense. Nothing here is trying to kill you actively. There are no hostiles, no traps, no malicious entities. But it is a profoundly *alien* place in its emotional register, and the psychological experience of being there deserves preparation.

### The Silence That Isn't Silent

The acoustic environment will work on you. The haze acts as a sound dampener, creating a sense of enclosure even in open spaces. You'll hear your own breathing, your own footsteps, your own heartbeat more clearly than usual. And layered into that self-awareness, the sounds of the animals — the shift of heavy bodies, the dripping water, the whisper of rodent feet, the distant clicking — create a soundscape that is simultaneously empty and occupied. Your brain will keep trying to categorize this as "quiet" and keep being contradicted by evidence of massive biological presence. This cognitive tension is fatiguing over time. Expect it.

### The Scale of Occupation

The first encounter with the large animals will recalibrate your understanding of the place. The description is precise about this: it "reorganizes your understanding completely." You arrived thinking you were visiting an empty city. You are in fact a guest — an uninvited one — in a thriving community. The psychological shift from explorer to intruder is significant. You may feel unwelcome, not because any animal has threatened you, but because the depth of their habitation makes your presence feel like a category error. You don't belong here. The city has moved on. You are visiting a place that has no use for you and no space designated for you. This is a subtly uncomfortable realization, and it accumulates.

### The Absence of Green

Humans are wired to find comfort in vegetation. The total absence of plant life — in a wet environment, with standing water — is deeply wrong at a level below conscious processing. Your hindbrain will register this as danger before your forebrain articulates why. The mineral landscape, the amber water, the chemical sharpness in the air — these are signals that the environment is hostile to biology, and your body is biology. You may feel a persistent, low-level urge to leave that has nothing to do with any specific threat and everything to do with the landscape's fundamental deadness to botanical life. Acknowledge this feeling. It's accurate. The environment *is* hostile to biology. The animals have adapted; you have not.

### The Directionless Light

No hard shadows. Pewter sky. Diffuse, flat illumination. This lighting condition degrades depth perception, flattens spatial relationships, and makes distances unreliable. Over hours, it creates a subtle dissociative effect — a feeling that the environment is slightly unreal, that you're moving through a photograph rather than a place. Combined with the visual monotony (grey concrete, umber rust, white mineral crust, brown fur — a palette of perhaps five colors), this can produce a dreamlike state that impairs judgment. Stay hydrated. Take breaks. Eat something. Ground yourself in sensory specifics: the texture of a wall under your gloved hand, the temperature of the air, the weight of your pack.

### You Are Being Watched

The animals are aware of you. The large ones in particular — they are social, territorial, and intelligent enough to maintain complex spatial and social arrangements. A novel two-legged creature moving through their territory is noticed. You may not see overt reactions, but you are being tracked. The birds on the upper stories can see you from above. The rodent-columns will adjust their routes around your position. The large animals will know which block you're on. This is not paranoia; this is ecology. You are a stimulus in an established system, and the system is responding. The response may be subtle — a group that was resting shifts to alertness as you pass, then relaxes after you've gone — but it is real, and on some level you will sense it. The feeling of being observed in an apparently empty landscape is psychologically taxing.

## Timing and Duration

### Best Approach Time

The description suggests the heat is oppressive and the haze traps warmth against the ground. If you have control over when you arrive, come in the early morning — the coolest part of the day, when the concrete hasn't yet absorbed the day's heat, and when the animals may be most active (giving you the best behavioral observation opportunities before the midday torpor sets in).

### Duration

A thorough visit — residential quarter, the avenue, the factory complex exterior, the elevated walkway, the processing yard observation — could be accomplished in six to ten hours if you move methodically and pause for observation. That's a full day in a hot, contaminated environment with heavy PPE and no resupply. It's physically demanding.

Do not plan to overnight inside the settlement unless absolutely necessary. The large animals are active at night (the description mentions they choose locations "where the thermal mass of concrete keeps the ground warm through the night," suggesting they're present and active throughout the full day-night cycle). Your navigation challenges increase dramatically in darkness, even with lighting. The bird roosts are probably most active at dawn and dusk transitions. The rodent-columns may be nocturnal or crepuscular. Nighttime in this settlement puts you in an occupied city in the dark, wearing a respirator, with impaired visibility and unknown animal activity patterns. Avoid it.

### Extraction

Know your exit route. Know it before you enter. The hardpan extends in all directions with no visible road or landmark, and the haze limits visibility. If you entered from a specific compass bearing, mark it, note it, and maintain awareness of it throughout your visit. If your transit method has a fixed extraction point, flag it visibly from a distance. The flat, featureless hardpan in limited visibility is exactly the kind of environment where people get lost within sight of safety.

## Ethical Considerations

You are visiting someone else's home. The animals did not invite you, and the place has no human claim anymore — whatever process the works served, whatever population lived in those workers' housing blocks, they left. The evacuation is in the name. What came after is not your inheritance.

Do not leave trash. Do not leave food. Do not introduce biological material. Your waste, your food scraps, your biological matter — all of it should leave with you. An introduced food source in a stable ecosystem creates disruption. A novel pathogen you're carrying asymptomatically could sweep through an immunologically naive population. These animals have no exposure to human-associated bacteria, viruses, or parasites. Your breath, your skin cells, your waste are all potential vectors. Minimize your biological footprint as much as your PPE allows.

Do not take samples of the animals — fur, droppings, feathers — unless you have a specific and justified analytical purpose and the means to do it without pursuit, capture, or significant disturbance. Observational data — notes, photographs, behavioral descriptions — is your primary product here.

Do not damage or alter structures. The infrastructure is habitat now. A wall you knock down for a better view might be the windbreak that makes a particular microclimate viable for a territorial group. A pipe you dislodge might be part of the functioning drainage that keeps the settlement hyditable. You are a visitor in a working city. Leave the infrastructure intact.

## What You'll Bring Back

Not materials, if you've followed the guidance above. What you'll bring back is the experience of having been in a place that has completed a transition most post-human landscapes are still in the middle of. You'll have stood in a city that works — that has drainage, shelter, water, social structure, territorial boundaries, a food web (those rodent-columns are eating something; the large animals are eating something; the birds are eating something — the trophic structure is present even if the description doesn't elaborate it) — and none of it requires a single human being to maintain.

You'll carry the image of the processing yard seen from thirty feet up. The amber water. The hundreds of heavy brown bodies in and around it, a parliament of creatures that have never known the place as anything other than theirs. The flat pewter light. The sound of dripping water in the dark interior of a building that once processed something that poisoned the earth, now serving as a cathedral for silent, clicking birds.

And the feeling — persistent, quiet, undeniable — that you were witnessed the entire time. That the city noted your passage. That after you left, some large animal walked your route, sniffed where you'd stood, and then went back to its business without concern.

The Evacuated Works does not need you to understand it. It simply is what it is. Your job is to see it clearly, pass through it safely, and leave it as you found it.

Everything here is exactly what it is.
