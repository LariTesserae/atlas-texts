# Preparing for the Thirstwood Plateau: A Comprehensive Traveler's Guide

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before I address any practical matter, I need to talk about the nature of this place, because your preparation will fail if it's organized around the wrong assumptions.

The Thirstwood Plateau is not a wilderness excursion. It's not a city visit. It's not a pilgrimage to a sacred site, though it will feel like one. It is an encounter with a living computational megastructure that has been running continuously for longer than your home timeline's hominid lineage, and which hosts a human community that has partially merged its cognitive life with that structure. You are visiting a place where the infrastructure thinks, where fallen branches crawl, where the light you see is only the light that has been *refused* by the canopy above you, and where the perpetual roar overhead is the voice of a forest-sized organism flexing in winds you will never feel on your skin.

You will be safe if you are careful. You will be profoundly changed if you are open to it. You will be in danger primarily from falling debris, dehydration, sensory disorientation, and the subtler risk of interfacing with a network intelligence without adequate preparation. Let me walk through everything.

## Physical Environment and Survival Priorities

### Water — The Central Constraint

There is no water here. Not in the ground. Not in the air as harvestable humidity. Not pooled anywhere on the plateau surface. Not running, not seeping, not condensing. The stone itself is calcium silicate — it does not hold moisture, it does not release it. The trees operate on a biochemistry that has entirely replaced water with some other solvent system (likely the same glycerol-analog fluid that the gliding amphibians sweat). You cannot drink their sap. You cannot extract water from the local biology. You are a water-based organism visiting a world that has moved on from water.

**Bring all of your water with you.** Every drop. Calculate your needs generously:

- Base metabolic needs in a cool, still environment: roughly 2.5 liters per day for a moderately active adult.
- The ground-level air is cool and still, which helps. You won't be sweating heavily from heat or exertion in most cases.
- However, the air is profoundly dry. You will lose moisture through respiration and through passive transepidermal evaporation at a rate significantly higher than you're accustomed to. Add at least an extra liter per day for this insensible loss.
- Plan for 3.5 to 4 liters per day minimum, and carry enough for your entire intended stay plus a 40% emergency buffer.
- If you're planning a stay of more than a few days, you need to either carry an impractical volume of water or bring a closed-loop recycling system — a high-efficiency still that can reclaim moisture from your own exhalation and waste. These exist in several multiverse tech tiers. Get one rated for zero-input environments.

**Water storage:** Use sealed, rigid containers, not bladders or flexible bags. The fibrous litter on the plateau floor is biologically active — fallen branch material *crawls* — and while it seems to be seeking tree trunks rather than your gear, you don't want anything puncturable sitting on the ground overnight. Similarly, any organic or semi-organic container material (leather, certain bioplastics) should be treated with suspicion in an environment full of organisms that break down biological substrates.

### Air Quality

The air is breathable. The trees produce oxygen — or at least the local equivalent gas mix is compatible with human respiration, given that three thousand people live here without apparent respiratory support. However:

- The air at ground level is very still. In the settlement areas, where people and shellers and biological processes concentrate, there may be localized accumulation of metabolic byproducts. Pay attention to headaches or drowsiness, which may indicate pockets of elevated CO2 or unfamiliar trace gases.
- The fibrous litter that coats parts of the floor is a powder-fine organic particulate. When disturbed by walking, it becomes airborne. A simple cloth face covering or filtered mask is worthwhile for long traversals across heavily littered areas.
- The bioluminescent fungal films on the bark, which the gliding amphibians eat, may release spores. Unknown biochemistry means unknown allergenic potential. If you have any history of respiratory sensitivity, bring appropriate medication and a particulate-filtering mask rated for biological agents.

### Temperature

Cool at ground level, but not cold — the canopy insulates the plateau floor from the temperature extremes that prevail at the exposed cliff edges. Think of it as a perpetual mild autumn evening. A base layer and a light insulating layer should suffice for the settlement zone.

At the plateau edges, however, temperature drops sharply as the canopy thins and wind reaches you directly. The wind coming off the bare, treeless lowlands is described as thin and cold. If you plan to visit the edges — and you should, for the acoustic experience and the perspective — bring a proper windproof outer layer and gloves. The transition from still, protected air to exposed, wind-scoured edge can be abrupt.

### Light

This is important. The ground level of the Thirstwood Plateau is *dim*. Not dark, but dim — lit only by the copper-colored wavelengths that the black leaves above have rejected as unusable, supplemented by the violet bioluminescence of the fusion nodes. Your eyes will adapt, but it will take time, and the color palette is deeply unfamiliar. You are navigating in a world lit by waste light and biological glow.

- Bring a reliable light source, but use it judiciously. The inhabitants live in this light and their settlement is organized around the encouraged bioluminescent nodes. A bright white light will be disorienting to *you* in this context, wrecking your dark adaptation, and may also disturb the local fauna and the photosensitive fungal films.
- A dim, warm-toned headlamp on its lowest setting is appropriate for navigating unfamiliar terrain.
- Better yet: bring a light source tuned to the red-orange end of the spectrum, which the canopy already allows through. This will feel more natural to the environment and to your adjusted eyes.
- Give yourself a full day to dark-adapt before attempting any significant navigation or exploration. Your first hours will feel much darker than the plateau actually is.

### The Stone Floor

The plateau surface is compacted calcium silicate, smoothed by ancient wind erosion into "undulating bone-colored shelves." This is a hard, unyielding surface. It is also described as undulating, meaning it rises and falls in broad, gentle waves.

- Wear shoes with firm, supportive soles. You'll be walking on stone for the duration of your visit. Cushioning matters.
- The surface is smooth but not polished — wind erosion creates a fine-grained texture that should provide adequate traction in dry conditions. And conditions are always dry.
- Watch for the fibrous litter drifts. They coat patches of floor "like dry snow" — and like dry snow, they may conceal slight variations in the stone surface, or may be slippery underfoot if the powdered material is deep enough.
- Most critically: **watch for crawling branch fragments.** Fallen branch material moves across the stone floor under its own power, probing toward the nearest trunk. A freshly fallen fragment can be the size of a shipping container and will splinter into pieces that "twitch and curl with apparent purpose," creeping several meters within hours. A piece of mobile wood the size of your arm, dragging itself by cilia-like fibers, is a tripping hazard you've never trained for. Scan the ground ahead of you, especially after you hear a crack and impact from a branch fall.

## The Falling Debris Problem

This requires its own section because it is your single greatest physical danger on the Thirstwood Plateau.

Branches fall from a hundred meters up. They are described as being the size of shipping containers. They hit the stone floor with impacts you feel through your feet. This is, effectively, living in an environment where multi-ton objects fall from the sky at irregular, unpredictable intervals.

The settlement exists in this environment, which means the inhabitants have either developed predictive methods (likely through the tree network interface), or have accepted the risk, or both. As a visitor without network access, you are at a significant disadvantage.

**Mitigation strategies:**

1. **Stay near the settlement.** The inhabitants have presumably arranged their structures in locations with lower branch-fall frequency, or in spots sheltered by trunk buttresses and trained root walls.

2. **Listen.** The crack that precedes a branch fall is described as rifle-shot loud and distinct from the general canopy roar. If you hear it, move immediately toward the nearest trunk. The branches fall in the *gaps between* trunks — the open columns of air. The safest place is directly beside a trunk, under the root crown, where the tree's own structure provides overhead cover.

3. **Watch the shellers.** These animals live here full-time and navigate the open stone flats. If a group of shellers suddenly breaks formation and moves toward trunks, follow them.

4. **Don't linger in the open flats.** When crossing the wide gaps between trunks, do so with purpose and awareness. Don't camp, rest, or sleep in the open stone areas far from any trunk.

5. **Consider a helmet.** Even small debris — splinters, seed pods, fragments of bark — rains down continuously from a canopy under constant wind stress a hundred meters up. A hard shell helmet with a brim is unglamorous but sensible.

## Nutrition and Sustenance

Beyond water, you need food. The local biology is profoundly alien in its biochemistry — trees that don't use water, animals that cultivate resin on their bodies, amphibians that sweat glycerol, black leaves tuned to absent wavelengths. The probability that any of this is nutritionally compatible with your metabolism is very low, and the risk of toxicity is very high.

**Do not eat anything native to the plateau without prior biochemical analysis.** Not the fibrous litter (which is biologically active and crawls), not the shellers (whose resin-based carapaces suggest exotic chemistry), not the gliding amphibians (whose glycerol secretions are already a warning sign), not the bioluminescent fungal films, and absolutely not anything from the trees themselves.

Bring all your food. Calorie-dense, shelf-stable, requiring minimal water for preparation. The cool, dry environment is actually excellent for food preservation, so spoilage is unlikely, but keep everything sealed against the fibrous litter dust and any fungal spores.

The settlement inhabitants eat *something*. They've been here long enough to establish a community of three thousand. If they offer you food, ask careful questions about its origin and preparation. It's possible they've developed cultivated food sources compatible with human biology, or that they import from elsewhere. It's also possible that their long integration with the tree network has altered their own biochemistry in ways that make their diet unsuitable for you.

## Sound and Hearing Protection

The acoustic environment of the Thirstwood Plateau is extraordinary, and it is relentless.

The canopy roar is compared to a stadium, to surf against cliffs. It is continuous. It modulates but does not stop. It includes frequencies low enough to vibrate in your sternum — infrasound components that you feel more than hear, that can induce unease, nausea, or a vague sense of dread in the unprepared. It includes mid-range components from the general friction and groaning of the branch network. It includes sharp percussive cracks from seed pods and falling branches. And it includes resonant tones from hollow branch segments acting as organ pipes — sounds that emerge suddenly, hang in the air as a felt vibration, and then dissolve.

This sound never stops. You will sleep in it. You will eat in it. You will try to think in it, and you will find that after a day or two your brain begins to parse it as background, the way city dwellers parse traffic noise, but the parsing is never complete because the sound is too complex, too variable, too *structured*. You will keep catching patterns, almost-melodies, rhythmic sequences that seem intentional, and your attention will be repeatedly pulled upward toward the roaring ceiling.

**Bring hearing protection.** Good-quality earplugs with a flat frequency response curve — the kind that reduce volume evenly across the spectrum without distorting the sound. You don't want to block the sound entirely (you need to hear branch-fall cracks as a safety warning), but you need the ability to reduce the overall level for rest and for cognitive function.

Consider also bringing noise-canceling earpieces with adjustable pass-through, which would let you tune the level of canopy roar you're experiencing in real time. This is the luxury option, but on a multi-day visit, it may preserve your sanity.

Pay particular attention to the infrasound. Prolonged exposure to low-frequency sound in the 7-19 Hz range can cause a variety of physiological effects: anxiety, disorientation, visual disturbance, feelings of presence or dread. The bass tones described as "vibrating in your sternum" are squarely in this territory. If you find yourself experiencing inexplicable unease, difficulty concentrating, or a persistent sense that something is watching you (beyond the actual things that are watching you from the canopy), consider that infrasound exposure may be the cause, not supernatural intuition. Rest, hearing protection, and a walk to the plateau edge where the canopy noise diminishes will help.

The acoustic interference zone at the plateau edge, where the canopy roar and the open wind create nodes of cancellation — this is worth experiencing deliberately. The locals use it for cognitive clarity, to "think in their own voices." You may find it profoundly relieving after days immersed in the sound-field, a sudden silence that feels like surfacing from deep water.

## The Mycorrhizal Interface: To Connect or Not to Connect

This is the most important decision you will make about your visit, and it requires the most careful preparation.

The tree network is a distributed biological computer of enormous scale and antiquity. The inhabitants interface with it through implanted mycorrhizal intermediaries — fungal filaments threaded into root crowns that translate between human neurology and the tree's electrochemical signal language. The interface points are the warm, opalescent calluses on the trunks, the ones that hum just below hearing.

You will be offered the opportunity to connect. Maybe not explicitly — the inhabitants may be oblique about it, or they may assume you understand what the calluses are and let you approach on your own terms. But the option will be present, and the temptation will be significant. You are standing inside a thinking forest that has been processing information for geological time scales. The curiosity is natural.

**My strong recommendation: do not interface on your first visit, and if you do interface, prepare extensively.**

Here's why:

1. **The fungal intermediary is an implant.** It involves threading biological filaments from an alien organism into the root crown of a tree, where they form a translation layer between the tree's signaling chemistry and your nervous system. This is not like putting on headphones. It is a biological merger, however temporary, with a non-human organism. The long-term effects on a first-time visitor from an unrelated timeline are unknown. The inhabitants have adapted over generations. You haven't.

2. **The network is old and vast and not human.** When queried about the canopy creatures, it returns signals interpreted as "kin" or "co-process." This is a mind — if "mind" is even the right word — that thinks in categories that don't map to human cognition. Interfacing with it means exposing your neurology to signal patterns that were not designed for human brains, mediated by a fungal translator whose fidelity and intentions are unclear.

3. **The inhabitants partially live within this network.** Their "city" extends to the horizon through the canopy connections. They go to the plateau edge specifically to experience thinking *without* the network. This suggests that the interface is not a simple tool you pick up and put down — it is immersive, possibly habit-forming, and it blurs the boundary between your own cognition and the network's processes in ways that may be difficult to reverse fully.

4. **You don't know the signal language.** The inhabitants have learned to interpret what the network sends. You will be receiving raw, uninterpreted signals from an alien computational substrate through a fungal intermediary you just met. This is the neurological equivalent of drinking from an unlabeled bottle.

**If you decide to interface anyway:**

- Do it briefly. Minutes, not hours. Hands on the callus, not full immersion.
- Have a companion who is NOT interfacing stand beside you and pull you away at a pre-agreed time signal.
- Do not interface alone, at night, or while fatigued, dehydrated, or emotionally distressed.
- Before touching the callus, sit beside it for a period. Feel the sub-audible hum. Let your nervous system acclimate to the proximity before making contact.
- Pay attention to the inhabitants' reactions. If they seem concerned, surprised, or if they try to dissuade you, listen to them. They understand the network and you don't.
- Afterward, go to the plateau edge. Stand in the cancellation zone. Think your own thoughts. Make sure they're still your own thoughts. Check that your inner monologue sounds like *you* and not like something translated from the electrochemical gradients of a forest.

## Gear List

Let me consolidate the practical items:

**Survival Essentials:**
- Water: 3.5-4 liters per day, full supply for your stay plus 40% buffer, OR a closed-loop water recycling system
- Food: complete calorie supply for your stay, sealed packaging, calorie-dense, minimal water needed for preparation
- Rigid, sealed water containers (not flexible bladders)
- Water purification as backup if you encounter any unexpected moisture source (unlikely, but carry it anyway)

**Clothing:**
- Comfortable base layer for the cool, still ground-level environment
- Light insulating mid-layer
- Windproof outer shell with hood for plateau edge visits
- Gloves for edge visits
- Sturdy, well-cushioned, hard-soled boots suitable for extended walking on stone
- Extra socks (your feet will thank you on bare rock)

**Protection:**
- Hard-shell helmet with brim (falling debris)
- Hearing protection: high-fidelity earplugs (flat frequency reduction) and/or active noise-canceling earpieces with adjustable pass-through
- Particulate-filtering face mask or respirator for dusty areas
- Eye protection for edge visits where wind-driven grit is possible

**Lighting:**
- Adjustable headlamp, preferably with a red/warm-orange mode
- Backup light source
- Avoid bright white lights; they'll ruin your adaptation and may disturb local biology

**Navigation and Safety:**
- Detailed notes on the settlement layout (if available from prior travelers)
- A reliable timepiece — the constant dim light makes it very easy to lose track of time
- A whistle or other non-electronic signaling device for emergencies
- Rope or cord (useful near the plateau edge, which is a cliff)
- A compact first-aid kit with emphasis on wound care (splintered alien wood in a wound is not something you want to leave untreated) and respiratory medications

**Documentation:**
- Audio recording equipment if you want to capture the canopy sound (you do — this is an unreproducible acoustic environment)
- Low-light-capable visual recording if you want images (standard cameras will struggle in the dim copper-and-violet light)
- A journal. Write things down. The acoustic environment, the potential infrasound effects, and the proximity to a network intelligence that blurs cognitive boundaries all mean your memory of this place may be less reliable than you'd like.

**Interface Preparation (if you choose to connect):**
- A trusted companion willing to stay un-connected and monitor you
- A pre-agreed time limit and extraction signal
- Baseline cognitive self-assessment: before you touch any callus, write down a few specific personal memories, your name, where you came from, what you care about. After the interface, check these against your experience. Make sure nothing has shifted, been rewritten, or feels unfamiliar.

## Social and Cultural Preparation

The three thousand inhabitants of the Thirstwood Plateau settlement are, in some sense, part of the forest. They live in structures grown by the trees, lit by encouraged bioluminescence, and their cognitive lives are partially networked through the mycorrhizal interface into a system that extends beyond the horizon. They use the word "encouraged" for how they interact with the trees, which tells you something about the relationship — it is collaborative, not commanding. They don't control the forest. They participate in it.

This means:

- **Respect the trees.** Not in an abstract, ecological sense. In the specific sense that the trees are arguably sentient, certainly computational, and the inhabitants consider themselves in a relationship with them. Don't carve bark, don't damage the opalescent calluses, don't interfere with the crawling fallen branch fragments that are trying to return to their trunks. These fragments are, in a sense, wounded parts of a thinking organism trying to rejoin their body. Let them.

- **Respect the shellers.** They walk through the settlement freely. They are not pets and not pests. They are part of the ecosystem that the community coexists with. Don't chase them, don't try to ride them, don't attempt to take their resin shells as souvenirs.

- **Respect the quiet at the edge.** If you encounter inhabitants standing in the acoustic cancellation zones at the plateau edge, give them space. They are there for a reason that is psychologically important — to hear themselves think without the network. It is, in a sense, a form of meditation or self-reclamation. Don't interrupt it.

- **Ask before touching the calluses.** Even if you don't intend to interface, don't casually put your hands on the opalescent interface points. You don't know the threshold for connection, and a casual touch might be more than you bargained for.

- **Be prepared for a different tempo of conversation.** People who are partially networked into a geological-age intelligence may not communicate the way you're used to. They may pause for long periods, processing input from the network as well as from you. They may answer questions you haven't asked yet, if the network has modeled your likely queries. They may seem distracted, or they may seem *more* attentive than any person you've ever met, because they have access to cognitive resources beyond their own brains. Be patient. Be genuine. Don't rush.

- **They chose this.** Or their ancestors chose it, and they've continued to choose it. Living fused with a computational forest on an arid plateau on a dying world, lit by rejected light, surrounded by eternal sound — this is not a hardship posting. This is a community that has found something extraordinary and committed to it. Approach with the curiosity and respect you'd bring to any culture that has discovered something you haven't.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section I want to emphasize most, because the Thirstwood Plateau's greatest challenges are not physical. They are perceptual and existential.

### The Dimness

You will spend your entire visit in light conditions roughly equivalent to deep twilight. Not darkness — but never full light, either. The copper glow filtering through the canopy is the light the trees don't want. You are living in the spectrum of refusal. This has a psychological effect that goes beyond simple low visibility. There's a persistent sense that you're seeing the world through a filter, that the real light is somewhere above you, being consumed by the black leaves of an organism you can't see clearly. Some travelers report a creeping claustrophobia despite the enormous open spaces between the trunks — the oppressiveness is not from the walls closing in, but from the ceiling being *alive* and *total* and *feeding* on the light above you.

**Mitigation:** Spend time near the trunks where the bioluminescent nodes are brightest. The violet glow is a different quality of light than the copper filter-light, and it breaks the monotony. Visit the plateau edge periodically to see open sky. Even the bruised coppery sky is a relief after days under the canopy ceiling.

### The Sound

I've discussed this above, but let me add the psychological dimension. The canopy roar is not just loud — it is *complex enough to sound intentional.* Your brain will try to find patterns in it, will try to interpret it as language or music. It is neither, or it is both, or it is something the tree network is doing that has aspects of both but is fundamentally neither. The intermittent bass tones that vibrate your chest will feel meaningful. The sudden silences when the wind shifts will feel ominous. You will, at some point, become convinced that the sound is trying to communicate with you.

It may be. Or it may not. The point is that your brain's pattern-recognition systems will be running at full tilt, constantly, for the duration of your visit, and this is exhausting. Budget your cognitive energy. Use hearing protection for rest periods. Don't try to interpret everything.

### The Scale

You are standing under a single connected organism that stretches from horizon to horizon. The columns supporting its canopy are the size of buildings. The canopy itself is a computational network of unknown but enormous processing capacity. The community of three thousand people, with all their intelligence and culture and technology, is a small, recent addition to a system that has been running since before their species existed.

This can trigger a very specific form of existential vertigo — a sense that you are trivially small, that your visit is meaninglessly brief, that the thoughts you are thinking are a tiny flicker against the slow, vast cognition happening above you. This feeling is accurate. It is also survivable. Humans are small and brief in most contexts. The difference here is that the context is visible and alive and *looking back at you* through reflective eyes from branch-holes a hundred meters up.

Sit with it. Breathe. Write in your journal. Remember that the inhabitants live here, have children here, tell jokes here, argue about trivial things here. Being small inside a vast thinking system does not erase the value of your own small thoughts. If the scale gets to you, find a sheller group and walk with them for a while. They are the right size for companionship. Their amber glow is comforting. Their clicking mandibles are cheerfully mundane.

### The Departure

Leaving the Thirstwood Plateau is, by many accounts, harder than arriving. Not physically — you walk back the way you came, or to whatever transit point your multiverse travel method requires. But psychologically, you are leaving a place where the infrastructure was alive and thinking, where the light had texture and meaning, where the sound was a constant companion, where the very air between the trunks felt *held* in a way that open air does not. You are returning to places where the buildings are dead and the light is untouched and the silence is not the silence of acoustic cancellation but the silence of absence.

Some travelers report a persistent sense of loneliness after leaving — not for the people, but for the *network*, for the vast background presence of a thinking forest that they could feel even without touching the calluses. This fades, but it takes time. Be gentle with yourself in the days after departure.

If you *did* interface, the adjustment period may be longer and more disorienting. You may find yourself listening for the canopy roar in environments where no such sound exists. You may feel an urge to press your palms flat against trees in your home world, reaching for a connection that isn't there. You may dream in violet light, or hear bass tones on the edge of sleep, or catch yourself thinking thoughts that don't quite feel like your own — thoughts with the patience and cadence of something that measures time in geological epochs.

This passes. It always passes. You were a visitor, not a resident, and your neurology will reassert its own patterns in time.

But you will remember. The dim copper light on bone-white stone. The cathedral of living columns and breathing ceiling. The roar above and the stillness below and the warm hum of the calluses under your palm. You will remember, and some part of you will always be listening for the sound of a forest that thinks.

## Final Notes

**Duration recommendation:** Three to five days for a meaningful visit. Less than two days won't give you time to dark-adapt, acclimate to the sound, and engage meaningfully with the community. More than seven days increases the risk of psychological dependence on the sound-field environment, especially if you're sensitive to the network's ambient influence.

**Best approach vector:** If your transit method allows you to choose your arrival point, arrive at the plateau edge rather than directly in the settlement. Walk in through the thinning canopy. Let the sound build gradually. Let the light dim by degrees. Let the trunks gather around you. Experience the transition instead of being thrown into the middle. The walk from edge to settlement takes a few hours, and those hours are among the most remarkable of the entire experience — the slow submersion into a living architecture, the moment when you realize the canopy has closed above you completely and the sky is gone.

**One last thing:** When you stand among the trunks and look up and see the faint violet glow of the fusion nodes pulsing slowly in the darkness a hundred meters above you, like the circulatory system of something impossibly large seen through translucent skin — when the bass tone hits your sternum and the canopy groans and a face with calm, reflective eyes looks down at you from the branch network — when the shellers walk past you in their amber glow, clicking, unhurried, unbothered by your presence —

Remember that you are not an observer. You are inside the organism. You have been inside it since the canopy closed over your head. The question was never whether you would be changed by this place. The question is what you do with the change afterward.

Go well. Carry enough water. Watch for falling branches. And when you press your palm to the trunk, if you choose to, listen carefully to what comes back.
