# Preparing for The Drowning Works

---

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a ruin. You are not diving a wreck. You are entering a living system that finished its transformation long ago and now exists in a state of deep, stable equilibrium. The Drowning Works is not hostile, but it is profoundly indifferent to your presence in the way that geology is indifferent — not through malice, but through operating on timescales where your visit is a statistical flicker. Every preparation you make should reflect this understanding. You are a temporary disturbance in something ancient and self-contained. Your goal is to witness it without breaking it, and to survive the witnessing.

---

## Thermal Preparation

This is the first and most serious concern, and you must not underestimate it.

Two to three degrees Celsius, sustained, with no prospect of warming up until you leave. The cold here is not a weather event. It is a material property of the place. The water will pull heat from your body at roughly twenty-five times the rate that air at the same temperature would, and it will do so constantly, patiently, without fluctuation. There is no warm room to retreat to. There is no sun cycle that will bring a few degrees of relief. The half-degree warm patch near the eastern wall is not a resource you can use — it is barely detectable, and lingering near it means lingering near whatever chemical processes are feeding the bacterial mats, which you should not do for extended periods.

**Dry suit, not wet suit.** This is non-negotiable. A wetsuit works by trapping a thin layer of water against your skin and letting your body warm it. At two degrees, your body will lose that fight. You need full thermal isolation. A trilaminate dry suit with a heavy-weight undersuit — 400-gram Thinsulate or equivalent. Test the seals obsessively before entry. A leak in a dry suit at these temperatures is not an inconvenience; it is the beginning of incapacitation. Your manual dexterity will begin to fail within minutes of cold water contacting skin.

**Layer your undersuit thoughtfully.** A wicking base layer against the skin. A mid-weight fleece. Then the insulating layer. You will not be exerting yourself heavily — the space is small, the movement is slow, and thrashing around would be destructive to the environment anyway — so you will not generate much metabolic heat. You are managing heat retention, not heat regulation.

**Protect your hands and head.** Dry gloves integrated with the suit's ring system. The thickest you can manage while retaining enough dexterity to operate your equipment. Your head — if exposed — will hemorrhage heat. A thick neoprene hood, 7mm minimum, properly fitted so it doesn't channel cold water down your neck. Consider whether your hood integrates with your mask seal.

**Chemical heat packs** in the torso area of your undersuit can extend your comfortable time, but understand their limitations — they are finite, they cannot be replaced during the dive, and if one shifts position it can create a hot spot that distracts you. Place them flat against your core, secured so they cannot migrate.

**Know your time limits.** Even fully suited, you should plan for a maximum immersion time based on conservative thermal models. The insidious thing about cold water is that hypothermia begins with impaired judgment before it progresses to impaired movement. You will not necessarily notice yourself becoming stupid. Plan your exit time in advance, set it on a timer you can feel vibrate against your wrist, and when it goes off, you leave. No exceptions. The Drowning Works will be there next time. It has been there for decades. It will wait.

## Breathing and Gas Management

The description does not specify whether the space is fully flooded with no air pockets, but the absence of any mentioned surface and the uniform submersion of all objects — including the sealed hatch, the ceiling-mounted pipes, the cable runs — suggests total inundation. Prepare accordingly.

**Closed-circuit rebreather (CCR)** is strongly preferred over open-circuit scuba for several reasons:

1. **Bubbles.** Open-circuit exhaust produces a constant stream of bubbles that will rise through the space, disturbing silt on every surface they contact on the way up, and potentially interacting with the existing bubble stream from the copper pipe in ways that disrupt the ninety-second rhythm that appears to be part of the room's established pattern. You are visiting an equilibrium. Every bubble you add is a perturbation. A rebreather produces no exhaust bubbles during normal operation.

2. **Gas duration.** You do not know how long the ingress route is. The description mentions flooded corridors, sealed openings, conduits disappearing into concrete. Getting to this room may involve a significant penetration dive through confined, silted, zero-visibility passages. Your gas supply needs to account for ingress, time on site, egress, and a healthy emergency reserve. A CCR's gas efficiency is dramatically superior for this kind of profile.

3. **Warmth.** Breathing cold gas cools you from the inside. A rebreather's scrubber reaction is exothermic — it warms the breathing loop slightly. At these temperatures, this is a meaningful advantage.

4. **Silence.** Open-circuit regulators are loud. The sharp hiss of inhalation and the roar of exhaust bubbles will obliterate the acoustic environment of the Drowning Works. The pump-throb that lives below hearing, the ninety-second tick of the bubble release, the occasional *tonk* of settling metal — these are the voice of the place, and you will not hear them if you are generating your own noise. A rebreather is nearly silent. It lets you listen.

**Carry a bailout bottle** regardless. Stage it at the entry point if the penetration route allows it, or carry it on your person if it doesn't. Your bailout plan should get you from the farthest point of your dive back to safe air. Do the calculations. Then add margin.

**Oxygen management is critical.** The water here is biologically active — bacterial mats metabolize, chemical processes consume and produce gases, the pipe systems may vent unknown gases into the water. Monitor your PO2 continuously. Carry redundant oxygen sensors. If your rebreather starts giving you readings that don't make sense, trust the instruments, not your instincts, and begin your exit.

## Navigation and Penetration

You need to find this room first, and the route in will likely be the most dangerous part of the visit.

The Drowning Works is described as being inside a flooded building — possibly an industrial facility, possibly a subterranean installation, with corridors, conduits, sealed hatches, and concrete walls. This is overhead-environment diving in a structure that was not designed for divers. Every principle of cave and wreck penetration applies, and some of them apply more forcefully than usual because of the unique conditions.

**Run a continuous guideline** from your entry point. This is the single most important piece of safety equipment you carry. In zero-visibility conditions — and you will encounter zero visibility; more on this shortly — the guideline is the only thing that connects you to the exit. Use appropriate reels, place directional markers, tie off at every significant junction. If you cannot maintain a continuous line to the exit, you do not proceed further.

**Expect constrictions.** The description mentions crawlspaces, collapsed stairwells, pipe-runs, openings that have since silted shut. Your route may require you to pass through spaces that are barely body-sized, potentially with equipment removal and push-through. Practice this in controlled conditions before you attempt it here. Panic in a constriction in cold dark silted water kills people.

**Expect silt.** Decades of mineral deposition and sediment accumulation mean every surface is coated. The corridors leading to the room will be worse than the room itself, because the room at least has currents from the pumps to distribute particles. The approach corridors may have silt deposits measured in tens of centimeters, unconsolidated, ready to bloom into total whiteout at the slightest disturbance. Use a modified flutter kick or frog kick. Keep your fins off the floor. Keep your hands off the walls unless you are following your guideline. Move slowly. Move deliberately. Accept that your visibility may drop to zero for periods and rely on your line and your touch.

**Map as you go.** Mental mapping is insufficient. Carry a wrist slate and note junctions, distances, constriction points, and any features that can serve as tactile landmarks. You will not be able to see on the way out if you have disturbed silt on the way in. You need to be able to navigate by feel and by the knowledge you recorded.

## Lighting

This requires careful thought, because the Drowning Works has its own light — barely, marginally, just enough to differentiate gray-green from absolute black — and your relationship to that existing light is part of the experience.

**Carry three independent light sources.** This is standard for overhead environments and not negotiable. Primary, backup, and tertiary. All pressure-rated, all cold-rated, all tested. Lights fail. In this environment, light failure means total darkness in a confined space full of obstructions. Three sources.

**But consider how you use them.**

Your primary light should be powerful enough to navigate safely and to illuminate hazards, but understand that any light you bring will fundamentally alter the visual character of the space. The Drowning Works exists in near-total darkness. The objects in it — the valve wheels, the control panel, the flowstone staircase, the coffee mug — have been unseen for decades. When you turn a light on them, you are seeing them for the first time in a way they have never been seen before, not even when the room was dry, because now they are coated in mineral time, and the way light interacts with calcium deposits and patina and bacterial film and suspended particles in cold water is unlike any other visual experience. The beam will scatter in the particulate water, creating a diffuse glow that illuminates a cone of murk. Objects will appear at the edge of this cone as shapes first, then textures, then details. The coffee mug will emerge from the silt like an artifact from an excavation.

**Consider carrying a red-filtered light as a secondary.** Red light will preserve what night vision you have, will not cause the same degree of backscatter in particulate water, and will give you a sense of the space that is less violently different from its natural state. Under red light, the room will look like what it is: something ancient and submerged and barely visible. Under white light, it will look like a crime scene.

**Turn your lights off.** At least once, if you can do so safely while maintaining contact with your guideline or a fixed reference point, turn everything off and sit in the dark of the Drowning Works and let your eyes adjust. Wait. It takes time — several minutes at least. And then see if you can detect it: the faint gray-green nothing that might be bioluminescence, might be your eyes lying, might be some residual photochemical process in the bacterial mats. Watch for the capacitor flash. You will not know when it is coming. You may wait five minutes or twenty. And then the briefest dull orange pulse from behind the corroded panel, and for an instant the room is revealed in its own light, on its own terms — full of shapes, full of objects, full of the dense geometry of machines that have become minerals — and then gone. This moment is the reason you came.

## Equipment Considerations

**Buoyancy control must be precise.** You are operating in a cluttered space where touching anything means potentially dislodging mineral formations that took decades to grow, disturbing silt that took decades to settle, or damaging organisms that exist nowhere else. Your buoyancy needs to be neutral to within a fraction of a kilogram, and you need to be able to hold position in the water column without finning, without hand-holds, without contact with any surface. Practice static trim. Practice hovering. Practice being motionless. The Drowning Works rewards stillness.

**Minimize your profile.** Clip everything tight, streamline everything, tuck away anything that could snag on the dense obstacle environment. Dangling gauges catch on valve wheels. Loose hoses loop around pipe runs. The room is described as architecturally dense — objects everywhere, cable runs, the staircase, the workbench, the control panel. You will be navigating a three-dimensional maze of industrial archaeology, and every piece of loose equipment is a potential entanglement point.

**Do not carry a dive knife on your leg.** Carry cutting tools — shears, a line cutter, a small blade — but mount them where they won't snag: on your harness chest, on your wrist, on your BCD shoulder. If you become entangled in the cable runs or in your own guideline, you need to be able to reach your cutting tool with either hand while potentially unable to change your body position.

**Consider a DPV (diver propulsion vehicle) — and then reject the idea.** If the approach corridors are long, a scooter might seem appealing, but in silted, confined passages with constrictions, a DPV is a liability. It creates prop wash that will destroy visibility. It is bulky in tight spaces. It adds a piece of equipment that can fail, entangle, or pin you. The Drowning Works is a place you reach slowly or not at all.

## Documentation

If you wish to record the Drowning Works:

**Still photography** will serve you better than video. The particulate water means that any light source mounted near the camera lens will create massive backscatter — every suspended particle between you and your subject will reflect light back as a bright spot, producing images that look like snowstorms. The solution is off-camera lighting: mount your strobes or continuous lights on arms extended well away from the lens axis, and aim them to sidelight or backlight your subjects. This will also produce more evocative images — the flowstone staircase lit from the side, each mineral-encrusted step casting shadows; the coffee mug on the workbench in a pool of light with darkness pressing in from every direction; the string of bubbles from the copper pipe, caught in a cross-beam, ascending like something celestial.

**Audio recording** in underwater environments is specialized but not impossible, and this is a place worth recording. A hydrophone rated for the depth (which is likely not great — this is a flooded building, not a deep ocean) could capture the pump-throb, the bubble-tick, the occasional settling *tonk*. The resulting recording would be unlike anything else: the sound of a machine ecology, the heartbeat of a system that outlived its purpose and kept running.

**Do not remove any objects.** I know you are thinking about the coffee mug. Everyone thinks about the coffee mug. The mug is part of the reef now. Its bacterial colony is part of the food web. Its silt fill is a micro-habitat. It is not a souvenir. It is a geological feature. Leave it where it is.

## Psychological Preparation

This is not an adrenaline dive. This is not a recreational dive. This is closer to meditation in a hostile environment, and it requires a particular mental posture that you should cultivate before you go.

**Comfort with darkness.** If you are uncomfortable in the dark — truly dark, not dim-room dark, but dark-as-the-inside-of-stone dark — you need to work on this before visiting the Drowning Works. Practice sitting in complete darkness for extended periods. Learn the difference between the darkness that frightens you and the darkness that simply exists. The Drowning Works has the second kind. It is not threatening. It is not concealing anything. It simply is.

**Comfort with confinement.** You will be in a room with walls you can sense, in water that reveals things only at arm's length. The space is definite and bounded. If enclosed spaces create anxiety for you, this will amplify it. Work with a therapist or a dive instructor experienced in overhead environments. Understand your responses. Know what your panic looks like from the inside so you can recognize it before it takes over.

**Comfort with slow time.** The Drowning Works operates on a timescale where ninety seconds between bubble releases is a rapid event. The capacitor flashes at intervals long enough that "memory blurs." The bacterial mats grow slowly enough to be functionally static during your visit. If you enter this space looking for stimulation, looking for the next thing, restless with the need for events, you will have a miserable and pointless experience. You need to be able to descend into the tempo of the place. Slow your breathing. Slow your eye movements. Slow your thoughts. Let the space teach you its rhythm. The pump-throb is not something you hear; it is something you eventually become aware of, and that becoming-aware takes time and stillness.

**Expect emotional responses you did not predict.** There is something about the Drowning Works — the combination of cold, dark, silence, industrial decay, and the persistence of the machinery — that affects people in ways that are hard to anticipate. Some visitors report a deep calm, a meditative peace, a sense of geological time that makes their own concerns feel trivially brief. Others report a crushing, suffocating sadness — not fear, but grief, a mourning for the unnamed people who built this place and installed these machines and drank coffee from that mug and are now so thoroughly gone that even the building they made has become something else. Others report a strange awe, almost religious in character, at the fact that the pumps are still running, that something is still faithfully maintaining a system that no longer has a purpose, that the machines have become their own reason for existence. All of these responses are valid. None of them are the "correct" response. The Drowning Works is a Rorschach test administered by infrastructure. You will see in it what you bring to it.

**Debrief afterward.** Not just a dive debrief — equipment checks, gas analysis, thermal recovery — but an emotional debrief. Talk to someone about what you experienced. Write it down. Sit with it. The Drowning Works has a tendency to linger in the mind in a way that other dives do not. The bubble-tick especially. You may find yourself counting ninety-second intervals for days afterward, listening for something that is no longer there, waiting for a small, soft sound that marks the passage of time in a room where time has almost, but not quite, stopped.

## Ethical Considerations

**You are entering an ecosystem.** It is small, simple, and fragile. The pale shrimp-things — the apex predators of this two-link food web — may exist only in this room. You are potentially the greatest disturbance this ecology has experienced since the water first came in. Every bubble you release, every current you create with your fins, every photon you introduce, every degree of heat your body radiates into the water — all of these are perturbations. Minimize them. The Drowning Works does not need you. You need it.

**Do not touch the machinery.** Do not turn valves. Do not attempt to open the hatch. Do not test whether the control panel's buttons still depress. Do not attempt to access the area behind the corroded steel panel where the capacitor discharges. These systems are in a state of dynamic equilibrium that has been decades in the making. The pumps are running. You do not know what they are connected to. You do not know what would happen if you changed something. The system is not yours to adjust.

**Consider whether you should visit at all.** This is a genuine question, not a rhetorical one. The Drowning Works has been running without human observers for a very long time. It does not benefit from your presence. The bacterial mats do not care that you find them interesting. The shrimp-things would prefer you not be there. The coffee mug has no opinion. You are visiting for yourself — for the experience, for the knowledge, for the strange peace or strange grief or strange awe that the place provides. That is a valid reason. But it comes with a responsibility to ensure that your visit does not degrade the thing you came to witness. If you cannot dive without disturbing silt, stay outside. If your equipment is not dialed in to the point of negligible environmental impact, train more. If you are not certain you can resist the urge to touch the coffee mug, do not go.

## The Exit

When your timer vibrates — and it will vibrate sooner than you want it to — begin your exit. Retrace your guideline. Move slowly. Manage your silt. Do not look back, because looking back means turning, and turning means fin wash, and fin wash means silt, and silt means reducing visibility for anyone who might follow your line after you.

As you move through the corridors away from the room, the pump-throb will fade. This is the moment that many visitors describe as the most difficult — not the entry, not the dark, not the cold, but the leaving, the feeling of the room's rhythm receding behind you as the concrete swallows its sound. You will be tempted to stop and listen for it one more time. If your gas and thermal margins allow a brief pause, take it. Feel the throb fade to nothing. Know that it continues behind you, in the dark, in the cold, in the room where the bubbles rise and the pale things drift and the machines run and run and run.

Surface. Breathe air. Feel warm. Feel light. Feel the wrongness of a world that changes and moves and makes noise and has weather.

Count to ninety.

Listen for the tick.

It isn't there.
