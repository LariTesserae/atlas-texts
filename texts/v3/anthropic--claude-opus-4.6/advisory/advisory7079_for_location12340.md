# Preparing for the Roaring Hollow

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it twice. Let the implications settle. This is not a place of spectacle. It is not dangerous in the way a cliff edge is dangerous or a predator is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that prolonged sensory deprivation is dangerous, in the way that ego dissolution is dangerous — it presents conditions under which your ordinary sense of yourself as a bounded, thinking creature may become difficult to maintain. The sound is the central fact. Everything else — the darkness, the wet, the cold, the isolation — serves to strip away the distractions that would normally let you ignore a sound like this. You are going into a stone bowl designed by erosion to amplify a geological exhalation directly into your body.

Understand also that this is a place you find by stumbling into it. That means the surrounding moorland offers no signposts, no trails, no infrastructure. You will be navigating open, featureless, cloud-covered terrain at what appears to be night or permanent overcast, and the hollow itself is a sudden depression. People have fallen into places like this. The twelve-foot drop onto wet stone could break an ankle, a wrist, a skull. Your first preparation is against the possibility that you arrive at the hollow involuntarily, by falling.

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing and Exposure

The moor is cold. The hollow is marginally warmer than the moor due to the geothermal updraft, but "a few degrees above freezing" is still profoundly cold when you are wet, and you will be wet. Everything in the hollow is saturated. The moss is saturated. The walls are slick with condensation. The air itself is thick with moisture — warm air from the fissure meeting cold air from above creates a perpetual fog zone inside the hollow. You will be enveloped in damp from the moment you descend.

Dress in layers of wool or synthetic insulation that retains warmth when wet. No cotton. Cotton kills in cold-wet environments — it wicks heat away from the body when saturated and dries slowly. Merino wool base layers against the skin, a mid-layer of fleece or synthetic fill, and an outer shell that is waterproof and windproof. The updraft from the fissure will push warm, moist air against you, but the surrounding environment is frigid, and the combination creates conditions where hypothermia can develop insidiously — you feel the warmth of the updraft and don't notice your core temperature dropping from exposure on the side of your body facing the cold walls.

Your hands will be doing work — feeling the walls, the spikes, the niche, the moss. Bring thin waterproof gloves with good tactile sensitivity, and bring a heavier insulated pair for when you're not actively exploring. Your hands will be in contact with near-freezing mineral water constantly.

Waterproof boots with aggressive tread. The stone is described as "slick black" — wet schist is treacherous. A fall inside the hollow, even from a standing position, could send you into the fissure, which is two feet wide and of unknowable depth. You do not want to fall into the fissure. More on that below.

Gaiters to keep water from running into your boots as you cross the saturated moss banks.

A hat that covers your ears. This is important for warmth but also for the sound — more on that below.

### Descent and Ascent

Twelve feet of vertical wet stone. The walls have scalloped ledges and grooves, which theoretically provide hand- and footholds, but they are coated in moss, liverwort, and condensation. Free-climbing down is unwise. Free-climbing back up may be impossible if you're cold, tired, or disoriented by the sound.

Bring a rope — at least twenty meters of static kernmantle rope, rated for single-person use. Bring a harness and a friction device for controlled descent and ascent. You'll need an anchor at the top; drive your own anchor into the moorland turf or find a solid rock feature on the rim. Do not trust the iron spikes inside the hollow — they are described as deeply corroded, possibly a century old, and may pull free under load. They were driven by someone else for an unknown purpose, and you have no way to assess their integrity in the dark.

A pair of ascenders or prusik loops will let you climb back up the rope if you can't find footholds. Practice ascending a fixed rope before you go. It is strenuous work, especially in cold and wet conditions, especially if your hands are numb, especially if you are rattled.

Consider also: you may need to be retrieved. If you become incapacitated in the hollow — through injury, hypothermia, or psychological overwhelm — you will need someone at the top who can help. This argues strongly for not going alone, although the description suggests the hollow is the kind of place one arrives at alone. More on the question of solitude below.

### The Fissure

The crack in the floor is two feet wide and of unknowable depth. It runs east-to-west across the bottom of the depression. Warm air issues from it continuously with enough force to drive pebbles into trembling and shatter the surface of standing water. This is not a gentle breeze — this is a sustained, pressurized exhalation from a geological system of unknown extent.

Do not enter the fissure. Do not attempt to descend into it. Do not lean over it. Do not place any body part into it. The air rising from it may carry gases — carbon dioxide pooling in subterranean chambers can be displaced upward by pressure differentials, and even a brief inhalation of high-concentration CO2 can cause immediate unconsciousness. The fissure is also the source of the sound, and proximity to the source will intensify the infrasonic component to potentially injurious levels — more on this below.

Stay at least an arm's length from the fissure at all times. Be aware of its position even when you can't see it. In the near-total darkness of the hollow, the fissure is an open wound in the floor that you could walk into. Map its location by sound and by the direction of the updraft the moment you reach the floor, and keep that map in your mind at all times.

## Hearing Protection and the Sound

This requires extended discussion because the sound is the defining feature of the location and the primary threat to your well-being.

### What the Sound Is

The description indicates a continuous drone at infrasonic to low-audible frequencies, with irregular surges that climb into audible range as moans, howls, and keening. The fundamental frequency sits "at the basement of audibility" — roughly 20 Hz or below — and the surges bring harmonics up through the audible spectrum. The hollow's geometry creates standing waves, reflections, and interference patterns. The sound is described as physically oppressive: felt in the sternum, buzzing in the teeth, causing clothing to vibrate against skin, making pebbles tremble and water shiver.

This is consistent with exposure to high-decibel infrasound combined with low-frequency audible sound in a resonant enclosure. The hollow acts as a natural resonating chamber, amplifying certain frequencies and creating nodes and antinodes of pressure throughout the space. You may find that certain positions in the hollow are significantly louder or quieter than others, and that moving even a foot in one direction changes the character of the sound dramatically.

### Risks

**Infrasonic exposure at high intensity can cause:**
- Nausea, dizziness, and disorientation
- Feelings of pressure in the chest and abdomen
- Visual disturbances (the eyeball has a resonant frequency near 18 Hz; vibration at this frequency can cause smearing of vision and perception of phantom shapes)
- Anxiety, dread, and panic — infrasound at sufficient intensity is strongly associated with feelings of unease and irrational fear
- Fatigue and difficulty concentrating
- With prolonged exposure: headache, tinnitus, and temporary threshold shift in hearing

**The audible components — the moans and howls — pose their own risks:**
- If the surges reach sufficient volume (and the description suggests they may exceed 100 dB given the physical effects described), prolonged exposure can cause permanent hearing damage
- In a reverberant enclosure, the perceived volume is higher than in open air
- The irregular timing of the surges means you cannot habituate — each one arrives unexpectedly, triggering a startle response that compounds the anxiety from the infrasonic component

**Psychological effects:**
- The description notes that "your own thoughts become difficult to locate" during the worst surges. This is a real phenomenon. High-intensity infrasound and low-frequency noise can disrupt cognitive function, interfere with working memory, and create a subjective sense of mental blankness or dissolution. In an already disorienting environment — dark, cold, wet, enclosed — this can escalate to panic.
- The impossibility of verbal communication during surges means you are cut off from even the sound of your own voice, which is a significant orienting cue. Hearing yourself speak is part of how you maintain a sense of self. When you can't, the boundaries of identity become less certain.

### Protection

Bring hearing protection, and bring multiple types.

**Foam earplugs** (NRR 32 or higher): These are your baseline. They will attenuate the audible frequencies significantly but will do less against the infrasonic component, which enters the body through bone conduction and resonance in the chest cavity as much as through the ear canal. Wear them from the moment you descend.

**Over-ear hearing protection** (earmuffs, NRR 25-30): Wear these over the foam plugs for maximum attenuation. This "double protection" approach is standard in extremely loud industrial environments and will reduce your audible exposure by 35-40 dB in practice.

**Even with maximum hearing protection, you will still feel the infrasound.** There is no practical way to shield your body from infrasonic pressure waves in an enclosed space. The vibration enters through your skeleton, your viscera, your skin. The best you can do is limit your exposure time and monitor yourself for symptoms of distress.

**Consider also:** active noise-cancelling headphones are designed for higher frequencies and will be largely useless against the deep infrasound here. Do not rely on them.

**A critical note:** hearing protection will make you more isolated. You will not hear your companion (if you have one), you will not hear hazard cues (trickling water indicating a change in conditions, rock shifting), and you will not hear your own footsteps, which in darkness is a significant spatial orientation cue. You are trading physical safety of your ears for reduced situational awareness. Accept this trade consciously and compensate with other senses — primarily touch.

## Light

The hollow is described as almost completely dark. The only natural light is a faint grey differentiation from the sky above. You will need artificial light to see anything in detail, but you should think carefully about when and how to use it.

### Practical lighting

A headlamp with red-light mode is ideal. Red light preserves dark adaptation better than white light, and in a place where your eyes are working at the extreme limit of their sensitivity, losing your dark adaptation to a blast of white light means minutes of readjustment during which you are effectively blind. Use the red mode for navigation and general awareness. Switch to white light only when you are examining something specific — the spikes, the niche, the pot, the mosses — and then switch back.

Bring a backup light source. A second headlamp, or a small handheld flashlight. If your primary light fails in the hollow, you are in total darkness twelve feet below ground with a fissure in the floor. This is not survivable casually.

Bring fresh batteries or a fully charged rechargeable cell, and bring spares. Cold reduces battery performance. Moisture can corrode contacts. Assume the worst.

### The case for darkness

There is something to be said for spending at least part of your time in the hollow without artificial light. The description makes clear that the place was shaped by and for darkness — the mosses grow by feel, the slugs are translucent, the previous visitors worked by touch or by whatever illumination they brought, which left no permanent trace. To experience the hollow as it exists most of the time, for most of its history, means to experience it in darkness. This is where the place reveals its true character: a space defined entirely by sound and texture and the felt presence of stone and water and the breathing of the earth below.

But do this only after you have thoroughly familiarized yourself with the layout of the hollow under light. Know where the fissure is. Know where the ledges are. Know where the walls curve and where the footholds lie. Map it in your mind and then extinguish the light and let the map guide you. This is the closest you will come to perceiving the hollow as the slugs perceive it, as the mosses perceive it — as a world of gradients and vibration, without visual form.

## Duration and Timing

### How long to stay

This is the central question, and it depends entirely on your tolerance and your purpose.

**Minimum meaningful visit: 30-60 minutes.** This gives you time to descend, orient yourself, explore the hollow with light, sit in darkness through several surge cycles, and ascend. You will get the basic experience — the sound, the dark, the wet, the strangeness. You will leave with your hearing intact and your psychology merely shaken rather than altered.

**Extended visit: 2-4 hours.** This is enough time to pass through the initial shock, the phase of fascinated exploration, the phase of discomfort and desire to leave, and into a phase of grudging accommodation where you begin to simply coexist with the sound. This is where the hollow starts to teach you something — about endurance, about the difference between pain and suffering, about the way the mind eventually stops fighting a stimulus it cannot defeat and begins instead to incorporate it. This is also the zone where the previous visitors must have operated, doing their work with spikes and chisel and chain, and you begin to understand what that would have been like — not heroic, not mystical, just dogged and patient and uncomfortable.

**Overnight stay:** Possible but inadvisable without significant preparation. Hypothermia risk is real. Sleep in the hollow would be difficult — the surges would wake you repeatedly, and the disorientation of waking in total darkness with your body vibrating and a howl filling the space around you could trigger panic. If you choose this, bring a bivvy bag, an insulated sleeping pad (crucial — the stone will drain heat from your body through conduction), a sleeping bag rated to well below freezing, and enough food and water for twelve hours. Tell someone where you are and when to expect you back. Leave your rope in place for emergency ascent.

**Do not stay longer than one night.** Prolonged infrasonic exposure at the intensities described here has poorly understood long-term effects, and the psychological impact of extended sensory deprivation combined with overwhelming auditory stimulus is unpredictable. This is not a place for endurance tests. The hollow has been here for millennia. You do not need to match its patience.

### When to go

The description suggests the sound varies with barometric pressure, tidal forces, and subterranean temperature. You will have no way to predict or control these variables. Go when conditions on the moor are stable — avoid high winds (which add their own sound and make the rim of the hollow treacherous), avoid rain (which will flood the depression and make the fissure harder to identify), avoid freezing conditions (which add ice to already treacherous wet stone). A still, overcast, above-freezing night is ideal — calm enough to navigate the moor safely, dark enough that the transition into the hollow's darkness is gradual rather than shocking.

## Navigation to the Hollow

The moor is described as a featureless dark tableland under heavy cloud. You find the hollow by stumbling into it. This is not a viable navigation strategy.

If you know the approximate location from prior intelligence (maps, accounts, coordinates from a previous multiverse survey), navigate by GPS. Bring a handheld GPS unit with the coordinates loaded, and bring a backup — a compass and a topographic map, if such a map exists for this version of the moor. The featureless terrain makes dead reckoning difficult, and under heavy cloud you will have no stars and no landmarks.

If you do not know the location and are genuinely searching, you need a systematic strategy for covering ground on the moor without walking into the hollow at speed. Move slowly. Use a walking stick or trekking pole to probe the ground ahead of you. Listen — as you approach the hollow, you will hear the sound before you see the depression. It is enormous, and on a still night on open moorland, the low-frequency components will propagate for a considerable distance. Follow the sound. Let it guide you. When it becomes directional and the ground begins to feel like it's vibrating underfoot, slow down further and probe each step. The rim of the hollow is your first landmark.

Mark the rim with something visible for your return — a cairn of stones, a stake with reflective tape, a GPS waypoint. You will need to find this spot again, and the moor will look the same in every direction.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

This is the most important section and the one most likely to be neglected.

### The sound will affect your mind

This is not a metaphor. Infrasound at high intensity is associated with anxiety, dread, panic, and cognitive disruption in peer-reviewed research. The conditions in the hollow — darkness, enclosure, isolation, cold, wet, unfamiliarity — amplify these effects by removing the coping mechanisms you normally rely on (visual information, the sound of other voices, the ability to move freely, the sense of being in a known and controlled environment). You will feel afraid, and the fear will not have an object. It will feel sourceless and fundamental, as though the fear is not in you but in the place itself, embedded in the stone and the sound.

This is normal. It is a physiological response to infrasonic stimulation combined with environmental stressors. Name it when it arrives. Say to yourself, if you can hear your own internal voice over the noise: "This is infrasound. This is my body responding to pressure waves. I am not in danger from the sound itself. The fear is real but the threat is not." This kind of cognitive reframing is the only reliable tool you have against the psychological effects.

### Ego dissolution and the loss of self-boundary

The description notes that during the worst surges, thoughts become difficult to locate, "as though the noise has displaced them the way water displaces air in a flooding room." This is a description of a transient altered state of consciousness induced by overwhelming sensory input. It is similar in character, if not in mechanism, to states induced by meditation, sensory deprivation, or psychedelic substances. The boundaries of the self become unclear. You may lose track of where your body ends and the hollow begins. You may feel that you are the sound, or that the sound is thinking you, or that there is no difference between the vibration in the stone and the vibration in your bones.

This can be ecstatic or terrifying, and sometimes both simultaneously. If you have experience with meditation, breathwork, or other practices that produce altered states, you will have a framework for navigating this. If you don't, be warned that it can feel like losing your mind, and that the natural response — to fight it, to reassert control, to claw your way back to ordinary consciousness — is exhausting and often counterproductive. The surge will pass. Your thoughts will return. Your sense of self will reconsolidate. Let the wave move through you rather than bracing against it.

### The previous visitors

You will think about them. In the dark, with the sound pressing in, your mind will reach for the human presence embedded in the spikes and the chain and the chiseled niche and the clay pot. Who were they? What were they doing? Why did they endure this? These questions will become vivid and pressing in a way they wouldn't in daylight on the surface, because in the hollow, the evidence of their presence is all you have to hold onto as proof that other humans have been here and survived.

Do not project too much onto them. You do not know their purpose. The description offers several possibilities — the spikes as anchor points for descent into the fissure, the niche as a storage place for something valued or sacred, the clay pot as a vessel for mineral water or an offering or an experiment. Any of these could be true. None of them might be. The human impulse to construct narrative from fragmentary evidence is powerful, and in the hollow it will be overwhelming, because narrative is how you maintain coherence in the face of the inhuman. Let the stories come, but hold them lightly. The hollow predates them and will outlast them, and that is part of what it teaches.

### The question of purpose

Why are you going? Answer this before you descend. The hollow doesn't care, as the description makes explicit, but you need to care, because your purpose is the thread that connects you to the surface, to the moor, to the world above. Without it, you are just a warm body in a cold hole being vibrated by the earth, and that is a state of affairs that can become unbearable very quickly.

If you are going out of curiosity: honor that. Curiosity is a legitimate and sustaining motive. Bring a notebook in a waterproof bag and write down what you observe, even if you have to do it by feel in the dark. The act of recording is an act of maintaining selfhood.

If you are going for the experience: honor that too, but know what you're buying. The experience is not pleasant. It is not sublime in the Romantic sense of awe before natural grandeur. It is closer to what the philosopher Edmund Burke meant by the sublime — an encounter with something vast and terrible that overwhelms the capacity of the mind to process it, producing a mixture of awe and horror that he considered the most powerful emotion a human being could feel. The hollow will give you this. Whether you want it is another question.

If you are going because the previous visitors went, because you want to understand what they sought — then you are on a pilgrimage to a shrine whose god is geological process, and your offering is your presence and your endurance. This is perhaps the most honest motive. But be aware that the hollow does not reward pilgrimage. It does not reciprocate. It breathes, and you listen, and whatever meaning you extract is yours alone.

## Equipment Checklist

Bringing this together into a practical list:

**Descent and safety:**
- 20m+ static rope
- Climbing harness
- Belay/rappel device
- Ascenders or prusik loops
- Two locking carabiners
- Ground anchor (stake or deadman suitable for moorland peat)
- Helmet (for the descent and for protection from falling debris)

**Clothing:**
- Merino wool base layers (top and bottom)
- Fleece or synthetic mid-layer
- Waterproof/windproof shell jacket and trousers
- Waterproof boots with aggressive tread
- Gaiters
- Thin waterproof gloves
- Insulated gloves (spare pair)
- Warm hat covering ears
- Spare dry socks in waterproof bag

**Hearing protection:**
- Foam earplugs, NRR 32+ (bring multiple pairs — they get wet and degrade)
- Over-ear earmuffs, NRR 25+

**Lighting:**
- Headlamp with red-light mode
- Backup headlamp or flashlight
- Spare batteries in waterproof bag

**Navigation:**
- GPS device with coordinates loaded
- Compass and map as backup
- Reflective tape or markers for the rim

**Sustenance and warmth:**
- Water (1-2 liters in insulated container)
- High-calorie food (energy bars, chocolate, nuts — things you can eat with cold wet hands in the dark)
- Emergency bivvy bag (reflective, windproof)
- Chemical hand warmers

**Recording and documentation:**
- Waterproof notebook and pencil (pens fail in cold and wet)
- Camera in waterproof housing (if you want visual documentation, though there is almost nothing to photograph)
- Audio recorder in waterproof bag (this is the medium that matters here — record the sound, if you want to bring anything back)

**Emergency:**
- Whistle (though its utility in the hollow is zero — no one will hear it over the sound)
- First aid kit with special attention to blister care, minor wound cleaning, and hypothermia management (emergency blanket, glucose tablets)
- Communication device — satellite messenger or personal locator beacon, since mobile phone coverage on a featureless moor under heavy cloud is unlikely
- Tell someone where you're going and when you expect to return

## What to Do When You're There

Descend on the rope. Reach the floor. Stand still. Let your eyes adjust — this will take ten to fifteen minutes for full dark adaptation, and during that time the sound will be the entirety of your experience. Breathe. Breathe deliberately and slowly, because your breathing is the one rhythm you control in a place defined by a rhythm you don't.

Orient yourself. Find the fissure by the direction of the updraft and the intensification of sound. Move away from it. Find a wall. Touch it. Feel the moss, the wet stone, the scalloped ledges. Follow the wall around the perimeter of the hollow — it's no wider than a barn floor, so this is a short walk, but take it slowly. Map the space with your hands.

When you feel oriented, use your light. Find the spikes. Find the chain. Cross to the other wall and find the niche, the pot. Look at the mosses — turn on the white light briefly and see the tapestry of colors that exists in perpetual darkness. Look at the slugs. Look at the ferns. Look at the water shivering on the stone surfaces.

Then turn off the light. Sit on a ledge, as far from the fissure as you can get while still being inside the hollow. Put on your hearing protection if you haven't already. And wait.

Wait through at least three surge cycles. Feel them build, crest, and recede. Notice that each one is different — different pitch, different duration, different quality of pressure. Notice that between surges, the drone continues, and that it has its own texture and variation that you couldn't perceive when the surges were dominating your attention. Notice the temperature of the air on different parts of your body. Notice the water soaking through your clothes where you sit on the moss. Notice the mineral smell — iron, chalk, wet stone.

Notice that you are alive in a place that is alive in a way that has nothing to do with you.

When you are ready — and you will know when you are ready, because readiness will feel like saturation, like having absorbed as much as you can hold — stand up. Return to your rope. Ascend. Emerge onto the moor. The silence of the open moorland will not be silence — it will be wind, and the rustle of heather, and your own breathing, and these sounds will seem impossibly delicate, impossibly varied, impossibly rich. You will hear the world differently for a while. The hollow will have recalibrated your ears and your attention, and for some hours or days afterward, ordinary sounds will arrive with a clarity and a beauty that you hadn't noticed before, because you had never had them taken away.

Mark the spot. Leave nothing. The hollow will continue without you, as it always has, the stone singing to itself, the mosses growing by feel, the slugs moving with the unhurried confidence of creatures that have never been observed and do not need to be. The clay pot will continue filling with calcite, molecule by molecule. The chain will continue its slow dissolution. The earth will continue to breathe.

You will carry the sound in your bones for longer than you expect.
