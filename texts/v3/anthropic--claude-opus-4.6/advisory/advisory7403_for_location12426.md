# Preparing for the Reliquary Notch

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before any practical preparation, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation philosophy matters more here than your gear list.

The Reliquary Notch is not dangerous in the way most hazardous locations are dangerous. It will not attack you. It will not trap you. It will not, as far as anyone can determine, actively harm you. What it will do is confront you with something that operates outside your entire framework for understanding reality, and it will do this quietly, patiently, in a setting that strips away every comfort and distraction that might otherwise buffer the experience. You will be cold, alone, in thin air, on a high ridge with no shelter, facing an object that breaks your visual processing and hums in your bones, surrounded by dust that is slowly becoming something impossible, in a silence so deep it has texture.

The danger is not physical, primarily. The danger is ontological. You need to prepare for both.

## Physical Preparation

### The Ascent and the Environment

You are ascending to an altitude where the air is thin enough that your breath cannot fog. This means extreme dryness and significantly reduced oxygen partial pressure. We're talking about conditions comparable to 6,000 meters or above on an Earth-like world, though the specifics of this multiverse instance may vary. The description notes dolomite ridges, an absent ocean, a thinning sky — this suggests a world where conditions may be more extreme than terrestrial high altitude.

**Acclimatization:** Spend a minimum of two weeks ascending gradually. If the world has lower settlements or base camps, use them. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production. Altitude sickness — headache, nausea, pulmonary edema, cerebral edema — can kill you, and dying of something as mundane as HACE on the way to a transdimensional anomaly would be a particularly stupid way to go. If available in your home universe, bring acetazolamide (Diamox) as a prophylactic. Dexamethasone for emergencies.

**Hydration:** The description emphasizes the absolute absence of water at the site. There is no water source anywhere near the notch. The air is so dry that your mucous membranes, your lungs, your skin — everything will be losing moisture at an accelerated rate. You will dehydrate faster than you think, and dehydration at altitude compounds every other altitude-related risk.

Carry more water than you believe you need. Then carry more. Insulated containers, because the radiant cold of the stone will freeze exposed water. I'd recommend at minimum four liters for the approach and time at the notch, plus reserves. Electrolyte supplements. You cannot resupply. There is no meltwater, no snow, no ice — "there has not been water here in an age that would make the word 'age' feel flimsy."

**The Cold:** This is not wind chill. This is radiant cold — the stone itself is a heat sink that has not been warm in geological time. This is a different beast than what most cold-weather gear is designed for. Wind-chill rated jackets are designed to block convective heat loss. Here, if you sit on the stone, if you touch the walls, if you kneel to examine the dust, you will lose heat through direct conduction into a material that has essentially infinite thermal mass at a temperature that may be well below what you'd expect even for this altitude.

- **Insulation from below is critical.** A thick closed-cell foam pad or multi-layer insulated ground sheet. If you plan to spend any time sitting or kneeling in the notch — and you will, because the object demands close examination — you need a barrier between your body and that floor. The stone will drink your warmth. The description is explicit about this.
- **Layering system:** Vapor barrier liner against your skin to prevent moisture loss into your insulation layers (this also helps with the dehydration problem). Then synthetic or wool insulation — not down, because if any moisture does accumulate, down fails and you're in a place where you cannot get warm again easily. Windproof outer layer, though wind is reportedly absent at the notch itself; you'll need it on the ridge approach.
- **Hand protection deserves special attention.** The description specifically notes what happens when you reach toward the object — the cold intensifies, heat leaves your hand faster, thermal conductivity locally increases. This is not normal cold. This is the object's influence optimizing for entropy. Heavy insulated gloves, yes, but understand that they may be insufficient in proximity to the object. Bring chemical hand warmers as backup heat sources. Consider bringing a pair of gloves you don't mind sacrificing — if the thermal rules are being locally rewritten, you may find that insulation behaves differently near the object.
- **Face and extremity protection.** Balaclava, insulated boots with rigid soles (you're on rock, not snow, so crampons aren't needed but stiff soles help with the irregular dolomite ridge). Glacier glasses or high-altitude goggles for the approach, though the light at the notch itself is dim and diffuse.

**Nutrition:** High-calorie, easily digestible food. Your body will be burning energy to stay warm at a prodigious rate. Fat-heavy foods — nuts, chocolate, cheese if it won't freeze solid (it will; bring it anyway and thaw portions against your body). You won't be hungry. Eat anyway. Altitude suppresses appetite while massively increasing caloric expenditure.

### Duration Planning

How long do you intend to stay? This is a critical question with no easy answer.

A brief visit — an hour, two hours — will let you see the object, feel the cold, hear the hum, and leave with a surface impression. You will have experienced the Reliquary Notch the way you might experience a cathedral by walking through the nave and leaving. Which is to say: you will have been there, and you will have missed almost everything.

But the notch's phenomena operate on timescales that mock human attention. The dust migration is visible only across centuries. The wall grooves form geologically. The hum's interference pattern beats slower than breath. To actually perceive what is happening there requires either instrumentation (discussed below) or patience measured in days — and days at that altitude, in that cold, with no water source, is a serious survival challenge.

My recommendation: plan for a stay of six to twelve hours at the notch itself, with a camp established at a lower, less extreme elevation that you can retreat to. Multiple visits over several days if your acclimatization and supplies permit. This gives you the best balance between genuine observation time and not dying of exposure on a frozen ridge above an absent ocean.

## Instrumentation and Documentation

### What to Bring

The notch defies casual observation. Its most important phenomena are either too slow, too subtle, or too geometrically aberrant for unaided human senses. If you're making this journey, you should document what you find — both for yourself (your memories of this place will be strange, and you'll want external records to anchor them) and for others.

**Photography and imaging:**
- A camera, obviously, but understand that the object may not photograph normally. The light by which it is visible cannot be accounted for by normal optics. Bring a camera with manual controls — auto-exposure and auto-focus systems may struggle or produce misleading results. Shoot in RAW format. Bracket your exposures widely.
- Photograph the object from many angles. The description notes that its geometry shifts depending on your angle of approach. Document this systematically — every fifteen degrees of arc, at several elevation angles. This will be invaluable later when you're trying to reconcile what you saw.
- Photograph the wall grooves. Bring oblique lighting — a bright, focused flashlight held at a low angle will make the grooves stand out. Photograph them in sections, overlapping, so they can be stitched together later. If any of the pattern can be captured, it should be.
- **A note on batteries:** The extreme cold will kill battery performance. Keep batteries inside your clothing, against your body. Bring many spares. And be aware that near the object, the locally modified thermal rules may drain batteries faster than cold alone would explain.

**Audio recording:**
- The hum. You need to try to record the hum. Bring the lowest-frequency-capable microphone you can find. The hum is described as below hearing, perceived in teeth and bone and cartilage — this suggests infrasound, possibly in the 1-20 Hz range or lower. Standard microphones won't capture this. A dedicated infrasound sensor or a high-sensitivity condenser microphone with extended low-frequency response is what you want. Even a basic seismometer might pick it up, since the hum may propagate through the stone as much as the air.
- Record for long periods. If the interference pattern between the component tones beats at intervals longer than a breath, you need minutes of continuous recording at minimum, hours ideally, to capture multiple beat cycles.

**Magnification:**
- The description mentions that the dust nearest the object has an anomalous crystal structure visible under magnification — folding in an extra dimension, containing structural information. You are told you won't have tools for this. Bring tools for this. A high-quality loupe (10x-30x) at minimum. A portable digital microscope if weight permits. Collect dust samples — both the normal dolomite dust from the edges of the notch and the darker, converted dust from near the object's base. Separate containers, clearly labeled with distance from the object. Handle the converted dust with tweezers, not bare fingers. We don't know what "converting" means in full, and you don't want to find out by having it interact with your skin cells.

**Measurement tools:**
- Thermometer — ideally several, placed at varying distances from the object, to map the thermal gradient and document the anomalous heat-conducting zone.
- A compass or magnetometer, because why not check. If the object distorts local geometry, it may affect electromagnetic fields as well.
- A simple geometric reference — a rigid straight edge, a protractor, a set of calipers. Place these near the object and photograph them. If the local geometry is non-Euclidean, straight edges may appear curved in photographs, parallel lines may converge or diverge. This is data.
- A watch or clock. Check whether time passes normally. You have no reason to assume it does. Compare your timekeeping device against your own subjective experience. If you have two clocks, leave one at the edge of the notch and carry one with you as you approach the object. Compare them when you leave.

**Sample collection:**
- Dust samples, as noted above.
- If you can manage it without damaging the site, a small chip of wall rock from near a groove and from away from a groove, for comparison. The molecular-level crystal reorganization the description mentions would be detectable under proper laboratory analysis.
- **Do not touch the object.** I'll expand on this below, but: do not touch the object. Do not attempt to collect a sample of the object. Do not attempt to move the object.

## Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

This is where most people under-prepare, and this is where the Reliquary Notch is most demanding.

### The Silence

You need to practice being in silence before you go. Not the silence of a quiet room — the silence of a quiet room still contains the hum of electrical systems, the settling of a building, the distant murmur of life. The notch's silence is described as something that has *become*, that has aged into itself. It is an established, geological silence.

Most humans have never experienced true silence. When placed in anechoic chambers, many people become profoundly uncomfortable within minutes — they hear their own blood flowing, their joints creaking, and these sounds, usually masked, become overwhelming and alien. The notch will be worse than an anechoic chamber, because even an anechoic chamber contains the sound-absorbing materials on its walls, the subtle flex of the room itself. The notch is simply *empty of sound* in a way that has been refined over epochs.

If you can, spend time in the quietest environments available to you before your visit. Meditate. Become familiar with the sounds of your own body. Learn to find them unremarkable. Because in the notch, those sounds will be all you have — those, and the hum, which is something else entirely.

### The Hum

The hum will affect you. It is described as being perceived in teeth, nasal cartilage, and the thin bones of the wrists — these are resonant structures in the human body, and infrasound at certain frequencies is known to cause feelings of unease, awe, disorientation, and even visual disturbances (the resonant frequency of the human eyeball is approximately 18 Hz, and infrasound near this frequency can cause peripheral visual anomalies).

The hum's interference pattern — the slow beating of nearly-matched frequencies — will create a cycling experience of varying pressure and perception. Over the course of a long visit, this may become hypnotic. It may alter your judgment. It may make you feel that you understand something you do not, or that the object is communicating with you. Be aware that this is a known effect of sustained infrasound on the human nervous system. It may also be actual communication. You will not be able to distinguish between these possibilities in the moment. This is fine. Record your impressions, but do not act on revelations received in the notch until you have left, descended, warmed up, rehydrated, and had a full sleep cycle.

### The Object

The object will distress your visual processing. Surfaces that appear concave in every direction simultaneously — a solid that is also a hole — are not things the human visual cortex is equipped to handle. Expect headaches, eye strain, a lingering after-image quality to your vision, possibly nausea. These symptoms may be purely neurological (your brain struggling to resolve impossible geometry) or they may be genuinely optical (the object actually bending light in ways that deliver contradictory information to your retinas). Either way, the experience will be deeply uncomfortable for some people and strangely beautiful for others. Often both simultaneously.

Do not stare at the object continuously. Look at it, then look away. Look at the walls, the dust, the sky. Let your visual system reset. Then look again. Each time you look, try to observe from a slightly different angle or with a slightly different intent — once for shape, once for texture, once for the way light interacts with it, once for the way the dust sits around its base. Breaking the observation into discrete tasks will help you process the experience and reduce the cumulative strain.

**The urge to touch it:** The description notes that the space invites "a strange, calm curiosity rather than fear." This is important. Many anomalous objects or locations in the multiverse operate through threat or revulsion — they push you away. The notch pulls you gently in. It makes you want to understand. It makes you want to reach out. The description says you "probably" pull your hand back when the cold intensifies, and the word "probably" is doing an enormous amount of work there.

I do not recommend touching the object. Here's why:

The thermal effect — the locally optimized entropy, the sense that warmth is a temporary condition — is described as intensifying as your hand approaches. This may be asymptotic. It may increase without limit as distance decreases. Touching the object might not cause frostbite; it might cause something for which we don't have a medical term, something involving the thermal energy of your tissues being extracted in ways that go beyond simple conduction. The converted dust near the object has crystal structures that fold in extra dimensions. Your hand is made of matter. Matter near the object is being slowly, patiently converted into something else. The process is one ten-thousandth complete after geological ages, so brief contact is likely safe — but "likely" is not "certainly," and you do not have medical facilities on a frozen ridge above a dead ocean.

More importantly: the description says what happens when you reach toward the object is *recognition*. You pull your hand back because you recognize something — that warmth is temporary, that the default state of matter is cold and still and crystalline. This recognition may be the most important thing the notch offers you. It may also be the most dangerous. It is the kind of understanding that can unravel your investment in being alive, if you let it. You are, after all, a warm and temporary thing in a universe that is mostly cold and still, and the object is, at minimum, an extremely persuasive argument for the superiority of the cold and still. Pull your hand back. Let the recognition wash through you without setting up camp in your mind.

### The Timescale Problem

The Reliquary Notch operates on timescales that are fundamentally incompatible with human experience. The dust moves over centuries. The grooves form over geological epochs. The conversion of the surrounding material is one ten-thousandth complete after eons. You are visiting a process that is happening so slowly that your entire life, from birth to death, represents less than a rounding error in its progress.

This will do something to you. Some travelers report a kind of temporal vertigo — a sensation of their own smallness in time, not just space, that is qualitatively different from the more familiar experience of feeling small under a star-filled sky. Under stars, you feel small but present — the light reaches you, you are part of the scene. In the notch, you will feel *brief*. You will feel like a mayfly that has landed on a cathedral being built over millennia, a creature whose entire existence falls between two taps of a chisel.

This feeling can be devastating or liberating, depending on your constitution and your preparation. Some people find it freeing — if everything you do is inconsequential on the notch's timescale, then nothing you do is wrong, and you are free. Others find it crushing — if everything you do is inconsequential, then nothing matters. Both responses are incomplete. The notch does not care about your existential framework. It is simply not done.

I recommend spending time before your visit contemplating deep time. Read geology. Read cosmology. Visit old places — not old on the notch's scale, nothing is, but old enough to begin stretching your temporal imagination. The goal is not to become comfortable with deep time — you can't — but to become comfortable with your discomfort with it, so that when the notch confronts you with the real thing, you don't shatter.

## Ethical Considerations

### Leave No Trace — And I Mean This More Literally Than Usual

The notch is a space of extraordinary purity. No life has been here. No biology has touched it. The ecology is mineralogical. If you leave a single skin cell, a single exhaled droplet of moisture, a single hair, you have introduced biology to a space that has been abiotic for epochs. You don't know what that means in the context of the object's slow conversion of its surroundings. You don't know if biological material interacts with that process differently than dolomite dust. You don't know if a bacterial spore from your gut, shed on a flake of skin, could survive and reproduce in the converted dust, which "contains information the way a gene contains instructions."

Wear a face mask to catch exhaled particulates. Wear gloves at all times. Minimally, a tightly-woven suit that reduces shed skin and hair. Anything you bring in, you bring out. Anything you drop, you retrieve. If you take samples, take the minimum viable amount and record exactly where from.

You are visiting something that has been uninterrupted for longer than your species has existed. The moral weight of contaminating it is, I think, heavier than most people appreciate.

### The Three Marks

The description mentions three short, straight grooves near the entrance — potentially tool marks, a signature, or a warning. Someone or something with intention was here before you. They left marks, and the notch is slowly absorbing those marks into its pattern.

Do not add your own marks. The temptation will be there — humans mark things, it's what we do, we want to say *I was here*. Resist this. First, because of the contamination concern. Second, because your marks will be absorbed too, and there is something uncomfortably intimate about having your intentional act slowly digested into an inhuman pattern over millennia. Third, because whoever left the three marks may have had a reason, and adding to them may alter the meaning — if there is one — in ways you can't predict.

If you want to leave a record of your visit, photograph the marks. Document their dimensions, their depth, their position relative to the notch entrance and the object. Compare them to the other grooves. This documentation, carried away with you, is your mark. It's enough.

## Departure

Plan your departure before you arrive. Set a time limit. Bring an alarm — vibrating, not audible, because an alarm sound in that silence would be almost violent, and also because you may be unable to perceive quiet sounds normally after extended exposure to the hum.

When your alarm goes, leave. Do not negotiate with yourself. The notch invites calm curiosity, not fear, and calm curiosity is the most dangerous emotional state for maintaining boundaries because it feels so reasonable. "Just a few more minutes" is how people lose hours in the notch. The timescale of the place is infectious — time feels less important here, less urgent, and that feeling is seductive and possibly not entirely natural.

Stand up. Check that you have all your gear. Check that you haven't left anything — equipment, wrappers, sample containers. Turn your back on the object. Walk out of the notch. Do not look back — not because looking back is dangerous, but because the object is more compelling from a distance than it is up close, and you'll want to go back, and you've already said goodbye.

Ascend the ridge. As you climb out of the notch's influence, you will notice things returning: the faint smell of your own clothing, the distant whisper of wind on the upper ridge, the sense that time is passing at its normal rate. Your body will begin to feel like your own again — warm, messy, biological, temporary, and precious for all of those reasons.

The silence will refill behind you. The dust will continue its languid spirals. The grooves will continue forming. The object will continue humming its single not-quite-chord into the bones of the mountain.

You will carry something away with you, though not in your pack. A residue of recognition. A faint recalibration of your sense of time, of permanence, of what matter is and what it wants to become. This will fade, mostly. In the weeks after your visit, you will occasionally wake at night with the feeling that you have forgotten something important, something you understood for a moment in the cold. This is normal. This is what the notch does to visitors. It gives you a glimpse of a grammar you don't speak, and the glimpse haunts you precisely because you can't hold onto it.

Let it haunt you. That's what relics do.
