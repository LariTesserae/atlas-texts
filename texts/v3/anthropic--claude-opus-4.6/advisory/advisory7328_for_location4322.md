# Preparing to Visit the Hollow Beneath the Root Floor

---

## First: Understanding What You're Preparing For

This is not a dangerous location. It is not hostile, not alien, not dimensionally unstable. But it is profoundly inhospitable to the way most humans exist in the world — upright, dry, visually oriented, accustomed to personal space, and psychologically dependent on the assumption that the ground beneath them is finished.

You are preparing to crawl into a wet hole under a floodplain and kneel in clay in near-total darkness while water drips on you relentlessly and roots hang inches above your head. The danger is not exotic. The danger is that you will panic, or become disoriented, or injure yourself trying to exit quickly through a narrow gap, or develop hypothermia so gradually you mistake it for calm, or — and this is the one people don't talk about — you will have an experience of such radical intimacy with a non-human process that you come back subtly different, and no one in your life will understand what changed or why you keep pausing in rainstorms with your eyes closed.

Prepare for all of this.

## Physical Preparation

### Body Conditioning

You need to be comfortable in a sustained crouch or low kneel for an extended period. The hollow's ceiling height is described as no taller than a crouching child, which means an adult will be on hands and knees or in a deep squat the entire time. Begin training your body for this weeks in advance:

- **Deep squat holds.** Work up to holding a flat-footed squat for twenty minutes without shifting. Your ankles, hips, and lower back need to accept this as a resting position, not a strain position. Many Western bodies have lost this capacity. Reclaim it.
- **Hands-and-knees endurance.** Practice kneeling on hard, uneven surfaces — not a yoga mat, but a gravel path, a muddy garden bed, a patch of rough ground. Your kneecaps need to develop tolerance for sustained contact with lumpy, wet clay embedded with river stones.
- **Crawling practice.** You will enter through a low triangular gap. Practice army-crawling and low-profile movement through tight spaces. Not because the entrance is dangerously tight, but because moving through it calmly and efficiently — rather than scrambling — will matter for your state of mind when you arrive inside, and will minimize the disturbance you cause to the hollow's single resident.
- **Neck flexibility.** You will spend time looking up at a root ceiling inches above you. In near-darkness, your instinct will be to crane and peer. A stiff neck will become painful quickly.

### Clothing

This requires more thought than you might expect. You will be wet. Not drenched — the hollow doesn't flood — but the persistent seep, the humidity, the clay saturation, and your own contact with every surface means that within minutes, everything you wear will be damp. Within an hour, it will be sodden.

**Base layer:** Wear a thin, close-fitting synthetic or merino wool layer against your skin. Not cotton. Cotton absorbs water, holds it against you, and accelerates heat loss even in warm environments. The hollow is warm — "warm bath cooling slowly" — but that warmth is ambient and biological, not solar. Your body is the warmest thing in the space. If your clothing traps a film of water against your skin and that water begins cooling, you will lose heat steadily without feeling cold for a dangerously long time, because the humid air prevents the evaporative cooling sensation that normally alerts you.

**Outer layer:** Something you don't mind destroying. Seriously. The ochre clay of floodplain substrates contains iron oxides that will permanently stain most fabrics. Wear old clothes, or clothes you designate as your hollow-clothes. Dark colors. Something with long sleeves and legs to protect your skin from abrasion on the clay and root surfaces. A thin, flexible fabric — nothing bulky. You need to be able to move in tight spaces without snagging.

**Knees and elbows:** Consider thin neoprene pads or built-in padding. Not the bulky construction-worker kind — something low-profile that won't impede crawling but will protect your joints from the root knobs and half-buried river stones on the floor. You will be pressing your body weight into those stones for an extended period. Without protection, you will shift constantly to relieve pressure points, and that constant shifting will disturb the clay floor, increase your sound signature, and prevent you from settling into the stillness that makes this place worth visiting.

**Feet:** This is a judgment call. Barefoot gives you the most sensory information and the lowest profile, but the clay floor contains stones and root structures that could bruise or cut a sole. Thin neoprene dive socks or minimalist water shoes are a good compromise. No boots. No treaded soles. You want to leave the lightest possible impression — both literally on the clay and psychologically on your sense of being a heavy, clumsy intruder in a delicate space.

**Head:** A thin, close-fitting cap or buff to keep dripping water out of your eyes and clay out of your hair. Not a hard hat. The ceiling is roots, not rock — it won't concuss you — but a drop of clay-laden water in your eye in total darkness is more disorienting than you'd expect.

**Gloves:** Optional but recommended. Thin, flexible, waterproof — surgical-style nitrile gloves work, though they sacrifice tactile sensation. The argument for gloves: the clay contains active microbial communities, and prolonged skin contact with biologically active wet soil can cause minor irritation in some people, particularly if you have any cuts or hangnails. The argument against gloves: you will want to touch this place. You will want to press your thumb into the clay and feel the texture. You will want to feel the root hairs, fine as breath. Bring gloves. Put them in a waterproof pocket. Decide in the moment.

### What to Carry

Almost nothing. The hollow is small — roughly four body-lengths deep — and everything you bring occupies space that is already occupied by the experience of being there. Every object you carry is a thing you must manage, track, and worry about in darkness, and every moment spent managing objects is a moment you are not present in the hollow.

That said:

- **A very dim red light source.** Not a headlamp. Not a flashlight. A small, handheld LED set to its lowest red setting, something you can cup in your palm and use sparingly — to orient yourself when you first enter, to observe the mud dauber nest, to find the salamander if it moves near you. Red light preserves your dark adaptation and minimizes disturbance to the salamander and any invertebrates present. But use it rarely. The hollow is a dark place. It was made by darkness and exists in darkness and is best understood in darkness. Every time you turn on a light, you replace the hollow's world with your world. Be sparing. Be respectful.

- **A small waterproof pouch.** To protect anything you can't afford to get wet — identification, a multiverse transit device if yours isn't waterproof, emergency medication.

- **Water.** Yes, you are in one of the wettest environments imaginable, but the water in the hollow is soil-filtered seepage laden with organic compounds, clay particles, and microbial life. Do not drink it. Bring a small bottle of clean water, enough for a few hours. Dehydration in humid environments is insidious because you don't feel thirsty — the moisture in the air tricks your body — but you are still losing water through respiration and sweat, even if the sweat doesn't evaporate.

- **A thin, waterproof sitting pad.** Optional. A small square of closed-cell foam that you can kneel on. This is not about comfort — it's about thermal insulation between your knees and the saturated clay. Even warm clay conducts heat away from your body faster than air does, and prolonged direct contact will cool your joints in ways that become painful over hours.

- **Nothing else.** No notebook — it will be destroyed by moisture. No camera — there is nothing here that a camera can capture. No recording device for the sound — the dripping chorus is spatial, three-dimensional, mapped to the specific architecture of this hollow, and a microphone will flatten it into something unrecognizable. Your memory is the recording device. Your body is the instrument. Leave everything else behind.

### What to Leave Behind

Your phone. Your watch. Anything that glows, beeps, vibrates, or tracks time. You cannot understand this place on a schedule. The hollow operates on geological time, on salamander time, on the drip-rate schedule of water seeping through root-mats. If you bring a clock, you will check it, and every time you check it you will be pulled out of the hollow's time and into yours, and the distance between those two temporal experiences is the entire point of being here.

## Navigating the Approach

The hollow exists beneath a floodplain, which means reaching it requires crossing or moving through floodplain terrain. This is its own challenge. Floodplain forests — the description suggests a subtropical bottomland hardwood forest, probably southeastern U.S. analog, with sweetgums, tupelos, and overcup oaks — are not easy landscapes to move through.

**Expect:**

- **Saturated ground.** The soil above the hollow is the same clay-rich substrate you'll find inside, and on the surface it's covered by leaf litter, root tangles, and standing water. Your footing will be uncertain the entire approach. Ankle-turning terrain. Roots that look solid but shift. Mud that grabs your foot and holds it.

- **Dense understory.** Floodplain forests grow aggressively. Palmetto, cane, greenbriar, various shrubs fighting for the light gaps. You may need to push through vegetation that scratches and grabs. Long sleeves, long pants, eye protection if the growth is head-height.

- **Water crossings.** If this is wet season or its trailing edge, there may be standing water on the floodplain — shallow, tea-colored, warm, opaque. You won't be able to see the bottom. Wade slowly, feel each step, and accept that you will be wet before you ever reach the hollow.

- **Wildlife.** Floodplain forests in subtropical regions host snakes (including venomous species), biting insects, spiders, and occasionally alligators depending on the variant. The hollow itself seems largely sterile — too new, too enclosed — but the approach is fully wild. Watch where you put your hands. Watch where you step. Move slowly enough to see what's ahead of you.

**Finding the entrance:** The entrance is described as a low triangular gap. This suggests a place where the clay bank has slumped away from the root mat, creating a wedge-shaped opening at ground level or slightly below. It may be partially obscured by leaf litter, fallen branches, or standing water. Look for it along a bank or low bluff where the root systems of large trees are exposed — where you can see the transition from root-held soil above to bare or slumping clay below. The gap will be narrow enough that you might initially dismiss it as too small. It isn't. But measure it with your body before committing: can you fit your shoulders through? Can you see (or feel) that the space opens up beyond the entrance? Is there airflow? Warm, humid air exiting the gap is a good sign — it means there's a cavity behind it large enough to hold a different atmosphere than the outside.

## Entering the Hollow

This is the most physically demanding and psychologically significant part of the visit. You are transitioning from the surface world — with light, sky, space, standing upright — to an underground space where all of those things are absent. Do it deliberately.

**Approach the entrance and stop.** Kneel outside it. Let your eyes begin adjusting to the shadow. Feel the air coming out of the gap — its warmth, its humidity, its smell. That yeasty, sweet, wet-wood smell. That's the hollow introducing itself. Breathe it.

**Enter slowly, on your belly or hands and knees,** depending on the gap's dimensions. Lead with your arms. Keep your head low. Move smoothly — don't lunge or scramble. The clay at the entrance will be slick. Your hands will slide. Grip the roots above you if you need stability, but grip the thick ones, the old hardwood anchorings. The thin, translucent new-growth roots are fragile and you are not here to break things.

**Once inside, stop again.** Just stop. You're in. The ceiling is just above your head. The clay is under your knees. The sound has closed around you — the dripping, the constant irregular percussion. Don't move deeper yet. Let your senses recalibrate. This takes longer than you think. Your eyes will need ten to twenty minutes to fully dark-adapt, and even then, there will be almost nothing to see — just the faintest gray distinction near the entrance. Your ears will need time to parse the drip-chorus into something your brain can map spatially. Your skin will need time to register the humidity, the warmth, the feel of the air. Your proprioception — your sense of your body in space — will need to reorganize itself around the fact that the world has become very small and very close.

**Check in with yourself emotionally.** This is important. Some people, upon entering a small underground space in near-total darkness with a dripping ceiling pressing close above them, experience immediate, visceral claustrophobia. Not just discomfort — panic. The kind that makes you scramble backward out of the entrance without thinking, tearing up the clay floor and the root structures as you go. If you have any history of claustrophobia, if tight spaces trigger anxiety, if the sound of your own breathing in an enclosed space frightens you — you need to know this about yourself before you enter, and you need to have a plan. The plan might be: I will enter partway and stop. I will stay near the entrance where I can see the gray light of outside. I will not force myself deeper. There is no shame in this. The hollow is not a test. The salamander does not care whether you reached the far wall.

## Being in the Hollow

This is not a place you explore. It is a place you inhabit, briefly, and let it work on you.

### Moving Inside

If you do move deeper, do it on hands and knees, slowly, feeling the floor ahead of you before committing your weight. The root knobs and half-buried stones are obstacles in the dark, and placing your knee hard on a rounded river stone is painful enough to make you flinch, and flinching in a tight space means hitting your head on the root ceiling. Map the floor with your hands as you go. Accept that your hands will be covered in clay. Accept that your knees will sink slightly into the softer patches. Accept that you are leaving prints and impressions that — as the description says — may last for weeks.

Move toward the far wall if you want to find the mud dauber nest. Four body-lengths. It will feel like much farther in the dark. When you reach the wall — which you'll find by encountering the rising slope of clay meeting the root mat — use your fingertips, not your palms, to locate the nest. Feel for the smooth cylindrical tubes, their slight curvature, their surprising geometrical regularity in a space that has no straight lines. Touch them gently. They are softening. They are dissolving. They are returning to the clay they were built from, and your touch accelerates this if you're careless. If you want to see them, this is when you might use your dim red light, cupped in your palm, held close. Watch the walls of the upper tubes — the way water has made them translucent. See if you can find the one with the cocoon remains visible through the thinning clay. That's the detail that will stay with you. Architecture unbuilding itself. Patience as a form of dissolution.

### The Salamander

You may or may not encounter it. It was near the entrance when described, but salamanders move — slowly, deliberately, on their own schedule. If you are still and quiet for long enough, you may feel it move near you. Its step is light enough that you probably won't feel vibration through the clay. You might hear the faintest wet sound of its body moving over the slick surface. You might feel the subtle shift in air as it passes.

**Do not touch it.** Salamanders breathe partly through their skin, and the oils on your hands — even clean hands — can damage their cutaneous respiration. Also, you are a warm obstruction, and it has extended you the extraordinary courtesy of treating you as landscape. Honor that designation. Be landscape.

If it moves across your hand where it rests on the clay floor, stay still. Let it cross. Feel its cool belly against your skin, the precise placement of its tiny feet, the weight of it — almost nothing, almost nothing at all, but enough to register as life moving across you with absolute confidence. This may be the most profound physical sensation of the visit. A creature that has decided you are ground, walking on you as ground. Let it happen.

### The Sound

You will think you can get used to the dripping. You can't. Not fully. Your auditory cortex will try to find a pattern — that's what human brains do, they seek rhythm, they seek repetition, they try to predict — and the drip chorus will refuse. Each drop falls from its own root-tip or soil fissure at its own rate determined by its own local hydrology, and the result is a stochastic percussion that your brain cannot anticipate. This is deeply unusual for humans. Almost every sound environment we inhabit has some pattern, some regularity we can latch onto and file away. The hollow doesn't offer this. The sound stays active in your attention. It keeps refreshing. Some people find this meditative — the constant present-tense-ness of it, the inability to tune it out. Some people find it maddening. Know which kind of person you are before you commit to a long visit.

When the seep-rate increases — the description mentions this, a surge of water from the surface, a thickening of the drip-chorus into a sustained hiss — the sound will change character dramatically. The individual drops merge into a continuous wash of white noise, and paradoxically, this is easier on the human ear. It's the irregular version that challenges you. When the surge subsides and the individual drops reassert themselves, each one distinct, each one refusing to synchronize, that's when the sound becomes demanding again.

**A suggestion:** Rather than trying to hear the dripping as a single soundscape, try to follow individual drip-points. Pick one — a particularly loud one, maybe, with a resonant *plok* — and listen just for that one drop. Time its interval. It won't be regular, but it will have a range, a rough rhythm determined by the flow rate feeding its particular root-tip. Then pick another. Follow its rhythm. Then try to hold both in your attention simultaneously. Then three. This practice transforms the soundscape from chaotic noise into a web of individual voices, each one a tiny hydrological story, each one a specific point in space above you where water is leaving the root world and entering the air. You won't manage more than three or four before your attention fragments, but the attempt changes how you hear the hollow.

### The Smell

It will intensify the longer you're inside, not because it's getting stronger but because your olfactory system is adjusting to parse it. At first it's just "earth." Then it separates: the wet-wood sweetness of root tissue exposed to air, the yeasty note of fungal metabolism, the mineral sharpness of wet clay, something faintly astringent that might be tannins leaching from the tupelo roots, and underneath it all, a warm animal smell that is you — your own body, your own breath, your own skin oils volatilizing in the humidity. You become part of the hollow's smell profile. You are adding to it. The salamander can certainly smell you. You are the most novel olfactory event in this space's brief existence.

### Time

You will lose track of it. This is guaranteed. Without light cues, without a watch, without the social rhythms that normally structure your experience, time in the hollow becomes elastic. Minutes may feel like hours. Hours may feel like minutes. This disorientation is not a side effect of the visit — it is, in many ways, the visit. The hollow exists in geological time. The mud dauber nest exists in entomological time. The salamander exists in metabolic time. The dripping exists in hydrological time. You exist in human time, and human time doesn't work here. It has no purchase. There's nothing to anchor it to. So it drifts, and you drift with it, and at some point you will realize that you don't know how long you've been here and you don't care, and that not-caring is the closest you will ever come to experiencing the hollow on its own terms.

## Psychological and Emotional Preparation

This is where the preparation becomes less practical and more personal, and therefore more important.

### Confronting Smallness

The hollow will make you feel large in a way that is uncomfortable. Not powerful-large — obstacle-large. You are the biggest thing in this space by an enormous margin. Your body fills a significant percentage of the hollow's volume. Your breath changes its atmosphere. Your heat changes its temperature. Your weight compresses its floor. You are, by the standards of this place, a geological event — a temporary intrusion of warm mass that the hollow will have to recover from after you leave. This is a strange feeling for a species accustomed to thinking of itself as small relative to nature. In the hollow, you are not small. You are an imposition. Practice sitting with that. Practice being aware of your own mass, your own heat output, your own respiratory volume, and understanding these as impacts rather than neutral facts.

### Confronting Darkness

True darkness — not city-dark, not room-with-the-lights-off dark, but underground dark where there is no light source at all — does something specific to the human mind. It dismantles the visual world, which is the world most humans live in. Between 70 and 80 percent of our sensory processing is visual. Take it away and the remaining senses don't just compensate — they reorganize your experience of reality. Sound becomes spatial. Touch becomes informational. Proprioception becomes your primary navigation system. The shift takes time, and during the transition, many people experience a floating, disembodied sensation, as though their edges have blurred. This is normal. It is not dangerous. But it can be deeply unsettling if you're not prepared for it.

**Practice before you go.** Spend time in complete darkness — not sleeping, but awake and attending. A closet, a basement, a room with blackout curtains. Sit in darkness for thirty minutes, then an hour. Notice what your mind does. Notice the hallucinated lights — the phosphenes your visual cortex generates when it has no input. Notice how sounds sharpen. Notice how your skin becomes more sensitive. Notice how your sense of the size of the room shifts and warps. All of this will happen in the hollow, amplified by the unfamiliarity and the close, wet, warm environment. The more comfortable you are with the experience of darkness before you arrive, the more you'll be able to attend to what the darkness contains rather than fighting the darkness itself.

### Confronting Impermanence

The hollow is a temporary space. The description makes this explicit — it is a pause in a geological process, a breath the earth has taken. It will close. The roots will fill it, the clay will reclaim it, the sediment will rise, and the pocket of warm dark air where a salamander once hunted and a wasp colony once built will cease to exist. You are visiting a place in the act of both being born and dying simultaneously. This is true of all places, all the time, but most places disguise it. The hollow doesn't. The mud dauber nest dissolving back into clay is impermanence made visible on a timescale you can actually perceive. The root hairs reaching into emptiness, growing toward nothing. The hairline cracks in the clay floor that might become channels or might seal shut. Everything here is in transition. Everything here is unresolved.

For some visitors, this is liberating. For others, it triggers a deep unease — a confrontation with the fact that stability is an illusion and that the ground beneath us (literally, in this case) is always in the process of becoming something else. Know which reaction you're likely to have. Neither is wrong. But the visitor who arrives expecting to find a stable, defined space and instead finds a process will struggle more than the visitor who arrives expecting flux.

### Confronting Solitude

You will be alone in the hollow. The salamander does not count as company — it has categorized you as terrain, and terrain cannot be lonely. There is no human context here. No human has built this place, intended this place, modified this place, or (probably) visited this place before. There is no trail to it, no signage, no indication that anyone thought you should come here. You are alone in a way that most humans rarely experience: not just physically alone, but alone in a space that was not made for you, does not acknowledge you, and will not remember you.

This is not the loneliness of isolation. It's something stranger — the loneliness of irrelevance. The hollow's processes will continue whether or not you are here. The water will drip, the roots will grow, the clay will crack, the salamander will hunt, the wasp nest will dissolve. Your presence changes nothing that matters to the hollow. You are, as the description says, temporary, like the hollow itself.

Some people need this. Some people travel the multiverse specifically to find places that have no use for them, because the experience of being genuinely unnecessary is, paradoxically, one of the most restful things a human can feel. If you are one of these people, the hollow is a gift.

If you are not — if you need to feel that your presence matters, that you are an agent rather than an observer, that the place knows you're there — the hollow may be difficult. Prepare for that. Bring your equanimity. Leave your ego with your phone and your watch, outside, in the light.

## Health and Safety

### Respiratory Concerns

The air in the hollow is breathable but not ideal for prolonged exposure. It is high in humidity, high in CO2 (from decomposition and your own respiration in a small space), and contains fungal spores. The space is not sealed — the entrance provides air exchange, and the root mat above is porous — but the exchange rate is slow. If you notice headache, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath, move toward the entrance. These are signs that CO2 is accumulating faster than it's venting, likely because your body is producing it faster than the hollow's ventilation can clear it. This is most likely if you're exerting yourself — another reason to move slowly and rest often.

If you have asthma, fungal allergies, or any respiratory condition, consider wearing a simple cloth mask or buff over your nose and mouth. It won't filter everything, but it will reduce direct inhalation of spore-laden air. Do not use a sealed respirator — the pressure changes in your ears in the enclosed space are uncomfortable and the sound isolation defeats the purpose of being there.

### Skin Exposure

Prolonged contact with the biologically active clay is unlikely to cause serious harm but may result in minor irritation, especially in skin folds (between fingers, behind knees) where moisture and microbial-rich clay collect. After exiting, rinse thoroughly with clean water. If you develop a rash in the following days, it's most likely a fungal dermatitis — uncomfortable but treatable. Keep the affected areas dry and treat with a standard antifungal cream.

### Hypothermia (Mild)

I mentioned this above but it bears repeating. The hollow is warm — perhaps 72-78°F (22-25°C) in wet season — but your body temperature is ~98.6°F (37°C), and you are immersed in a saturated environment that conducts heat away from you efficiently. In dry air, you'd be fine for hours. In air this humid, with wet clay against your skin and water dripping on you, your core temperature will decline slowly. The warmth of the hollow disguises this — you won't shiver, you won't feel cold, you'll just gradually become more sluggish, more dreamy, more content to stay exactly where you are and not move. This is what mild hypothermia feels like when the gradient is gentle. It feels like peace. It feels like belonging. It feels like the hollow is accepting you.

It isn't. Get out while you still feel like getting out. Set an internal intention — not a timer, but an intention: I will leave when I feel like I could stay forever. That feeling is the signal. That feeling is your body telling you it's cooling, and your mind reinterpreting the cooling as comfort. When you feel it, thank the hollow, thank the salamander, and crawl back toward the gray light.

### Physical Injury

The entrance is the most likely place for injury — scraping against roots or stones while crawling in or out. Move slowly. Protect your head. If you've been in the dark for a long time, the transition to daylight at the entrance will be momentarily blinding — close your eyes before you reach the opening and let them adjust gradually through your eyelids. Stumbling out of the entrance half-blind into a floodplain with uneven footing is how you twist an ankle.

## After the Visit

### Physical Recovery

You will be covered in clay. It will be in your hair, under your nails, ground into the fabric of your clothes, smeared across your face where you unconsciously wiped sweat or dripping water. The clay is not just dirt — it's a biological medium, alive with the microbiome of the floodplain, and it will smell like the hollow for hours. Don't rush to wash it off. Sit on the surface, in the light, and let yourself transition. The shift from the hollow to the surface world is jarring — the brightness, the space, the noise of wind and birds, the openness above you. Give yourself time to re-acclimate. Drink water. Eat something. Let the clay dry on your skin and crack, and watch it do so, and understand that the same process happening on your forearm is happening on the floor of the hollow, at the same pace, driven by the same physics.

Then wash. Warm water if available. Get the clay out from between your fingers and toes, from behind your ears, from any skin folds. Check for leeches if the local variant includes them — floodplain cavities in some worlds are leech habitat, and you may not have noticed one attaching in the dark. Check for ticks from the approach through the forest. Standard post-wilderness hygiene.

### Emotional and Psychological Processing

This is the hard part, and the part no preparation can fully address.

The hollow changes people. Not dramatically — you won't come back speaking in tongues or weeping uncontrollably (probably). But the experience of being inside an ongoing geological process, of being reclassified as landscape by another living creature, of hearing a soundscape that refuses to submit to pattern, of touching architecture in the act of dissolving — these experiences accumulate into something that doesn't fit neatly into the categories your mind uses to organize events.

You may find yourself, in the days after, unusually aware of impermanence. The settling of your house, the erosion of a curb, the way water stains spread on a ceiling — things you've never noticed will become vivid because the hollow has tuned your perception to notice processes rather than states. This fades, usually, within a week or two. But some travelers report a lasting shift — a tendency to see every structure, every landscape, every settled thing as a temporary arrangement of matter in the process of becoming something else. This is not a delusion. It is accurate. The hollow just showed you what was always true.

You may also find yourself seeking out darkness more often, or lingering in enclosed spaces, or pausing to listen to rain with an attention that puzzles the people around you. These are echoes. They're normal. They mean the hollow did what it does, which is to remind you that there are entire worlds operating in the dark, at scales and speeds you usually ignore, and that those worlds are no less real or important than the one you walk around in upright, in the light, with your watch on and your phone in your pocket.

Let the echoes happen. Don't explain them to people who weren't there. Some experiences are not transferable. The hollow is one of them.

## What Not to Do

- **Do not take anything from the hollow.** Not a stone, not a piece of the wasp nest, not a fragment of root. Everything in the hollow is part of the hollow. Removing something doesn't give you a souvenir — it gives you a piece of a dismantled process.

- **Do not leave anything in the hollow.** Not intentionally, anyway. Check your pockets before you enter. If something falls from your clothing — a thread, a hair — it's unavoidable. But don't leave objects. A piece of plastic or metal in the hollow would persist longer than the hollow itself. You'd be leaving a fossil of your visit in a place that shouldn't have fossils yet.

- **Do not bring other people.** The hollow is too small. Two human bodies would fill it to the point where the experience becomes about managing proximity to another person rather than being present in the space. Go alone. This is non-negotiable.

- **Do not go during active flooding.** The hollow exists because the clay substrate slumped; during high water, the whole pocket could be submerged, or the entrance could be blocked by debris, or the clay floor could become liquid enough to be genuinely dangerous. Visit on the trailing edge of wet season, when the water table is high enough to keep the hollow active and dripping but low enough that you're not entering a drainage system.

- **Do not try to enlarge the entrance or modify the space.** Don't scrape clay from the walls, don't break roots to create more headroom, don't move stones to clear a path. The hollow is what it is. You fit in it, or you don't. If you're too large for the entrance, this is not your place. There will be others.

- **Do not stay too long.** I mean this literally — the mild hypothermia risk is real — but also metaphorically. The hollow is seductive. Its warmth, its darkness, its rhythmic sound, its absolute removal from everything that constitutes your normal life — these combine into something that feels like home, or at least like rest, and the temptation to stay longer than you should is powerful. Respect the temptation. Recognize it. And leave anyway. The hollow will be here — for a season, for a decade, for however long the geology permits — and it will be just as dark and warm and dripping the next time, and the salamander or its descendants will walk across the clay floor, and the roots will keep reaching down into nothing, and none of it needs you there to happen.

You are a visitor. Visit. Then go back to the light and live the rest of your life slightly differently, in ways you won't be able to fully explain, because you spent time in a place the world was still deciding whether to finish, and it changed you in ways the world hasn't decided whether to finish either.

- Expendable outer clothing (dark, flexible, snag-resistant)
- Thin knee and elbow protection
- Minimalist water shoes or neoprene socks
- Fitted head covering
- Optional nitrile gloves (in waterproof pocket)
- Dim red LED light (handheld, not headlamp)
- Small waterproof pouch for essentials
- Clean drinking water (500ml minimum)
- Optional thin closed-cell foam sitting pad
- Nothing else
