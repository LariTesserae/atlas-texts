# Preparing to Visit The Drowned Courts

## First: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

This is not a ruin. Ruins imply something that stopped. The Drowned Courts have not stopped. They have *continued* past the point where continuation and stillness become the same thing. You are visiting a place that has achieved a state of geological self-perpetuation — water dissolving stone, stone reshaping water, the feedback loop running without interruption for a duration that has begun to erode the structural properties of space itself. This is not a hostile environment in any conventional sense. Nothing here will attack you, poison you, or trap you with intent. But the place operates on principles that have drifted away from the ones your body and mind evolved to navigate, and that drift is the danger.

You are not exploring. You are *entering a medium*.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear and Lower Body

The water is shin-to-knee depth, blood-warm, and possesses a described "thickness" — a resistance that is not current but something more like viscosity or reluctance. Standard waterproof boots will keep your feet dry for approximately no time at all. The water here does not respect barriers the way water in baseline realities does; it exists in a state of deep equilibrium with stone, and anything that enters the water becomes part of the negotiation between medium and material.

Instead of waterproofing, optimize for:

- **Drainage and quick-dry capability.** You will be wet. Accept this immediately. Lightweight wading shoes or neoprene dive boots with thick soles will give you traction on the worn-smooth flagstones without creating suction problems. The stone surfaces are described as "smooth as soap" — friction coefficients will be very low, especially on inclines and stairs.

- **Shin and knee guards.** Not armor — something low-profile, like neoprene sleeves with light rigid inserts. If the water depth fluctuates to knee height and you're navigating eroded stairs or submerged thresholds you can't see clearly in the murky non-light, you will bark your shins on stone edges that have been rounded just enough to not cut you but not enough to not hurt. The water's warmth will mask minor injuries. You won't feel a scrape until it becomes something worse.

- **Nothing with laces, buckles, or complex fastening systems.** The mineral content of this water is significant — calcium-rich, saturated with dissolved stone. Anything with small mechanical parts will calcify. Given enough exposure, metal clasps will develop a thin mineral film that bonds them shut. Slip-on designs. Velcro if you must, but understand that velcro loops will fill with mineral deposits and lose grip over the course of a long visit.

### Clothing

The heat is described as "close and total," sourced from the water, the stone, and the air itself. Humidity is absolute or near-absolute. You will not evaporate sweat. Your body's primary cooling mechanism is inoperative here.

- **Wear as little as your comfort and safety allow.** Lightweight, loose-fitting synthetics. Nothing cotton — cotton will absorb the mineral-laden water, become heavy, and begin to stiffen as dissolved calcium deposits in the fibers. Over a long enough visit, a cotton garment will slowly calcite itself into something approaching fabric-shaped stone. This is not a metaphor. This is what happens when you soak organic material in supersaturated mineral water in a warm environment with no evaporation.

- **Consider a wide-brimmed hat or hood**, not for rain or sun (there is neither) but for the condensation mist inside larger structures. The mist in the colonnade building is described as warm and persistent. It will bead on your face, run into your eyes, and carry dissolved minerals that will sting mildly and, over time, leave a faint chalky residue on your skin.

- **Bring a thin, hydrophobic neck gaiter or face covering** for when you enter interior spaces where the air is more moisture than gas. You are breathing the exhalation of dissolving stone. The mineral sweetness described is calcium bicarbonate in vapor form. Short-term exposure is not dangerous. Extended breathing of heavily mineralized warm mist can irritate airways and, in theory, begin depositing trace calcium in your lungs. A damp cloth filter is primitive but functional. Something with activated carbon filtration is better.

### Skin Protection

Your skin will be in contact with warm mineral water for the duration of your visit. Consider:

- **Pre-application of a barrier cream or medical-grade moisture barrier** on your lower legs and any skin likely to be submerged. The water is not caustic, but it is saturated, and prolonged immersion in supersaturated mineral solution can leach moisture from your skin even as it surrounds you with water. You may emerge with skin that feels simultaneously waterlogged and dehydrated — puffy but cracked. Barrier cream prevents the mineral exchange from reaching your actual epidermis.

- **Inspect your skin regularly** during the visit. If you notice white mineral deposits forming on your body hair or in your pores, it is time to rinse with clean carried water and reapply barrier protection. The mineral deposition is not harmful at trace levels but indicates that the water is beginning to treat you the way it treats the stone — as substrate.

## Navigation and Orientation

This is where preparation becomes critical and also where preparation becomes, to some degree, an act of faith, because the space itself is not stable.

### The Geometry Problem

The Drowned Courts exhibit spatial properties that have been altered by the same erosive processes that have smoothed the stone. Angles have softened. Parallel lines are no longer parallel by small, accumulating fractions. Streets curve in excess of 360 degrees without closing. Straight lines bend behind you once you've walked them. Distances lie.

This is not an illusion. This is not a cognitive effect. The space itself has *eroded*. The way a right angle in a carved stone block becomes an almost-right-angle after millennia of water exposure, the right angles of the city's spatial geometry have dissolved into approximations, and those approximations compound over distance to produce navigational impossibilities.

**What this means for you:**

- **Conventional mapping is useless.** Do not attempt to draw a map. You will produce something that contradicts itself, and the contradictions will not be errors — they will be accurate representations of a self-contradictory space. If anything, the act of trying to map the city will damage your spatial reasoning faster than simply accepting the wrongness.

- **Breadcrumb trails are unreliable.** Leaving markers — stones, chalk marks, dropped items — will work for short distances but fail over longer routes, because the spatial distortion means the straight line back to your marker may not exist as a traversable path, even if you can see the marker. Additionally, the water will dissolve chalk, and any small object left in the shallows may slowly be incorporated into the mineral deposition cycle — not quickly, but fast enough that a coin left on a flagstone might, over hours, acquire a thin mineral bond to the stone beneath it.

- **Your best navigational tool is the depth of the water.** The water is shallower at the edges of the city and deeper toward the center. This is the one gradient that appears to remain consistent, because it follows the city's original topography (which slopes inward) and the water's own behavior (which pools toward the center). If the water is rising on your legs, you are moving inward. If it is dropping, you are moving outward. **This is your compass.** Calibrate all your decisions to it.

- **Do not navigate by landmarks.** The buildings are too similar — same material, same state of erosion, same weeping walls. The carvings that might once have distinguished one district from another have been smoothed into ambiguity. You will think you recognize a specific column or doorway and you will be wrong, or you will be right but the space between you and it will have shifted, and arriving at a recognized landmark from a different direction than expected will only compound your disorientation.

### A Recommended Navigation Strategy

Move radially. Enter the city along one of the processional avenues that spoke inward from the perimeter. Walk inward to whatever depth feels sustainable. When you wish to return, turn around and walk outward, using the water depth as your guide. Do not attempt to explore laterally — do not take side streets, do not circle blocks, do not attempt to visit "one more district over." Every lateral movement introduces angular error that the eroded geometry will amplify. Think of the city as a whirlpool of broken space: you can move in and out along the current, but moving across it will spin you.

If you become disoriented and cannot determine which direction leads outward:

1. Stand still.
2. Feel the water against your legs.
3. Wait.

The water has a tidal rhythm — sourceless, moonless, but present. If you stand still long enough (minutes, not hours, though time-sense may also have softened here — more on this below), you will feel the water rise or fall. When it falls, note which direction it seems to recede — that direction is outward. Follow it. When the tide rises, the water approaches from the center; when it falls, it withdraws toward the edges. This is the respiration of the city, and it is the most reliable directional signal available.

### The Center

Do not go to the center.

This is not a prohibition based on danger in the conventional sense. The description makes clear that the center is where the erosion is most advanced — where spatial geometry has dissolved past approximation into genuine impossibility. Stairs that descend to rooftops. Cul-de-sacs that are throughways. Water flowing uphill because "downhill" no longer has a consistent local definition.

If you enter the center, you may not be able to leave by any method that relies on spatial reasoning, because spatial reasoning requires space to have consistent properties, and it does not, there. The water depth trick may still work — the center should be the deepest point — but if "down" has become unreliable, then depth itself may have become a concept that the center has dissolved.

The center is not forbidden. It is simply *further along* in the process that the rest of the city is undergoing, and "further along" here means "closer to a state where the distinction between being somewhere and not being somewhere has eroded to the point of meaninglessness." You can prepare for many things. You cannot prepare for the dissolution of the concept of position.

If you are an experienced multiverse traveler and you want to approach the center, do so on a subsequent visit, after you have calibrated your senses to the city's baseline strangeness. And bring an anchor — something I'll discuss below.

## Temporal Preparation

### Time in The Drowned Courts

The description does not explicitly state that time behaves abnormally, but consider: the same erosive process that softened the city's angles, smoothed its carvings, and dissolved its spatial geometry has been running long enough to achieve geological results. If that process affects space, there is no principled reason to assume it has not also affected time. Time is a dimension. Dimensions here erode.

**Practical signs that your time-sense is being affected:**

- You lose track of how long you have been standing still. Not in the normal way — not distraction or reverie — but in a way where the question "how long" begins to feel like it has the same soft edges as the carvings on the cornices. You know you've been standing. You know you stopped at some point. The interval between those two facts is... approximate.

- The drip you hear in the interior courtyards — the one with "the regularity of a mechanism" — begins to seem faster or slower than it did when you first noticed it. Both are possible. Neither may be real. The drip may be the most stable temporal reference in the city, or it may be the thing your brain latches onto *because* it mimics regularity, the way the eroded scroll-shapes on the walls mimic ornamentation.

- You feel no urgency. This is the most insidious symptom. The Drowned Courts are saturated with a quality that the description captures precisely: the continuous present tense. Everything here is happening *now*, in the same way it has been happening for millennia, and the warmth of the water and the closeness of the air and the sourceless murky non-light all conspire to create an environment where the difference between "I have been here for twenty minutes" and "I have been here for six hours" becomes a soft-edged question with no sharp answer.

**Countermeasures:**

- **Bring a mechanical timepiece.** Not electronic — electronics in environments with this level of mineral-saturated moisture will corrode or malfunction. A sealed mechanical watch, ideally one rated for diving, will give you an external temporal reference. Check it frequently. If you notice yourself forgetting to check it, check it immediately. If the intervals between checks are growing longer, you are being temporally eroded. Leave.

- **Set a firm exit time before you enter and do not negotiate with yourself about it.** "I will leave after two hours" is a bright-line rule. The Drowned Courts will present you with countless soft reasons to stay — another colonnade to see, another mosaic to watch turn, another street to follow — and each reason will feel gentle and reasonable, because nothing here is urgent, and that lack of urgency is the trap. The city does not need to hold you. It only needs to make leaving feel like an interruption of something that wasn't finished, and nothing here is ever finished, and nothing here will ever be finished.

- **Bring a partner, or, failing that, a tether.** A physical line connecting you to something outside the city — tied to a post or a stone at the perimeter — serves both as a navigational aid (you can follow it back) and a temporal anchor (the act of feeling the line in your hand reminds you that you are a person who entered from somewhere and intends to return there). The line should be synthetic cord, not rope (rope will waterlog and mineralize), and should be thicker than you think necessary (thin cord will become difficult to feel in the warm water, which erases the sensation of edges against your skin).

## Psychological Preparation

### The Mood of the Place

The Drowned Courts are not threatening. This needs to be understood deeply, because your threat-detection systems — the ones that keep you alive in dangerous multiverse locations — will not activate here, and their silence will feel like safety, and it is not safety. It is *irrelevance*. The Drowned Courts are not safe or unsafe. They are *solved*. They have reached an equilibrium that does not include you, does not exclude you, and does not care about the distinction.

The dominant emotional experience, based on the description, will be:

**A profound, warm, encompassing melancholy that does not hurt.**

This is the most dangerous thing about the place.

You will feel a sadness that is not your sadness. It is the sadness of the inscriptions that no longer know what they said. The sadness of the faces on the cornice that have become smooth lozenges. The sadness of the market stalls still shaped for use in a place where nothing uses them. But this sadness will not sting or ache — it will feel like warm water around your legs, something you barely notice, something that is simply *present*, and its warmth will make it feel almost like comfort, almost like being held, and that almost is where people get lost.

**You must maintain the distinction between beauty and invitation.**

The erosion has made everything beautiful. The description is explicit about this: water, given enough time, finds the shapes it wants, and those shapes are always beautiful, and the beauty is always indifferent. You will see curves in the stone that look designed by a master sculptor. You will see the mosaic turning on the floor like a living mandala. You will feel the warm mist on your face and hear the deep hum of the city's foundations and think: *I could stay here. I could watch this forever.* And you could. The city will not stop you. The city will not even notice. You will become another presence-that-is-not-a-presence, another impression in the stone, another smooth channel worn into a threshold by passage.

**Before entering, choose a memory — a specific, sharp-edged memory — and hold it.**

Something with strong sensory detail. The sound of a specific person's laugh. The taste of a specific meal. The feeling of cold air (cold — not warm, there is no cold here). Something that has *edges*, because the Drowned Courts dissolve edges. When you feel the melancholy settling over you like warm water, retrieve that memory. Feel its specificity, its angularity, its refusal to be smooth. That is your psychological anchor. The city erodes generalities — "happiness," "purpose," "home" — these are abstract enough for the warm water to soften. But a specific sensory memory, a precise and irreducible moment, resists erosion the way a harder stone resists water. Not forever. But long enough.

### The Absorption Effect

The description notes that the water "absorbs light the way a throat absorbs sound." This is a recurring principle of the place: it absorbs. Light, sound, edges, angles, distinctions. It will absorb your attention. It will absorb your sense of separateness from the environment. The water is blood-warm — the same temperature as your body — which means that after enough time wading, you will lose the sensory boundary between yourself and the water. You will not feel where your skin ends and the water begins. This is a physical fact with psychological consequences.

Periodically — every fifteen to twenty minutes, by your mechanical watch — do something that reasserts your boundary. Splash yourself. Slap the water's surface. Speak out loud (your voice will sound wrong here — flat, absorbed, as though the murky air is drinking it — but the act of producing sound from your own body is the point). Pinch your own skin. Remind your nervous system that you are a discrete entity moving through an environment, not a component of the environment. The city will not try to absorb you. But the city will not try to *not* absorb you, and in an environment this passive, the default outcome is incorporation.

## Equipment

### What to Bring

- **Light source:** Bring one, but understand its limitations. The murky non-light of the Drowned Courts is sourceless, and directed light (flashlights, lanterns) will behave strangely — the beam will be visible as a column of illuminated mist, and the mineral-saturated air will scatter it in all directions, reducing effective range and creating a glowing halo around you rather than a directional beam. Still useful for examining specific surfaces up close — the inscriptions, the mosaics, the almost-carvings. Use a sealed, waterproof light with a warm spectrum. Cool white LEDs will look alien and wrong here in a way that is not just aesthetic but somehow *offensive* to the environment — the murky non-light has a warm tonal quality, and introducing a sharp blue-white light feels like shouting in a library. This may be purely psychological or it may not.

- **Water.** Bring your own drinking water. Sealed containers. Do not drink the water of the Drowned Courts. It is supersaturated with dissolved minerals and will taste chalky-sweet. More importantly, it is the medium of the city's ongoing dissolution process, and introducing it into your body invites the same mineral-exchange cycle that is slowly reshaping the stone. Your kidneys can handle mineral water. They cannot handle water that is actively participating in a geological feedback loop that has been running for longer than your species has existed. This may be an irrational concern. Bring your own water anyway.

- **Food.** Simple, sealed, calorie-dense. Nothing that needs to be unwrapped and held in the open air for long — the moisture will get into it. Nothing that can absorb water from the environment. Energy gels in sealed packets are ideal. Trail mix will turn into a bag of damp chalk-coated pebbles within an hour.

- **Notebook and pencil**, if you want to record observations. Not pen — ink will run in this humidity. Pencil on waterproof paper (such as "Rite in the Rain" brand or equivalent from your home reality). Be warned: the act of writing in the Drowned Courts feels *transgressive*, not because it is forbidden but because it is the act of making sharp marks — specific, angular, deliberate — in a place where all marks are being slowly unmade. You may find yourself reluctant to write. You may find your notes, when you read them later, seem less precise than you intended, the words chosen softer and less certain than your usual voice. This is either the environment influencing you or your own accurate response to a place where certainty is an eroding quality. Record your observations anyway. Soft notes are better than no notes.

- **Synthetic cord**, as discussed, for a tether line. At least 200 meters. The city is extensive, and you will want to penetrate at least some distance inward.

- **A sealed sample container**, if you are scientifically inclined. The water, the not-quite-lichen rust-orange growth, the mineral deposits — all are worth analysis. Collect samples near the perimeter where the spatial geometry is least distorted. Do not collect samples from deep in the city. Objects removed from areas of heavy spatial erosion may carry trace geometric distortions with them — a sample jar that is slightly larger on the inside than the outside, a flagstone chip whose angles don't quite add up to what they should. These are curiosities in small doses and deeply unsettling structural problems in large ones.

- **Mechanical watch**, as discussed. Waterproof. Check it. Keep checking it.

### What Not to Bring

- **Electronic devices.** The humidity will kill them, and the mineral water will corrode contacts. GPS, digital compasses, and electronic mapping tools are additionally useless because the spatial geometry does not conform to the assumptions those tools are built on. A GPS unit in the Drowned Courts will either show you standing still while you walk, or moving in directions that don't correspond to your actual movement, or — in areas of heavy erosion — experiencing a position error that grows without bound.

- **Iron or steel tools.** The mineral water is not just calcium-rich; the rust-orange deposits suggest iron in solution or in transition. Ferrous metals will oxidize rapidly. Your dive watch should be titanium or ceramic. Your knife (if you bring one, though there is nothing here that needs cutting) should be ceramic or high-grade stainless.

- **Books.** I know. You think a book would be good company, a reminder of the outside world, something to anchor you. But paper will absorb the moisture, swell, warp, and the ink will bleed, and watching your book slowly dissolve — the words blurring, the pages merging — will be a visceral and deeply distressing enactment of what the city does to all inscriptions. You will watch a book undergo in hours what the wall-inscriptions underwent in centuries, and it will feel like a warning. Leave your books outside.

- **Anything you are emotionally attached to.** The Drowned Courts will not destroy your belongings. But they will *soften* them. A piece of jewelry exposed to the water will develop a mineral film that dulls its shine. A photograph will smear. A keepsake made of organic material — wood, leather, bone — will begin to absorb the water's mineral character, will become slightly heavier, slightly smoother, slightly less like the thing it was. You will not lose your possessions. You will have them returned to you *approximated*, and that is worse.

## The Inhabitants (Or Their Absence)

There are no inhabitants. The description is emphatic about this. No animals, no plants beyond the ambiguous rust-orange non-growth, no people. The city is empty of life.

And yet.

The city remembers use. Depressions in counters where vessels sat. Channels in thresholds where feet passed. The architecture of daily life — markets, baths, public halls, residential quarters — all still legible, all still arranged for a population that is not here. The city is shaped by its former inhabitants the way a riverbed is shaped by a former river.

You may feel watched. This is not because anything is watching you. It is because every surface you touch, every threshold you cross, every room you enter, is *shaped for interaction* — shaped by millennia of use, worn into forms that expect a body, accommodate a body, welcome a body. The doorways are the right height. The counters are the right width. The steps are the right depth. The city's erosion has not removed these human-scaled proportions; if anything, the softening has made them *more* comfortable, more perfectly fitted to a body moving through space, because water erodes away the unnecessary and leaves what works. You fit here. You fit here in a way that is not random, because the city was shaped by people and the water has refined that shaping.

This is uncomfortable in a way that takes time to identify. It is the discomfort of being perfectly accommodated by something that does not know you are there. A glove that fits your hand but has no idea it's a glove.

**Do not interpret the city's accommodation as welcome.** It is residual. It is erosion. It is the shape left behind when the purpose that created it has dissolved, the way the inscriptions are shapes left behind when the language that created them dissolved. You fit the city the way you would fit a fossil — not because the fossil was made for you, but because you and it share a common template, and the template is what survives when everything specific is worn away.

## Contingencies

### If You Become Lost

Follow the water-depth gradient outward. If you cannot determine the gradient, stand still and wait for a tidal shift. If you cannot feel a tidal shift, you may have moved closer to the center than intended, where the tidal rhythm, like all other rhythms, has been eroded into ambiguity. In this case: follow your tether line, if you brought one. If you did not bring one, follow the *sound* — listen for the drip-rhythms of the outer districts, which are more regular and distinct than the inner districts where all sounds blur into the pervasive hum. Move toward distinctness. Move toward the parts of the city where things are still *different from each other* — where one building is distinguishable from the next, where a street is clearly a street and a plaza is clearly a plaza. The center is where distinctions dissolve. The edge is where they persist. Walk toward distinction.

### If You Feel You Have Been There Too Long But Don't Want to Leave

Leave. This is the city working on you. The warm water, the beautiful curves, the melancholy that doesn't hurt, the continuous present tense — they are all eroding your will to depart, not through force but through the gentle dissolution of the urgency that would drive departure. You don't *need* to leave, the city whispers in its language of warm water and smooth stone. Nothing is threatening you. Nothing is hurrying you. There is always more to see, and it will always be here, and you will always be here, and the difference between "visiting" and "staying" is just another sharp edge that the water can soften.

Check your watch. If the time you set for departure has passed and you are still here and you are having this internal debate, you are already experiencing erosion of intent. Leave immediately. Walk outward. Do not stop to look at one more thing.

### If You Notice Mineral Deposits on Your Skin That Do Not Wash Off with Carried Water

This means the mineral exchange has progressed beyond surface deposition into actual bonding with your tissue. This is not immediately dangerous but indicates a far longer exposure than is advisable. Leave, and upon leaving, soak the affected area in mildly acidic solution (dilute vinegar, citrus juice) to dissolve the calcium deposits. If the deposits have reached your nail beds or the corners of your eyes, seek medical attention from a practitioner familiar with cross-reality mineral contamination.

### If the Space Becomes Noticeably Non-Euclidean Around You

You are too deep. The eroded geometry has reached a degree of distortion that your body can detect. Signs include: hallways that feel longer than they look, rooms whose corners you cannot count consistently, the feeling that a wall you are facing is also somehow *behind* you. Turn around — if "around" is still a meaningful concept — and move outward along the water-depth gradient. Move slowly. Abrupt movement in heavily eroded space can feel like nausea or vertigo, because your vestibular system is receiving contradictory information about your orientation. The space is not dangerous in the sense of collapsing or trapping, but navigating it requires accepting that your proprioception is unreliable, and the psychological cost of that acceptance is real.

## The Experience

If you do everything right — if you stay to the outer and middle districts, if you keep to radial paths, if you check your watch and maintain your sensory boundaries and hold your sharp-edged memory — then visiting the Drowned Courts will be one of the most extraordinary and quietly devastating experiences available to a multiverse traveler.

You will wade through warm water in dim amber murk, past buildings that look like they were dreamed rather than built, through streets that curve with the logic of water rather than the logic of planning. You will touch stone that weeps, and it will feel like touching something that is alive and patient in a way that no living thing is patient. You will stand in the colonnade building and watch a mosaic older than any civilization you've ever heard of slowly rearrange itself, one molecule at a time, into a pattern that might be a map of what the city was or what it is becoming or both, and you will realize that the difference between those two things is exactly the kind of distinction that this place dissolves.

You will feel a sadness that is bottomless and gentle. You will feel accommodated by a world that has forgotten what accommodation means. You will hear the hum of stone vibrating in water and it will sound like the lowest possible note of the longest possible song, and you will understand that the song is not for you, was never for you, and will continue long after everything that could listen has been worn smooth.

And then you will check your watch, and it will be time to go, and you will walk outward through the warm shallows, and the city will close behind you with the patience of something that has been doing this forever, and you will emerge at the perimeter, and the air will feel thin and cold and sharp, and you will realize that you are shivering, and you will realize that you are crying, and you will not be entirely sure when either of those things started.

Bring a towel for after. A rough one. You will want the texture.
