# Preparing for the Forges of Thaal Kethis: A Comprehensive Traveler's Guide

---

## First: A Reckoning With What This Place Is

Before you pack a single item, before you research a single piece of gear, you need to sit with the description of this place and understand what you're walking into, because your preparation will fail if it's organized around the wrong assumptions.

Thaal Kethis is not a ruin. It is not a city you visit the way you visit a city. It is not a wilderness you traverse the way you traverse a wilderness. It is an active geological-industrial process that happens to be shaped like a city, and you are proposing to walk through it the way a blood cell walks through a living organ. The organ will not attack you. It will not welcome you. It will note your presence the way your liver notes the passage of a molecule — with a kind of systemic, non-conscious registration that is neither hostile nor hospitable but simply *attentive* in a way that has nothing to do with care.

This means your preparation must be oriented around three realities:

1. **You are in an environment of persistent, inescapable heat** — not the dry heat of a desert, not the wet heat of a jungle, but a mineral, metallic, geological heat that radiates from below, from the structures around you, and from the air itself, which is thick with thermal convection and copper-tasting particulates.

2. **You are alone.** There is no one to help you. There are no markets, no inns, no other travelers, no communication infrastructure. The arthropods are not hostile, but they are not friends. The larger shape you might see climbing the scaffolding is not a guide. You are a foreign body in a closed system, and everything you need, you must carry.

3. **You will be there for longer than you think.** The city is miles across. Three days of walking and you haven't reached an edge. The terrain is uneven, vertical, broken by sudden drops and new stone upwellings. Your pace will slow. The heat will slow you further. Plan for a minimum of seven to ten days inside the city, with contingency supplies for fourteen.

Hold those three truths in your mind. Everything that follows flows from them.

## Hydration: The Central Problem

Let me be blunt: water is the thing that will kill you in Thaal Kethis, or rather the lack of it. Every other preparation is secondary to this. You are walking through an environment where:

- The ambient temperature is extreme and sustained, meaning you will sweat constantly and profusely.
- The air is thick and hot, which paradoxically can suppress the sensation of sweating (your sweat evaporates so quickly in some conditions, or fails to evaporate at all in others, that you may not realize how much fluid you're losing).
- Your water supply will warm to ambient temperature within an hour of entering the city, meaning you'll be drinking hot water, which is less effective at cooling your core and less psychologically satisfying, which can lead to under-drinking.
- The only local water sources are condensation channels and thermal pools, both of which are mineral-heavy, tasting of iron and that unnamed blue-black crystal, and of completely unknown chemical composition.

### What to Carry

You need a minimum of **four liters of water per day** for heavy exertion in extreme heat, and realistically more like five to six given the sustained nature of the thermal exposure. For a ten-day traverse with a four-day contingency, that's 56-84 liters, which weighs 56-84 kilograms. You cannot carry this. No human can carry this and also carry everything else needed and also navigate vertical terrain.

This means you **must** plan to supplement from local sources, which means you need:

- **A robust water filtration system.** Not a simple hiking filter — those are designed for biological contaminants. You need something that addresses dissolved minerals and metals. A portable system with activated carbon filtration combined with a ceramic pre-filter is a starting point, but honestly, you need to go further. Consider carrying a small portable **distillation setup** — something that can boil the mineral-laden condensation water and collect the steam. The thermal energy is free and abundant; you just need a vessel, a condensation coil or surface, and a collection container. A compact copper still or a purpose-built solar (thermal) distiller that can be jury-rigged to use ambient heat from the iron structures rather than sunlight. Given that every surface around you is warm, you could construct a passive distillation system using a metal container placed on a warm girder, with a cooler collection surface above it (perhaps elevated into the slightly cooler upper air or wrapped in a wet cloth that you periodically re-dampen).

- **Carry at minimum eight liters of clean water** for the initial approach and entry, enough for a day and a half of heavy exertion, buying you time to locate, test, and begin processing local water.

- **Electrolyte supplementation.** Critical. You will be losing sodium, potassium, magnesium, and other salts at a tremendous rate through sweat. Carry powdered electrolyte mixes — not sports drink powder, but proper oral rehydration salts. Enough for every day you plan to be inside the city, plus your contingency days. This is non-negotiable. Hyponatremia from drinking mineral-depleted distilled water or simply from massive electrolyte loss through sweat can kill you or incapacitate you, and in Thaal Kethis, incapacitation is death.

- **Water testing capability.** Carry a basic water quality testing kit — pH strips at minimum, and if you can source them, heavy metal test strips for iron, copper, and lead. The condensation water may be relatively pure (it's essentially distilled by the thermal process that created it), but it runs across iron and the blue-black mineral before you collect it, picking up dissolved metals. The thermal pool water is almost certainly unsafe to drink without treatment, given its mineral load and the fact that it's been sitting in contact with freshly extruded, chemically active stone.

- **Multiple water containers**, some insulated if possible. An insulated bottle will keep water merely warm rather than hot for longer. The psychological benefit of drinking water that is 30°C rather than 50°C is real and meaningful.

### The Condensation Channels

These are your best local water source. The description notes them as narrow channels and cisterns worked into the framework, collecting moisture that condenses where hot foundry air meets cooler upper air. This water is essentially distilled — it evaporated from thermal sources, condensed on metal surfaces, and collected in channels. However:

- It has been in contact with iron (rust, dissolved iron) and the blue-black mineral (unknown composition, unknown solubility, unknown toxicity).
- It tastes of iron and that crystal, which means dissolved metals are present.
- It may contain metallic particulates from the arthropods, which appear to use the channels.

Filter and/or distill this water before drinking. If distillation equipment is impractical to carry in sufficient quantity, at minimum filter through activated carbon and ceramic, and monitor your intake for any signs of metal toxicity: metallic taste that intensifies over time, gastrointestinal distress, unusual fatigue, joint pain, or discoloration of urine.

## Thermal Protection: Living Inside the Heat

You are not visiting a hot place. You are entering a thermal system. The heat in Thaal Kethis is not weather — it is infrastructure. It comes from below (geological vents), from the sides (radiating iron and stone), from objects you must touch (handrails, walkways, gantry surfaces), and it is modulated but never absent. Even the "coolest" areas — upper gantries, elevated walkways exposed to whatever breeze exists at this altitude — are still profoundly hot by any normal standard.

### Clothing

Your instinct will be to wear as little as possible. Resist this. Exposed skin in Thaal Kethis faces:

- **Radiant heat burns** from proximity to foundry vents, newly extruded stone, and heated metal surfaces.
- **Contact burns** from touching surfaces that range from warm to genuinely dangerous.
- **Particulate exposure** — the air contains copper filings and sulfurous mineral particles that will irritate and damage exposed skin over days of contact.
- **UV exposure** — you are at extreme altitude with minimal atmospheric protection, and the amber sky may not filter UV the way you expect. The sun is described as flattened, white, and too close. Until you know the UV index, treat it as extreme.

Wear:

- **Loose, lightweight, long-sleeved clothing** made of a fabric designed for extreme heat. Merino wool is good (it wicks, breathes, resists odor, and provides moderate UV protection), but consider purpose-built technical fabrics designed for desert or industrial environments. Light colors to reflect radiant heat. A fabric with a tight weave to block particulates but enough breathability to allow sweat evaporation.

- **A head covering that shades your neck and face.** A wide-brimmed hat or a desert-style shemagh/keffiyeh that can be wrapped to protect the face and neck while allowing breathing. The copper-and-sulfur taste of the air suggests particulates you don't want to inhale directly — more on this below.

- **Gloves.** Essential. You will be gripping iron gantries, handrails, and walkways that are warm to hot. Thin, heat-resistant work gloves — not oven mitts, you need dexterity — made of leather or a heat-resistant synthetic. Consider the type used by welders for light work: thin enough to grip and manipulate, thick enough to protect against surfaces up to maybe 60-70°C. Carry two pairs, because sweat will saturate them and wet gloves transmit heat faster.

- **Boots.** The ground is raw stone — basalt and that blue-black glassy mineral — that is warm and may be hot in places. Standard hiking boots with thick soles will provide some insulation, but consider boots with a heat-resistant sole rated for industrial environments. The soles must be rigid enough to protect against the uneven, fractured stone terrain (hexagonal columns, mirror-smooth faces, sudden edges) while insulating against heat transmitted from the ground. Ankle support is critical — the terrain is irregular, with sudden drops and angled surfaces.

- **Gaiters or high boot coverage** to protect lower legs from steam vents at ground level. The description mentions thin constant mist at lower levels — this mist is near-boiling water vapor, and walking through it with exposed calves is inviting scalding.

### Cooling Strategies

You cannot cool yourself with the environment — the environment is hot in every direction. Your only cooling resources are:

- **Evaporative cooling from your own sweat**, which works only if the air is dry enough. The air in Thaal Kethis is described as thick, and the lower levels have persistent steam mist, suggesting significant humidity in many areas. Upper gantries may be drier. Plan to spend the hottest periods (if there is diurnal variation — the description is ambiguous on this) at higher elevations where evaporative cooling may be more effective.

- **Wet cloths.** Soak a bandana or cloth in your water supply and drape it around your neck or under your hat. The evaporation will cool you. This uses water, so it competes with drinking, but the thermoregulatory benefit can be worth it.

- **The condensation areas.** Where the city's hot and cool air meet, the temperature gradients suggest zones of relatively cooler air. These won't be *cool* — they'll just be less hot. Find them, map them, use them as rest spots.

- **Rest during the hottest periods.** If the amber sky brightens further at midday and the flattened sun is overhead, the radiant heat load will increase. Travel during what passes for dawn and dusk, and shelter during peak heat inside enclosed corridors where the thermal mass of the structure may moderate temperature slightly. Although — those corridors are where it rains indoors, so you'll be trading heat for humidity. Choose your discomfort.

- **A reflective emergency blanket** can be repurposed as a radiant heat shield — hold it between you and a particularly hot surface or foundry vent. It's lightweight, packable, and surprisingly effective at bouncing thermal radiation.

## Respiratory Protection

The air in Thaal Kethis is described as tasting of copper filings and sulfur, with something older and more mineral beneath it. This is not atmosphere you want to be breathing unfiltered for ten days.

**Copper dust** inhalation can cause metal fume fever, lung irritation, and over longer exposures, more serious respiratory damage. **Sulfur dioxide** (the likely source of the sulfurous taste) is a respiratory irritant that can cause bronchospasm, coughing, and in high concentrations, pulmonary edema. The "something older" and "something mineral" could be almost anything — silicates from the volcanic stone, trace metals from the foundry processes, or particulates from the blue-black crystal whose composition you don't know at all.

### What to Carry

- **A half-face respirator with combination particulate/acid gas cartridges** (P100/OV/AG or equivalent). This is your primary respiratory protection. The particulate filter handles the copper filings and mineral dust. The acid gas element handles sulfur dioxide and other gaseous irritants. Carry at least four replacement cartridge sets — the hot, humid air will load the filters faster than normal, and you don't want to be on day seven with saturated cartridges.

- **A backup:** several N95 or P100 disposable masks for use if your primary respirator fails or during periods when you need to eat/drink and want to transition quickly between protection and access to your mouth.

- **A buff or cloth face covering** impregnated with activated carbon for light protection during periods when the air seems clearer. Not a substitute for the respirator, but useful for sleeping or resting in areas with lower particulate counts.

- **Note:** Wearing a respirator in extreme heat is miserable. It adds resistance to breathing, traps heat around your face, and makes you feel claustrophobic. You will be tempted to take it off. Think carefully about the tradeoff. Consider testing the air quality at various locations and elevations — the upper gantries may have less particulate load, and the wind (if any) at the city's edges may provide better ventilation. If you have access to a portable gas detector that reads SO2 and particulate concentration, carry it and use it to make informed decisions about when the respirator is truly necessary versus when you're being cautious.

## Navigation and Orientation

Thaal Kethis is miles across, three-dimensional (extending vertically through multiple levels of gantries and scaffolding), has no streets or addresses or signage, and is actively changing — new structures rising, old structures being folded into geological upwellings. You cannot get a map because the map would be obsolete before you finished drawing it.

### How to Navigate

- **Altimeter and compass.** The compass may or may not work — the massive quantities of iron in the city's infrastructure may distort magnetic readings. Test it immediately upon entry. If the compass is unreliable, navigate by the sun (the flattened white disc is presumably east-to-west in its transit, giving you directional reference during the day) and by the deep orange glow of the newest geological vents (which seem to be concentrated in areas of active growth — probably the city's expanding edge, giving you a reference for direction of geological advance).

- **Mark your path.** Not with paint or flags on the structures — you don't know how the arthropods or the city's systems would respond to foreign markings, and the structures may be consumed or rebuilt. Instead, keep a detailed written log with bearing, elevation changes, time elapsed, and notable landmarks (distinctive structures, thermal pools, geological features). Your log is your map.

- **Climb high regularly.** The upper gantries give you sightlines across the city. Use them to orient. Look for the plateau's edge — the description mentions gaps where the edge falls away into immeasurable depths, with clouds below. The edge is your exit route. Knowing which direction the nearest edge lies is essential survival information.

- **GPS may not function**, depending on the multiverse's relationship with satellite infrastructure. Do not rely on it.

- **A lightweight, high-powered monocular or compact binoculars** for observing the city from high vantage points, identifying routes through the lower levels, and — yes — watching the larger shapes that move through the scaffolding at a distance, so you can make informed decisions about whether to approach or avoid.

### Route-Finding

The terrain is hostile to walking. Mirror-smooth hexagonal crystal faces will be slippery (especially when wet from condensation). Fresh basalt can be razor-sharp at its edges. Sudden twenty-to-forty-foot drops exist where rock sheets have calved away. The gantries and walkways are your best travel surfaces, but they may dead-end where construction is ongoing or where geological upwelling has deformed the structure.

Carry a short length of **climbing rope** (30-50 meters of lightweight static line), a **harness**, and a few **carabiners and slings**. You may need to descend or ascend faces of calved rock, cross gaps between structures, or lower yourself from a gantry to ground level where the stairs (if they exist) have been consumed by new stone. Basic rappelling and self-belay skills are essential — practice before you go.

**Trekking poles** will help with balance on uneven stone and can be used to test surfaces ahead of you for stability and temperature.

## Food and Nutrition

There is nothing to eat in Thaal Kethis. The arthropods are metallic and are part of the city's recycling system; eating one would be like eating a component, and likely about as nutritious and far more toxic. The thermal pool water contains dissolved minerals, not food. There are no plants, no animals in any conventional sense, no soil for anything to grow in.

Carry all food for the entire duration, plus contingency.

### What to Carry

- **High-calorie, low-weight, no-cook foods.** You do not want to carry a stove. You have ambient heat if you need to warm something (place a foil packet against a warm girder for ten minutes), but primarily you want foods you can eat at ambient temperature because ambient temperature is already warm to hot.

- Nut butters, energy bars, dried fruit, jerky, hard cheeses (in sealed packaging to protect from mineral dust), dense calorie-rich trail mixes, pemmican or similar preserved high-fat-high-protein foods.

- **Caloric density is king.** You need at minimum 3,000-4,000 calories per day for sustained exertion in extreme heat (your metabolism is working hard just to thermoregulate), and you need to carry ten to fourteen days' worth. At roughly 125-150 calories per ounce for the most efficient foods, that's 200-320 ounces, or 12.5-20 pounds of food. Plan accordingly.

- **Salt.** Extra salt, beyond what's in your electrolyte supplements. You are losing salt at a rate you may not appreciate. If you start craving salt, you're already behind. Salt your food aggressively.

- **Appetite suppression warning:** Heat kills appetite. You may feel nauseated by the thought of eating, especially during the hottest hours. Eat anyway. Force yourself on a schedule — every three to four hours, eat something, whether you want it or not. Caloric deficit compounds with dehydration and electrolyte loss to produce a catastrophic decline in function that is hard to reverse in the field.

## Sleep

You will need to sleep, and sleep will be difficult. The heat is persistent (it comes from below, not from the sun, so it does not abate at night), the metal surfaces conduct and radiate heat, and the low subsonic hum of the geological processes is constant, living in your bones, disrupting the deep-sleep stages even if you've habituated to it consciously.

### Sleep System

- **Do not sleep on the ground.** The ground is warm to hot and geologically active. A steam vent can open beneath you. New stone can push up through existing rock on a timescale of hours.

- Sleep on the **gantries or elevated walkways**, as high up as you can comfortably access. Higher means slightly cooler air and distance from the geological heat sources. Choose a gantry that is structurally sound — check for signs of geological deformation or active construction nearby.

- **A lightweight hammock** strung between girders is ideal. It gets you off the warm metal surface, allows air circulation beneath you, and is the most comfortable option in a place with no flat, cool ground. Carry suspension straps wide enough to wrap around the girders (the riveted surface may have sharp edges that could abrade thin cord).

- **Sleeping pad:** If you can't hammock, a closed-cell foam pad (not inflatable — you don't want a hot air mattress) provides insulation from the metal surface. Double it for more insulation if needed.

- **No sleeping bag.** You won't need insulation from cold. Carry a lightweight cotton or silk liner for the psychological comfort of having something over you and for protection against the condensation drips that may fall from structures above.

- **Earplugs** may help with the subsonic hum, though given that it's felt as much as heard, they may not. Worth carrying.

- **An eye mask** for sleep if the geological glow from below and the ambient light of the amber sky create too much illumination during rest periods.

- **Sleep in shifts if traveling with others.** The larger shapes in the scaffolding are uncharacterized. You don't know what they are, what they do, or whether they become more active during particular periods. Having someone awake at all times is prudent.

## Encountering the Fauna

The arthropods and the larger shapes require a strategy. The strategy is: **observe, do not interfere, give way**.

### The Arthropods

They are hand-sized, metallic-carapaced, many-legged, and appear to be part of the city's maintenance/recycling system. They taste rivets, they cluster in condensation channels, and when they die, they dissolve back into trace metals that enter the foundry cycle. They are, for all practical purposes, the city's immune cells or maintenance workers.

- **Do not handle them.** You don't know if they can bite, sting, or inject. Their metallic carapaces may have sharp edges. And more importantly, interfering with them may register with the city's systems in ways you can't predict.

- **Do not block their paths.** If you see arthropods moving with purpose along a seam or channel, step aside. They are doing something the city needs them to do. Being an obstacle to a geological-industrial process is not wise.

- **If they investigate you** — and they might, you are a novel material in their environment — hold still, let them taste or examine whatever they want, and wait for them to move on. Your boots, your metal gear, your sweaty skin — any of it might register as interesting to a system designed to monitor and process metals and minerals. Be boring. Be patient. Let them lose interest.

- **Watch for clustering behavior.** If you see a large number of arthropods converging on a location, that location may be about to undergo change — a new vent opening, a structural incorporation, a recycling event. Move away from the area.

### The Larger Shapes

You know almost nothing about these. One sighting at several hundred yards through thermal shimmer, climbing vertically with fluid grace, the size of a large dog or small person, possibly merging with a structure under construction.

- **Do not approach.** Not out of fear necessarily, but out of ignorance. You don't know what it is, what it considers a threat, or what it's doing. Observe from a distance.

- **Do not follow.** If it leads you somewhere, that somewhere may not be designed for a biological organism. The upper reaches of structures under construction are active mechanical environments — steam-powered arms positioning girders, thermal expansion riveting, new components being integrated. You are soft. Those processes are not.

- **If approached by something large,** remain calm, avoid sudden movements, and if possible, create distance without appearing to flee. Move to a different gantry level. The creature (component? both?) appears to use the vertical scaffolding as its primary medium of movement — if it climbs, moving to an open horizontal area may take you out of its operational space.

- **Document everything.** If you have a camera or recording device that functions in this environment (the heat and mineral dust may challenge electronics — see below), photograph or record any sighting. Information about these entities is essentially nonexistent, and your observations may be critical for future travelers.

## Equipment Considerations

### Electronics

The environment is hostile to electronics:

- **Heat** degrades batteries, warps circuits, and can cause device failure. Keep electronics insulated from ambient heat as much as possible — wrapped in reflective material, stored in a small insulated pouch, kept away from direct contact with hot surfaces.

- **Mineral dust** (copper filings, sulfur, unknown particulates) can infiltrate openings and cause shorts. Seal all ports and openings with tape or purpose-built covers. Consider a waterproof/dustproof case for any critical device.

- **Electromagnetic interference** from the massive iron infrastructure may affect radios, compasses, and any device relying on electromagnetic signals. Test everything upon entry and have non-electronic backups for all critical functions (navigation by sun position, timekeeping by a mechanical watch, fire-starting by ferrocerium rod).

- **The subsonic geological hum** may interfere with sensitive instruments. Seismographs, accelerometers, and similar devices may be overwhelmed by the constant low-frequency vibration.

Carry spare batteries, stored in an insulated container. Lithium batteries are more heat-tolerant than alkaline but still degrade above about 60°C. Monitor battery temperature and keep them out of direct contact with heated surfaces.

### Light Sources

The amber sky provides illumination during the day, and the geological glow from the vents provides dim illumination at all times. But the lower levels of the city, between dense-packed scaffolding, may be genuinely dark, especially the enclosed corridors. Carry a **headlamp** (hands-free, essential for climbing and navigating gantries) and a backup light source. Avoid open flame — the sulfurous atmosphere may contain pockets of gas with unexpected combustion properties, and introducing fire into an environment you don't fully understand is unwise.

### Tools

- **A multitool with pliers.** You may need to manipulate hot metal objects, tighten or loosen connections on your own equipment, or improvise repairs in the field.

- **Heat-resistant tape** (aluminum HVAC tape or similar) for repairs, marking, sealing, or improvised heat shielding.

- **A small mirror** — for signaling (if anyone is looking), for seeing around corners in the enclosed corridors, and for examining surfaces above and below you without craning into potentially dangerous positions.

## Medical Preparedness

### The Likely Injuries and Illnesses

Based on the environment, your medical kit should be optimized for:

1. **Heat-related illness.** Heat exhaustion progressing to heat stroke is the most likely way you die in Thaal Kethis. Know the signs: confusion, cessation of sweating, rapid pulse, high core temperature. Carry a thermometer. If your core temperature exceeds 40°C, you are in a medical emergency. Wet your body with whatever water you have, find the coolest location available (elevated gantry, condensation area), rest, hydrate with electrolytes. If you are alone and you reach heat stroke, you are in critical danger. Prevention is everything — hydrate, rest, don't push through warning signs.

2. **Burns.** Contact burns from hot surfaces, radiant burns from proximity to vents, steam scalds from walking through the lower-level mist. Carry **burn dressings** (gel-impregnated, several sizes), **silver sulfadiazine cream** or an equivalent burn treatment antibiotic, and a generous supply of **non-adhesive wound dressings** and **cohesive bandage wrap.** A burn in this environment is bad because: you can't cool it properly (no cold water), the hot ambient temperature impairs healing, and infection risk is elevated by the particulate-laden air contacting broken skin. Prevent burns by wearing full coverage clothing and gloves, testing surfaces before gripping, and maintaining awareness of vent locations and steam sources.

3. **Respiratory irritation or injury.** Despite your respirator, you will have some exposure to the atmospheric contaminants. Carry a **rescue inhaler** (albuterol or equivalent bronchodilator) even if you have no history of asthma — acute bronchospasm from sulfur dioxide exposure can affect anyone. Carry **throat lozenges** for the persistent irritation from the copper-tasting air.

4. **Cuts and lacerations.** The fractured blue-black mineral has mirror-smooth faces that can slice like obsidian. Fresh basalt can have razor edges. The city's metal surfaces are riveted and may have sharp protrusions. Carry wound closure strips, a small suture kit if you're trained, antiseptic (povidone-iodine is robust and heat-stable), and plenty of adhesive and gauze bandaging.

5. **Eye irritation/injury.** Mineral dust, steam, thermal shimmer, and the intense quality of the light (that too-close sun through the amber sky) can all damage eyes. Carry **sealed safety goggles** (not just sunglasses) for areas with heavy particulate or steam exposure, **wraparound UV-filtering sunglasses** for general travel, and **eye wash solution** (individual-use vials, not a large bottle that will contaminate). A scratched cornea in this environment, with mineral dust in the air, can become a serious infection.

6. **Metal toxicity.** If your water purification fails or is inadequate, or if you inhale enough metal dust over the course of your stay, you may experience symptoms of heavy metal poisoning: nausea, abdominal pain, metallic taste, fatigue, headache, confusion. Carry **activated charcoal** (oral) as an emergency measure. It's not a cure, but it can reduce absorption of ingested metals if administered promptly. The real treatment is removing yourself from the exposure — which means reaching the edge of the city and descending.

7. **Fatigue, sleep deprivation, and psychological stress.** You are alone, in an alien environment, for an extended period, with impaired sleep and constant physiological stress. Cognitive function will degrade. You may begin to perceive the city as more sentient than it is, or attribute meaning to the patterns of the arthropods' movements, or become convinced the larger shapes are following you. This is normal stress response in isolation under harsh conditions. Maintain routines: eat on schedule, sleep on schedule, keep your journal, record observations methodically. The structure of routine is your psychological lifeline.

## Interacting with the City's Systems

### The Harmonic Identity

The description notes that pressing your palm against any girder and holding still long enough gives you the name — Thaal Kethis — as a kind of harmonic resonance in the metalwork. This suggests the city's infrastructure carries information in vibration, readable through direct physical contact.

This is fascinating and should be explored cautiously. If you press your palm against different structures, do you receive different information? Is there a gradient — more complex harmonic data from older or larger structures, simpler from newer ones? Can you distinguish the "identity" of different areas or systems?

**Caution:** You don't know what the city reads from *you* during these contacts. Your palm has a thermal signature, a pressure pattern, biochemical traces in your sweat. You are inputting as well as receiving. Consider whether you want to be fully legible to this system. Consider wearing gloves for initial contacts, then making bare-skin contact only deliberately, at times and locations of your choosing, as an intentional act of investigation rather than an accidental one.

### The Mechanical Arms and Construction Processes

Stay well clear of active construction zones. The mechanical arms move slowly, but they are positioning girders and heavy components, and a slow-moving multi-ton object is just as lethal as a fast one. The thermal-expansion riveting process involves heating components to extreme temperatures — proximity to an active riveting event means proximity to an object at temperatures that could cause severe burns at a distance.

### The Rail Conveyances

The slow conveyances carrying components along rails are at ground level and gantry level. They move at walking pace and probably won't surprise you, but they are heavy and ride on that liquid silver-white metal with negligible friction, meaning they have considerable momentum and no capacity to stop quickly. Stay off the rails. If a conveyance is approaching and you're on the rail path, step aside. Do not attempt to ride a conveyance — you don't know where it's going, and the destination may be an active foundry or assembly structure.

### The Foundries

The open-pit foundries with their crucibles the size of houses are not tourist attractions. The metal in those crucibles is molten, the ceramic-lined troughs carry flowing liquid metal, and the heat radiating from these features is intense enough to cause burns without contact. Give them wide berth. If your route requires passing near a foundry, move quickly, shield exposed skin, and keep your respirator on — the fumes above molten metal are concentrated and toxic.

## The Edge: Getting Out

You must plan your exit from the beginning. Thaal Kethis is miles across and actively growing — the edge you entered from may have moved, with new construction extending the city's footprint beyond where you crossed onto the plateau.

### Strategy

- Upon entry, **note the direction of the nearest plateau edge** and take a bearing.
- Regularly climb to high vantage points and check that you can still see the edge (the place where the plateau falls away and clouds are visible below).
- **Track the direction of geological advance** — where the newest stone is being extruded, where construction is most active. This is the direction the city is growing. Your exit should generally be in the opposite direction, toward older, more stable terrain.
- **The descent from the plateau** will have its own challenges — the description mentions immeasurable depths and a drop-off to the middle atmosphere. The route up may not be the best route down if geological activity has altered the plateau's edge. Scout before committing to a descent path.

### Emergency Exit

If you become ill, injured, dehydrated, or otherwise compromised and need to exit urgently, follow this priority:

1. Move toward the nearest plateau edge by the most direct passable route.
2. If disoriented, climb high, find the edge visually, and take a bearing.
3. If unable to find the edge, follow water — the condensation channels and any ground-level water flow generally trend toward the plateau's edge, where the topography allows drainage.
4. If immobile, establish yourself in a visible location on a high gantry and signal (mirror, light, anything reflective or attention-getting) in case any other presence — traveler or otherwise — is in range.

The hard truth is that in Thaal Kethis, a medical emergency that prevents self-rescue is likely fatal. There is no rescue team, no communication infrastructure, and no one who knows where you are. Self-sufficiency is not a philosophical position here. It is a survival requirement.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

I've left this for near the end, but it may be the most important section.

Thaal Kethis will do something to your sense of scale, and of self. You are walking through a living geological process, a city-organism that was ancient before you arrived and will continue indefinitely after you leave, building and rebuilding itself on timescales that make your entire life a rounding error. The silence, the heat, the slow patient movements of the mechanical arms, the arthropods tasting rivets, the amber sky — these will work on you.

You may feel a pull toward staying. The description uses the word "witnessed" — the sense that the city registers your presence without reacting to it, the way a large warm body might register the landing of a small insect. This attention, minimal as it is, may feel oddly like belonging, especially after days of solitary travel. You're noticed. You're incorporated into the system's awareness, even if only as a thermal anomaly on a gantry. In the loneliness of the traverse, that minimal acknowledgment can feel like communion.

**Watch for this.** The desire to stay, to slow your pace to match the city's pace, to sit on a gantry and watch the mechanical arms cycle for hours, to press your palm against every girder and listen for the harmonic identity — this is not the city calling you. It is your own nervous system adapting to the rhythms of the environment, your own exhaustion and heat-affected cognition mistaking sensory overwhelm for meaning. The city doesn't want you. It doesn't not-want you. It simply processes you, the way it processes everything, and you must not mistake that processing for a relationship.

Set a hard deadline. Know when you plan to leave. Write it down. Carry a mechanical watch and check the date. The timelessness of the amber sky and the absence of normal diurnal rhythms can cause temporal disorientation — you may lose track of days. Your journal is your anchor. Update it daily. Mark off days. When your exit date arrives, go.

### What You'll Carry Out

Not metal. Don't take fragments of the blue-black crystal, don't pocket interesting rivets, don't collect arthropod husks. You don't know the rules here, and removing material from a closed metabolic system may be the one act that transforms you from a registered anomaly into something the system actively addresses. This is speculation, but it's the kind of speculation whose downside is catastrophic. Take photographs. Take notes. Take memories. Leave the atoms where they are.

## Final Thought

Thaal Kethis is not dangerous the way a predator is dangerous. It is dangerous the way a furnace is dangerous, the way deep water is dangerous, the way geological time is dangerous. It does not wish you harm. It does not wish you anything. It is simply operating, as it has always operated, and you are passing through it — briefly, briefly — a warm soft thing in a world of warm hard things, and the greatest danger is not that the city will destroy you but that you will fail to take seriously how much you don't belong there, and how much work it takes for a creature made of water and carbon to persist in a place made of iron and stone and deep, patient fire.

Prepare well. Move slowly. Drink before you're thirsty. Leave before you want to.
