# Preparing for the Sopping Warrens

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this: the Sopping Warrens is not a place that will try to kill you quickly. It is a place that will try to *absorb* you slowly. The danger here is not violence, not predation, not hostile inhabitants. The danger is the gradual, patient erasure of the boundary between you and a waterlogged depression in the earth. Every preparation you make should be oriented around that central fact. You are visiting a place that dissolves things — not with acid or force, but with presence, with saturation, with an endless, unhurried permeation that treats your body and your gear and your resolve as just more surfaces to wet.

You need to think like the inhabitants think. They haven't solved the water. They've negotiated with it. Your preparation is your own negotiation, conducted in advance.

---

## Footwear: The Single Most Critical Decision

This is where most travelers will fail first, and failure here cascades into everything else.

**Do not wear leather boots.** Leather in the Warrens will begin absorbing water from the moment your foot touches the clay. Within an hour, the leather will be saturated. Within three hours, it will begin to lose structural integrity. Within a day, the stitching will loosen and the sole will begin to separate. Leather is a skin, and skins are exactly what this place knows how to penetrate.

**Do not wear fabric-based hiking shoes.** Mesh panels, woven synthetics, anything with a weave — the mineral-grey clay will find its way into the fibers and stay. It is not mud that dries and flakes off. It is a fine, slick, mineral-heavy clay that bonds with fabric at a near-molecular level. You will not clean it out. The shoes will become heavier, colder, and eventually rigid with mineral deposit, the same way the scratches on the walls get sealed under a glassy film.

**What you want:** Solid, seamless, vulcanized rubber boots. Knee-height at minimum, and ideally with a neoprene lining for insulation rather than fabric. The kind of boot that has no seams below the waterline, no stitching that touches the ground, no entry points for capillary action. Think commercial fishing boots, chemical-resistant waders, or high-quality Wellington boots rated for prolonged immersion. The sole should have minimal tread — deep lugs will fill with clay immediately and turn the bottom of your boot into a skating surface. A relatively flat sole with shallow siping, the kind designed for wet boat decks, will serve you far better on that slick clay.

**Fit them with removable insoles** made of closed-cell foam. Not open-cell — open-cell foam is a sponge, and you don't want sponges in this environment. Closed-cell foam will insulate your foot from the cold transmitted through the rubber sole and will not absorb moisture from condensation inside the boot. Bring two pairs of insoles. Swap them every few hours and let the resting pair air out — though "air out" is optimistic language in an environment with this much atmospheric moisture. At minimum, wiping them down and keeping them against your body inside your clothing will be more effective than leaving them exposed.

**Socks:** Merino wool, and nothing else. Not cotton — cotton holds water against the skin and will give you macerated, white, wrinkled feet within hours, the prelude to trench foot. Not synthetic wicking fabrics — there's nowhere to wick to; the ambient humidity approaches saturation. Merino wool retains insulating properties when wet and manages moisture against the skin better than any alternative in a permanently humid environment. Bring at least four pairs. Change them every three to four hours. Wring out the used pairs and store them in a sealed bag — you will wash and dry them when you leave this place, not before.

**Gaiters** are worth considering, not for keeping water out of the boot tops (the boots should be high enough) but for preventing the clay from getting onto your lower trouser legs and beginning its patient migration upward through the fabric of your clothing.

## Clothing: Layering for Saturation, Not Rain

The Warrens don't rain on you. They *humidify* you. This is a critical distinction because it changes your entire clothing strategy.

Rain gear — hardshell jackets, waterproof membranes — is designed for directional water, water coming from above or driven by wind. In the Warrens, the water comes from everywhere: below (through the clay), around (through the air), and from surfaces you brush against in passages barely wider than your shoulders. A hardshell jacket will keep external water off your outer layer, but it will also trap your body's moisture inside, because breathable membranes require a humidity differential to function, and there is no differential here. The outside air is as wet as the air inside your jacket. You will end up just as damp, but from the inside, and now you've also created a sealed envelope of warm, humid air against your skin that will cool rapidly when you stop moving and chill you thoroughly.

**Base layer:** Lightweight merino wool, long-sleeved, fitted close to the body. This manages moisture against your skin, provides insulation even when damp, and resists the bacterial growth that causes odor — relevant because you will not be drying anything out while you're here, and you'll be wearing this layer for the duration.

**Mid layer:** A thin fleece or wool sweater. Something that insulates through loft rather than by being dry. Avoid down insulation categorically — down collapses when wet and becomes worse than useless, a sodden mat that actively conducts heat away from your body. Synthetic insulation (like Primaloft) is acceptable if wool isn't available, but wool is preferable because it manages moisture more gracefully over extended periods.

**Outer layer:** This is where the counterintuitive choice lives. Instead of a waterproof shell, consider a **tightly woven, non-waterproof soft shell or wind shirt.** Something that blocks the slight movements of air within the passages without sealing you into a moisture trap. You will get damp. Accept this. The goal is not to stay dry — that goal is impossible here, and pursuing it will exhaust you and your gear. The goal is to stay *warm while damp*, which is a completely different engineering problem.

However — and this is important — **bring a waterproof shell anyway**, packed and accessible, for the specific circumstance of sitting or resting in one of the shelter structures where water drips from ceiling slabs. When you're moving, your body generates enough heat to compensate for the moisture. When you're still, that calculus changes fast. Throwing the shell over your shoulders like a rain cape while you rest will keep the drip-water from saturating your insulating layers during the periods when you're generating the least heat.

**Trousers:** Synthetic, quick-drying hiking pants are acceptable here. Avoid denim or cotton entirely. The pants will be damp within the first hour regardless of material; the question is whether they become heavy and cold (cotton) or merely clammy and functional (synthetic). Some travelers prefer wool trousers, which work well but are heavier and slower to manage. If your boots are knee-height, the lower legs of your trousers matter less than the upper portion — tuck the lower legs into the boot tops.

**Hands:** Your hands will be touching wet stone constantly, especially in the narrow passages where you'll instinctively brace against the walls. Thin neoprene gloves — the kind used for cold-water kayaking or diving — will protect your hands from the mineral-laden water and the sharp edges of the freshly broken sedimentary slabs. These slabs split along bedding planes into pages, and pages have edges. The edges are not knife-sharp but they are *paper-cut* sharp, and paper cuts in a constantly wet, mineral-rich environment are an invitation to irritation and slow-healing wounds. Neoprene gloves also provide grip on wet stone, which you will need.

**Head:** A thin wool beanie or buff. Heat loss from the head is meaningful in this cool, damp environment, and the beanie serves double duty as a barrier against the ceiling drips. Alternatively, a wide-brimmed hat will channel drips away from your face and neck, but it may catch on the low ceiling slabs in the narrower passages. A compromise: wear the beanie in the tight passages and switch to a brimmed hat in the more open courtyard spaces.

## Navigation and Spatial Awareness

The Warrens are described as roughly sixty paces across, which is not large. But the density of the construction — the narrow, twisting passages, the leaning walls, the partial ceilings, the absence of right angles — means that effective navigation is much harder than the raw distance suggests. This is essentially a maze built by structural improvisation rather than design, where the passages change shape as walls shift and new bracing is added.

**Do not rely on electronic navigation.** GPS is meaningless at this scale, and the pervasive moisture will challenge any electronic device you bring. If you bring electronics — camera, communication device, whatever — they must be in waterproof housings rated for immersion, not just splash resistance. The moisture here is not splashing. It is *ambient*. It will find its way into any gap, any port, any seal that isn't designed for submersion.

**Mark your path.** Not on the walls — the inhabitants have their own mark system (those groups of three and five), and adding your marks would be disrespectful and confusing. Instead, use small, brightly colored markers that you place at junctions and retrieve on your way out. Silicone or rubber markers are ideal because they won't absorb water or degrade. Breadcrumb-style trail markers made of paper or fabric will become saturated and unrecognizable within hours.

**Move slowly.** The passages are narrow enough that you'll be passing through them sideways in places. The footing is slick clay that records every step. You will leave footprints that immediately fill with water, and so will everyone else who moves through these passages. In high-traffic areas, the merged footprint-pools can obscure the actual ground level, making it hard to tell if you're stepping into a shallow impression or a deeper basin. Test each step. Keep your center of gravity low. Use the walls for balance, but be aware that the walls themselves are shifting structures held together by friction and wedging — leaning hard on a wall could accelerate its slow slide apart.

**Learn the inhabitants' spatial system.** The scratch marks on the walls — groups of three and five — may function as navigation aids, territorial markers, or water-level records. Probably all three simultaneously, in a system that makes internal sense to the people who live here. Observe patterns. Note which passages have marks and which don't. Note the height at which marks are placed. If you can identify the logic, you'll navigate better and you'll understand the settlement better. If you can't identify the logic, at least you'll know which areas are actively monitored and maintained — the presence of marks means someone comes here regularly to check and add to them.

## Health Considerations: The Slow Assault

The Warrens are not immediately dangerous to your health, but they are *cumulatively* challenging in ways that compound over time. Your preparation should account for an environment that degrades your physical condition gradually, like a low-grade fever — you feel fine, you feel fine, you feel fine, and then suddenly you don't.

### Skin

Prolonged exposure to the mineral-laden water will affect your skin. The clay here is described as mineral-grey, and the water has deposited glassy films of mineral on the stone surfaces. This water is carrying dissolved minerals — likely calcium, silica, possibly iron given the amber tint of the steeping water in the courtyard basin. Extended contact will dry your skin paradoxically (mineral-rich water can strip natural oils even as it saturates), leading to cracking, especially on the hands and between the fingers and toes.

**Bring a barrier cream** — a heavy, lanolin-based or petroleum-based skin protectant. Apply it to your hands, feet, and any skin that will be in regular contact with the water or wet surfaces before you enter, and reapply every few hours. This creates a hydrophobic layer between your skin and the mineral water. It won't last forever, but it buys you time.

**Bring foot powder** — but not for use while you're in the Warrens. The powder will become a paste immediately in this humidity. Use it only after you've left, cleaned and dried your feet, and are recovering in a dry environment. In the Warrens, your foot care is limited to changing socks regularly, inspecting for maceration or early signs of breakdown, and applying barrier cream between the toes.

### Respiratory

The air here is described as carrying a "slight heaviness in the lungs." This is consistent with near-saturation humidity combined with possible mineral or organic particulates. The fibrous grey-green sealing material (rushes or lichen) may release spores or fine particles when disturbed, especially in the narrow passages where you might brush against it.

**A thin, breathable face covering** — not a sealed respirator, which would fog and become intolerable in this humidity, but a light buff or gaiter pulled over the nose and mouth — will filter the largest particulates and warm the inhaled air slightly, reducing the thermal shock to your lungs from breathing very cool, very moist air. If you have any pre-existing respiratory sensitivities — asthma, reactive airway disease — bring your usual medications and be prepared to leave if breathing becomes labored. This is not an environment for compromised lungs.

### Hypothermia

The description places the temperature at "early morning that never progresses to afternoon" — cool enough that breath almost mists. Combined with pervasive damp and the conductive cold of wet stone and clay, this creates meaningful hypothermia risk, especially during rest periods. This is not the dramatic hypothermia of blizzards and ice water. This is the insidious hypothermia of cool, wet conditions sustained over hours, where your core temperature drops so gradually that you don't notice until your fine motor skills deteriorate and your thinking gets fuzzy.

**Eat before you enter. Eat while you're there.** Your body is burning calories to maintain core temperature in this environment, even if you don't feel cold. High-calorie, high-fat foods are ideal — nuts, hard cheese, cured meats, dense calorie bars. Keep them in sealed waterproof containers; anything exposed to the air will become damp and potentially contaminated with mineral residue.

**Set a time limit.** Decide before you enter how long you will stay, and leave when that time is reached regardless of how you feel. If you feel fine, that's good — you left before the environment caught up with you. If you feel fine but your hands are clumsy and you're having trouble remembering which passage you came through, you've already stayed too long.

### The Water

**Do not drink the water in the Warrens.** Not from the footprint-pools, not from the meniscus-films on the stone joints, not from the amber-tinted basin in the courtyard. The amber water is steeping something organic — it's essentially an unidentified infusion, and you have no idea what's in it. The seep-water emerging from the ground is mineral-rich in unknown concentrations and may contain dissolved elements that are harmless in small external contact but dangerous when ingested. The translucent larvae living in the footprint-pools suggest biological activity in the water. Bring all the water you need, in sealed containers, enough for your entire visit plus a margin.

## What to Bring, What to Leave

### Bring:

- **Water:** In sealed, rigid containers. At least two liters more than you think you need. Rigid containers because flexible bladders and soft bottles can be punctured by the sharp slab edges in the narrow passages.

- **Light:** The description suggests perpetual overcast and deep shade within the structures. A headlamp with a red-light mode is essential — white light in the narrow, glistening passages will reflect off every wet surface and create a dazzling, disorienting hall-of-mirrors effect. Red light will be absorbed by the water films rather than reflected, giving you a clearer view of the actual surfaces and spaces. Bring spare batteries in a waterproof case, and consider a secondary light source (a small, sealed flashlight) as backup.

- **A small, flat sitting pad:** Closed-cell foam, something you can place on any surface to create a dry(ish), insulated seat when you need to rest. Sitting directly on the wet stone will conduct heat out of your body with alarming efficiency.

- **A compact, waterproof notebook and pencil (not pen):** If you want to record your observations — the scratch patterns, the tool arrangements, the water levels — pen ink will run. Pencil graphite is hydrophobic and will write on damp paper. Waterproof paper (like Rite in the Rain) is specifically designed for this. You may find that recording things helps you process an environment that is, by all accounts, psychologically intense in its quiet, relentless wetness.

- **A small towel:** Not for drying yourself — that's futile while you're inside. For wiping your eyes, clearing water from your face, drying your hands momentarily before handling tools or food. Microfiber works. Wring it out frequently.

- **Plastic bags:** Multiple sizes, sealable. For wet socks, for protecting dry items, for separating wet gear from dry gear in your pack. In an environment where everything gets wet, the ability to compartmentalize wet and dry is a form of wealth.

- **A basic first aid kit** with emphasis on skin care: adhesive bandages that work on wet skin (look for waterproof or hydrocolloid bandages), antiseptic wipes, blister treatment (moleskin, gel pads), and a small tube of antibiotic ointment. Cuts and abrasions in this environment will not heal well while you're here; the goal is to protect them from the water and treat them properly after you leave.

- **A small gift or offering:** This is a living settlement. People are here, or will return shortly. You are a visitor in someone's home — a home that demands constant effort to maintain, where every tool has its place on its raised stone, where every wall is tended and every shift is tracked. Arriving empty-handed in a place like this feels wrong. What to bring? Think about what would be useful here. Cordage — strong, waterproof line, paracord or tarred twine — would be invaluable for people who brace failing walls and seal joints with packed rushes. A good blade, if your conscience and the local customs allow it. Even food — something sealed, durable, calorie-dense, something they couldn't get from this wet grey bowl of a landscape. Observe before offering. The tool arrangement in the courtyard speaks of specific, methodical intelligence. These are not people who want random objects. They want *useful* objects, placed *correctly*.

### Leave behind:

- **Anything you can't afford to lose or have ruined.** The Warrens will degrade whatever you bring. Accept material loss as part of the cost.

- **Impatience.** This environment operates on a timescale measured in the slow merging of footprint-pools and the imperceptible shift of stone walls. If you rush, you'll slip on the clay, catch yourself on a sharp slab edge, knock a bracing pole loose, and disturb a settlement that is held together by careful, patient balance. Move at the speed of the water. Which is to say: move slowly, but never stop.

- **The assumption that you are separate from this place.** The description is explicit: "the boundary between your body and this place is under quiet negotiation." Your body will absorb moisture from the air. Your lungs will take in the mineral-tinged humidity. Your footprints will join the silver-pooled record of every other foot that has pressed into this clay. You will leave a trace here, and this place will leave a trace in you. That's not a metaphor for danger. It's a description of what happens when a permeable being enters a permeable environment.

## Behavioral Guidelines: Being a Guest in a Dissolving Home

### Do not touch the tools.

The tools arranged around the courtyard basin — the bone awl, the grooved hammerstone, the resin-tacky flint scraper — are placed in a specific, deliberate order on their raised stone pedestals. This arrangement is not casual storage. In an environment where the water will claim anything set on the ground, the elevation and ordering of tools is an act of resistance, a declaration that these objects are *in use*, *maintained*, *not yet surrendered*. Moving them, even to examine them, disrupts a system you don't fully understand. Observe from a respectful distance. If you must look closely, crouch and look without touching.

### Do not lean on the walls.

Every structure in the Warrens is load-bearing in multiple directions simultaneously. The leaning slabs support each other through interlocking weight distribution — remove one force and the whole local geometry shifts. You weigh more than the water. Your lean exerts a lateral force these structures weren't designed to accommodate. Use the walls for light, fingertip balance only, and even then, test before committing weight.

### Do not add to the scratch marks.

The tally system on the walls — groups of three and five — is the inhabitants' record. It may be practical (water levels, time, resource counts), it may be territorial, it may be something you don't have a framework for. Whatever it is, it belongs to them. Adding your own marks, even with good intentions, corrupts a dataset someone is maintaining with care. If you need to leave a message, use your own notebook.

### Be prepared for the inhabitants to return.

The description makes it clear that the settlement is active, not abandoned. The people are elsewhere — gathering stone, perhaps, or checking traps, or doing whatever the bone awl's purpose implies — but they will return. When they do, you will be standing in their home, in their passages, leaving your footprints in their clay.

How they will react to a multiverse traveler is unknowable in advance. But you can control how *you* present. Be still when they arrive. Be visible — don't let them discover you lurking in a narrow passage. Position yourself in one of the more open spaces, the courtyard if possible, where they can see you and you can see them, and where your presence doesn't block movement. Keep your hands empty and visible. Speak softly if you speak at all — the acoustic environment of the Warrens is close, intimate, amplified by the wet stone surfaces. A loud voice will sound aggressive in a place where the loudest natural sound is a water drop hitting hair.

Observe their reaction. If they seem alarmed, retreat slowly toward the edge of the depression. If they seem cautious but not hostile, remain still and let them approach on their terms. If they seem unsurprised — if they've seen travelers before — then follow their cues.

**Note:** People who live in a dissolving settlement, who build and rebuild daily, who track water levels with scratched tallies and raise their tools above the creeping wet — these are people with a high tolerance for impermanence and a practical, unsentimental relationship with difficulty. They may be more welcoming than you expect, because they understand that everything here is temporary, including your visit.

## Psychological Preparation: The Weight of Wet Silence

This is the preparation most travelers neglect, and it may be the most important.

The Sopping Warrens are quiet. Not silent — there is the sound of water drops, the faint whisper of the pearlescent creatures repositioning, the distant non-repeating call from outside the depression. But the dominant quality is a *held* silence, a hush that feels thick, absorptive, like the clay itself. The close walls, the low ceilings, the narrow passages, the perpetual overcast — this environment is designed (not intentionally, but effectively) to produce a specific psychological state: **compression**.

You will feel enclosed. You will feel damp. You will feel a growing awareness that the boundary between inside and outside — inside the structures, outside your body, inside your lungs — is less solid than you're used to. The description says it explicitly: the moisture "enters you with each inhalation." This is a place that gets *into* you, physically and psychologically.

Some travelers find this meditative. The slow pace, the enforced attention to each step, the intimate scale of everything — it can produce a deep, contemplative focus, a narrowing of awareness to the immediate square foot of glistening stone and silver water.

Other travelers find it claustrophobic, oppressive, smothering. The sense that the place is slowly swallowing you — that the water is rising, the walls are sliding, the footprint-pools are merging, and you are just another surface being absorbed — can produce anxiety that escalates in the enclosed spaces.

**Know yourself.** If you are someone who is unsettled by enclosed spaces, persistent dampness, the inability to get dry or warm or clear, or the slow loss of familiar sensory landmarks — prepare accordingly. Set a firm time limit. Have an exit strategy. Know which direction the depression's edge lies at all times. Give yourself permission to leave before you've seen everything. The Warrens will not chase you. They will simply continue their slow process whether you're there or not.

If you're someone who can sit with discomfort, who finds beauty in precariousness, who is moved by the spectacle of intelligent beings wrestling with an environment that will eventually win — then the Warrens will reward you. Find a spot in one of the courtyard spaces. Sit on your foam pad. Watch the water rise by imperceptible fractions. Listen to the drops fall. Watch the glassy meniscus tremble in the joints between stones. Let the place be what it is: an argument between human persistence and geological patience, conducted in water and stone, with neither side willing to concede.

## On Leaving

When you leave the Warrens — climbing out of the shallow depression, stepping from the mineral-grey clay onto whatever ground surrounds it — the first thing you'll notice is the sound. Or rather, the change in sound. The held-breath quality of the air inside the depression gives way to something more open, more ordinary, and the contrast will be startling, like stepping out of a cathedral into a parking lot.

The second thing you'll notice is that you're wet. Not soaked — not like you fell in a river — but *permeated*. Your clothes are damp. Your skin is cool and slightly pruned in places. Your boots are slick with clay that holds every contour of the ground you walked on. Your lungs feel heavy in a way you didn't fully register while you were inside.

**Change your clothes as soon as possible.** Have dry clothes waiting in a sealed bag outside the depression, or at whatever base camp or transit point you're returning to. Strip the wet layers, towel off, apply moisturizer to your hands and feet, check for any cuts or abrasions and treat them. Drink something warm if you can. Eat.

And then, if you want, sit for a moment and look back at the depression. From outside, it just looks like a low place in the ground, a shallow bowl of grey clay studded with an inexplicable cluster of leaning stones. You might hear the sound of a drop falling, transmitted through the still air. You might not.

The people will return with their slabs. The water will continue to rise. The scratches on the walls will receive their next mark. The tools will be adjusted on their pedestals. The bracing poles will be wedged into new gaps.

The Warrens will hold. For now.

Take the clay out of your boot treads when you can. It'll keep the impression of the sole perfectly, a silver-skinned cast of everywhere you stood, a record in the same material that holds every other record this place has ever kept.

Then let it dry. It'll be the only dry thing that ever came out of that place, and even that feels like something the Warrens simply chose to let go.
