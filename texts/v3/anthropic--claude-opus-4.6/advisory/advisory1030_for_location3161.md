# Preparing to Visit The City That Finished

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before I address gear and logistics, I need to address something more fundamental: the nature of the threat here.

This place will not hurt you. Nothing will attack you, poison you, trap you physically, or collapse beneath your feet. The architecture is sound. The water appears clean. The animals are indifferent to your presence. By every conventional metric of multiverse travel danger assessment, this location registers as one of the safest you will ever visit.

That is precisely what makes it dangerous.

The danger here is ontological. It is the danger of *resolution*. You are a creature of wanting — of hunger, of curiosity, of the constant low-grade dissatisfaction that propels you from one moment to the next, one breath to the next, one destination to the next. You are a process, not a state. The City That Finished is a state. It has completed the journey from process to product. And it will, if you are not prepared, offer you that same completion with a gentleness so total that you will not recognize the moment you accept it.

You must understand this with full clarity before you step onto that plateau: **the primary survival challenge of this location is that you may not want to leave, and then you may not be able to want at all.**

## Pre-Visit Psychological Preparation

### Months Before Departure

**Establish a robust "pull" structure in your home reality.** This is not optional. You need people, obligations, unfinished projects, unanswered questions — things that *tug* at you. Not responsibilities you resent (resentment is still a form of wanting, but it's brittle and the City's stillness will dissolve it quickly). You need things you genuinely, warmly, achingly want to return to. A conversation you're in the middle of. A book you're writing where you don't yet know the ending. A person whose face you want to see again not out of duty but out of active, living desire.

Write these down. Make a physical list. We'll discuss what to do with it later.

**Practice noticing your own wanting.** This sounds absurd — wanting is the water we swim in, why would you need to practice noticing it? Because the City's equilibrium works by making the absence of wanting feel natural, feel like relief, feel like something you chose rather than something that was done to you. You need to be able to recognize the sensation of desire — even trivial desire, the desire for a glass of water, the desire to scratch an itch, the desire to know what time it is — as a *vital sign*. In the City, when you stop feeling these small wants, that is your canary in the coal mine.

Meditation practitioners may have a complicated relationship with this location. If your practice involves observing desire without attachment, you may find the City validates that practice in a way that feels like enlightenment but is actually something else — something that looks like peace from the inside but looks like a stopped clock from the outside. I am not telling you not to visit. I am telling you to be very honest with yourself about whether you are seeking the City because part of you wants to stop wanting, and whether that is a direction you can safely travel.

**Undergo a thorough psychological assessment** with someone familiar with multiverse exposure phenomena. Specifically, screen for:
- Depression, active or in remission (the City may present as the logical endpoint of anhedonia, and someone already struggling with the absence of motivation may find it almost impossible to leave)
- Burnout or profound exhaustion (the City will feel like the rest you've been desperate for, and it *is* rest, but it is rest that does not end)
- Grief (the City's completeness may map onto the feeling of a life that has already contained its most important moments, and the bereaved are vulnerable to the seduction of finality)
- Perfectionism (the City is, in some sense, a *perfect* place — everything in its right position, nothing left to fix — and the perfectionist's relief at encountering it may be overwhelming)

None of these are absolute disqualifiers. But they are risk factors, and you should know your risk profile.

**Study accounts of other visitors.** There are not many, and they are brief, because most people who visit the City either return quickly and struggle to articulate what they experienced, or do not return. The accounts that exist share certain features:
- An initial period of fascination and careful observation (hours to days by the visitor's subjective reckoning)
- A middle period of profound calm that the visitor describes in uniformly positive terms
- A transition point — described variously as "settling," "fitting in," "finding my spot" — that some visitors recognized as dangerous and others did not
- Those who left report that leaving required a *decision* of unusual intensity, not because anything prevented them but because generating the volition to leave required actively choosing incompleteness over completeness, noise over silence, desire over satisfaction

The visitors who did not return may be fine. They may have found what they were looking for. But "fine" and "finished" may not be the same thing, and you will not be able to determine which from the outside.

### Weeks Before Departure

**Create your anchor protocol.** This is the most important piece of preparation you will do. An anchor protocol is a structured system for maintaining your connection to your own temporality, desire, and forward motion while in a location that has none.

Your anchor protocol should include:

1. **A physical journal** — not digital, nothing that requires power or mechanical function, because you don't know how the City's equilibrium interacts with complex mechanisms. A bound notebook, a good pencil (not pen — you don't know how ink will behave in that atmosphere; graphite is more reliable). In this journal, before you enter the City, write the date, the time, your name, where you came from, and *why you are visiting*. Be specific. "Curiosity" is not specific enough. "I want to understand what kind of event or process causes an entire settlement to reach completion" — that's better. Write down what you expect to feel. Write down what you're afraid of. Write down the name of someone you want to tell about this when you return.

    Once in the City, write in the journal at regular intervals. Since there is no diurnal cycle and time may lose meaning rapidly, you will need to create your own intervals. (See: timekeeping, below.)

2. **A timekeeping device.** Mechanical watches are your best option — a hand-wound mechanical watch with a sweep second hand. Bring two, in case one stops. Digital devices may function but I would not rely on them. The City exists in a state of thermodynamic equilibrium, and while this should not affect a battery-powered quartz oscillator in theory, theory is less reliable than caution in multiverse travel. The mechanical watch serves double duty: it tells you how long you've been there (critical — set a maximum visit duration before you arrive and *honor it*), and the act of winding it is a small, productive, forward-looking action. You are winding the watch because you expect it to need to run in the future. That expectation of future is an anchor.

    **Set an alarm.** If you have a mechanical alarm (a small travel alarm clock), bring it. Set it to sound at your predetermined departure time. The sound of an alarm is a demand — it wants something from you, wants you to respond — and in the City, being *wanted from* may be the jolt you need to mobilize your departure.

3. **Incomplete objects.** Bring something unfinished. A half-knit scarf. A crossword puzzle you haven't solved. A letter you started writing but didn't finish. Something that is *conspicuously not done*, that radiates its own incompleteness, that your hands itch to continue working on. The City is a hymn to completion. Your unfinished object is a counterargument. When you feel yourself settling, pick it up. Do a few rows. Fill in a few words. The act of *continuing a process* is prophylactic against the City's stillness.

4. **Sensory anchors from home.** The City's sensory environment is muted, diffuse, and uniform. You want things that are vivid, specific, and tied to your own living context. A small vial of a spice you cook with. A photograph of a cluttered, messy, beautifully imperfect place you love. A piece of fabric with a texture that reminds you of something. A recording of a song — though again, I'd be cautious with electronic playback devices. Better: bring a small instrument. A harmonica. A tin whistle. Something you can play badly and happily. The City's acoustics are remarkable (the basalt streets ring underfoot; the plateau may be a resonant body), and making music in it — imperfect, human, process-oriented music — is both an experience worth having and an act of resistance against the silence.

5. **Food from home.** Bring food. You may not feel hungry in the City — the description suggests that metabolic needs may attenuate — but eat anyway. On a schedule. The act of eating is one of the most fundamentally process-oriented things a body does: you eat because you will be hungry again, because the body is a system that perpetually needs input, because you are not a closed loop. Eat before you feel hungry. Eat on your timekeeping schedule. Eat foods with strong flavors. The City is the taste of still water and cool stone. You want chili flakes and sharp cheese and the sour bite of preserved lemon.

## Physical Preparation and Gear

### Clothing and Personal Protection

The temperature is described as perpetually cool — root-cellar cool, approximately 10-13°C / 50-55°F by my reading. This is above freezing but below comfort for extended periods, especially given the moisture in the air.

**Base layer:** Merino wool, mid-weight. Not synthetic — and this is a preference I state carefully, because normally synthetics are fine — but in a place where nothing decays and nothing changes, I have a superstitious but not entirely irrational preference for materials that are themselves biological, that carry the memory of living process in their fibers. Merino regulates temperature well, manages moisture (you will be in a condensation-heavy environment), and does not develop odor quickly.

**Mid layer:** A wool or fleece pullover. Nothing fancy. Something warm that you've worn before, that your body knows, that fits into the category of *your things* rather than *expedition gear*.

**Outer layer:** A light, water-resistant shell. Not waterproof — you are not dealing with rain, and full waterproofing will cause you to sweat and feel clammy. You want something that sheds condensation and dew, that you can pull on when the moisture beads on your arms, that breathes. A waxed cotton jacket has good properties here and contributes to the sense of bringing living materials.

**Footwear:** This matters more than usual because the basalt paving rings underfoot, and your footsteps may be the primary soundtrack of your visit. Heavy boots will produce a more definite, declarative sound. Soft-soled shoes will let you move more quietly, more compatibly with the silence. I recommend the boots. You want to announce yourself. You want the stone to know you are there and are *moving*, that you are a thing in transit, not a thing at rest. Good leather hiking boots with a hard sole. Ankle-supporting — the basalt beyond the city's paved zones is described as sharp-edged and unweathered, and a turned ankle on raw volcanic rock is a real injury risk.

**Hat and gloves:** Bring a warm hat and a pair of light gloves. The cool air will leach heat from your extremities over time, and cold hands are demotivating — they make you want to stop, to tuck your hands in, to curl up, to be still. You are fighting stillness here. Keep your hands warm and functional.

### Camping and Shelter

Do not sleep in the city's buildings.

I want to be clear about this. The buildings are described as intact, furnished, comfortable, well-insulated against the cool climate. They are obviously the logical place to take shelter. **Do not use them.** Sleeping in a bed in the City That Finished is sleeping in a bed that was made for someone who became part of the City's completion. The bed fits. The blankets (if there are blankets) are the right weight. The room is the right temperature. And you will sleep, and the sleep will be the best sleep of your life, and when you wake — if you wake — you may find that waking up was harder than it should have been, that the interval between sleep and waking has grown soft and indistinct, that the boundary between rest and cessation has blurred.

Bring your own shelter. A small, lightweight tent — freestanding, since you'll be pitching on stone. Your own sleeping bag. Your own pad. Keep them outside the city proper, at the outskirts where the paving gives way to raw plateau. Sleep at the margin, not the center. Sleep in your own space, surrounded by your own smells and textures, with the raw wind-that-isn't-quite-wind on the tent fabric.

Better yet: minimize sleeping. Visit the City in a single sustained wakeful period if you can. Twelve hours. Sixteen at the outside. Plan your visit as a long day trip, not an overnight. Every sleep cycle in that place is a risk.

### Navigation and Documentation

**Mapping:** Bring a compass, but do not expect it to work normally — there's no indication of magnetic anomaly, but the absence of cardinal-direction indicators (no sun, no stars, no clear north) suggests the place may not interact with directional systems the way you expect. Bring it anyway. If it works, orient yourself. If it doesn't, note that.

More importantly, **map as you go.** Bring graph paper or a dedicated mapping notebook. Sketch the city's layout as you explore. This serves two purposes: it gives you a practical navigation tool, and it keeps you in the mode of *learning*, which is a mode of *wanting to know*, which is an active, forward-oriented state. The city resists easy geometric summary. Trying to summarize it anyway keeps your analytical mind engaged.

**Photography:** Bring a film camera. A fully mechanical one — no batteries, no electronics. Something like a manual SLR with a good lens. The City's flat, diffuse light will produce unusual photographic conditions: no harsh shadows, extraordinary tonal range, a quality of illumination that portrait photographers spend careers trying to achieve artificially. The photographs will be remarkable. But more importantly, taking photographs is an act of *extraction* — you are taking something from the City and carrying it elsewhere, asserting that the City's contents have relevance *outside the City*, that there is an outside, that you are going there.

Bring more film than you think you need. You will want to photograph everything.

**Sound recording:** If you bring a mechanical or analog recording device, capture the city's soundscape. The click-speech of the birds. The trickle of condensation. The ring of your footsteps on basalt. The resonance of the central platform. These recordings, if they survive the return journey, will be among the most valuable documentation you produce. The City's acoustic properties sound genuinely extraordinary — an entire urban landscape functioning as a tuned instrument — and that information has value to researchers across multiple disciplines.

### Water and Food

Bring all your own water. Yes, the City's condensation-fed cisterns appear clean and the water appears safe, and the translucent eel-creatures living in it show no signs of toxicity or disease. **Do not drink the City's water.** This is not a toxicology concern. This is a participation concern. The water is part of the City's closed loop — the same molecules tracing the same paths forever. Drinking it makes you part of the loop. You are not part of the loop. You are visiting. Drink your own water, carried from outside, water that was once rain that fell from clouds that moved across a sky where the sun rose and set.

Bring calorie-dense, flavorful food for the duration of your visit plus an emergency reserve. Trail mix with chilies. Hard salami. Strong cheese. Chocolate. Dried fruit with tartness to it — apricots, cranberries. Anything that demands your mouth's attention, that refuses to be neutral. Eat on schedule even if you don't feel hungry. Especially if you don't feel hungry.

## The Visit Itself: Conduct and Protocol

### Arrival

Approach the plateau on foot. Note the transition from the surrounding landscape (whatever it may be — the description focuses on the plateau itself) to the basalt uplift. Note the point at which you first see the city. Write down what you feel at that first sighting. Be honest. If it looks beautiful, say so. If it looks like relief, note that with a flag — that response needs monitoring.

At the city's outskirts, before you enter the paved streets, establish your base camp. Pitch your tent. Leave your main pack. Take a day pack with your journal, camera, timekeeping devices, water, food, and anchor objects. Set your alarm for your predetermined departure time.

Mark your entry point clearly. A cairn of loose basalt, a bright piece of fabric tied to something visible. The city is extensive and you will need to find your way back. Do not trust that you will remember. The city's resistance to geometric summary means your spatial memory may not encode it as efficiently as you're used to.

### Exploration

Move through the city systematically but not exhaustively. You do not need to see everything. You cannot catalog a completed city in a single visit, and the attempt to do so will keep you there too long. Choose a route that takes you from the outskirts to the center and back, with laterals as time and interest allow.

**Observe the birds.** They are described as the primary animate presence, and their behavior is the most accessible window into the City's ecology. Note their distribution. Count them from a fixed vantage point, wait a while, count again. The description suggests the count will be identical. If it is, that's significant. If it isn't, that's also significant. Try to distinguish individuals — look for plumage variation, size differences, behavioral quirks. If every bird is identical, note that. If they're distinguishable as individuals despite their collective statistical behavior, note that too. Are they truly non-reactive to your presence, or is there a subtle response — a bird that watches you a beat too long, a group that adjusts its spacing when you pass through?

**Examine the architecture.** The construction techniques described — mortise-and-tenon timber joinery, ash-cement, slate roofing — suggest a technological context roughly equivalent to skilled pre-industrial construction, but the precision is described as exceptional. Look at the joinery closely. How tight are the fits? Is there evidence of repair or adjustment? If every joint is perfect and no building shows signs of renovation, that tells you something (either the builders were preternaturally skilled, or the city was constructed in its final form all at once, or damage heals itself). If you find evidence of renovation — a replaced beam, a patched wall — that tells you something different (the city has a history, a process, and the completion came *after* a period of normal urban development).

**Look for writing.** Any marks on surfaces that might be intentional communication. The description mentions chisel marks on foundation stones, but look beyond construction marks. Look for inscriptions, tallies, graffiti, painted signs. A city of several thousand people that developed organically would produce written language; a city that was created already-complete might not. The presence or absence of writing is one of the most diagnostic observations you can make.

**Enter buildings cautiously.** The interiors are described as furnished and intact. Document what you find — the arrangement of living spaces, workshop configurations, storage. Look for personal items. Look for *variation* in personal items. Are the residences identical, or do they show individual taste? Is there evidence of social hierarchy, of wealth disparity, of specialization? A completed city that was once alive will show the fingerprints of individual lives. A completed city that was *always completed* will show the consistency of a single design intention.

**Approach the central plaza.** This is the heart of the place and you should visit it, but do so deliberately, not casually. The birds avoid it. The water routes around it. The moss does not grow there. These are the City's own warnings — or its own reverence — and either way, they are worth heeding.

When you step onto the polished platform, you will feel the resonance. The description says it sustains longer than it should and becomes indistinguishable from silence. **This is the most dangerous moment of your visit.** The sensation of your own weight producing a tone that the entire plateau sustains — the feeling of being *acoustically integrated* into the geology itself — will be profound. It may feel like belonging. It may feel like the answer to a question you didn't know you were asking. Stand on it. Feel it. Document what you feel with as much precision as you can. Then step off.

Do not sit on the platform. Do not lie down on it. Do not stay longer than a few minutes. And if you find that stepping off requires effort — if your legs feel heavy, if the thought of moving away produces a reluctance that feels physical — **that is your signal to leave the city entirely.** Not the plaza. The city. Go back to your camp. Wind your watch. Eat something spicy. Play your harmonica badly. Reread your journal entry about why you came and who you want to tell about it.

### The Seduction of Staying

I need to address this directly, because every preparation I've described is designed to counter it, and you should understand the mechanism.

The City That Finished does not lure. It does not promise. It does not seduce in the way that word usually implies — there is no active temptation, no voice whispering *stay*, no illusion of paradise. What it does is worse: it *subtracts*. It subtracts urgency. It subtracts the low-grade anxiety of an organism that needs to keep moving to keep living. It subtracts the friction of desire, the gap between what-is and what-is-wanted that is the engine of every conscious action you have ever taken.

And in the absence of that friction, you will feel peace. And the peace will be real. It will not be a trick or an illusion. The City has genuinely solved something — some fundamental tension at the heart of existence — and the solution is available to you, and it is gentle, and it is complete.

The question is whether you want to be solved.

The preparations I've described — the anchors, the timekeeping, the incomplete objects, the strong flavors, the bad harmonica music — are all methods of *reintroducing friction*. They are small, deliberate imperfections, tiny engines of wanting, that you carry with you to keep the engine of your own consciousness turning over. They are the equivalent of wiggling your toes to prevent yourself from falling asleep. They will feel silly. They will feel unnecessary. The moment they feel unnecessary is the moment they are most necessary.

### Departure

Leave on schedule. Leave on schedule. **Leave on schedule.**

When your alarm sounds — or when your watch reaches the time you set — stop what you are doing, orient toward your entry point, and walk. Do not take a last look. Do not go back for one more photograph. Do not tell yourself five more minutes. The city will be there if you ever want to return. It is, after all, finished. It is not going anywhere.

Walk out of the paved streets. Walk past the thinning buildings. Walk across the raw basalt to your camp. Pack your tent. Shoulder your pack. And walk off the plateau without looking back.

The urge to look back will be significant. It will feel like leaving a conversation at exactly the right moment — satisfying but also foreclosing — and part of you will want to verify that the City is still there, that it was real, that the experience you just had actually happened. It is still there. It was real. It happened. Keep walking.

### Post-Visit Decompression

When you return to your home reality, you will experience a period of adjustment. The noise will seem louder. The chaos will seem more chaotic. Food will taste too strong. People will seem frantic, purposeless, running in circles. The question *what's the point?* will arise with unusual frequency and unusual force.

This is normal. This is the afterimage of equilibrium. It fades.

To facilitate fading:
- Immerse yourself in sensory richness. Cook a complicated meal. Visit a loud, messy, joyful place. Hold a conversation where you don't know what the other person will say next.
- Engage in creative work — anything that involves building something that doesn't exist yet, anything that is *in process*.
- Talk about the City. Tell someone. Show them the photographs. Describe the birds. Describe the central platform and the sound it made. Let the City exist as a *story you are telling*, which is an ongoing act, a process with a listener, a thing that is not yet finished because the listener hasn't heard the end yet.
- If the sense of "what's the point" persists beyond two weeks, or if you find yourself making plans to return, seek support from someone experienced with multiverse reintegration. This is not weakness. The City is genuinely compelling, and its pull does not respect the boundary of the visit. Some travelers report a persistent low-level longing — not painful, not urgent, but present — that can last months. It generally resolves. If it doesn't, there are protocols.

## What You Will Bring Back

If you visit the City That Finished well — with preparation, with boundaries, with your friction-generating anchors intact — you will bring back something genuinely valuable.

You will bring back the knowledge that completion is possible. That there exists, somewhere in the multiverse, a place where every tension has been resolved, every process has reached its terminus, every desire has been fulfilled or rendered unnecessary. You will know that this is real because you stood on the platform and felt the plateau hum with it.

And you will bring back the knowledge that you chose not to stay. That you heard the silence and found it beautiful and complete and *walked away from it*, back into the noise, back into the mess, back into the glorious ongoing incompleteness of being a living creature in a universe that has not yet said everything it intends to say.

That knowledge — that completion exists and that you chose continuation — is a gift. It is the gift of choosing your own life not because you don't know the alternative, but because you do.

Pack your harmonica. Wind your watch. Write down the name of someone you want to see again.

Go.
