# Preparing for the Boiling Groves

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are preparing to enter a place that does not respect the boundary between living and built, between forward and backward, between organism and apparatus. This is not a ruin you're exploring. It is not a forest you're hiking through. It is not a factory you're inspecting. It is a process — an ongoing, thunderously loud, warm, dim, all-consuming process — and you will be inside it, and it will not pause for you.

The Boiling Groves have no predators, no hostile inhabitants, no sentient guardians. The danger is not attack. The danger is incorporation. Everything inert that enters the basin is eventually grown-over, absorbed into the living-mechanical matrix. Your equipment will begin to accrete. Your anchoring lines will sprout. The platform you rest on will develop roots. You are not visiting a place. You are introducing foreign matter into a system that converts foreign matter into more of itself.

Plan accordingly. Plan to leave. Plan to leave *on schedule*. Plan to leave before the schedule says you should, because the schedule was made by someone who hadn't been there yet and didn't know what "too long" felt like.

## The Sound: Preparing Your Hearing and Your Sanity

This deserves its own section because most travelers underestimate it catastrophically.

The Boiling Groves are not loud the way a concert is loud or an engine room is loud. They are loud the way being underwater during an earthquake is loud — the sound is *in the medium*, conducted through the warm dense water directly into your body. Your skeleton will vibrate. Your sinuses will resonate. Your chest cavity will become an acoustic instrument whether you want it to or not. Standard decibel ratings are nearly meaningless here because so much of the energy is infrasonic and subsonic, felt rather than heard, and because the water conducts it with brutal efficiency.

**Hearing protection is non-negotiable, but understand its limits.** The best approach is a layered system:

- **Inner layer:** Custom-molded in-ear plugs rated for underwater use, with a sealed membrane that attenuates high-frequency mechanical chatter — the clattering of internal works, the shrapnel-crack of bark, the hiss of superheated exhaust plumes. These should be made from a non-porous synthetic that won't absorb particulates from the water. Silicone works, but inspect and replace frequently — the dissolved compounds in the water can degrade some polymers.

- **Middle layer:** A full-head acoustic dampening hood integrated into your dive helmet. This should contain a layer of viscoelastic foam or equivalent vibration-absorbing material. It won't stop the infrasound — nothing wearable will — but it takes the edge off the midrange roar enough that you can think.

- **Active noise management:** If your multiverse-standard kit includes active noise cancellation tuned for underwater acoustic environments, bring it. Program it to target the 40-200 Hz range, which is where the turbine harmonics concentrate. Do *not* program it to cancel everything — you need to hear the environment to navigate, especially in low-visibility zones where sound replaces sight. More on this below.

- **Bone-conduction awareness:** No amount of ear protection will stop the infrasound from reaching you through your skeleton. The deep tones — particularly the sweeping call from the basin center — will bypass every barrier you wear and vibrate your organs directly. This is not harmful in short exposures, but over hours it produces a cumulative fatigue that manifests as nausea, disorientation, emotional flatness, and a creeping sense that you are vibrating apart. Limit continuous exposure. Build in rest periods outside the basin. Monitor yourself for the early signs: a feeling that your heartbeat has "synced" to an external rhythm, a sense that the roaring has become meaningful (like speech just below comprehension), or sudden unexplained calm. That last one especially — if you feel peaceful in the Groves, get out. Peace is not a thing this place produces.

**Communication with dive partners** must be handled through a closed-circuit system — throat mic and bone-conduction speaker, hardwired if possible, because the acoustic environment will destroy any open-water voice communication and may interfere with some wireless protocols. Keep transmissions short and confirmatory. Develop a set of physical signals (light patterns, tether-tugs) as backup.

## Thermal Preparation

The water is blood-warm. That sounds comfortable. It is not comfortable. It is *relentlessly, inescapably* warm, and it never varies enough to give your thermoregulatory system anything to work with. Your body cannot cool itself through the water because the water is already at or near core temperature. You will not feel hot in the dramatic sense — no burning, no scalding — but over hours, your core temperature will creep upward because you have no gradient to dump heat into.

**This is compounded by exertion.** Swimming through a viscous, current-heavy, debris-filled underwater forest while wearing protective equipment is hard work. You will generate metabolic heat you cannot shed.

**Solutions:**

- **Active cooling garment.** Wear an undersuit with integrated liquid cooling channels fed by a small chiller unit. This is your single most important piece of comfort equipment. Without it, you face a hard limit of perhaps two to three hours in the basin before hyperthermia begins degrading your judgment — and in the Groves, degraded judgment has specific and alarming consequences (see: accretion, reversal zones, the call).

- **Hydration.** You will sweat into your suit even with cooling. Carry an integrated hydration system in your helmet with electrolyte-balanced water. Cold if you can manage it. Plan to consume significantly more fluid than you would on a normal dive of equivalent duration.

- **Avoid the exhaust plumes.** The superheated water columns from machine exhaust ports are dramatically hotter than the baseline — hot enough to cause burns through a standard dive suit. They're visible as shimmering distortion columns and often carry a faint violet or rust tint. Learn to recognize them. Give them wide berth. They shift with the currents, so a clear path one minute may have a plume drifting across it the next. This is one of the reasons you do not rush in the Groves.

- **Be aware of viscosity pockets.** The thicker, more gelatinous zones of water have different thermal conductivity. Some of them are cooler, which will feel like blessed relief and will tempt you to linger. Don't. These pockets migrate, and when they collide with a heat plume they generate standing vortices that can trap you, tumble you, and disorient you in a low-visibility environment full of jagged pipe fittings and sharp stone ridges. Others are *warmer* than the baseline, carrying concentrated dissolved compounds that may include irritants. If you enter a viscosity anomaly and your skin begins to tingle or your cooling system's intake shows elevated temperature, reverse course.

## Breathing and Atmosphere

You will need a fully self-contained breathing apparatus. The water is not breathable — it is water, however strange — and whatever dissolved gases it contains are not suitable for respiration. Do not trust any pockets of gas you find trapped in the canopy or in enclosed structures; the machine exhaust includes unknown compounds, and the biological processes of the trees may produce gases that are toxic or narcotic or simply displace oxygen.

**Rebreather systems** are preferable to open-circuit for several reasons: longer duration per unit of gas carried, no exhaled bubbles to create additional turbulence in an already chaotic environment, and reduced acoustic signature (your bubbles will not add meaningfully to the roar, but they will add locally, right next to your ears, which compounds fatigue).

**Carry more gas than you think you need.** Navigation in the Groves is uncertain, currents can push you off course, reversal zones can appear in your path and force detours, and the periodic seismic shudders can collapse root-corridors you were planning to use as a return route. Plan for a bottom time of X, carry gas for 3X, and set your turnaround point at one-third supply.

**Monitor your gas mix for contamination.** The water contains dissolved compounds that are not fully characterized, and some of them may be able to permeate certain hose and membrane materials. If your breathing gas develops an unfamiliar taste or smell — particularly anything botanical, anything like crushed celery — switch to your backup supply immediately and begin exiting.

## Visibility, Navigation, and Light

Visibility is limited to a few hundred meters at best, significantly less in turbulent zones, and effectively zero during the chaotic surges that follow the deep call. The ambient light is warm amber-green, sourceless, and shifts with turbulence in ways that make it unreliable as a directional cue. You cannot navigate by light here. You cannot navigate by sight beyond the immediate surroundings.

**Primary navigation: inertial and acoustic.**

- Carry an inertial navigation unit that tracks your position relative to your entry point via accelerometer and gyroscope. Calibrate it before entry. Understand that the seismic shudders will introduce drift, and recalibrate whenever possible against known fixed points (the chain-post boundaries near the periphery, identifiable structures, distinct geological features).

- Learn to navigate by sound. Every machine has a unique acoustic signature. Every sector of the basin has a characteristic soundscape. Before penetrating deep into the Groves, spend time at the periphery mapping the sound environment — which directions carry the heaviest turbine harmonics, where the pipe-clatter is loudest, what the ratio of mechanical to vegetative noise is in various quadrants. Your active noise cancellation system should have a mode that attenuates overall volume while preserving directional cues. Use it. In zero-visibility conditions, sound is your map.

- **Do not rely on visual markers you place yourself.** Any line, tag, flag, or marker you attach to the environment will begin accreting within hours. A fluorescent tag tied to a branch may be covered in bark-mineral laminate by the time you return. A guideline run through a root corridor may be grown-over at attachment points, trapped, impossible to follow or retrieve. If you use physical guidelines, use thick, high-visibility line and plan to write it off — you probably won't get it back.

**Supplemental lighting:**

- Carry powerful dive lights, plural, with spares. The ambient glow is enough to see by in most conditions, but in the dim zones and during the post-call chaos, you will need active light.

- Be aware that your light beam will behave strangely in the viscosity anomalies — bending, diffusing, sometimes apparently *brightening* as it passes through zones of concentrated luminosity in the water. Do not use the behavior of your light beam as a reliable indicator of distance or direction.

- In the reversal zones, light reportedly behaves anomalously in other ways — some travelers describe their beams appearing to shorten, or to illuminate things that were behind them, or to cast shadows that don't correspond to objects. These reports are difficult to verify. If your light begins behaving in ways you cannot explain, you are likely in or near a reversal zone. Leave. The reversal zones are not just disorienting; they are the sites where the most violent snap-back events occur, and those events produce shrapnel, pressure spikes, and sudden current changes.

## Exposure Protection: The Accretion Problem

This is the core survival issue and the one most unique to the Groves. The basin converts inert matter into more of itself. This process — accretion — manifests as thin laminar deposits of a bark-like mineral substance building up on any surface exposed to the water. On the native machines, this accretion takes months or years to become significant. On foreign objects, reports suggest it begins faster — possibly because foreign materials present a novel surface chemistry that the process attacks aggressively, possibly because the Groves can tell the difference between self and not-self and are more aggressive about assimilation at the boundary.

**What this means for you and your equipment:**

- Metal surfaces will begin to develop a thin film within hours of immersion. This film looks like patina at first — greenish, faintly textured. Within several more hours it will begin to develop structure: tiny ridges, laminations, the beginnings of those bark-like layers. It is not merely cosmetic. It bonds to the underlying material. On moving parts — valve mechanisms, buckle hardware, regulator components — it will introduce friction and eventually binding. On sealed joints and o-rings, it will compromise seals.

- **Inspect and scrape your equipment regularly.** Carry non-metallic scraping tools (the accretion bonds less aggressively to certain ceramics and hard polymers). During rest stops, go over every piece of equipment and remove any buildup you find. Pay particular attention to your breathing apparatus, your buoyancy control system, any quick-release mechanisms, and your communication equipment.

- **Favor non-metallic equipment where possible.** Certain high-density polymers, ceramics, and composites accrete more slowly than metals. Some travelers report that equipment with a mirror-polished surface resists accretion longer than textured surfaces. Anything with a rough, porous surface — woven straps, fabric, exposed foam — is particularly vulnerable.

- **Your suit itself will accrete.** A hard-shell dry suit with a smooth exterior surface is strongly preferable to a neoprene wetsuit, which will absorb particulates and provide nucleation sites for accretion. Even a smooth suit will develop buildup over a long dive. The buildup adds weight, reduces flexibility, and — if it reaches joint areas — can restrict movement. This is one of several hard limits on dive duration.

- **Do not touch the trees or the machines with bare skin.** Some travelers have reported that the accretion process is dramatically accelerated on biological surfaces — skin, hair, exposed tissue. There are unverified accounts of a thin mineral film forming on unprotected hands within minutes, initially painless but increasingly constrictive as it thickens and hardens. Whether this would progress to full incorporation if left unchecked is unknown, and you do not want to find out. Full suit coverage, sealed gloves, no exposed skin. Check your seals obsessively.

- **Watch your tether points.** If you anchor yourself to any part of the environment — root, branch, machine housing — the anchor point will begin to be grown-over. Set a timer. Check your anchors. Do not sleep while tethered to the basin's structures. (Do not sleep in the basin at all, but this bears specific emphasis.)

## The Reversal Zones: Recognition and Avoidance

The reversal zones are where the growth process runs backward — bark reattaches, leaves re-furl, machines run in reverse. They are the most dangerous navigational hazard in the Groves, not because the reversal itself is harmful but because of what happens when reversal *stops*.

**How to recognize an approaching reversal zone:**

- **Visual:** Debris is moving upward instead of settling. Bark ribbons are rising. Leaves are ascending toward the canopy. The drift pattern is inverted. If you see particulate matter moving against what you've established as the prevailing "down," stop advancing.

- **Auditory:** The sound is wrong. Specifically, the attack-decay relationship of sounds is inverted — you hear the tail of a clank before the clank itself, the fade-in of a hiss before the hiss peaks. This is subtle and difficult to detect consciously against the background roar, but your brain will register it as wrongness, as something that makes you uneasy in a way you can't immediately articulate. Trust that unease.

- **Mechanical:** If your own equipment begins behaving anomalously — gauges counting the wrong direction, compass needles drifting, your buoyancy shifting without explanation — you may be in the gradient of a reversal zone. These effects on foreign equipment are not well documented, and it's possible that reversal only affects native matter. But err on the side of caution.

- **The light:** Reversal zones tend to be dimmer than surrounding areas, though some are paradoxically brighter. What they share is a quality of *wrongness* in the light — an amber that's slightly too red, or a green that's shifted toward blue, as though the luminescent properties of the water are also running backward through some spectral sequence. Difficult to quantify. You'll know it when you see it.

**When you identify a reversal zone:**

- Do not enter it.
- Chart its approximate location and mark it on your inertial navigation.
- Give it a wide berth — at least fifty meters if possible, more in the direction the currents would carry you if you lost propulsion.
- Remember that they migrate. A zone that was in one location an hour ago may have moved. Do not assume a path is safe because it was safe last time.
- If you hear the deep call — the sweeping low tone from the basin center — every reversal zone is about to collapse and reform simultaneously. Find the nearest open space away from machinery and brace for turbulence.

**If you find yourself inside a reversal zone:**

- Remain calm. The reversal of native matter around you is not directly affecting your body (probably — see equipment anomalies above).
- Do not touch anything. Especially do not touch any surface that is in the process of un-accreting, because if the zone collapses, it will resume accreting violently and anything in contact with it may be caught in the snap-back.
- Exit the way you came in. The geometry of the zone is unstable, and moving deeper in hopes of finding an exit on the other side is gambling against unknown odds.
- Move quickly but do not rush. Rushing leads to collisions with pipe fittings and stone ridges, and injuries inside the Groves are an emergency you are profoundly ill-equipped to handle.

## The Deep Call: What to Do When the Basin Surges

The sweeping low tone from the center of the basin occurs at irregular intervals — reports range from once every few hours to multiple times in a single hour, with no discernible pattern. When it comes:

1. **You will feel it before you hear it.** A sudden dropping-away of the background noise, a moment of relative silence that feels like the air being sucked out of a room. Your gut will clench. Your vision may briefly tunnel.

2. **You have approximately ninety seconds.** Use them. Identify your nearest open space — a cathedral gap between widely-spaced trunks, ideally away from heavy machinery. Move there. Orient yourself with your back to a trunk (but not touching it — maintain a few inches of clearance). Secure all loose equipment. Tighten your straps. Verify your breathing gas supply.

3. **The surge will be violent.** Explosive acceleration of growth. Doubled machine output. Collapsed reversal zones snapping back. Chaotic currents. Flaring and dimming light. Shrapnel from bark explosions and dislodged machine components. Pressure waves from seismic shudders below. The noise will exceed anything you've experienced to that point — a wall of sound so dense it ceases to be sound and becomes simply pressure.

4. **Hold position.** Do not attempt to navigate during a surge. Visibility drops to near zero. Currents become unpredictable. The soundscape is temporarily useless for orientation because everything is happening at once. Tuck in, protect your head and your breathing apparatus, and wait.

5. **The settling takes two to five minutes.** When the noise drops back to its baseline roar (and you will come to think of that roar as *quiet*, which tells you something about adaptation), wait another five minutes before moving. Secondary collapses and late-breaking reversal zone snap-backs can occur during the settling phase.

6. **After a surge, check everything.** Equipment integrity, gas supply, accretion on suit and gear, navigation calibration, communication with partners. The basin has just rearranged itself — new stone formations, shifted root corridors, relocated reversal zones. Your planned route may no longer exist.

## Dive Planning and Duration

**Solo diving in the Groves is not recommended.** The environment is too complex, too changeable, and too disorienting for a single individual to manage all hazards simultaneously. A minimum team of two, with a surface tender (or at least someone stationed at the periphery in shallower water) monitoring time and maintaining a communication link.

**Maximum recommended immersion time:** Four to six hours for a well-equipped team, with aggressive accretion management and active cooling. This is not a hard physiological limit — it is a practical limit imposed by the convergence of accretion buildup on equipment, thermal stress despite active cooling, acoustic fatigue, and the cumulative psychological weight of the environment. Travelers who have stayed longer report increasing difficulty distinguishing their own thoughts from the patterns of sound around them. The roaring begins to *organize* in the mind, to seem structured, to seem like it's *saying something*. This is a sign of cognitive degradation from sustained infrasonic exposure and thermal stress and it is your signal that you have stayed too long.

**Plan your route as a loop with multiple bail-out paths.** Enter from the shallow periphery, penetrate toward the center along a planned corridor, and return via a different route if the first is compromised. Identify at least three potential exit corridors from any point on your route.

**Do not attempt to reach the center of the basin.** The deep center, where the call originates, where no structures have been found, where the trees become indistinguishable from the mass — this is not a place for visitors. The density of machinery, the frequency of reversal zones, the intensity of turbulence, the accelerated accretion, and the total loss of navigable sight lines make the deep center a commitment from which extraction is uncertain. Several travelers have entered the deep center. Their structures were found months later, in various stages of digestion, on newly-risen stone ridges. The travelers themselves were not found.

## Equipment Checklist (Summary)

**Worn:**
- Hard-shell dry suit, smooth non-porous exterior, sealed at all joints, active liquid cooling undergarment
- Full-face dive helmet with integrated acoustic dampening, bone-conduction communication, heads-up display for navigation data
- Custom in-ear hearing protection under helmet
- Sealed gloves, high-dexterity, smooth exterior
- Weighted boots with non-metallic soles for basin-floor traversal (some areas are shallow enough for walking)

**Carried:**
- Closed-circuit rebreather, primary, with extended-duration scrubber
- Backup breathing gas supply (bailout bottle, ideally ceramic or composite construction)
- Inertial navigation unit, calibrated, with manual reset capability
- Dive lights ×3 (primary, secondary, emergency), non-metallic housings preferred
- Non-metallic scraping tools for accretion removal
- Hydration system integrated into helmet
- Cutting tools — for freeing yourself from lines or growth, not for interacting with the environment
- Emergency ascent/exit float with gas inflation (for rapid vertical evacuation if conditions deteriorate in a shallow zone)
- Hard-wired communication tether to partner (with excess slack to allow independent movement)
- Timer with multiple programmable alarms (dive duration, accretion check intervals, turnaround point)
- Waterproof documentation tools if conducting research (note: cameras and recording equipment accrete aggressively; bring spares and expect losses)

**Do not bring:**
- Anything you can't afford to lose. The Groves consume equipment.
- Biological samples or materials. No food, no wood, no plant matter, no natural fibers. If the accretion process recognizes biological material as *closer* to itself, incorporation may be dramatically faster.
- Magnetic navigation equipment. The iron in the basin — in the machines, in the accretion, possibly in the stone — creates a local magnetic environment that renders compasses useless.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section no checklist can adequately cover, but I'll try.

The Boiling Groves are not hostile. They are not evil. They are not even, in any recognizable sense, aware. But they are *relentless*, and they are *everywhere*, and they do not stop. The growth does not stop. The noise does not stop. The warmth does not stop. The amber murk does not stop. There is no horizon, no sky, no distance, no silence, no stillness, no cold clean breath of air. There is only the inside of the process, and you are in it.

The absence of fauna is psychologically significant in ways that sneak up on you. No fish, no crabs, no worms, no microbial mats (that you can see), nothing that moves with intention, nothing that has eyes, nothing that could be a companion. The trees are alive but they are not *creatures*. The machines are active but they are not *agents*. You are the only thing in the basin that is choosing to be there, and after a few hours this knowledge becomes heavy.

The temptation — and multiple travelers have reported this independently — is to begin attributing intention to the environment. The machines seem purposeful. The growth seems directed. The reversal zones seem *playful*. The deep call seems like communication. The way the forest-machines converge — wood becoming metal, metal becoming wood — seems like it's building toward something, like there's a plan, like the next step is almost *visible* if you could just stay a little longer and watch a little more carefully.

**This is the Groves' most dangerous property.** Not the accretion, not the reversal zones, not the surges. The pull of meaning. The sense that you are *almost* understanding something. You are not. There is nothing to understand. Or if there is, it is not something that a human mind can hold without being changed in ways that are not reversible, and the travelers who stayed long enough to find out are the ones whose structures were found on the new stone ridges, accreted and growing and humming and photosynthesizing, with no sign of the travelers themselves.

Set your time. Honor your time. When the timer says leave, leave. Do not negotiate with the part of your mind that says *five more minutes*. Do not follow the interesting pipe to see where it goes. Do not investigate the structure that looks almost like a doorway. Leave.

The Groves will still be there. They will always be there. They will be there long after every structure they have consumed has become indistinguishable from the rest of the process, and they will still be growing, and running, and calling out from the deep center with that long low rising tone that means *more*, that always means *more*.

## Upon Exit

When you leave the basin and reach dry ground (or whatever passes for it at the periphery — the boundary is gradual, the water shallowing over hundreds of meters of increasingly sparse tree growth and increasingly quiet machinery), do the following immediately:

1. **Remove and inspect all equipment.** Scrape every surface. Photograph any accretion patterns for documentation. Note any equipment that has developed structural changes — swelling, discoloration, unusual warmth, or the presence of the bark-mineral laminate in places it could not have reached from outside (inside sealed compartments, on the interior of hoses, beneath o-ring seats). Equipment showing internal accretion should be quarantined and not reused.

2. **Inspect yourself.** Have your partner examine every square inch of your suit exterior and every seal. Remove the suit and inspect your skin. Look for any patches of discoloration, any roughness, any mineral residue, particularly around the hands, face, and any area where your suit fit was tightest and the pressure might have forced particulate-laden water through a micro-gap in the seal. If you find anything, wash thoroughly with clean water and a mild acid solution (dilute vinegar works) and monitor the site closely over the following days.

3. **Rest.** Your body has been under thermal stress, acoustic bombardment, and sustained cognitive load. Your hearing may feel muffled — this is temporary in short exposures. You will be dehydrated despite your hydration system. You will be more tired than you think. Sleep somewhere quiet, if quiet exists in whatever staging area you've established near the basin.

4. **Debrief.** Talk about what you saw. This serves two purposes: it creates a record, and it helps you externalize the experience, which reduces the tendency — common among Groves visitors — to replay the sensory memory obsessively in the days that follow. The roaring has a way of persisting in the mind. Some travelers report hearing it for weeks afterward, faintly, especially in quiet moments, especially at the edge of sleep. It fades. If it doesn't fade — if it gets louder, or if you begin to perceive structure in it — seek out someone who specializes in multiverse-exposure aftereffects. You may have been more affected than you realized.

5. **Inventory what you're leaving behind.** Because you will leave things behind. A scraping tool dropped in the murk. A guideline you couldn't retrieve. A backup light that was torn loose in a surge. Accept these losses. Do not go back for them. They belong to the Groves now. In a year they'll have bark and copper pipes and turbines and leaves, and they'll be growing, and they will not remember being yours.

---

Go carefully. Go briefly. Go with the knowledge that you will return from the Boiling Groves changed — not because the place has done something to you, but because you have seen something that cannot be unseen: the line between life and machine, between growth and construction, between forward and backward, drawn so thin it vanishes, and on the other side of its vanishing, a warm amber-green world that just keeps building.
