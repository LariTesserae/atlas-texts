# Preparing for the City That Grew Into Itself

## First: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before any gear list, before any tactical consideration, you need to sit with what this place is and let it settle into your thinking, because the greatest danger here is not physical. The greatest danger is categorical confusion — the slow dissolution of your ability to distinguish between the built and the grown, the intentional and the emergent, the navigable and the recursive. People who enter this place without a clear internal framework for processing what they are experiencing tend to drift toward the center, where the spatial logic breaks down, and some of them have difficulty finding the edge again. Not because the city traps them. It doesn't trap. It simply becomes increasingly self-consistent in a way that excludes the traveler's frame of reference, and if you don't maintain your own frame, you will find yourself walking loops that feel like straight lines, standing in plazas you are certain you have never visited that are in fact the same plaza you stood in an hour ago, rotated by some imperceptible process into an orientation your spatial memory cannot reconcile.

So the first preparation is mental. You need to accept, before you arrive, that this is a place where your navigational instincts will be systematically undermined — not by hostility, not by illusion, but by a living substrate that is genuinely reorganizing itself around you. The changes are real. The geometry shifts are not perceptual errors. And your memory of how things were arranged five minutes ago is probably accurate — the city has simply moved on.

This means you need external reference systems, and you need to decide in advance how much you trust them versus how much you trust your own senses. This is not a trivial decision.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear

This is more critical than it first appears. The root-mat surface is hard — harder than hardwood, approaching stone in the oldest areas — but it is not uniform. It has grain. It has texture. In some places it is worn glass-smooth by centuries of its own micro-movements; in others it is ridged and furrowed where newer root growth is actively threading through older substrate. The surface also transmits vibration in ways that affect your body over time.

You want stiff-soled boots with vibration-dampening insoles. Not soft hiking boots — those will let the root-frequency through, and after a few hours of walking on a surface that hums at infrasonic frequencies, you will develop a bone-deep ache in your feet, ankles, and knees that conventional fatigue does not explain. The vibration is not harmful in short exposures, but it is persistent, and it is cumulative. Mountaineering boots with rigid soles work. Some travelers have had success with boots designed for operating jackhammers or other vibrating heavy equipment — anything with anti-vibration technology built into the sole.

Avoid rubber. The root-mat surface interacts strangely with rubber over extended contact — not quickly enough to notice in a day trip, but if you are staying multiple days, you will find that rubber soles begin to adhere slightly to the surface, developing a tacky quality that is not glue but something more like biological compatibility. The surface is *interested* in rubber, perhaps because of its organic polymer origins. Leather, synthetic composites, and hard plastics are all fine.

### Clothing

The altitude means intense UV exposure and significant temperature swings. During the day, the thin air lets solar radiation through with minimal filtering — you will sunburn faster than you expect, especially because the pale bleached-wood surfaces of the buildings reflect a surprising amount of light. The city is bright. It is a high plateau of white wood under direct sun, and the albedo is considerable.

At night, the temperature drops sharply. The root-mat retains some thermal mass — it is alive, metabolically active, and generates heat — but the surface radiates freely into the thin high-altitude atmosphere. You will go from needing sun protection in the afternoon to needing insulation by midnight.

Layers. UV-protective outer layer for daytime. Insulating mid-layers for evening. A wind shell, because the plateau is exposed and the wind can be steady and cold, especially along the wider avenues where it channels between the building-forms. Nothing cotton — you will be dealing with moisture from the city's own condensation cycles, and cotton will soak through and not dry.

A hat with a brim. Sunglasses. High-SPF sunscreen for exposed skin, though be cautious about which formulation you use — more on this in the section on chemical interactions.

### Hydration and Nutrition

**Water:** You cannot rely on the city's water. The cisterns are technically potable — the water in them is condensation, essentially distilled — but they are also actively inhabited by those gelatinous bodies, those ambiguous organisms that drift in slow circles and may or may not be detached plant organs. The water has a faintly sweet, tannin-rich flavor that is not unpleasant but suggests dissolved organic compounds of unknown composition. Some travelers have drunk from the cisterns without apparent ill effect. Others have reported altered perception — subtle changes in time sense, a heightened awareness of the infrasonic vibrations, a tendency to lose track of which circuit of a loop they are on. Whether this is caused by something in the water or by the general environment is impossible to determine, and this ambiguity is itself a reason to bring your own supply.

Carry all the water you need. Plan for at least four liters per day — the altitude and low humidity will dehydrate you faster than you expect, even though the city produces periodic mist. That mist is metabolic exhalation, not clean water vapor. It smells of chlorophyll and tannin and that old mineral undertone, and breathing it is unavoidable, but drinking it intentionally — collecting it on surfaces and consuming it — introduces concentrated plant metabolites into your system. Not recommended.

If you must extend your water supply from local sources, the cisterns farthest from the center, in the peripheral districts where the architecture is still geometrically stable, are your safest bet. Filter and treat. Activated carbon filtration will remove most dissolved organics. Do not use iodine-based purification — there are reports of iodine reacting with something in the water to produce compounds that are mildly psychoactive. UV purification is fine for biological contamination but does nothing for the dissolved organics. Your best combination is carbon filtration followed by UV treatment.

**Food:** Bring everything. There is nothing to eat here that is safe to eat, not because it is poisonous but because the food web is closed and the boundary between organism and environment is so blurred that consuming any biological material from this city means introducing an unknown quantity of self-organizing plant tissue into your digestive tract. The beetle-seed-things are not food. The gelatinous cistern organisms are not food. The moss on the walls is not food. The bark and wood are so dense as to be effectively inedible anyway, but even scraping some off and chewing it would be inadvisable. Calorie-dense, shelf-stable provisions. Enough for your entire planned stay plus a two-day margin, because navigation out can take longer than navigation in if you stray toward the center.

### Shelter

You can sleep inside the buildings, and in fact this is the best option. The living walls regulate temperature with remarkable precision — the interiors maintain a comfortable range regardless of exterior conditions, presumably through the same metabolic processes that drive everything else in the city. The air inside is fresh, circulated by the gentle respiration of the structure itself. The floors are smooth, warm root-mat. You will be comfortable.

But bring a sleeping pad with a vapor barrier. You do not want prolonged direct contact between your body and the root-mat surface while you sleep. The surface is warm. It is alive. It will not harm you, but over the course of a night, a sleeper in direct contact with the root-mat may wake to find fine root tendrils — thread-thin, pale, exploratory — extending across the surface toward their body heat. These tendrils are not aggressive. They are not fast. They are simply the root-mat doing what it does: growing toward energy sources, exploring, incorporating. They will not penetrate skin. But they will reach your clothing, your gear, anything that sits directly on the surface for extended periods, and they will begin the very preliminary stages of the process that turned the original city into what it is now. Over a single night, this amounts to nothing more than a thin film of rootlets on the underside of your pack, easily brushed away. Over multiple nights, if you are not rotating your contact points and checking your equipment, you may find that items left in place have begun to be incorporated — a boot left sole-down on the root-mat overnight may resist being picked up in the morning, not because it is stuck, but because threads of living tissue have begun to grow through the stitching. An hour of patient separation fixes this. But it is unsettling, and it accelerates the longer you stay.

A ground cloth with a smooth, non-porous underside — silnylon, DCF, or similar — prevents this entirely. The tendrils cannot gain purchase on the slick surface and will redirect their growth elsewhere. Sleep on this. Place all gear on this.

You do not need a tent. The building interiors are the shelter. But bring a bivy sack or lightweight emergency shelter in case you need to stop and rest in an open area — the avenues and plazas, where no interior is immediately accessible.

## Navigation

This is the core challenge. This is what will determine whether your visit is a fascinating day trip or a disorienting multi-day ordeal.

### GPS and Electronic Navigation

GPS will work on the plateau. The sky is open over the avenues — the foliage crests on the rooflines do not close overhead, as noted in the description. Satellite signals reach. Your device will give you coordinates.

The problem is that coordinates are only useful if the territory they describe is stable, and in the central districts, it is not. Your GPS will show you standing at a specific latitude and longitude. The city around that point will have reorganized since the last time you stood there. Your GPS is not wrong — you are where it says you are. The buildings have simply rearranged their spatial relationships to each other while remaining at their nominal positions. This is the nature of the reinterpretation the city performs. The physical materials stay approximately where they are. The geometry they express changes.

In the peripheral districts, GPS is fully reliable. The architecture there is stable, maintaining its original plan faithfully. Use GPS freely in the outer ring.

As you move inward, supplement GPS with direct observation. Mark your position relative to the plateau edge — this is your most reliable fixed reference, because the edge is geological, not biological, and the city cannot reorganize the literal rim of the plateau. If you can see the edge, you can orient. If you cannot see the edge, you are too deep in.

### Physical Markers

Do not rely on markers you leave on the city's surfaces. The root-mat surface will incorporate foreign objects over time — a cairn of stones will be threaded with rootlets within hours, and the stones themselves may be slowly repositioned by the growth processes of the substrate. Chalk marks on wooden walls will be overgrown. Flagging tape tied to structures may find itself on a different structure by the next day, not because it was moved but because the structure it was tied to has exchanged spatial positions with a neighbor.

Carry tall, freestanding markers — lightweight poles or stakes that can be driven into the root-mat and that rise above the surface enough to be seen from a distance. Metal stakes are better than wooden ones, for obvious reasons. Use brightly colored flags. Check them frequently. If a marker appears to have moved, it probably has, and you should trust your GPS over your markers in those situations.

### The Rail Cuttings

The rail network is simultaneously the most tempting and the most dangerous navigational resource. The cuttings are smooth, level, easy walking. They run in straight lines through the city, cutting through blocks and districts with industrial directness. They feel like reliable paths.

They are reliable in the outer districts. In the inner districts, they are the primary mechanism by which the city's spatial reorganization expresses itself. The switches move. The junctions reconfigure. A cutting that runs east-west at noon may curve imperceptibly northward by evening, not because the cutting has moved but because the switch you passed through an hour ago has rerouted you onto a different branch without any perceptible junction. The loop phenomenon is real and is most pronounced along the rail network. If you are following a cutting in the inner city and you begin to feel that the walk is taking longer than it should, or shorter than it should, stop. Check GPS. Check your sightline to the plateau edge. If you cannot see the edge, turn around and retrace your steps — but be aware that retracing may also be unreliable if the switches behind you have repositioned.

The safest strategy in the inner city is to stay on the avenues, not the rail cuttings. The avenues are wider, offer better sightlines, and while they are also subject to the city's reorganization, the process is slower and more gradual on the broad open streets than in the narrow, switch-controlled rail network.

### Time Limits

Set a strict time limit for any foray into the inner districts, and honor it. Three hours is reasonable for an initial visit. This gives you enough time to experience the spatial anomalies, hear the accelerated clicking of the factory-buildings, observe the shift in rail junction geometry, and still retrace your route to the outer ring before the accumulated reorganization makes your path unrecognizable.

Longer stays in the inner city are possible but require a fundamentally different approach — you stop navigating and start *residing*, accepting the city's geometry as it presents itself in each moment rather than trying to maintain a consistent mental map. Some travelers have spent weeks in the inner districts this way, moving from building to building as the streets reorganize around them, sleeping in whichever interior is most convenient, allowing the city to route them through itself like one of its own beetle-seed processions. These travelers report that the experience is profoundly disorienting for the first two or three days and then becomes strangely peaceful, as if the brain gives up trying to impose a stable map and simply accepts the environment as it is: a living, self-modifying space that is not hostile, not helpful, not indifferent, but *busy* — occupied with its own processes, tolerant of your presence the way a forest is tolerant of a single deer, registering you without being particularly concerned.

The risk of this approach is that leaving becomes a matter of chance or patience. You cannot navigate out of the inner city by intention; you can only move through it and wait for the reorganization process to deliver you to a district stable enough to navigate from. This usually happens within a day or two. But "usually" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

## Equipment Considerations

### What to Bring

- **Rigid-soled boots** with vibration dampening. Not rubber-soled.
- **UV-protective clothing** and high-SPF sunscreen. Mineral-based sunscreen (zinc oxide, titanium dioxide) is preferable to chemical sunscreen — the organic chemical compounds in conventional sunscreen interact unpredictably with the city's moisture. Specifically, when the metabolic mist condenses on skin coated with avobenzone or oxybenzone, some travelers have reported a warming sensation, mild skin irritation, and an intensification of the root-mat's interest in their contact points. Mineral sunscreens do not produce this effect.
- **4+ liters of water per day**, all carried in.
- **Activated carbon water filter** plus UV purifier as backup.
- **Calorie-dense food**, fully self-contained. Nothing that requires cooking on an open flame — do not make fire in the city. This should be obvious, but it bears stating: every surface here is living wood. A fire of any size would be an existential event for the immediate structures and would provoke a response from the root-mat that you do not want to be standing on when it happens. The vibration frequency shifts. The substrate contracts. The city closes streets. This has been reported by exactly one group who lit a camp stove in a plaza, and they described the experience as the ground beneath them "clenching" and the buildings on all sides leaning inward by several noticeable degrees in the space of a minute. The stove was extinguished. Over the following hour, the buildings returned to vertical and the streets reopened. Cold food. Always cold food.
- **Sleeping pad with impermeable underside.** DCF ground cloth. Do not sleep directly on the root-mat.
- **Metal stakes** for marking. Aluminum or titanium, tall enough to be visible at a distance. Colored flags.
- **GPS device** with fresh batteries and a spare. Mark the point where you step onto the plateau. Mark the point where you enter the city's outer ring. Mark every junction and turn. In the outer districts, this gives you a reliable return path. In the inner districts, it gives you a psychological anchor, which is nearly as valuable.
- **Notebook and pen.** Not for the usual reasons — not for recording observations, though you will want to do that too. The notebook is for maintaining a running log of your spatial experience: what direction you turned, what you see to your left and right, what landmarks (smokestacks, distinctive building-forms, cisterns) you can identify. When the city begins to reorganize around you, this log becomes your primary tool for detecting it. If your log says the three-stack factory was on your right and it is now on your left, you know. Without the log, you will rationalize the discrepancy. The brain is eager to rationalize discrepancies here. The city's reorganization is smooth and convincing, and your own spatial memory will betray you by updating itself to match the new geometry. Writing things down prevents this. Trust the notebook over your memory. Always.
- **Ear protection.** Not because the sound is dangerously loud — it is not. But the infrasonic component of the root-mat's breathing is persistent and cumulative. After 48 hours of continuous exposure, some travelers report difficulty sleeping, a pervasive sense of unease, and a tendency to feel that the ground is slowly moving beneath them (it is, technically, but not at a rate that should be perceptible — the fact that it becomes perceptible suggests the infrasound is sensitizing the vestibular system). Foam earplugs reduce the higher-frequency components but do nothing for infrasound. Active noise-canceling headphones with specific low-frequency attenuation are better. Some travelers use bone-conduction dampening pads under their sleeping pads. The goal is not silence — silence here would be its own kind of unsettling — but reduction of the sub-40Hz component that does the cumulative neurological work.
- **A watch with an alarm.** The city's sound environment has a lulling quality. The breathing, the clicking, the periodic creak of the switches — it is rhythmic, it is complex, and it pulls attention inward. Travelers have reported losing track of time in the most literal sense: sitting down to rest against a warm wooden wall, listening to the factory-clicking, and discovering that two or three hours have passed in what felt like twenty minutes. This is not supernatural. It is the well-documented effect of immersion in a complex, low-frequency sound environment on time perception. But it can cost you daylight and disrupt your navigation schedule. Set alarms. Stand up when they go off. Move.
- **Binoculars.** The plateau is large and the city extends for miles. From the higher structures — the smokestacks, the taller factory buildings — you can see the plateau edge in most directions, and this sightline is your most reliable orientation tool. Binoculars let you use it from ground level, spotting the edge through gaps between buildings.

### What Not to Bring

- **No open-flame devices of any kind.** No stoves. No lighters. No matches. No signal flares. If you need emergency signaling, use electronic or mirror-based methods. The city's response to fire is swift, coordinated, and deeply uncomfortable to experience at close range.
- **No rubber-soled shoes**, as discussed.
- **No fresh plant material.** Do not bring flowers, wooden utensils, herbs, botanical specimens from other locations. The root-mat is interested in biological material and will prioritize investigation of foreign plant matter over almost anything else. A traveler who left a bunch of dried herbs in a side pocket of their pack woke to find the entire pack enveloped in a dense mat of root tendrils, all converging on the pocket, with the herbs already partially absorbed. The pack was recoverable but required an hour of careful extraction. Living plants would be worse.
- **No audio recording equipment left unattended.** This sounds specific because it is. The factory-clicking, when recorded and played back, does not sound the same as it does in person. It sounds more organized, more patterned, more clearly like a signal or a language. Several research teams have become obsessed with analyzing these recordings, spending weeks in the inner city running longer and longer sessions, positioned in different buildings, trying to triangulate the source or decode the pattern. The recordings are compelling. They suggest meaning. Whether meaning is actually there is unresolvable, and the pursuit of it has a way of pulling people deeper into the city and keeping them there longer than is wise. Record if you wish — the sounds are extraordinary and worth preserving. But do not begin analysis while you are still inside the city. Take the recordings home. Listen there. The distance helps.

## Psychological Preparation

### Accepting the Uncanny

The city provokes a specific emotional response that is consistent across travelers: a deep, slow-building uncanniness that does not resolve into fear or wonder but sits in between, occupying a register that most people have no name for. It is the feeling of being in a place that was made by your own kind, that has been taken over by something that is not your kind, and that has been *maintained in the form your kind created* by the thing that took it over, as if in homage, or in continuation, or simply because the form was useful and the new tenant saw no reason to discard it.

This feeling intensifies in the inner districts, where the maintenance begins to break down — or rather, where the maintenance begins to serve a different plan. The curving walls, the leaning buildings, the looping rail lines — these are not decay. They are development. The city is not falling apart; it is growing into something else, something that started from the template of a human industrial city and has been iterating on that template for centuries, departing from the original in ways that are small enough to be individually unremarkable and cumulatively profound.

Some travelers find this exhilarating. Others find it deeply distressing. The distress tends to come from a specific source: the realization that the human city was not destroyed, was not abandoned, was not even really lost. It was *continued*. By something that is not human. And the continuation is so faithful, so detailed, so structurally sound that it raises the question of whether the original human city was already, in some sense, this — already a living system running its own logic, already iterating, already computing, already something that the plants simply recognized and joined and eventually subsumed because the difference between what the city was doing and what they do was not as large as anyone wanted to believe.

This is the thought that keeps people up at night on the root-mat, listening to the clicking. Prepare for it. Sit with it before you go. Decide what you think a city is, and then be ready to have that definition tested.

### Solitude

Go alone or with one trusted companion. Groups larger than three tend to fragment in the inner city — not because of interpersonal conflict, but because the spatial reorganization affects different people's navigation differently. Two people walking side by side through a shifting junction may end up on different branches without either of them noticing the divergence, because each one's experience of the transition was smooth and continuous. In a pair, you can maintain physical contact — a hand on a shoulder, a rope between waists — and this keeps you together through reorganizations. In a group of four or five, the outer members drift.

If you go alone, you must be comfortable with solitude at a depth that most recreational travel does not require. You will be the only warm-blooded, intentional consciousness in a city that extends for miles in every direction. The beetle-things are not company. The birds do not descend. The city itself is *something*, but it is not a conversational partner. You will be alone with a vast, patient, inhuman process, and the process will continue around you with absolute indifference to your presence, and this is either the most liberating or the most terrifying thing you have ever experienced, depending on your relationship with solitude.

## Timing Your Visit

### Season

The plateau has seasons, though they are muted by altitude. Summer brings the longest days and the warmest temperatures, making the temperature swings less extreme and the daylight hours more generous. This is the easiest time to visit. The city's condensation cycles are less active in summer — the mist is thinner, the cisterns lower — and the smokestack exhalations are reduced, which means better visibility across the avenues.

Winter is harder. The days are short, the cold is significant, and the city's metabolic activity increases in apparent compensation — the root-mat generates more heat, the smokestacks exhale more vigorously, the mist is thick and persistent, and the inner districts are sometimes obscured entirely by the city's own breath, a warm fog that fills the streets and reduces visibility to a few dozen feet. Navigating the inner city in winter fog is not recommended under any circumstances. The spatial reorganization continues and you cannot see it happening.

Spring and autumn are intermediate. The thermal cycling is active — warm days, cold nights — and the root-mat's expansion-contraction groaning is at its most pronounced. The infrasound is louder. The switches move more frequently. The city is, in some sense, more active in the transitional seasons, as if the changing conditions stimulate its processes. This makes for a more dramatic visit but a more disorienting one.

### Time of Day

Enter in the early morning. The pre-dawn dew phase is already underway when you arrive, and watching the city drink — watching every surface absorb its condensation in real time, the beaded moisture disappearing into the living wood as the first light hits — is one of the most remarkable things you will ever see. It is also a good calibration for your senses: you watch a process that is clearly biological, clearly the city feeding itself, and you establish in your mind the baseline understanding that *everything here is alive, everything here is metabolizing, everything you see is a living organism doing organism things at architectural scale.*

Plan to reach the inner districts by mid-morning, when the light is strong and direct and the mist has cleared. Spend your allotted time there. Begin your return by early afternoon, so that you are back in the stable outer ring before the light begins to angle and the shadows lengthen. The city at dusk is beautiful — the low light turns the bleached wood golden, and the smokestacks cast shadows that stretch across entire districts — but navigating the inner city in slant light is harder than in direct light, because the shadow patterns change the visual landscape in ways that compound the spatial reorganization. You see a shadow where there should not be one, and you cannot tell whether the building that casts it has moved or whether the sun has simply shifted. Both are happening. Distinguishing between them is exhausting.

Night in the outer city is fine. Night in the inner city is inadvisable.

## Duration

For a first visit: one full day, dawn to dusk, sleeping one night in an outer-district building, departing the next morning. This gives you enough time to walk the outer ring, make a controlled foray to the inner districts, experience the sound environment, observe the beetle-processions, investigate a factory interior, and return. You will leave with a vivid, coherent impression of the place and a strong desire to return.

For a deeper visit: three to five days, with a base camp in the outer ring and daily excursions inward. This allows you to observe the city's cycles — the diurnal breathing, the condensation rhythms, the switch-movement patterns — over multiple iterations and begin to detect regularities that a single day cannot reveal. You will notice that the switches move in sequences. You will notice that the beetle-processions follow routes that change on a multi-day cycle. You will notice that certain smokestacks exhale in response to others, in chains that propagate across the city in consistent patterns. Whether these regularities constitute a language, a program, a metabolism, or something else entirely is a question you will take home with you and never fully answer.

For an extended stay: two weeks or more. This is for the committed. You will need to resupply — there is no local food or potable water. You will need to manage the cumulative effects of infrasound exposure, root-mat contact, and metabolic mist inhalation. You will need to accept that your sense of the city as a fixed place will erode entirely, replaced by an understanding of it as a process — a thing that is happening, continuously, at a pace that encompasses both the geological and the momentary. Travelers who stay this long report that the uncanny feeling fades and is replaced by something else: a deep calm, a sense of participation, a feeling of being *included* in the city's process without being consumed by it. They also report that the clicking begins to sound less like machinery and more like weather — a constant, complex background condition of the environment, no more requiring interpretation than the sound of wind or rain.

These travelers tend to come back.

## On Leaving

Leave the way you came in. Follow your GPS markers back through the outer ring to the point where you stepped onto the plateau. Walk across the ancient bedrock — and notice how *silent* it is, how dead the stone feels under your feet after days on the living root-mat. The absence of vibration is startling. Your feet will feel strange on inert ground for days afterward, as if expecting a response from the surface that does not come.

Turn around at the plateau edge. Look back. The city is there, stretching to the horizon, its smokestacks exhaling their slow fog into the thin air, its white-wood buildings catching the light. From the edge, at this distance, it looks exactly like what it was: a city. A working city, well-planned, well-maintained, going about its business.

It is. It is exactly that. It's just that the nature of the business has changed, and the entity conducting it is one continuous organism the size of a county, thinking its slow thoughts in the language of root-growth and rail-switches and beetle-seed processions, and it will still be here when you come back, and it will still be here when you don't, and the smoke from its stacks is not smoke but breath, and it breathes, and it breathes, and it breathes.

Check your gear before you descend from the plateau. Brush off any root tendrils. Check boot seams and pack stitching for infiltration. Remove and discard any fragments of bark or wood that the beetle-things may have deposited in your pockets or pack pockets — they do this, apparently, adding you to their routes, treating you as a waypoint in whatever logistical operation they are perpetually conducting. The fragments are harmless but should not be taken off the plateau. The city's biology is self-contained, and it should stay that way. What grows here, stays here. What computes here, stays here.

Everything else — the sounds in your memory, the clicking that you will hear in quiet moments for weeks afterward, the feeling of standing in a plaza at the center of something vast and alive and busy and utterly unconcerned with you — that, you take with you. That is yours.
