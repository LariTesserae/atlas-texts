# Preparing for a Visit to The Roost at Shale's Tooth

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Let the implications settle.

You are going to a volcanic pinnacle at extreme altitude—a young basalt spire with a single concavity near its summit, approximately forty feet across and twenty feet deep, open on one side where the rock has fractured away. The floor is not a floor but a field of hexagonal basalt columns at varying heights, with gaps between them that descend into unsounded darkness. The hollow is densely inhabited by hundreds or perhaps a thousand eyeless, six-limbed, tympanum-bearing creatures of no clear taxonomic affiliation, clinging to every surface in overlapping clusters and extending in interlocked chains down into the interior of the spire. A persistent sub-audible thrumming fills the space, originating from below and sustained by the colony. An unidentified glowing organism occupies one corner. The air is thin, ionized, damp, and charged with static electricity. There is no path in or out.

This is not a hostile environment in the conventional sense. Nothing here is described as aggressive toward visitors. But it is deeply alien, physiologically demanding, and saturated with phenomena you will not fully understand. Your preparation must account for all of this simultaneously.

## Physical Conditioning and Acclimatization

### Altitude

The description strongly implies you are well above 4,000 meters, possibly above 5,000. The air "tastes of iron and altitude." The atmosphere is too thin to scatter light properly—stars might be visible during what should be daytime. Breath doesn't quite steam, but fingers ache with cold. This is the high alpine or subnival zone, possibly higher.

**Acclimatize before arrival.** If your multiverse transit deposits you directly at the Roost, you will have no acclimatization period, and acute mountain sickness is a near-certainty. If there is any way to stage your arrival—spending days at progressively higher elevations in this world or a similar one—do so. Spend at least a week working up from 2,500 meters. If this is impossible and you arrive cold at extreme altitude:

- Carry acetazolamide (Diamox). Begin taking it 24–48 hours before arrival if you can time your transit. Standard prophylactic dose: 125–250 mg twice daily. It accelerates acclimatization by promoting bicarbonate diuresis, shifting your blood pH to stimulate deeper breathing.
- Carry dexamethasone as an emergency measure for high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE). 8 mg initial dose, then 4 mg every six hours. This is a rescue medication, not a prophylactic for casual use.
- Carry nifedipine for high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) if you have any history of susceptibility. 30 mg extended-release every 12 hours.
- Understand that at extreme altitude, your cognitive function will be impaired. Judgment degrades. Memory softens. Reaction time slows. You will be making decisions in a thin-air fog, surrounded by phenomena that demand careful observation. Accept this limitation and plan for it—write things down, set timers, establish hard rules for yourself before you arrive (when to leave, what not to touch, how long to stay).

**Supplemental oxygen** is worth carrying if you can manage the weight. A small closed-circuit system or even a few cans of supplemental O₂ can buy you clarity during critical moments. The creatures don't appear to need mammalian oxygen levels, but you do.

### Cold Exposure

The temperature is described as not quite freezing but never warm—a persistent, damp cold that saps heat through contact and evaporation. The condensation that coats every surface will wick heat from your hands and body relentlessly. This is the worst kind of cold: not dramatic enough to force you to take it seriously, insidious enough to cause progressive hypothermia if you're careless.

- **Layer for sustained damp cold, not for extreme freezing.** A moisture-wicking base layer (merino wool, not cotton, never cotton), a mid-layer of synthetic or down insulation (synthetic is better given the pervasive dampness—down will lose loft if it gets wet), and a wind/waterproof outer shell. The shell matters less for rain than for the condensation that will coat you.
- **Insulated, waterproof gloves are critical.** Your fingers will ache. You'll be stepping across basalt column-tops with your hands for balance. You need dexterity but you also need warmth. Bring thin liner gloves under insulated overmitts, so you can strip down to liners for fine work and re-mitten quickly.
- **Insulated footwear with rigid soles.** You'll be stepping from column-top to column-top—some waist-high, some ankle-high, some flush. This is essentially bouldering on wet, polished stone pedestals with dark gaps between them. You need soles stiff enough to stand on small surfaces without your foot curling over the edge, grippy enough for wet basalt, and insulated enough that the stone doesn't leach all warmth from your feet. Mountaineering boots are the right category. Approach shoes are too flexible. Make sure the soles have aggressive tread—wet basalt, especially polished basalt, is treacherous.
- **A warm hat and neck gaiter.** Heat loss from the head is significant at altitude, and the damp air will make your neck and ears miserable.
- **Hand and toe warmers (chemical).** Cheap insurance. Tuck them into gloves and boots. In an environment of sustained marginal cold, these small heat sources can mean the difference between functional hands and useless ones.

### Fitness

You need the cardiovascular fitness to operate at extreme altitude while performing moderately technical movement across an uneven stone surface. This is not a hike—it's closer to scrambling or easy bouldering, but at elevation, while hypoxic, in the dark, on wet stone. Your heart will be working hard just to oxygenate your blood. Adding the physical demands of navigating the column-field means you should be in strong aerobic condition.

Train with weighted hiking, stair climbing, or any sustained cardiovascular work. If possible, train at elevation. Practice scrambling on uneven rocky terrain in low-light conditions. Get comfortable with exposure—the open side of the tooth drops away into void, and you will feel it.

## Equipment

### Navigation and Stability on the Column Floor

The floor of the Roost is a field of hexagonal basalt columns at varying heights. Some are waist-high. Some are ankle-high. Some are flush. The gaps between them go down "a long way"—possibly into the deep interior of the spire. The columns where the creatures congregate are polished glass-smooth by generations of contact. This is a fall hazard of the most serious kind: one misplaced step puts your foot into a gap that could be meters deep, surrounded by sharp-edged young basalt.

- **Trekking poles** are worth considering but may be more hindrance than help on the column-tops. The surface areas are small and irregular. Poles work best on continuous terrain. Here, you need your hands free for balance and for catching yourself. Consider instead:
- **A short mountaineering axe or ice tool**, used not for ice but as a balance aid and anchor point. Something you can hook over a column edge or jam into a cooling joint for stability.
- **Gaiters or lower-leg protection.** If you slip between columns, the edges of young basalt will slice skin and fabric alike. Hardshell gaiters or lightweight shin guards (like those used in canyoneering) will protect your shins from the worst of it.
- **Mark your path.** The darkness is near-total in the interior. You need to know which columns you've stepped on, which gaps are safe, where the creatures are dense enough that stepping would mean stepping on them. Small reflective markers, placed without adhesive (don't chemically interact with this stone—you don't know what consequences that might have), or a trail of small UV-fluorescent tags visible only under your own light source, could help. But see the section on light below—light use in this environment requires extreme caution.

### Light

This is the most complex equipment decision you will make.

The Roost is nearly dark. The only light source is the unidentified blue-green glow in the corner, which illuminates almost nothing. You will need artificial light to navigate safely. But you must consider:

1. **The creatures are eyeless.** They perceive the world through tympanic vibration and possibly through their crystalline lattice structures. Light may be completely irrelevant to them—or it may not. The glow in the corner is avoided, with a clear perimeter of bare stone around it. You don't know if the avoidance is due to the glow's light, its vibration, its chemistry, or something else. But the correlation between "thing that emits light" and "zone of avoidance" should make you cautious about introducing new light sources.

2. **The glowing organism pulses arrhythmically.** Whatever it is, it has its own frequency behavior. Your light sources will have their own electromagnetic signatures. The creatures' tympanums vibrate at specific frequencies. The entire hollow is a system of overlapping vibrations. Introducing a new electromagnetic emitter—even a visible-light one—could interact with this system in ways you cannot predict.

3. **Your eyes will adapt.** After 30–45 minutes in near-total darkness, your scotopic (rod-based) vision will be fully engaged. You will see more than you expect. The glow will become a useful landmark. The faint differences between dark stone and darker creatures will become perceptible. Silhouettes against the dim sky through the open fracture will give you spatial reference.

**Recommendation:** Bring multiple light options and use the dimmest one that works.

- **A red-light headlamp** at its lowest setting. Red light preserves your dark adaptation better than white light and has a longer wavelength, lower energy. Use it only for navigating the column-tops, pointed downward at your feet, shielded with your hand when possible.
- **A UV flashlight**, small and focusable, to check for fluorescence in the environment. The glowing organism, the creatures' wing-fin membranes, the crystalline lattice in their tympanums—any of these might fluoresce under UV, revealing structures invisible in white light. Use briefly and observe any reaction from the colony.
- **Chemical light sticks** (low-intensity green or red) as emergency backup. No electrical signature, no flicker, no battery to worry about. Crack one and set it at your feet for persistent low-level ambient illumination.
- **No strobes, no camera flashes, no high-intensity beams directed into the colony.** Even if the creatures cannot "see" the light, the thermal or electromagnetic effects of intense illumination in this confined, ionized, static-charged space could trigger responses you're not prepared for.

### Sound and Vibration

This may be more important than light.

The entire Roost is a vibration-processing system. The creatures perceive through tympanic membranes. The thrumming is continuous, layered, and travels through the stone. Your body—your footsteps, your breathing, your heartbeat, your voice—will introduce vibrations into this system. The creatures don't flinch from your footsteps on the column-tops, which suggests either that your vibrations are beneath their notice, or that they've already incorporated you into their processing, or that they simply don't care. But "don't care" and "haven't yet responded" are different things.

- **Move quietly.** Not sneaking—sudden movements are worse than steady ones. Move with consistent, deliberate rhythm. Let each footstep land cleanly. Don't drag your feet across column-tops; the scraping will send high-frequency vibrations into the stone that contrast sharply with the low thrumming.
- **Do not speak.** Or if you must, speak in a low monotone, matching the register of the thrumming as closely as you can. High-pitched, staccato sounds—exclamations, laughter, sharp whispers—will stand out in this acoustic environment like a gunshot in a library.
- **Do not bring electronic devices that emit sound or vibration.** No phones on vibrate. No beeping altimeters. No clicking cameras. If you need to record observations, write by hand or use a device with all sound and vibration alerts disabled.
- **Consider bringing a tuning fork.** This may sound eccentric. It is not. A tuning fork struck and held against the basalt will produce a clean, sustained, single-frequency vibration that you can control. If you need to test how the creatures respond to specific frequencies—or if you need to blend your presence into their tympanic landscape—a tuning fork in the right register (very low; you may need a custom-made one in the 20–40 Hz range, at the bottom of human hearing) could be a useful tool. Strike it, press it to the stone, observe. If the creatures' tympanums modulate in response, you're communicating, or at least participating. If they don't react, you've lost nothing.

### Electrical and Static Considerations

The air inside the tooth is ionized. Your hair moves. Your skin prickles. Condensation flows upward along electrostatic gradients. This is a persistent static-charge environment generated by a thousand vibrating tympanums in thin, damp air.

- **Ground yourself before touching any equipment.** Static discharge could damage electronics, but more importantly, a sudden spark in this environment—especially near the creatures—could produce unpredictable reactions.
- **Anti-static wrist straps** are cheap and worth wearing. Clip to a metal carabiner and let it dangle against the stone for grounding.
- **Be cautious with metal equipment.** Metal objects may accumulate charge. A metal trekking pole or ice axe held above the column-field could act as a lightning rod for ambient static. This won't electrocute you, but a visible spark or audible snap in the dark, quiet hollow could have consequences.
- **Monitor your own physiological response to the ionization.** Ionized air can cause headaches, nausea, and altered mood—effects that overlap with and could be confused with altitude sickness. If you feel worse than your altitude medications should account for, the ionization may be contributing.

## Clothing and Material Choices

Beyond the layering system described above:

- **Avoid synthetic fabrics that generate static readily.** Nylon, polyester, and fleece are all static-prone, which is unfortunate because they're the backbone of most outdoor layering systems. Merino wool is your best option for base and mid layers—it generates minimal static, regulates temperature well, and handles moisture without collapsing. If you must use synthetic insulation, choose tightly woven fabrics over loose fleece, and treat everything with an anti-static spray before arrival.
- **Avoid bright colors.** Not because the creatures can see them—they almost certainly can't—but because in the near-total darkness, your own light sources reflecting off bright fabric will destroy your dark adaptation and make it harder to observe the subtle visual phenomena (the glow, the translucent tympanums, the crystalline lattice in unfurled wing-fins). Dark, matte clothing lets you become part of the darkness.
- **Wear close-fitting clothes that won't snag.** The basalt is sharp-edged and young. Cooling joints are narrow crevices. Loose fabric will catch, tear, and potentially trap you if you slip between columns. No dangling straps, no loose hoods, no billowing layers.
- **Knee pads.** You may need to crawl across the column-tops in places where the height variation is extreme. Basalt on bare knees is brutal.

## What to Bring for Observation and Recording

You are visiting something extraordinary. You will want to document it. But your recording tools must be chosen to minimize interference with the environment.

### Written Notes
- **Waterproof notebook and pencil.** Not pen—ink will smear in the pervasive condensation. Pencil on waterproof paper (Rite in the Rain or similar) is reliable in any condition. Graphite on paper makes no sound, emits no light, produces no vibration.
- **Bring extra pencils.** Your fingers will be cold and clumsy. You will drop them into the gaps between columns. They will be gone.

### Audio Recording
- **A high-quality, low-frequency-capable audio recorder** is arguably the single most valuable observation tool you can bring. The thrumming is the dominant phenomenon of the Roost—the creatures' primary output, the environment's defining characteristic. Standard audio recorders often have a low-frequency cutoff around 20 Hz, which may miss the fundamental. Bring a recorder capable of capturing infrasound down to at least 5 Hz, with a sensitive external microphone.
- **A contact microphone (piezo pickup)** that you can press directly against the basalt columns. This will let you record the vibration as transmitted through the stone, which is how the creatures experience it. The difference between the airborne sound and the stone-conducted vibration may be revelatory.
- **Set the recorder running and leave it.** Don't check it, don't adjust it, don't fiddle with it. Put it in a protected position on a column-top, start recording, and forget about it until you leave. You'll analyze the data later. In the moment, be present.

### Visual Recording
- **This is difficult.** The darkness makes conventional photography nearly impossible without supplemental lighting, and supplemental lighting carries the risks described above. A camera with extreme high-ISO, low-light capability (modern full-frame sensors can shoot usably at ISO 25,600 or higher) paired with a fast, wide lens (f/1.4 or faster) might capture something using only the ambient glow. But it might capture nothing.
- **Thermal imaging** is potentially more useful. A FLIR-type thermal camera will show you the heat signatures of the creatures against the cold basalt—the "fractionally warmer" envelope of creature-heat inside the hollow, the thermal gradients along the walls, possibly the heat patterns of the thrumming itself. Thermal cameras require no visible-light emission. They are silent. This is your best imaging tool.
- **Sketch.** In the red-light glow of your headlamp, with cold fingers, in a waterproof notebook, sketch what you see. Sketching forces observation in a way photography does not. You will notice details while drawing that you would miss while pointing a camera.

### Scientific Sampling
- **Do not collect specimens.** Not of the creatures, not of the glow, not of the condensation, not of the basalt. You are a traveler, not a collector. You don't know what removing a piece of this system does to the system. The creatures form interlocked chains that descend into the dark interior of the spire; pulling one free might trigger a cascade. The glow is avoided by the colony for unknown reasons; disturbing it could release something or remove a boundary. The condensation flows along electrostatic gradients and feeds the glow; it is not ordinary water. Leave it all where it is.
- **If you absolutely must sample:** bring sterile glass vials and collect only loose condensation from column-tops where no creatures are present. A few milliliters. Seal immediately. Do not sample the glow. Do not touch the creatures. Wear nitrile gloves—the creatures have a faint acid in their skin or secretions capable of slowly dissolving basalt; you don't want that on your hands.

## Medical Preparation

Beyond the altitude medications:

- **Standard wilderness first aid kit** adapted for the specific hazards: lacerations from sharp basalt (adhesive wound closures, antiseptic, gauze), sprains and fractures from falls between columns (SAM splint, elastic bandage, tape), hypothermia (emergency bivouac sack, chemical warmers).
- **Ear protection.** The thrumming is sub-audible in the conventional sense—it arrives as pressure in the sternum, vibration through the feet—but sustained exposure to infrasound at sufficient intensity can cause nausea, disorientation, anxiety, visual disturbance, and a pervasive sense of dread. These are documented physiological effects of infrasound in the 7–19 Hz range. Earplugs won't help much—the vibration enters through your skeleton, not just your ears. But earplugs with moderate attenuation can reduce the airborne component enough to keep you from being overwhelmed. Bring them. Use them if you start to feel wrong in ways that don't match altitude sickness.
- **Eye drops.** The ionized, damp air may irritate your eyes, especially if you're trying to maintain dark adaptation and keeping your eyes wide open for extended periods. Preservative-free artificial tears.
- **Electrolytes and calories.** Altitude suppresses appetite. The cold burns calories. The thin air demands harder breathing, which dehydrates you. Bring concentrated calorie sources (nut butter packets, energy gels, chocolate) and electrolyte powder for your water. Drink more than you want to. Eat on a schedule, not by hunger.
- **A pulse oximeter.** Small, clips to your finger, tells you your blood oxygen saturation. At extreme altitude, your SpO₂ will be lower than at sea level—85% might be your baseline here. But if it drops below 75%, you need supplemental oxygen or descent. Monitor regularly. The Roost's atmosphere—thin, ionized, possibly altered by the creatures' metabolic outputs—may affect oxygenation in ways beyond simple altitude.

### The Acid

The creatures exude something that slowly dissolves basalt. Basalt is a silicate rock—dissolving it requires either strong acid (hydrofluoric) or sustained weak acid over geological time. Given that the wear patterns are visible but not extreme, this is probably a mild organic acid, something akin to the lichen acids that weather stone on Earth. But you don't know the concentration in fresh secretion, and you don't know whether it's specific to silicates or broadly corrosive.

- **Nitrile gloves, not latex.** Nitrile resists a wider range of chemicals.
- **If you contact creature secretion on bare skin:** flush immediately with water. Do not rub. Monitor for irritation, discoloration, or numbness. If the acid is an HF analog (extremely unlikely but worth preparing for), calcium gluconate gel is the specific antidote—apply topically. Bring a tube.
- **Protect your gear.** Don't set equipment directly on surfaces where creatures have recently been. The polished, acid-worn stone is old residue, probably inert, but fresh secretion might attack metal, fabric, or rubber. Use a small sitting pad or square of chemically resistant material (silicone, PTFE, glass) as a barrier between your gear and the stone.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most visitors will fail, and it matters more than any piece of equipment.

### Infrasound and Dread

Sustained infrasound exposure—especially in the 18–19 Hz range, near the resonant frequency of the human eyeball—causes a documented syndrome: anxiety, unease, the feeling of being watched, peripheral visual disturbances (smearing, shadowy figures at the edge of vision), nausea, and a deep, irrational conviction that something is wrong. These are not emotional responses to a strange place. They are **physiological artifacts** of your body resonating with low-frequency sound. Your organs vibrate. Your eyes distort. Your vestibular system sends confused signals.

You need to know this before you go. You need to have it memorized, internalized, tattooed on the inside of your eyelids: **the dread is mechanical.** When you stand in the tooth and the thrumming enters your bones and you feel, with absolute certainty, that something terrible is about to happen, that something in the dark below the columns is aware of you, that you must leave immediately—that is infrasound doing what infrasound does to human bodies. It is not intuition. It is not a warning. It is acoustics.

This does not mean there is nothing to fear. It means your fear-detection system will be miscalibrated for the entire visit, firing constantly, and you will have to override it with deliberate rational assessment every time it activates. This is exhausting. Budget your mental energy accordingly.

**Practice exposure to infrasound before your visit if possible.** Some research facilities and sound studios can generate controlled infrasound. Spend time in it. Learn what your body does. Learn to recognize the onset of the dread response. Learn that you can sit with it, note it, and continue functioning.

### Darkness and Sensory Deprivation

The near-total darkness, combined with the persistent thrumming (which your brain will eventually begin to tune out, causing intermittent "silence" that is actually sensory fatigue), can produce a mild sensory-deprivation state. Hallucinations are possible—visual (your brain generating patterns in the absence of input), auditory (phantom sounds layered over the thrumming), tactile (the feeling of being touched when nothing is touching you). In this environment, surrounded by hundreds of actual creatures that are actually clinging to surfaces all around you, distinguishing hallucination from reality will be difficult.

**Anchor yourself physically.** Keep one hand on the stone at all times. Feel its texture, its temperature, its vibration. This is real. When something seems to move in your peripheral vision, turn slowly and look directly. If it resolves into a creature—it was real. If it dissolves—it was your visual cortex improvising.

### The Scale of the Unknown

You will not understand what is happening here. The creatures' purpose, the glow's nature, the source of the deep thrumming, the significance of the chains descending into the dark—none of this will be resolved during your visit. You are walking into an ongoing process that has been operating for longer than you've been alive and will continue after you leave. You are not a participant. You are not even an observer in any meaningful sense—you are a brief irrelevance in a system optimized for something else entirely.

This can produce a profound sense of insignificance that some people find liberating and others find annihilating. Know which type you are before you go. If the idea of standing in a place that does not need you, will not acknowledge you, and will not yield its meaning to you fills you with distress rather than wonder, reconsider the visit. The Roost does not care about your need for narrative. It is not a puzzle waiting to be solved. It is a process, ongoing, and you can watch it for a while, and then you leave.

### Resist the Urge to Test

You will want to touch a creature. You will want to put your hand near a tympanum to feel the vibration. You will want to shine a light directly into the glow. You will want to drop a stone into one of the dark gaps to hear how far it falls. You will want to pluck one of the glowing filaments. You will want to hum at the thrumming frequency and see if the creatures respond.

Resist all of this. At least at first. On a first visit, do nothing. Change nothing. Introduce nothing. Watch, listen, feel, record passively, and leave. If you must interact—and I understand the temptation, because some of these experiments would be genuinely illuminating—save it for a return visit, when you've had time to process your observations and form hypotheses worth testing. A first visit spent in purely passive observation is worth more than a hundred visits spent triggering unknown responses in a system you don't understand.

## Logistics and Access

The description gives no information about how to reach the Roost. You're "already on" the massif, "already near the top." This implies either that your multiverse transit deposits you directly at or near the tooth, or that there is an ascent route you've already completed and the description begins at the top.

### If You Arrive Directly at the Roost

You have no acclimatization. You have no knowledge of descent routes. You are instantly at extreme altitude in a challenging environment. **Time-limit your visit aggressively.** Two hours maximum on a first visit, less if symptoms of altitude sickness appear (headache, nausea, confusion, ataxia). You need to be capable of operating your transit method (whatever it is) under impaired cognitive conditions to get yourself out.

### If You Must Climb

The spire is basalt, young and sharp-edged, with hexagonal columnar jointing. Columnar basalt is among the best natural climbing surfaces—the columns provide regular features, cracks between them accept gear, and the hexagonal cross-sections offer good hand- and footholds. But young basalt is also brittle. Holds can snap off. Cooling joints can widen unexpectedly under load. And the condensation means everything is wet.

Bring a full trad climbing rack if a technical ascent is required. Cams sized for the cooling joints (which are probably in the 1–4 inch range). Long slings—the columns may require creative anchor-building. Helmets mandatory—loose basalt from above is a constant risk on young volcanic formations.

### Duration and Timing

If you can choose when to visit:

- **Go during the colony's sleeping period,** which appears to be most of the time. The description portrays a largely dormant colony with occasional individual movement. This is optimal for a first visit—fewer active creatures means fewer variables.
- **Go when the sky beyond the fracture is at its darkest.** Counterintuitive, but hear me out: if you arrive during brighter conditions, the contrast between the lit opening and the dark interior will destroy your dark adaptation. You'll see the sky and nothing else. If you arrive when the sky is already dim (as described—the "deep bruised indigo of altitude twilight"), your eyes will adapt to the overall low light level and you'll see more of the interior.
- **Don't stay through a full light cycle.** You don't know what the colony does when conditions change. An awakening, a departure, a feeding event—any mass movement of a thousand creatures in a space where you're standing on narrow stone pillars above dark gaps would be extremely dangerous.

## Final Counsel

When you arrive, you will step onto the column-tops and feel the thrumming come up through your feet and you will want, immediately, to understand. Your mind will reach for categories: *what are these creatures? what is the glow? what does the sound mean? what is below?* And none of these questions will be answered.

Let them go. Not permanently—carry them home with you, turn them over, build hypotheses—but for the duration of the visit, let them go. Stand on the basalt. Feel the hum in your sternum. Let your eyes adjust. Watch the condensation bead and tremble on the column-tops, each tiny pool a dark lens shivering with a frequency that began before you arrived. Watch a single creature open its tympanum and hold that bead of moisture trembling at its center, and watch it close again, and be still.

You are inside the tooth of a young volcanic spire at the edge of the sky, surrounded by a thousand beings engaged in a process of deep mineral listening that you cannot comprehend, and the correct response to this is not analysis. Not yet. First, the correct response is attention. Full, patient, undivided attention. The kind these creatures pay to the vibration from below—with maximum surface area, pressed flat against the world, absorbing what comes.

Then you go home and think about it for the rest of your life.
