# Preparation Guide for The Engines Beneath the Canopy

---

## First Principles: Understanding What You Are Entering

Before any discussion of gear, supplies, or strategy, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because the nature of the environment will dictate every decision, and most of the standard frameworks for expedition planning will fail you in specific and dangerous ways.

You are not entering a forest. You are not entering a cave. You are not entering a machine shop. You are entering the interior of a living organism that has replaced the geological and atmospheric substrate of an entire region, one that operates on industrial principles but at biological timescales, and one that has no fauna—meaning it has no conceptual or chemical framework for tolerating your presence, but also no predatory response to it. You are, in the most literal sense, an irrelevance to it. This is both your greatest safety and your greatest danger: it will not hunt you, but it will also not accommodate you. It will grow through the space you occupy if you stay still long enough. It will seal the path you walked. It will recycle the air you need into metabolic processes you cannot interrupt.

You are a bubble of emptiness moving through a world that abhors emptiness. Prepare accordingly.

## Thermal Management

### The Problem

The ambient temperature is approximately 45°C (113°F) with extremely high humidity—the description specifies that moisture is held in the air "the way a furnace holds smoke." This is a wet-bulb scenario that sits very close to the threshold of human survivability. At 45°C with high humidity, your body's evaporative cooling mechanism—sweating—ceases to function effectively. The air is already so saturated that sweat will not evaporate, or will evaporate so slowly that it cannot offset metabolic heat production plus environmental heat gain. This is not a "drink more water" problem. This is a "your core temperature will rise until you die" problem if unmanaged.

The metabolic heat from the wood-floor adds a radiant component from below that most hot-environment gear does not account for, since it's typically designed for overhead solar radiation.

### The Solution

You need active cooling. Passive systems—breathable fabrics, shade-seeking, rest cycling—will buy you time but will not solve the fundamental thermodynamic problem of a body producing roughly 100 watts of heat at rest in an environment that cannot absorb it.

**Primary cooling system:** A closed-loop liquid cooling garment (LCG), similar to those worn under spacesuits or by racing drivers. This is a network of thin tubes sewn into a base layer, circulating chilled water across your skin. The heat exchanger and pump can be carried in a backpack-mounted unit. The critical question is the heat sink: where does the absorbed heat go? In a normal environment you'd reject it to ambient air via a radiator, but the ambient air here is already 45°C and humid, so a simple radiator won't work efficiently.

Options for heat rejection:
- **Phase-change heat sinks:** Packs of material (paraffin wax blends, salt hydrates) that absorb heat by melting. These are heavy but reliable and require no power for the heat-absorption phase. A system carrying 10-15 kg of phase-change material could provide 4-6 hours of cooling before needing replacement or recharging. You'd need to carry multiples or have a way to re-solidify them, which requires access to a cold sink you won't have.
- **Thermoelectric (Peltier) cooling:** Electrically powered heat pumps that can move heat against the gradient—pulling heat from your skin-side coolant and rejecting it at a higher temperature to ambient air. These consume significant electrical power (figure 200-400 watts for meaningful body cooling) and generate waste heat that makes the area around the rejection side even hotter. But they work continuously as long as you have power.
- **Evaporative assist with carried water:** If you carry purified water and use it as an expendable evaporative coolant (not for the LCG loop, but sprayed onto an external radiator surface in controlled amounts), you can get evaporative cooling even in humid air by using the water at a rate faster than the ambient humidity can suppress evaporation. This is wasteful of water but effective. One liter of water evaporated removes approximately 2.4 MJ of heat.

**My recommendation:** A hybrid system. Peltier-cooled LCG as the primary, powered by high-density lithium batteries (plan for 300W continuous draw), supplemented by phase-change packs as backup/supplement, with a small reserve of expendable water for emergency evaporative cooling if the electrical system fails. Carry enough battery capacity for the duration of your planned visit plus a 50% margin. This is heavy. Accept that. The alternative is hyperthermia.

**Footwear:** The floor is warm. Standard boots will transmit this heat into your feet, which are vascular and sensitive to overheating. Insulated soles—not just thick, but actively insulating, with aerogel or multi-layer reflective barriers—are essential. Think of what you'd wear to walk on hot sand, then double it, then add the requirement for it to also grip a surface described as dense-wood-like-stone that may be slightly damp with cambial fluid. Vibram-type soles with thermal barriers. Custom work, probably.

### Clothing Choices Beyond Cooling

Wear the LCG as a base layer. Over it, you want something that protects against:
- **Contact with resinous surfaces:** The spiral grooves on the piston-trunks "glisten with some dark, resinous lubricant." Pipeline junctions exude cambial fluid. Root-hairs are damp. You will brush against all of these. The chemical composition of these biological fluids is unknown. They could be mildly caustic, allergenic, or simply impossibly sticky. A smooth, non-porous outer layer—a light shell jacket and pants of a material like siliconized nylon or a Tyvek-type fabric—will let you shed contact with unknown biologicals. It should be loose enough to not compress the LCG tubes beneath.
- **Mechanical abrasion:** You'll be ducking under pipelines, squeezing between root-buttresses, possibly crawling. The environment is described as densely packed. A durable outer layer that can take scraping against mineralized wood surfaces without tearing.
- **The root-hairs:** These "hang from the ceiling-branches in curtains so dense they look like fur" and are described as damp, seeking, and capable of withdrawing when they detect you are not wood. This is unsettling but suggests chemical sensing on contact. You want a barrier between your skin and these root-hairs not because they're dangerous (they withdraw) but because repeated chemical interrogation by an unknown biological sensor system is not something you want happening on bare skin for hours. Cover your neck, your arms, your hands.

Gloves: lightweight, dextrous, chemical-resistant. Nitrile-coated work gloves or thin leather with a sealant. You'll be touching things constantly for balance and navigation.

## Atmosphere and Respiration

### The Problem

The description does not explicitly say the atmosphere is toxic or oxygen-depleted, but everything about the environment implies you should not trust it. You are inside a massive living organism that:
- Conducts intense metabolic processes (the "industry" of wood fabrication, the peristaltic pumps, the lathes)
- Has no photosynthesis occurring at the floor level (no light penetrates—photosynthesis, if it occurs at all, happens at the canopy surface far above)
- Produces metabolic heat sufficient to maintain 45°C
- Has eliminated all animal life from its domain—possibly through chemical exclusion

The metabolic activity of wood growth at this scale would consume oxygen and produce CO2. In an enclosed space with no photosynthetic oxygen replenishment from below, CO2 levels could be elevated—potentially dangerously so. Additionally, anaerobic processes in the deep wood strata might release methane, hydrogen sulfide, or volatile organic compounds. The resinous lubricants on the machines could off-gas terpenes or other volatiles at 45°C in concentrations far beyond what you'd encounter in any normal forest.

The fact that no animals exist here is a red flag. It might be physical exclusion. It might be that the atmosphere is subtly incompatible with animal respiration—not acutely lethal, but chronically hostile. Elevated CO2 at, say, 3-5% would be survivable short-term but would cause headaches, confusion, and eventual incapacitation. You might not notice the onset.

### The Solution

**Carry breathing gas.** Full stop.

Not a filter mask—a filter mask removes particulates and certain chemical contaminants but does not add oxygen or remove CO2 below a certain concentration. You need a supplied-atmosphere system.

**Option 1: Self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA).** Compressed air tanks on your back, full face mask, standard firefighter/industrial configuration. Pros: completely independent of ambient atmosphere, well-understood technology. Cons: heavy (a 45-minute rated cylinder weighs about 11 kg), limited duration, bulky in a space where you're threading between obstacles.

**Option 2: Closed-circuit rebreather.** Recirculates your exhaled air through a CO2 scrubber (soda lime canister), adding supplemental oxygen from a small O2 cylinder. Duration: 2-4 hours per scrubber canister, much longer O2 supply per unit weight than open-circuit SCBA. Cons: more complex, requires training to use safely, failure modes are less obvious (CO2 breakthrough can cause unconsciousness without warning).

**Option 3: Ambient air with supplemental O2 and CO2 monitoring.** Wear a face mask with a powered air-purifying setup that also feeds supplemental O2 from a small bottle, regulated by real-time O2 and CO2 sensors. If ambient O2 is above 19.5% and CO2 is below 1%, you breathe ambient air with minimal supplementation. If conditions deteriorate, the system increases O2 flow and alerts you. If conditions are acutely dangerous, you switch to a small emergency SCBA bottle and evacuate.

**My recommendation:** Option 3 for primary use, with a small emergency SCBA bottle (15-minute rating) as bailout. The ambient atmosphere may be perfectly breathable—the description doesn't indicate anyone dying immediately upon arrival—but you need to verify this continuously rather than assume it. Carry redundant gas sensors. CO2 sensors, O2 sensors, and a broad-spectrum VOC detector. Mount them on your chest where they sample the air you're actually breathing. Set alarms conservatively.

Also: the "thin haze" in the air is particulate. At 45°C, in an environment where cellulose particles fall from active lathes and resinous compounds are being aerosolized from machine surfaces, you're breathing wood dust and volatile terpenes even if the bulk gas composition is fine. A P100 particulate filter layer on whatever breathing system you use. Your lungs will thank you.

## Hydration and Nutrition

### The Problem

There is no accessible water. The entire hydraulic cycle has been internalized into xylem channels "the diameter of railway tunnels." The moisture in the air is a thin haze at 45°C—functionally steam, not drinking water. The root-hairs capture ambient moisture before it can condense. You cannot tap the pipelines without potentially breaching a pressurized biological system of unknown chemistry (and the organism might respond to damage—the description mentions it "heals faster than it can be cut").

At 45°C with high humidity, even with active cooling, you will sweat. Your water loss rate will be 1-2 liters per hour at moderate activity levels, potentially more. Without replacement, you will be critically dehydrated within 3-4 hours.

### The Solution

**Carry all your water.** Every drop. Do not plan to source it locally.

For a 12-hour visit (which I'd recommend as an absolute maximum for a first expedition): 12-15 liters of water, plus an additional 5 liters for the evaporative cooling backup system. That's 17-20 kg of water alone.

This is where the weight budget starts to become genuinely frightening. Between cooling systems, breathing apparatus, and water, you're already looking at 35-45 kg of carried weight before we even discuss navigation, documentation, shelter, or contingency supplies.

**Solutions to the weight problem:**
- **Reduce visit duration.** A 4-6 hour visit requires 6-8 liters of water (plus cooling reserve), saving 10+ kg.
- **Establish a base camp at the entry point** with cached supplies, making multiple shorter forays rather than one long traverse.
- **Use a travois, sled, or wheeled cart** adapted for the terrain. The floor is dense wood, relatively smooth in some areas, which might support wheels—but the root-buttresses, pipeline crossings, and shifting topology make wheeled transport unreliable. A sled with broad runners might work better, sliding over the smooth wood-stone surface.
- **Accept a support team.** This is not a place for solo exploration. Two or three people can distribute the weight burden meaningfully, and the safety implications of solo travel in a shifting, lightless, hot environment are severe.

**Nutrition:** For a single-day visit, high-calorie, compact, heat-stable food. Energy gels, nuts, dried fruit, dense calorie bars. Nothing that requires water to prepare. Nothing that generates significant packaging waste—you carry everything out, and you don't want to leave anything the organism might encounter and respond to. Eat small amounts frequently to maintain energy without diverting blood to digestion during thermal stress.

**Water purification as emergency backup:** A small portable purifier (pump or UV type) is worth carrying in case you find a way to collect condensation from root-hairs or ambient humidity, but do not plan on this as a primary source. The chemistry of the moisture here is unknown—it may contain metabolic waste products, dissolved resins, or chemical signals that the organism uses for coordination. You don't want to drink its neurotransmitters.

## Navigation

### The Problem

This is one of the most navigationally hostile environments conceivable. Consider:

1. **No sky, no sun, no stars, no GPS.** Magnetic compass may work but gives you only bearing, not position. There are no visible landmarks at distance because visibility is limited to the range of bioluminescent glow—perhaps 40-50 meters in good patches, much less in dark zones.

2. **The terrain changes.** Paths that existed an hour ago are blocked by new growth. Root-buttresses emerge. Pipelines reroute. The floor tilts. Any fixed-reference navigation system (marked trails, cairns, breadcrumb trails) is unreliable because the surface you marked may have physically moved or been overgrown.

3. **The space is three-dimensional.** You may need to climb over pipelines, duck under branch-plates, navigate bore-shafts that descend into deeper strata. A 2D map is insufficient.

4. **There is no reliable "edge."** The organism extends to "the limit of the dim bioluminescent glow and beyond, fading into a blackness populated by sounds." There may be no clear boundary you can use as a reference.

### The Solution

**Inertial navigation.** A dead-reckoning system that tracks your position relative to your starting point using accelerometers and gyroscopes. Modern MEMS-based inertial measurement units (IMUs) are small, lightweight, and reasonably accurate over the distances you're likely to cover. Carry two independent units for redundancy. Accept that accumulated drift error will grow over time—over a 6-hour traverse, you might accumulate 50-100 meters of position error, which is manageable if you're staying within a few hundred meters of your entry point.

**Lifeline system.** A physical tether back to your entry point. Use a thin, high-strength cord (Dyneema, 2mm diameter, rated to several hundred kg) on a reel. Pay it out as you move. It doesn't need to be taut—it will drape over obstacles, run along the floor, possibly get incorporated into new growth if you leave it too long—but it gives you a physical, followable connection to your exit. Carry 500 meters minimum on a lightweight spool. This is your most critical navigation tool.

**The issue with the lifeline:** New growth. If you leave a cord in place for hours, the organism may grow around it, through it, incorporate it. The root-hairs will investigate it chemically, find it is not wood, and probably leave it alone—but the shifting floor could bury it, a new pipeline could route across it, a root-buttress could pin it. Check your line frequently. If it becomes immovable, you need to have marked enough of your route by other means to navigate back without it.

**Supplementary marking:** Carry glow sticks or small LED markers that you can affix to stable structures (piston-trunks are your best bet—they're large, persistent features that move vertically but don't relocate horizontally). Chemical light sticks in non-natural colors (blue, red) will stand out against the amber-green bioluminescence. Place them at decision points—where you changed direction, where you chose one gap between obstacles over another. Retrieve them on your way back to minimize trace.

**Depth awareness:** A barometric altimeter will tell you if you're descending into deeper strata or climbing toward the canopy. Even small elevation changes matter in an environment where "down" takes you into deeper, potentially hotter, potentially more oxygen-depleted wood-mass, and "up" takes you into denser canopy with no room to move. Keep track of your vertical position relative to your starting elevation.

**Mapping in real time:** A head-mounted or chest-mounted LIDAR unit can build a 3D point-cloud map of your surroundings as you move, which can be reviewed on a wrist-mounted display. Even though the environment changes, the large-scale features (piston-trunks, major pipeline routes) probably persist for days or weeks. A LIDAR map combined with inertial tracking gives you a moving picture of where you are relative to where you've been. This is power-hungry equipment—budget for it in your battery planning.

## Lighting

### The Problem

Near-total darkness with only intermittent bioluminescent patches providing ambient light. The bioluminescence is described as amber-green and patterned—it pulses, propagates, and may be part of the organism's signaling system. You need to see, but you also need to consider whether bright artificial light will provoke a response from a system that uses light as information.

### The Solution

**Primary: Headlamp with adjustable output.** Red or amber LEDs at low power for general navigation (preserves night vision, less likely to interfere with bioluminescent signaling, less likely to trigger phototropic growth responses). White LED at full power for detailed inspection of specific features. Keep white light use brief and targeted.

**Secondary: Diffuse area lighting.** A small lantern clipped to your pack or chest, emitting dim red light in all directions, giving you spatial awareness of the obstacle field around you. In a space this cluttered, you need peripheral spatial awareness more than you need a focused beam.

**Tertiary: UV light source.** Carry a small UV flashlight. Biological materials often fluoresce in UV in ways that reveal structures invisible in visible light—fungal hyphae, chemical deposits, moisture films, active growth zones. A UV scan of your surroundings could reveal which surfaces are actively growing (and thus potentially mobile), which are dormant, and where chemical activity is highest. This is a diagnostic tool as much as an illumination tool.

**Concern: phototaxis and phototropism.** The organism has no evolutionary experience with artificial light at this level. Its internal signaling system uses dim bioluminescence. A bright white headlamp might register as a signal—an anomalous one—and trigger some response. This could be benign (local brightening of bioluminescent patches as the system tries to "answer") or it could be problematic (accelerated growth toward the light source, root-hairs becoming more aggressive in their chemical investigation of the light-emitting area). Observe how the bioluminescence responds to your lights in the first few minutes. If you see coordinated changes in pulsing patterns correlating with your light use, reduce intensity and shift to red.

## Tools and Interaction Protocol

### What to Bring

**Sampling equipment:** If you intend to study this environment (and how could you not?), bring sealed sample containers, sterile swabs, small core-sampling tools, and airtight bags. Take samples of: the floor-wood (different layers if accessible), cambial fluid, resinous lubricant from piston-trunks, root-hair tissue, bioluminescent fungal patches, ambient air, and condensation from root-hairs. Label everything with position, time, and local conditions (temperature, perceived humidity, proximity to machines).

**Be aware that sampling is damage, and the organism responds to damage.** It "heals faster than it can be cut." A core sample into the floor may provoke a localized growth response—fresh cambial tissue flowing to seal the wound, possibly faster than you expect. This is unlikely to be dangerous to you physically (the growth rate, while fast for wood, is still measured in minutes to hours, not seconds) but it means your sample site will be altered immediately, and the organism's signaling system may flag the damage event with a bioluminescent pulse, drawing whatever distributed attention the system has to your location. Take samples quickly, don't return to the same site repeatedly, and observe the wound-healing response from a respectful distance—it's one of the most informative things you'll witness.

**A good knife and a small saw.** Not for combat—nothing here fights back in any conventional sense—but for cutting through soft new growth that blocks your path, for freeing your lifeline cord if it gets pinched by a shifting root-buttress, for emergency self-extraction if you become physically trapped by growing wood. The saw should be carbide-tipped; the mineralized wood may dull standard steel rapidly.

**Do not bring fire.** Do not bring anything that produces open flame, sparks, or extreme heat. You are inside a structure made of incredibly dense, resin-impregnated wood. The air contains aerosolized terpenes and cellulose particles. The humidity might make ignition difficult, or the resin content might make it horrifyingly easy. Either way, a fire in this environment would be catastrophic—not just to you but to the structure around you, potentially triggering a burn front through the canopy layers that could propagate for kilometers. Chemical light only. Battery-powered heat sources only. No pyrotechnics, no signal flares.

### How to Behave

**Move slowly.** Not just because the terrain demands it, but because the organism's sensory systems—whatever they are—appear to operate on slow timescales. The root-hairs investigated you and withdrew. If you move slowly and predictably, you register as a temporary anomaly. If you move quickly, bang into things, break structures, and generate vibrations, you may register as a mechanical event—a falling branch-plate, a pipeline breach—and trigger a response. You don't know what that response looks like and you don't want to find out.

**Touch things gently.** Use the backs of your gloved fingers first. Feel the temperature, the texture, the dampness. If something pulses or moves under your touch, remove your hand and observe. The valve-junctions "click softly like a room full of old clocks"—these are mechanical systems in operation, and putting your hand inside one could result in a painful compression injury as wood-valves close on your fingers.

**Do not block pipelines or impede piston-trunks.** These are infrastructure. A piston-trunk descending with "glacial inevitability" will exert immense force—it's a column of wood eight meters in diameter being driven by osmotic pressure on a geological scale. You cannot stop it. You would not slow it. You would be incorporated. Maintain a minimum distance of two meters from any piston-trunk, and if you feel the floor vibration change character near one, move away immediately—it may be changing direction.

**Do not stay in one place too long.** The description is explicit: "There is no place to sit that will be the same shape when you stand." The floor grows. New root-buttresses emerge. If you sit down and rest for an hour, the wood may begin to deposit around you—not aggressively, but as part of its normal process of filling space. Your boots might become slightly embedded. Your pack might get a thin film of cambial tissue growing across its bottom surface. You are a void, and voids get filled. Take rest breaks standing, leaning against a piston-trunk (monitoring for movement), or sitting on your pack on a smooth section of floor—and check every 10-15 minutes that you can still move freely.

## Psychological Preparation

This is not optional. This is as important as the cooling system.

### What the Place Will Do to Your Mind

**Claustrophobia amplification.** You are in an enclosed space with no visible exit, no sky, and walls that are alive and growing closer. Even if you are not normally claustrophobic, the combination of darkness, heat, physical constriction, and the knowledge that the space around you is actively shrinking will challenge your psychological equilibrium. The sound environment—constant clicking, groaning, sub-bass vibration—adds a layer of low-grade anxiety that accumulates over hours.

**Temporal disorientation.** No day/night cycle, no external time cues, no changes in lighting that correspond to time passage. The organism's activity is continuous. After a few hours, you may lose track of how long you've been inside. Set regular timer alerts on your wrist device—every 30 minutes—to anchor your time awareness.

**The uncanny absence of life.** You are a biological organism in a space devoid of other biological organisms that resemble you in any way. No animals, no insects, no birdsong, no rustling of creatures. The only "life" is vegetable, slow, and utterly alien in its logic. This produces a specific kind of loneliness that is different from isolation in a desert or on an ocean—it's the loneliness of being the wrong kind of thing entirely, of standing in a world that has no concept of you and no category for you. Some travelers report this as peaceful. Others report it as deeply distressing. Know which type you are before you go.

**The awareness of being inside something alive.** This one is insidious and builds over time. The first hour, you see a fascinating environment. The second hour, you notice that the environment has changed since you arrived. The third hour, you realize that the changes are not random but purposeful—the organism is *doing* something, *building* something, executing a plan you cannot read. The fourth hour, it hits you that you are inside the plan. Not as a participant, not as an obstacle—as a nothing. An irrelevancy inside an intelligence (or at least a purposefulness) so vast and so slow that your entire visit occupies less of its attention than a single cell replacement occupies yours. This realization can be either transcendent or terrifying. Be ready for either.

### Mitigation Strategies

- **Bring a companion.** Human conversation, human presence, human scale. Someone to talk to, to check on, to confirm that you are real and this is real and you will leave.
- **Bring familiar sensory anchors.** A specific scent (peppermint oil on a cloth, sealed until needed), a piece of music on earbuds (one ear only—you need to hear the environment), a photograph you can look at. Something from your world to remind you that your world exists.
- **Set hard turnaround times.** "We leave in four hours regardless." No negotiation, no "just a bit further." Discipline on this point prevents the creeping extension of stay that leads to exhaustion, dehydration, and psychological deterioration.
- **Debrief afterward.** Talk about what you saw and felt with someone who was there, or with someone who understands multiverse travel. The experience will sit strangely in your memory—the sensory totality of it, the heat, the dark, the sound—and it benefits from being narrated, structured, turned into a story you're telling rather than an experience that happened to you.

## Medical Preparation

**Heat illness protocol:** Know the signs of heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, dizziness, headache) and heat stroke (high body temperature, altered mental state, dry skin or profuse sweating, rapid heartbeat). Carry a core temperature monitor (ingestible capsule type or temporal artery sensor). If your core temp reaches 39°C, begin emergency cooling and prepare to evacuate. At 40°C, evacuate immediately.

**Respiratory monitoring:** Pulse oximeter on your finger or ear, continuous reading. If SpO2 drops below 94%, increase supplemental O2 flow. Below 90%, switch to emergency SCBA and move toward exit.

**Allergic reaction preparation:** You are exposed to novel biological compounds—resins, saps, fungal spores, volatile chemicals. Carry injectable epinephrine (two auto-injectors), antihistamines (oral and injectable), and corticosteroids. An allergic reaction in this environment, where evacuation may take an hour or more, could be fatal without immediate pharmacological intervention.

**Wound care:** Any skin break in this environment is a concern. The ambient air is full of biological particulates, and the warm, humid conditions are ideal for infection. Carry wound closure strips, topical antibiotics, and sterile dressings. Clean any wound immediately, even a scratch. The organism's cambial fluid is not sterile—it's a rich biological medium.

**Compression injuries:** The piston-trunks, the valve-junctions, the shifting floor—this environment has multiple mechanisms for trapping or crushing a limb. Carry a basic splinting kit and know how to perform self-rescue from a pinned position. A compact hydraulic spreader tool (like a small rescue jack) might be justifiable weight given the environment.

## Entry and Exit Planning

### How You Get In

This varies by multiverse transit method. The critical considerations for your arrival point:

- **Arrive at the periphery, not the center.** The description mentions "margins of the world" with lowlands. There may be a transition zone where the organism thins—where the canopy opens slightly, where the floor-strata are shallower, where the temperature is lower and the air more breathable. Arriving here gives you a gentler introduction and an easier exit.
- **Mark your entry point indelibly.** Multiple methods: physical (driven metal stakes into the floor, which will resist the organism's growth response longer than organic materials), chemical (a scent marker that your instruments can detect at distance), electronic (a radio beacon, though range will be very limited in this RF-absorbing environment). The lifeline anchors here.
- **Confirm atmospheric safety before removing your transit apparatus.** Take 5 minutes at the entry point with all sensors running before you commit to walking in.

### How You Get Out

**The same way you came in.** Follow the lifeline. There is no "other exit." The organism extends in every direction without end. You cannot go up (the canopy heals faster than you can cut). You cannot go down (deeper strata are hotter, denser, less breathable). You go back to where you started.

**If the lifeline fails:** Follow your inertial navigation bearing back to the entry point. Use your LIDAR map to cross-reference your position. Look for your glow-stick markers. If all navigation fails, stop moving, conserve resources, and activate any emergency beacon you've brought—though in an RF-absorbing wood environment, a beacon's range may be measured in tens of meters rather than kilometers.

**Dead-man protocol:** Before entering, leave a time-triggered alert with someone outside. "If we haven't returned by [time], assume we are incapacitated at a location along the lifeline route." An outside rescue team can follow the lifeline cord to find you—but they need to know the cord exists and where it's anchored, and they need to be equipped for the same environment.

## What to Expect Emotionally When You Return

You will step out of the organism and feel the sky like a physical sensation. After hours in compressed, lightless, purposeful density, open air will seem implausible—too much nothing, too much space, too much light. You may feel agoraphobic. You may feel grief, which will surprise you. You will smell normal air and it will seem thin, lacking, insufficiently meaningful.

You will think about the machines for a long time afterward. The piston-trunks. The lathes. The valves clicking like clocks in the dark. The organism that ate a factory and became a factory and has been building itself, out of itself, for itself, for longer than your species has existed.

You will wonder what it's building.

You will not find out. But you will carry the question like a seed.

## Final Note

Go with respect. Go with preparation. Go with the understanding that you are visiting something that has had more time to become what it is than you can conceptually grasp, and that it did not invite you, and it will not miss you, and the space you occupied will be filled with new wood before your scent has faded from the root-hairs.

Go anyway. It is one of the most extraordinary things in any world.
