# Preparing for The Roaring Stillness

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

I want to be honest with you before we get into gear lists and procedures. This place is going to be one of the most psychologically demanding environments you will ever visit, and it has almost nothing to do with the physical dangers — though those are real and significant. The danger of this place is that it is *complete without you*. Every other hostile environment a traveler prepares for — volcanic worlds, vacuum, predator-heavy ecosystems — at least has the decency to be chaotic, to present problems that demand your attention and keep your mind engaged. The Roaring Stillness will not do this. It will present you with an environment that is perfectly stable, perfectly legible, and perfectly indifferent, and it will do so for every single second you are there, and the sound will never stop, and nothing will happen, and you will be the only thing in a world-sized ocean that is capable of panic.

Prepare for that first. Everything else is engineering.

## Physical Preparation

### The Pressure

You need to understand the pressure regime before anything else, because it dictates every piece of equipment you will bring.

This is a world-ocean with no surface. You are not diving *into* water from air — you are transiting directly into a liquid environment at whatever depth your entry point corresponds to. The reports suggest that the "habitable" zones — the mid-water layers where the singers reside, the ridge crests where the stations sit — exist at pressures ranging from roughly 200 to 2,000 atmospheres, depending on your position relative to the compressed-liquid core. For reference, the bottom of the Mariana Trench on Earth is about 1,100 atmospheres. You may be operating in that range or beyond it.

This means:

**No soft-suit diving.** You are not wearing a wetsuit or a flexible pressure suit into this environment. You need a rigid atmospheric diving system — a personal submersible, essentially — that maintains internal pressure at 1 atmosphere (or whatever your home-normal is) while the exterior bears the full hydrostatic load. Think of it less as "diving equipment" and more as "a small submarine you wear."

Your suit (and I'll keep calling it a suit, but think *vehicle*) needs to be rated for sustained operation at a minimum of 1,500 atmospheres with a safety margin that takes you to 2,500. This is not optional. The pressure here is omnidirectional and absolutely constant — there are no waves, no surges, no sudden changes — which actually makes engineering easier in some respects. You are designing against a static load, not a dynamic one. But the static load is enormous, and any structural failure is instant and total. You will not have a slow leak. You will have an implosion.

Materials: titanium-ceramic composites for the hull. Viewports made from synthetic sapphire or diamond-analogue materials, spherically curved to distribute load. Every seal, every joint, every pass-through for cables or manipulator arms needs to be pressure-tested to destruction before you trust it. Bring spares for every seal. Bring spares for the spares.

**Buoyancy management** is critical and tricky. In a normal ocean, you manage buoyancy relative to a surface — you go up, you go down, there's a clear reference frame. Here, there is no surface. "Up" is "away from the gravitational center of the world-ocean," and the density of the water changes in stratified layers based on salinity and pressure. You will pass through density boundaries where your buoyancy shifts abruptly. Your suit needs active ballast control — variable-volume tanks that can adjust your displacement in real time — and you need to understand that "neutral buoyancy" in one layer may mean "sinking like a stone" fifty kilometers deeper. Map the density layers before you commit to a depth. Use expendable probes dropped ahead of you.

### The Temperature

2°C, steady, everywhere, forever. This is actually one of the *easier* parameters to manage, but don't get complacent.

Your suit will lose heat to the surrounding water constantly. Water conducts heat roughly 25 times faster than air, and at 2°C, the thermal gradient between your body-temperature interior and the environment is significant. You need active heating — not just insulation, active thermal generation — and you need it to be redundant. If your primary heating system fails, you have hours before hypothermia sets in inside a well-insulated rigid suit, but "hours" is not "days," and your excursions here may last days or weeks.

Power source for heating: more on this below, but plan for a sustained thermal output of at least 500 watts continuous, with surge capacity for rewarming after any system interruption.

The cold also affects your equipment. Lubricants thicken. Battery chemistry slows. Seals contract microscopically. Everything you bring should be cold-rated and cold-tested. The good news is that the temperature doesn't fluctuate — you can calibrate for 2°C and trust that calibration — but 2°C is unforgiving if you haven't designed for it.

### The Sound

This is where preparation gets complicated, because the sound is simultaneously the defining feature of this world and the thing most likely to incapacitate you.

Let me describe what you're facing in acoustic terms. The singers produce infrasound — frequencies below 20 Hz, extending down into the sub-hertz range. Individually, each singer produces a single tone at modest amplitude. Collectively, hundreds of thousands of them, spread across a world-ocean that transmits sound at approximately 1,500 meters per second with negligible absorption, create a standing-wave field of extraordinary complexity and power. At antinodes — where constructive interference reinforces the tones — the sound pressure levels may exceed 190 dB re 1 μPa, which in water translates to energy densities that can vibrate solid structures.

You will feel this in your body. Through the hull of your suit. In your bones, in your organs, in your eyeballs. The description of vision shaking because your eyes vibrate in their sockets is not poetic exaggeration — at sufficient infrasound intensity, this is a documented physiological response. Other effects of high-intensity infrasound exposure include:

- Nausea and vertigo
- Chest pressure and difficulty breathing (the diaphragm resonates)
- Visual disturbance (blurred vision, phantom motion in peripheral vision)
- Anxiety, dread, and panic (infrasound stimulates fight-or-flight responses in humans — this is well-documented even at moderate levels on Earth)
- Cognitive impairment (difficulty concentrating, confusion, time distortion)
- At extreme levels: organ damage, internal hemorrhaging

**Your suit must incorporate active vibration damping.** This is not a luxury. Passive acoustic insulation — foam, air gaps, mass barriers — will attenuate higher frequencies but is largely ineffective against infrasound because the wavelengths are so long they pass through solid barriers as if they weren't there. You need active countermeasures: arrays of sensors that detect incoming pressure oscillations and actuators that generate equal-and-opposite vibrations in the hull. This is the same principle as noise-canceling headphones, scaled up to a submarine hull and extended into frequency ranges that most noise-canceling systems don't address.

Even with perfect active damping (which you won't have — the standing-wave field is complex, multi-frequency, and varies spatially), some infrasonic energy will reach your body. You should:

1. **Map the standing-wave field before you enter.** Use remote acoustic sensors deployed from your entry point to identify nodes (where tones cancel — these are your safe havens) and antinodes (where tones reinforce — avoid these, especially during initial acclimation). Plan your route to stay near nodes whenever possible.

2. **Wear an internal damping garment** beneath your suit — a close-fitting layer with embedded piezoelectric fibers that sense and counteract vibrations transmitted through the hull to your body. Think of it as a second, body-level noise-canceling system.

3. **Bring pharmaceutical countermeasures** for nausea, anxiety, and vestibular disruption. Anti-emetics, anxiolytics, vestibular suppressants. Do not be proud about this. The sound *will* affect you physiologically, and fighting through it unmedicated is not bravery, it is foolishness that will degrade your decision-making capacity in an environment where bad decisions kill you.

4. **Set strict exposure limits.** Even with all countermeasures, do not spend more than 6-8 hours in an antinode zone before retreating to a node for recovery. Cumulative infrasound exposure causes effects that persist after the exposure ends — fatigue, cognitive fog, emotional flatness. Build rest periods into your schedule. Real rest, in a node, with the damping systems at maximum.

5. **Prepare psychologically for the permanence of the sound.** Even at nodes, even with damping, you will hear/feel it. It will be there when you sleep. It will be there when you eat. It will not stop. Travelers who have endured sustained infrasound exposure report that the most distressing quality is not the intensity but the *constancy* — the human nervous system is wired to habituate to stimuli that change, and to remain vigilant toward stimuli that don't. A sound that never changes never lets your brain relax. You will be in a state of low-grade alertness that you cannot turn off, and over days, this is exhausting in a way that sleep does not fully repair. Know this going in. Accept it. Plan for shorter visits than you think you need.

### The Light

The white dwarf illumination is even, shadowless, and constant. There is no day-night cycle. The light penetrates deep into the clear water, creating an environment of uniform grey-white luminosity that extends for dozens of kilometers in every direction.

This will disorient you. Humans rely on light gradients — shadows, brightness variations, the direction of sunlight — for spatial orientation, depth perception, and circadian regulation. In The Roaring Stillness, you will have none of these cues. The water looks the same in every direction. There are no shadows to indicate the shape of objects. The light does not tell you which way is "up."

**Your suit must include:**

- An inertial navigation system with gyroscopic reference, so you always know your orientation relative to the gravitational center of the world-ocean. Mount a heads-up display that shows you a persistent artificial horizon, a gravity vector indicator, and your position relative to known landmarks (ridges, stations).

- Artificial circadian lighting inside the suit. Your interior illumination should cycle through warm/cool color temperatures and brightness levels on a 24-hour schedule (or whatever your home circadian rhythm is). This is essential for sleep regulation. Without it, your circadian clock will drift, your sleep will fragment, and your cognitive performance will degrade rapidly.

- Directional external lighting with adjustable beam angles. The ambient light is sufficient for observation over long distances, but for close work — examining station interiors, studying crawlers on ridge surfaces — you want focused illumination that *creates* shadows. Shadows are how you perceive texture, depth, and three-dimensionality. Working in shadowless ambient light is surprisingly disabling; everything looks flat, distances become ambiguous, and you will misjudge gaps, edges, and the positions of objects. A good spotlight restores your depth perception.

### Breathing and Life Support

Your suit maintains its own atmosphere. Standard considerations apply: oxygen supply, CO2 scrubbing, humidity control, trace contaminant removal. But plan for a longer consumables duration than you think you need, because:

1. There is no resupply source. The water here is extremely pure but it's *water* — you can't crack it for oxygen without significant power expenditure, and you may want to conserve power for heating and vibration damping.

2. Navigation is slow. Distances between points of interest (ridges, stations) are enormous — hundreds or thousands of kilometers. At any reasonable transit speed through water, you're looking at days of travel between landmarks. Your life support duration needs to cover your entire planned stay plus a generous margin for getting lost, getting stuck, or simply misjudging distances in an environment with no visual scale cues.

3. The psychological temptation to stay longer is real. This place is, despite everything, *fascinating*. The singers, the stations, the ridges — you will want to explore more, stay one more day, check one more site. Build in a hard abort time that you commit to before entry and do not negotiate with yourself about.

I'd recommend a minimum of 30 days of life support capacity for a planned 10-14 day visit. Yes, that's a large margin. Yes, you'll be glad you have it.

### Propulsion and Navigation

You need to move through water. How much water? Potentially thousands of kilometers of it between points of interest.

**For transit between distant sites** (ridges, stations): You need a vehicle. Not just a propulsion module bolted to your suit — a proper submersible with a hydrodynamic hull, efficient thrusters, and the endurance to cover hundreds of kilometers at speed. Something capable of 20-30 km/h sustained, with a range of at least 3,000 km. This is your lifeboat, your home, your reference frame. It carries your supplies, your backup systems, your communication equipment, and your sleeping quarters.

**For local exploration** (ridge surfaces, station interiors, singer observation): Your suit should have its own maneuvering thrusters — low-power, fine-control units that let you navigate tight spaces and hold station in a specific location. The absence of currents is actually a gift here; you won't fight water movement, and once you stop thrusting, you stay where you are (modulo the density-layer buoyancy considerations mentioned above).

**Navigation is the hard problem.** In an environment without surface features, without sun position, without magnetic poles (the world-ocean may or may not have a magnetic field — check before you go), and with visibility limited to "only" a few dozen kilometers in a world that spans hundreds of thousands of kilometers, you can get lost in a way that is genuinely, permanently fatal. Not "wandering for a while" lost — "*you will never find your way back to your entry point*" lost.

Mitigations:

- Inertial navigation, backed by multiple redundant systems. Gyroscopic drift is real; recalibrate regularly against known positions.
- Drop acoustic beacons as you travel. Small, battery-powered pingers at regular intervals that create a breadcrumb trail back to your entry point and your vehicle. In this world's acoustically transparent water, a well-designed beacon should be detectable at enormous ranges. Choose a frequency well above the singers' infrasound range so your beacons don't get lost in the ambient drone.
- Map the ridge lattice. The ridges are the closest thing to permanent geography. Their geometry follows pressure differentials, which means it's predictable if you understand the physics. Build a rough map of ridge positions early and navigate relative to them.
- Mark the stations. If you find one, tag it. These are irreplaceable reference points — the only artificial structures in the entire world.

## Equipment and Supplies

### The Suit

I've described the requirements above, but let me consolidate:

- Rigid atmospheric hull, titanium-ceramic composite, rated to 2,500 atm
- Synthetic sapphire viewports, spherical geometry
- Active vibration damping array (hull-mounted)
- Internal piezoelectric damping garment
- Active thermal regulation, 500W continuous with redundant systems
- Integrated life support: O2 supply, CO2 scrubbing, humidity and temperature control, 30-day endurance
- Heads-up display with artificial horizon, gravity vector, navigation data
- Circadian lighting system (interior)
- Directional external lighting with adjustable focus
- Maneuvering thrusters for local movement
- Manipulator arms for sample collection and interaction with station equipment
- Multiple redundant communication links to your main vehicle

### The Vehicle

- Hydrodynamic hull, same pressure rating as suit
- Sustained speed: 20-30 km/h
- Range: 3,000+ km
- Sleeping quarters (you need real sleep, in a real bed, in a space large enough to feel like a room, not a coffin — this is a psychological necessity, not a comfort preference)
- Extended life support reserves
- Acoustic beacon launcher
- Sensor suite: sonar (passive — listen for singer tones to map their positions; active — for ridge mapping and navigation), electromagnetic field detectors (for locating powered stations), water chemistry analyzers, pressure/temperature/salinity profiling instruments
- Sample storage (if you plan to bring anything back)
- A library. Music. Recorded human voices. Anything that *changes*. I'll come back to this.

### Power

Your power source needs to sustain heating, vibration damping, life support, propulsion, and instrumentation for up to 30 days without resupply. In a 2°C aqueous environment where solar power is unavailable and combustion is impossible, your options are:

- **Radioisotope thermoelectric generator (RTG):** Reliable, long-lived, no moving parts. The thermal gradient between the RTG's hot side and the 2°C water is excellent for efficiency. Produces constant power. Downside: heavy, and the radioactive source requires careful handling.
- **High-density fuel cells** with stored reactants: Higher power-to-weight ratio than RTGs but limited by fuel supply. Good for supplementing an RTG during high-demand periods (transit at speed, for example).
- **Note on the stations:** Some of them still have power. You *might* be able to tap into a station's reactor for recharging. But "might" is doing a lot of work in that sentence — you don't know the power output specifications, the connector standards, or the stability of reactors that have been running unattended for centuries. Treat station power as a bonus if you find it, not a plan.

### Medical

- Anti-emetics (for infrasound-induced nausea)
- Anxiolytics (for infrasound-induced panic and the ambient existential dread of this place — and yes, I'm listing those as separate line items)
- Vestibular suppressants
- Anti-inflammatory agents (infrasound exposure may cause low-grade tissue inflammation over time)
- Standard trauma kit (though frankly, if your suit is breached at these pressures, a trauma kit isn't going to help — this is more for injuries sustained inside the suit, bumps and strains from maneuvering)
- Sleep aids. The sound does not stop. You will have difficulty sleeping. Do not let this compound over days into a sleep deficit that degrades your judgment.
- Stimulants, for emergency use if sleep deprivation becomes critical despite sleep aids. Not a substitute for actual sleep.

### Psychological Supplies

This section is not optional. I am not being gentle or sentimental. The single most dangerous thing about The Roaring Stillness is what it does to a human mind over time, and I have thought about this carefully.

The problem is *constancy*.

Human beings are adapted for change. Our sensory systems are literally built to detect *differences* — edges, transitions, movements, the onset and cessation of stimuli. We get bored, and boredom is a survival mechanism: it tells us "nothing is happening here, go find something that is." Our emotional regulation depends on variation — the contrast between effort and rest, noise and quiet, company and solitude, engagement and disengagement.

The Roaring Stillness denies you all of this. The sound doesn't change. The light doesn't change. The temperature doesn't change. The view doesn't change (unless you're actively transiting, and even then, "clear water gives way to more clear water" is not exactly a rich visual stream). There are no other minds to interact with. The living things here do not react to you. The stations are abandoned and static. The ridges do not erode.

Over hours, this is eerie. Over days, it becomes psychologically corrosive. The reported effects track closely with what we know about sensory deprivation and monotony:

- Time distortion (hours feel like days, or days feel like hours — both are reported)
- Depersonalization (the sense of self begins to blur; you feel less like a person and more like another object suspended in the water)
- Intrusive thoughts (in the absence of external stimulation, the mind generates its own, and it doesn't always generate pleasant ones)
- Compulsive checking behaviors (re-reading instruments, re-checking seals, cycling through system diagnostics — your mind looks for *anything* that changes and latches onto it)
- Emotional flattening, followed by sudden intense emotional episodes (weeping, rage, euphoria — these come without warning and pass without resolution)
- A specific phenomenon that travelers to similar environments have described as "merging" — the feeling that you are dissolving into the environment, that the boundary between your body and the water is becoming permeable, that you are becoming part of the stillness. This is deeply unsettling, and for some people, deeply seductive. *Resist it.* It is a dissociative response to sensory deprivation, not a mystical experience, however much it feels like one.

**Countermeasures:**

1. **Bring things that change.** Music — lots of it, and varied. Audiobooks. Podcasts. Recorded conversations. Video, if your suit supports it. Anything with human voices, human laughter, human argument. Anything with *rhythm that varies*. The singers' rhythm does not vary. Your music must.

2. **Maintain a schedule.** Wake at the same time. Eat at the same times. Do maintenance checks at the same times. Exercise at the same time (yes, you need to exercise inside your suit — isometric routines, resistance bands, whatever your space allows). The schedule is your artificial day-night cycle, your imposed rhythm of change on a world that offers none.

3. **Keep a journal.** Write down what you see, what you think, what you feel. The act of writing forces you to observe, to differentiate, to notice things. More importantly, it creates a *record of change* — proof that time is passing, that you are having sequential experiences, that you are not frozen.

4. **Set tasks.** Do not go to this place to "just experience it." Have research objectives, sampling goals, station investigation plans, routes to follow. Give yourself problems to solve. An idle mind in The Roaring Stillness is a mind that begins to dissolve.

5. **Talk to yourself.** Out loud. Narrate what you're doing. Ask yourself questions and answer them. The sound of your own variable, responsive, *living* voice is an anchor to your own identity in a way you won't appreciate until you've been surrounded by an unchanging drone for 72 hours straight.

6. **Have an abort criterion for psychological distress, and honor it.** Before you go in, define the line: "If I experience X, I leave." X might be sustained depersonalization lasting more than an hour, or auditory hallucinations beyond the ambient sound, or the inability to remember what day it is, or the first time you catch yourself thinking that leaving doesn't matter. *Whatever line you set, do not move it once you're inside.* The environment will make you feel like the line was arbitrary, like you're fine, like you can handle a little more. The environment is wrong. The line was right. Leave.

## What to Expect, Phase by Phase

### Entry (Hours 1-4)

The transition from your transit environment into the water will be the most physically jarring moment of the trip. The sound hits you immediately. Even with full damping, even having read this briefing and knowing what to expect, the first minutes inside The Roaring Stillness will be overwhelming. The drone is *physical* — you will feel it in your chest, in your teeth, behind your eyes. Your body will interpret it as danger. Your heart rate will spike. You may feel the urgent, primal need to *leave*, to claw your way back out of the water, to get away from the sound.

This is normal. Breathe. Check your systems. Confirm your damping is active. Look at your instruments — they will show you that nothing is wrong, that the pressure is managed, that your hull is intact, that the sound, however enormous, is not damaging your suit. Trust your instruments. Do not trust your feelings for the first hour.

Take this phase slowly. Stay near your entry point. Acclimate. Let your body's panic response run its course and subside. It will subside — not fully, but enough. The human stress response is intense but unsustainable; after 20-30 minutes without actual harm occurring, it will dial back from "emergency" to "vigilant." That's where you'll live for the rest of the trip: vigilant. Low-grade unease. Manageable.

### Exploration (Days 1-7)

This is the productive phase, when the novelty of the environment is still doing some work to keep your mind engaged. Use it. Transit to the nearest ridge. Examine the clathrate formations up close — they are genuinely extraordinary, and the prismatic light effects through their crystal faces are unlike anything you've seen. Take samples if your mission calls for it. Find crawlers and observe them; their mechanical precision is mesmerizing in its own way, and they're the most "active" organisms you'll encounter.

Visit a station if one is within range. The interiors are haunting — flooded machine halls filled with equipment that still runs, still pumps, still monitors parameters that haven't changed. The gauge in the control room will fascinate you disproportionately. You will stare at it. You will check it repeatedly, hoping the needle has moved. It has not moved. It will not move.

If you can locate a singer, approach it carefully. They are vast and nearly invisible, and the sound intensity increases dramatically as you get closer to one. Your damping system will be working at maximum capacity. The experience of being near a singer — of feeling a single sustained tone emanating from a living body the size of several city blocks, a tone that has been sustained without interruption for longer than human civilization — is the closest this world comes to awe. It is worth the discomfort. But limit your exposure. Fifteen to twenty minutes near a singer, then retreat to a node. Do not push it.

### The Middle (Days 7-10)

This is the danger zone, psychologically. The novelty is spent. You've seen the ridges, the crawlers, the stations, the singers. You know what this world is. And it has not changed, and you know now — really know, in your body — that it will not change. The sound is still there. It was there when you woke up and it will be there when you fall asleep and it is there now, the same frequency, the same amplitude, the same eternal chord.

This is when the depersonalization begins, if it's going to. This is when the intrusive thoughts start. This is when you look at your schedule — "0800: System check. 0830: Transit to waypoint 7. 1200: Sample collection. 1400: Meal" — and it feels absurd, a child's pretend-game imposed on an eternity of nothing.

*Follow the schedule anyway.* This is what the schedule is *for*. Not for the days when it feels meaningful, but for the days when it feels pointless. The schedule is the structure that keeps you differentiated from the environment. The crawlers have been doing the same thing for millennia. The drifters have been pulsing at the same rate since they began. You are not them. You change. You *choose*. Prove it. Follow the schedule.

Play music. Loud music. Music with vocals and drums and unpredictable key changes. Sing along. Badly. The sound of your own bad singing is the most human thing in this entire world.

### Departure (Days 10-14)

Leave on schedule. Do not extend. You will feel, at this point, one of two things:

1. A desperate urgency to leave. Honor it. This is healthy. Your mind is telling you it has had enough of unchanging stimuli, and it is correct.

2. A strange reluctance. A feeling that leaving doesn't really matter, that you're fine here, that the stillness has a kind of peace to it, that maybe you could stay a little longer, what's the harm. **This is the more dangerous feeling.** It means the depersonalization has progressed further than you realized. It means the boundary between you and the environment is blurring. It means the Roaring Stillness is becoming *your* stillness, and you are beginning to match its frequency.

Leave. In either case, leave. Follow your beacon trail back to your entry point. Transit out. And when you emerge — into whatever noisy, chaotic, variable, *alive* environment your multiverse transit takes you to — you will feel the most extraordinary relief. The silence on the other side will be deafening in its own way. The *changefulness* of a normal world — wind, birdsong, shadows moving, temperatures shifting, people talking — will feel almost violent after the stillness. You may weep. Many travelers do. It's not sadness. It's the sound of a system that was locked in a single state finally being allowed to vary again.

## Final Notes

### What to Bring Back

Samples of the clathrate ice are scientifically valuable and reasonably stable if kept under pressure and temperature during transit. Water samples from different density layers are straightforward. Biological samples — tissue from drifters or crawlers — are more complex to preserve but worth attempting.

Do not attempt to bring back a singer. This should be obvious, but I'm listing it because the thought will occur to you, and in the cognitive haze of day eight, "obvious" becomes negotiable. The singers are the size of stadiums. They are also, as far as anyone can determine, the source of the world's fundamental acoustic architecture. Removing one would be like pulling a single string from a harp that has been playing the same chord for geological time. The ethical and ecological implications are staggering, and besides, you have no way to transport it.

The stations contain artifacts — equipment, instruments, the gauge. Some of these could be removed. But consider: these stations have been running, unattended, in perfect equilibrium with their environment for what may be centuries or millennia. They are the evidence of a civilization that came here, built, extracted, stored, and left. They are the only record of intention in a world that otherwise contains only persistence. Taking things from them is not just sampling — it's disrupting the only narrative this world has. Photograph. Scan. Map. Measure. Leave the gauge where it is. It's pointing to a value that hasn't changed, and it should go on pointing to it.

### What Not to Do

Do not attempt to communicate with the singers. They are not communicating. They are resonating. There is nothing to say back.

Do not attempt to alter the clathrate ridges, even for sampling, without understanding the pressure dynamics. Clathrate ice is metastable — it exists because the pressure and temperature hold it in its lattice state. If you breach a formation and release the trapped methane, the local pressure change could destabilize a cascade. On a small scale, this means a sudden gas release that could throw your suit violently. On a larger scale — well, the ridges have been stable for a very long time, and you do not want to be the variable that changes that.

Do not stay at an antinode "just to see what it's like at full intensity." You will regret it. The description of bones humming and vision shaking is for moderate antinodes. At strong antinodes, the sound pressure is sufficient to cause disorientation so severe that you cannot read your instruments, nausea so intense that you cannot function, and — at the extreme end — mechanical vibration of your suit hull that could stress seals beyond their design tolerance. The sound is not hostile, but it does not need to be hostile to kill you. It just needs to be *strong enough*, and at the worst antinodes, it is.

Do not turn off your circadian lighting to "experience the real light of this world." You will lose track of time. You will lose track of time *faster than you think possible*. In uniform, shadowless grey-white light with no temporal cues, the human sense of duration collapses within hours. Travelers who have tried this report losing entire days — not sleeping through them, but *being conscious and unable to account for them*. Keep your artificial day-night cycle running. Always.

### A Note on the Builders

You will wonder about them. In the flooded corridors of the stations, running your gloved manipulator along the furred surfaces of machines that are still working, reading the unchanging gauge, you will wonder: who built these? Why? What were they extracting? Where did they go?

You will not find answers. The stations contain no text, no signage, no personal artifacts, no biological traces. They are pure function — machines and structures and plumbing, with nothing extraneous, nothing decorative, nothing communicative. They were built by minds that either did not think to leave messages or did not think messages were necessary. Perhaps they expected to come back. Perhaps they are still coming, on timescales that make your visit a flicker too brief to notice.

The storage tanks are full. Whatever was being extracted and refined, it's still there, waiting. The pumps are still running. The turbines are still turning. The gauge is still reading the same value.

It has always read this value.

### The Last Thing

When you are deep in the mid-water, far from any ridge or station, suspended in perfectly clear, perfectly cold, perfectly still water that extends in every direction farther than you can see, and the sound is passing through you as it has passed through everything in this world since the world began — in that moment, you will understand something about equilibrium that you cannot learn any other way. You will understand that stability is not peace. That constancy is not calm. That a system can be abundant with energy and activity and life and still be *finished* — locked into its final state not by death but by the absence of any force capable of pushing it into a new one.

And you will understand, viscerally, why change matters. Why even painful, unwelcome, destructive change is preferable to an eternity of sameness. Why the human compulsion to *do something*, to *alter the state*, to *leave a mark* is not restlessness or immaturity but the deepest expression of what it means to be alive in a universe that trends toward equilibrium.

You will bring that understanding home with you. It will be worth the trip.

But leave on schedule.
