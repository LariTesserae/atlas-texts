# Preparing to Visit The Nursery Plateau

---

## First: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before I get into gear lists and protocols, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation philosophy matters more here than your equipment loadout.

You are visiting a location that is in the process of becoming. Not in the way a construction site is becoming a building — in the way an embryo is becoming an organism. Everything here is mid-genesis. The ground, the creatures, the structures, possibly the local physics. This means your normal multiverse traveler instincts — catalog, interact, sample, document — need to be tempered by something closer to the ethics of entering a neonatal ward. You can observe. You can be present. But everything you do has the potential to influence a formative process that you do not understand, and the consequences of that influence may not manifest for timescales you cannot predict.

This is not a warning to stay away. It is a warning to be deliberate.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### Developing the Right Mindset

**Practice sitting with ambiguity.** The Nursery Plateau will confront you with things that are between states — rock that isn't fully rock, creatures that might not be creatures, technology that might be biology, fluid that behaves like nothing you've catalogued. If you are the kind of person who needs to classify things to feel safe, this place will exhaust you. In the weeks before your visit, practice observing without naming. Go to a park, watch something happen — ants moving, wind in grass, water over stones — and resist the urge to narrate it, even internally. Just watch. Let the thing be the thing without forcing it into a category. This is the single most useful skill you can bring to the Nursery Plateau.

**Prepare for ontological vertigo.** The stone beneath your feet has not committed to being stone. The crystalline structures flicker between geometries. The fluid in the basin does not behave according to known phase states. You will be standing on a surface that is, at the molecular level, still choosing what it is. Some travelers report that prolonged exposure to ontological ambiguity produces a kind of sympathetic uncertainty in their own thinking — a loosening of mental categories, a softness in their sense of self. This is not dangerous in short visits, but it is disorienting. Ground yourself before you go. Know who you are. Know what your body feels like. Have a physical anchor — a familiar object in your pocket, a scar on your hand, anything concrete and decided — that you can touch to remind yourself that *you* are resolved even if your surroundings are not.

**Accept that you will not understand everything.** Probably you will not understand most things. The post's hum operates on principles you cannot name. The filament web moves water through space by means that aren't spatial. The crawlers participate in geological crystallization through some process that looks like decision-making. You will leave with more questions than you arrived with. That is correct. That is what this place offers. If you need closure, visit a ruin. This is the opposite of a ruin.

**Manage your sense of scale.** The plateau is small — fifteen minutes to walk its perimeter. The creatures are small. The structures are modest in size. But the *process* underway is vast. You are watching a world's geology, ecology, and possibly physics being bootstrapped into existence simultaneously. The smallness of the physical space can trick you into underestimating the significance of what's happening. Don't let it. Stay reverent. Stay humble.

### Emotional Preparation

Some travelers report that the Nursery Plateau produces a profound emotional response — not fear, not awe exactly, but something closer to tenderness. The vulnerability of the place, its unfinished quality, the way everything is trying so earnestly to become what it will be — it can hit you unexpectedly hard. People who have recently experienced significant beginnings or endings in their own lives (births, deaths, new relationships, career changes, moves) seem particularly susceptible. This is not a reason not to go. But know that it might surface feelings you didn't expect, and plan your post-visit decompression accordingly. Have someone to talk to afterward. Journal if that's your practice. Give yourself time.

Others report a deep, almost primal unease — a sense that they are somewhere they should not be, that they are witnessing something too intimate, too early. This response is also normal and may be adaptive. It may be the plateau itself, or the systems operating on it, communicating that outside presence is noted and not entirely welcome. If this feeling intensifies to the point of genuine distress, honor it. Leave. You can come back. The plateau will still be becoming.

## Physical Preparation

### Fitness and Body Readiness

The physical demands are mild. The plateau is small, relatively flat, and the terrain — while unusual — is walkable. You do not need mountaineering fitness or jungle endurance. What you need is:

**Good proprioception and balance.** The stone gives slightly under your weight, like memory foam. This is profoundly strange to walk on, because your nervous system expects rock to be rigid. You will feel mildly off-balance for the first several minutes as your body recalibrates. Practice walking on unstable surfaces — foam pads, sand, balance boards — to give your vestibular system some preparation. People with inner ear issues or balance disorders should be cautious and may want to bring a walking staff (see equipment section).

**Comfortable sustained standing and squatting.** You will want to spend long periods observing things at ground level — the crawlers, the filament web, the crystalline structures in the stone. Being able to hold a comfortable squat or kneel for ten to fifteen minutes without distress will dramatically improve your experience. Stretch your hip flexors and ankles in advance.

**Hydration and electrolyte readiness.** The atmosphere is warm, humid, and saturated with mineral salts. You will be breathing aerosolized minerals continuously. Some travelers report mild dehydration despite the moisture-rich environment — possibly because the body's systems get confused by the combination of wet skin and warm air and stop signaling thirst accurately. Hydrate well before arrival. Bring water. Drink on schedule rather than by thirst. An electrolyte supplement is wise, as the mineral-rich dew on your skin may create osmotic gradients that pull water from your tissues. I'll address this more under equipment.

### Health Considerations

**Respiratory sensitivity.** The air tastes of mineral salts, ozone, and something biological — described as the faintest sweetness of amniotic fluid. If you have asthma, reactive airway disease, or chemical sensitivities, this environment may trigger symptoms. The ozone concentration is unknown but likely low — enough to taste, which means at least 0.01 ppm, but the biological component is completely uncharacterized. A lightweight respirator with particulate and organic vapor filtration is a reasonable precaution for sensitive individuals. For healthy travelers, the air appears breathable without issues for the duration of a typical visit.

**Skin exposure.** The perpetual warm dew will coat every exposed surface of your body. This dew is not pure water — it contains mineral salts at minimum, and likely trace compounds from the plateau's ongoing geological and biological processes. Most travelers report no skin irritation, but some have noted a slight tingling or warmth where the dew accumulates (creases of elbows, behind ears, between fingers). This is likely benign — possibly mild osmotic effects or dissolved minerals interacting with skin chemistry — but if you have eczema, psoriasis, or sensitive skin, consider a barrier cream on vulnerable areas. Do not use heavy waterproofing, as it will make you overheat in the warm, humid conditions.

**The subsonic hum.** The dark post produces a tone that is felt in the bones of the jaw rather than heard. Subsonic frequencies in our world can cause discomfort, anxiety, nausea, or visual disturbance at sufficient amplitude. The post's hum appears to be low-amplitude — travelers report awareness without distress at moderate proximity — but individual sensitivity varies. If you are prone to motion sickness, migraines with aura, or have TMJ disorders, the jaw-bone resonance may be particularly uncomfortable. Approach the post slowly and incrementally, and retreat if you experience nausea, visual blurring, or a sense of pressure in your chest. The hum is described as "not vibration in the conventional sense," which suggests it may operate through a mechanism that doesn't follow standard acoustic physics, making earplugs or noise-canceling technology potentially useless. Your skeleton is the receiver, not your eardrums.

**Cognitive effects of proximity to undecided matter.** This is speculative but worth noting. You will be standing on, touching, and breathing in material that is ontologically unresolved — matter that has not committed to its physical properties. No one has established whether this has physiological effects on a fully-resolved biological organism. Anecdotal reports are limited. Some travelers describe a pleasant looseness of thought. Others describe nothing unusual. A few describe brief moments of confusion about their own physical boundaries — a sense that the edges of their body are less certain than usual, a momentary ambiguity about where their skin ends and the air begins. If this happens, it appears to be temporary and mild. Touch your anchor object. Remind yourself of your own specificity. You are decided. The stone is not. Maintain the distinction.

## Equipment and Gear

### Footwear — This Is the Most Important Equipment Decision

The plateau's surface is warm, slightly yielding, and coated in mineral dew. You need footwear that addresses all three properties:

**Thin, flexible soles.** You want to feel the ground. This sounds counterintuitive — normally we want protection from unknown terrain — but the Nursery Plateau communicates through its surface, and thick boots will isolate you from information you want. The crawlers respond to vibration (or whatever the local equivalent of vibration is in not-yet-decided stone). Heavy, rigid footwear will transmit more impact force into the surface than necessary, potentially disrupting crystallization processes or alarming the fauna. Minimalist hiking shoes, approach shoes, or even wetsuit booties with a reinforced sole are good options.

**Non-marking, non-reactive soles.** You will leave impressions in the stone regardless — it yields to weight and slowly refills. But you want to minimize chemical interaction between your sole material and the proto-rock. Avoid rubber compounds with aggressive tread patterns that might shed material. A smooth, relatively inert sole (silicone-based, or clean polyurethane) is preferable. The stone is still learning what it is. Don't give it confusing chemical input.

**Water-friendly.** Your feet will be wet the entire time. Choose footwear that handles saturation without becoming slippery, heavy, or uncomfortable. Quick-draining designs are ideal. Waterproof boots are pointless — the dew is omnidirectional and perpetual; you cannot keep it out.

**Warm enough.** The ground is warm, so your soles won't get cold, but wet feet lose heat fast if there's any air movement. Neoprene booties (3mm wetsuit boots with a firm sole) are probably the single best option, combining flexibility, water tolerance, minimal chemical reactivity, and thermal protection.

**Do not go barefoot.** I know the temptation will be enormous. A warm, yielding surface that responds to your presence, that you want to feel — of course you'll want to take your shoes off. Don't, or at least don't do it extensively. Your bare skin will have direct osmotic and chemical interaction with the undecided stone. The crawlers may investigate your feet as they investigate any warm surface in contact with ambiguous rock. You do not want to be part of a geological crystallization process. More practically, the translucent stone may have sharper-than-expected micro-structures where crystals are mid-formation — imagine stepping on a lattice that is flickering between hexagonal and cubic packing. Those geometric transitions could produce microscopic edges.

If you absolutely must feel the stone with bare skin — and I understand, I really do — do it with your hands, briefly, in a spot where you can see into the stone and verify that the crystal structures are relatively stable. Keep it to seconds, not minutes. And monitor the spot on your skin afterward for any unusual sensation, warmth, or change in texture.

### Clothing

**Light, breathable layers that you don't mind getting wet.** Synthetic or merino wool base layers. Nothing cotton — it will saturate and cling and stay cold if you encounter any cooler air pockets at the plateau's edges. The ambient temperature seems warm and stable, so a single light layer may suffice, but bring a second in case. The humidity is essentially 100%, so nothing will dry.

**No bright colors, no UV-fluorescent materials.** The local fauna navigate partly by the ambient glow — the amber from the stone, the blue-green from the basin. Introducing high-visibility colors or fluorescent materials into this environment could confuse or attract creatures you'd rather leave undisturbed. Earth tones, warm neutrals, or amber tones that blend with the local light palette are considerate choices.

**A light, water-resistant hat or hood.** Not for rain protection — the dew is inescapable — but to keep the perpetual moisture from running into your eyes. A wide brim will also reduce the visual distraction of constantly falling droplets and let you observe more clearly.

**Minimize synthetic chemical signatures.** No perfume, no scented sunscreen (no sunscreen at all — there's no sun), no insect repellent (we don't know what it would do to the local fauna, and the fauna don't appear to bite), no laundry-scented clothes if you can manage it. The environment has its own specific chemical character — mineral salts, ozone, amniotic sweetness — and introducing foreign volatiles could interact unpredictably with developing systems.

### Observation Equipment

**Macro lens or jeweler's loupe (10x-20x magnification).** The most astonishing details on the Nursery Plateau are at the small scale — the flickering crystalline lattices visible through the stone's translucency, the etched textures on the filament-web anchor points, the structures on the crawlers' shells. A handheld magnifying lens is low-tech, reliable, and requires no power source that might interact with the local systems. A good loupe that you can hold to your eye while crouching over a patch of undecided stone will give you minutes of fascinated observation.

**A notebook and pencil.** Not a phone, not a tablet. Pencil on paper. The plateau contains technology that operates on principles you don't understand — the filament web moves matter non-spatially, the post speaks to stone's crystalline structure, the basin fluid transmits light faster than its own wave speed. Electronic devices *may* function normally here. They also may not. Several travelers report that digital recordings from the Nursery Plateau are strangely incomplete — photos that show blank amber fields where structures should be, audio recordings that capture only silence where the soundscape was dense and rich. Whether this is a property of the local physics interfering with electromagnetic storage, or something more intentional, is unknown. Pencil and paper don't have this problem. Sketch what you see. Write what you hear. Draw the crawlers, the web, the basin. Your hand-drawn records may be more accurate than any digital capture.

If you do bring electronic devices: keep them in a sealed pouch when not actively in use, powered off. Use them sparingly. Do not place them on the stone surface or against the post. If your device begins behaving strangely — screen flickering, producing unexpected warmth, displaying colors that don't match what you're seeing — turn it off immediately and stow it. The post forces crystalline resolution in stone; you do not want it forcing resolution in your semiconductor lattices.

**A small glass or ceramic vial (sealed, clean) for fluid sample.** If you decide to take a small sample from the basin — and there are ethical considerations here I'll address below — you'll want a container that is chemically inert and non-reactive. Glass is ideal. Plastic might interact with the fluid's unknown chemistry. Metal might respond to whatever principle the post operates on. Bring the vial sealed and empty. Think carefully before you fill it.

**A tuning fork or pitch pipe.** This sounds strange, but hear me out. The soundscape on the plateau is dense and complex — the post's subsonic hum, the filament web's glass-marimba pinging, the fliers' chirring, the crawlers' clicking, the periodic deep thrum from below. If you have any musical training, a tuning fork will help you identify reference pitches and start mapping the harmonic relationships between these sounds. Several travelers with musical backgrounds have reported that the combined soundscape has structure — not random, not chaotic, but something approaching composition, as though the various sound sources are tuned to each other. A reference pitch will help you investigate this. Even if you're not musical, striking a tuning fork and holding it up in the air could produce interesting interactions with the local sound environment. The filament web, in particular, is described as responsive to vibration. But be gentle — you're introducing a foreign frequency into an emergent system.

### Safety Equipment

**A reliable light source that doesn't depend on the local glow.** The ambient illumination comes from within the stone, and it *pulses* — it is not constant. If it dims or goes out, you want your own light. A small headlamp or flashlight with a red-light mode is best — red light will be less disruptive to the local fauna's light-adapted vision than white light, and it won't overwhelm the bioluminescence of the basin, which you'll want to keep visible for navigation reference. Bring spare batteries, and keep them in a sealed container away from moisture and the post's influence.

**A length of thin, high-visibility cord (10-15 meters).** The plateau's edges are soft and gradual — not cliffs but curves dissolving into vapor. There is no clear boundary between "on the plateau" and "falling off the plateau." If visibility drops (which could happen if the moisture ceiling descends or the stone-glow dims), you want to have a tethered reference to a known safe position. Anchor your cord to something stable — your pack, a heavy object you've placed — at the plateau's center, and pay it out as you explore toward the edges. If you lose visual reference, follow the cord back. The vapor beyond the edges obscures whatever is below. Don't find out what's below by falling into it.

**Basic first aid, biased toward chemical exposure.** Sterile saline for eye irrigation (the mineral dew in your eyes could cause irritation over time), clean water for skin rinsing, antihistamines in case of unexpected immune response to local biologicals, and a small supply of activated charcoal in case you accidentally ingest basin fluid or other unknown substances. Also bring wound care supplies with good moisture barriers — any cut or scrape will be perpetually wet in this environment, and you don't want the local mineral-rich moisture interacting with an open wound. Waterproof wound sealant or liquid bandage is preferable to adhesive bandages, which won't stick.

**An exit plan and a timer.** The Nursery Plateau is captivating. The combination of warmth, ambient sound, visual fascination, and the general atmosphere of tender emergence can produce a state of absorbed contemplation that causes you to lose track of time. Set a timer — a mechanical one if you don't trust electronics here, or simply agree with yourself on a maximum visit duration before you arrive. Two to three hours is a good initial visit. You can always come back. Longer exposure to the ontologically undecided environment is not well studied, and the progressive loosening of mental categories described by some travelers is easier to manage if you keep your first visit bounded.

## Ethical Preparation — The Most Important Section

### The Problem of Interference

The Nursery Plateau is not a park. It is not a museum. It is not a tourist destination. It is a genesis event. Everything on it is in the process of becoming what it will be, and that process appears to be delicate, coordinated, and purposeful even if you cannot decode the purpose.

Your presence *will* interfere. This is unavoidable. Your weight on the stone introduces a variable the stone must account for. Your body heat, your breath, your chemical signature — these all enter a system that is calibrating itself. The question is not whether you interfere but how much and how thoughtfully.

**Do not touch the post.** I know the description mentions holding your palm against its surface and feeling nothing. Other travelers have done this. The post did not react to them in any observable way. But the post is forcing ontological resolution in undecided matter — turning ambiguity into certainty, convincing flickering crystals to commit to granite. Your body is made of decided matter, yes, but it is also made of matter that is *maintained* in its decided state by ongoing biological processes. You are not granite. You are a dynamic system of constant cellular decision-making — differentiation, apoptosis, gene expression. You do not want to expose your dynamic biology to a force that speaks the language of permanent crystalline commitment. This may be overcautious. It may not be. Don't touch the post.

**Do not step on the filament web.** The filaments are shin-height and you might be tempted to step over the anchor points and walk among them. Don't. You don't know what the web is harvesting moisture for, or where the vanished water goes, or what the etched instructions on the anchor points say. The web is infrastructure for a process you can't comprehend. Observe it from outside its perimeter.

**Do not put your hands in the basin.** Something lives in there. The fluid has properties beyond your understanding. The crawlers orient toward it like compass needles when the creature rises. The basin may be a womb, a brain, a power source, a seed, or something for which no analogy exists. It is not a tide pool for you to explore. Watch. Appreciate. Do not reach in.

**Be cautious with the crawlers.** They are geological midwives. They are helping the stone choose its crystal structure. If one approaches you — and they may, if you stand still long enough on a patch of undecided stone — step aside slowly. Do not pick them up. Do not block their path. They are working. Their work may be more important than your curiosity.

**Think very carefully before taking any samples.** A chip of stone, a vial of basin fluid, a dead filament — these are fragments of an unfinished process. Taking them removes components from a system that may need those components. The stone chip you pocket might have been destined to resolve into something essential. The fluid you vial might contain information the basin creature needs. Moreover, you don't know how undecided matter will behave when removed from its context. Will the stone chip continue flickering between crystal states indefinitely? Will it resolve suddenly, violently, into a random configuration? Will it influence other matter around it — the contents of your pack, your other samples, your own tissues? I'm not saying never take samples. I'm saying: if you do, take the smallest amount possible, from the least active area you can find, and store it isolated from everything else. And acknowledge to yourself that you may be disrupting something you have no right to disrupt.

### The Question of the Pale Shape

You will probably see it — the cat-sized pale shape that one account mentions atop the post, watching with eyes that reflect the basin's glow. Other travelers have also reported it. It appears once, briefly, and does not return during a given visit. It watches.

This may be something like a guardian, or an observer, or an immune response. You don't know. But it sees you, and it is aware, and it has the only eyes on the plateau that look at you with what multiple travelers describe as *recognition* — not just stimulus-response but actual apprehension of you as a specific entity.

If you see it: hold still. Hold eye contact if it feels right. Don't approach. Don't speak to it, or if you feel compelled to speak, speak softly and honestly. Some travelers have murmured simple things — "I won't touch anything," "I'm just watching," "it's beautiful" — and felt, without evidence, that they were understood.

Whether any of this matters is unknown. Do it anyway. It's good practice for being a guest.

## Arrival and Approach Protocols

### Transit Considerations

The method of arrival will depend on your multiverse travel technology, but some general principles apply:

**Arrive light.** The plateau is small and there is nowhere to set up a camp or staging area. Bring only what you carry on your person. No base camp, no equipment cases, no group infrastructure.

**Arrive alone or in a pair at most.** The plateau can be walked in fifteen minutes. More than two people will turn it into a crowd. The fauna will react to multiple large organisms differently than to one. The stone will be subjected to more weight, more chemical signatures, more variables to process. Solo is ideal. A trusted partner who understands the ethics and maintains silence is acceptable. Groups are not appropriate.

**Arrive quietly.** If your transit method is noisy or energetically dramatic — a flash, a boom, a spatial distortion — try to arrive at the edge rather than the center. Give the plateau and its inhabitants a moment to register your presence before you move into the active zones. Stand still for five full minutes upon arrival. Let the dew coat you. Let the soundscape fill your ears. Let the crawlers continue their circuits without disruption. Let the pale shape (if it's watching) take your measure.

**Orient yourself upon arrival.** You'll emerge into amber dimness, warm humidity, and a complex soundscape. Take stock. Identify the three structures: the post (near the "eastern" edge, dark cylinder, humming), the filament web (central area, low to the ground, pinging), and the basin (luminous, blue-green glow, hardest to miss). These are your landmarks. The basin in particular, as the brightest object, is your primary reference point. If you become disoriented, look for the blue-green glow.

## Suggested Visit Structure

### Phase One: Acclimatization (30-45 minutes)

Stand near the edge where you arrived. Don't move toward the interior yet. Let your senses adjust. The light is dimmer than you're used to — your pupils will dilate, and after ten minutes you'll see much more detail. The soundscape will initially register as noise; give it time to separate into its component layers — the hum, the pinging, the chirring, the clicking, the dripping. Feel the stone under your feet. Notice how it yields. Notice the warmth from below. Breathe the air — salts, ozone, sweetness. Let the dew settle on your skin and clothes.

During this phase, observe the crawlers. They are numerous and visible and their behavior is endlessly watchable. Note how they cluster on ambiguous stone and how the stone changes after they move on. This is the most accessible phenomenon on the plateau and a good entry point for understanding what the place is doing.

### Phase Two: The Filament Web (30-45 minutes)

Approach the central web area and find a comfortable position at its perimeter — sitting or squatting, not standing, so your eyes are at the level of the filaments. Watch the dew-collection cycle: accumulation, sag, run to node, vanish, spring back. Listen to the glass-marimba sounds. Try to discern patterns. Note whether the pace changes while you're watching — some travelers report that the web's activity increases in the presence of an observer, though this could be confirmation bias.

If you brought a magnifying lens, this is the time to examine the anchor points and their etched surfaces. Get as close as you can without touching. Sketch what you see.

### Phase Three: The Basin (30-45 minutes)

Approach the basin slowly and sit at its edge. Watch the luminous fluid. Wait for the creature (or creatures) to rise toward the surface. Note the crawlers' compass-needle reaction when it does. Watch for the slow, sourceless ripples and the way they test the edge and retreat.

This is often the most emotionally intense part of the visit. The basin's glow, the warmth, the sense of something patient and purposeful gestating in the fluid — it provokes a response. Allow it.

### Phase Four: The Post (15-20 minutes)

Approach the post last. Observe the perfect meniscus of dew on its flat top. Note the ring of committed granite around its base. Listen — or rather, feel — the hum. Don't stay too long in close proximity. The jaw-bone resonance can produce a cumulative fatigue that you don't notice until you step away and feel sudden relief.

Look for the pale shape atop it. Probably it will already be gone by this point in your visit, if it appeared at all. But look.

### Phase Five: The Edges (15-20 minutes)

Walk carefully toward the plateau's perimeter. Note how the stone becomes softer, more ambiguous, less resolved as you approach the edge. The crawlers thin out here. The vapor thickens. You are approaching the boundary between something that exists and something that doesn't yet exist. Do not go too far. If the stone begins to feel less like memory foam and more like nothing at all — if your footsteps stop registering resistance — you are too close to the edge. Step back.

### Phase Six: Departure

When your timer sounds or you feel complete, return to your arrival point. Stand still for another few minutes. Offer the place whatever gratitude feels genuine. Then leave.

## Post-Visit Protocols

### Immediate

**Rinse your skin and eyes with clean water as soon as feasible.** The mineral dew will have deposited a fine layer of salts and unknown compounds on every exposed surface. Most of this is probably harmless, but wash it off. Pay attention to mucous membranes — eyes, nostrils, mouth.

**Check your clothing and gear for crawlers.** They are palm-sized and slow-moving and it is possible that one has climbed onto your pack or shoes. If you find one, return it to the plateau immediately if possible. If transit has already occurred and return is not immediately feasible, keep it warm, keep it on a hard surface (it will be confused and possibly distressed by surfaces that are already geologically decided — give it something ambiguous if you can, though this is difficult outside the plateau), and return it at the earliest opportunity. Do not keep it. It has work to do.

**Note any persistent physical sensations.** A lingering warmth in the soles of your feet. A feeling of vibration in the jaw, hours after leaving. A sense that surfaces you touch are slightly softer than they should be. These are commonly reported and appear to fade within twenty-four to forty-eight hours. If they persist beyond a week, consult a multiverse medical specialist with experience in ontological exposure.

**Write or record your experience as soon as possible.** Memories of the Nursery Plateau have an odd quality — they soften and lose specificity faster than memories of more resolved locations. Whether this is psychological (the experience is dreamlike and thus stored like a dream) or something else (your memories of an ontologically unstable location are themselves ontologically unstable) is debated. Get your account down quickly, in detail, while it's fresh.

### Longer Term

**Monitor your sample, if you took one.** Keep it isolated. Check it daily. Note any changes in translucency, temperature, luminescence, or apparent density. If the stone sample resolves — if it suddenly commits to a crystal structure and becomes hard, opaque, definitive — document the moment of resolution and the final structure. If it doesn't resolve — if it continues flickering indefinitely — that is also significant. Either way, do not store it near other geological samples, electronics, or biological specimens.

**Consider whether to return.** The Nursery Plateau is a process, not a snapshot. It is different each time, because it is further along each time. The stone will be slightly more decided. The crawlers' work will have progressed. The basin creature may have grown. The filament web may have expanded or contracted. Repeat visits, spaced months or years apart, offer a rare opportunity to witness emergence in progress — to see a world at age one hour, age one year, age one century.

But also consider whether the plateau needs your absence. Every visit introduces variables. Every footstep asks the stone to account for you. Perhaps the kindest thing is to visit once, bear witness, and let the place become what it is becoming without further interruption.

## What to Expect to Feel

You will feel warm. You will feel wet. You will feel a deep, encompassing sound in your bones and your ears and your teeth. You will feel the ground give way beneath you, gently, temporarily, forgivingly, as though it is accommodating you as a guest while continuing its own important work.

You will feel small. Not insignificant — small. The way you feel small in a hospital nursery, surrounded by new lives that have no idea you exist, that are entirely absorbed in the enormous work of beginning.

You will feel the urge to help. To nudge a crawler toward a patch of undecided stone. To hum along with the post. To touch the basin's surface, just to participate, just to be part of what's happening. This urge is natural and should be resisted. You are not part of this process. You are a witness. That is enough. That is the gift.

You will, at some point during your visit, almost certainly feel the deep *thrum* from beneath the plateau — the one that makes everything pause for half a second before resuming. When it happens, stand still. The crawlers will freeze. The fliers will hover. The filament web will hold its water. The basin will go dark for an instant. Everything will wait for something. And then it will resume, and you will not know what was waited for, or whether it arrived.

That unknowing is the heart of the experience. You are visiting a place that is being born, and birth is not something that explains itself to its audience.

Stand on the warm stone. Let the dew fall on you. Listen to the glass marimba and the subsonic hum and the chirring of things that aren't quite things yet. Watch the stone learn what it is. And when you leave, carry with you the knowledge that somewhere, in some fold of the multiverse, a world is still in the act of choosing what it will become — that existence is not a given but a process, not a fact but a decision being made, crystal by crystal, creature by creature, moment by moment, in warm amber light, under a ceiling of perpetual dew, on a plateau that hums with the sound of its own becoming.
