# Preparing for the Sweating City: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before addressing any specific preparation, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparations will fail if they're calibrated for the wrong kind of danger.

The Sweating City is not a ruin. Ruins are dead. This place is alive in the way a liver is alive — performing a function, cycling fluids, maintaining itself — without any need for consciousness or intent. It is a functioning metabolic system that happens to be shaped like a city. The danger is not that it will attack you. The danger is that it will *process* you. That you will become substrate. That the mineral water will begin to treat your body the way it treats a crack in a wall: as a surface to deposit on, a channel to flow through, a feature to slowly, patiently incorporate.

Your preparation must address survival, yes, but it must also address *resistance to incorporation*. These are different problems.

## Physical Conditioning and Medical Preparation

### Altitude and Heat Acclimatization

The city sits on a high plateau with noticeable altitude effects — thin air that nonetheless feels oppressively heavy due to heat and humidity. This is a particularly miserable combination. At altitude, your body wants to breathe faster and deeper to compensate for reduced oxygen partial pressure. In extreme humid heat, your body wants to minimize exertion and slow respiration to reduce thermal load. The city forces both conditions simultaneously.

**Spend at least two weeks acclimatizing before entry.** If you can find a staging location on the same plateau but outside the city's haze boundary, use it. If not, simulate the conditions as best you can:

- Train at moderate altitude (2,000–3,000 meters) in humid conditions.
- Practice working in temperatures above 40°C with humidity above 80%.
- Learn to recognize the early signs of heat exhaustion versus altitude sickness, because in this environment they will present simultaneously and their treatments partially contradict each other. Heat exhaustion wants cooling and hydration. Altitude sickness wants descent. In the Sweating City, you may not be able to do either quickly.

**Bring supplemental oxygen.** Not as a primary breathing system — you'd run out — but as an emergency reserve for moments when the altitude-heat combination becomes cognitively impairing. A compact system with a few hours of reserve could save your life if you need to make critical decisions while your brain is starving for oxygen in soup-thick air.

### The Water Problem

This is your most critical medical concern. The city's water is warm, mineral-laden, contains iron and lithium and an unidentified numbing agent, and is apparently hostile to all biological life. **Do not drink it.** Do not let it collect on your skin any longer than necessary. Do not let it pool in your ears, your eyes, your nose, your mouth.

The lithium alone is concerning. Lithium in mineral water at natural concentrations is harmless, even potentially beneficial. But we don't know the concentration here, and chronic dermal exposure to lithium at elevated concentrations can cause:

- Thyroid dysfunction
- Renal impairment
- Neurological effects — tremor, cognitive dulling, that dangerous fog that might make you forget why you wanted to leave
- Skin reactions

The numbing agent is worse because it's unidentified. Something that causes localized numbness on oral mucosa could be:

- A sodium channel blocker (like tetrodotoxin or certain mineral salts)
- A heavy metal in unusual oxidation state
- Something with no terrestrial analog whatsoever

**Bring your own water. All of it.** Calculate your needs generously. In extreme humid heat at altitude, you may need 8–12 liters per day. A week's expedition requires 56–84 liters. This is heavy — 56 to 84 kilograms — and represents your single greatest logistical challenge. Plan your carry capacity around this. Consider a wheeled cart or sled that can navigate the city's worn streets. Consider caching water at a base camp near the entry point and making radial forays rather than linear transits.

**Bring a water testing kit** — a good one, capable of identifying unusual mineral compositions, heavy metals, and biologically active compounds. Test the city's water thoroughly. If it turns out to be potable with filtration, your logistics improve dramatically. But assume it is not until proven otherwise.

**Bring electrolyte supplements** calibrated for your own hydration — your sweat losses will be enormous, and you need to replace sodium, potassium, and magnesium on your own terms, not the city's.

### Skin and Membrane Protection

The city will try to make you wet. The humidity, the sweating walls, the pooling water in every depression — you will be damp constantly. This creates multiple problems:

**Skin maceration:** Prolonged moisture exposure softens and breaks down skin, especially on the feet, in skin folds, and around wounds. Once the skin barrier is compromised, the mineral water has direct access to your bloodstream.

- Bring moisture-wicking base layers.
- Bring barrier creams — zinc oxide, dimethicone-based skin protectants — and apply them to vulnerable areas twice daily.
- Bring foot care supplies: multiple pairs of moisture-wicking socks, foot powder, moleskin, the understanding that your feet are the weak point where the city will try to get in.
- Change socks every few hours. Dry your feet whenever you stop. This sounds fussy. It is survival.

**Eye and respiratory protection:** The mineral-laden humidity will coat your mucous membranes. The air itself tastes of iron and lithium. Over hours and days of breathing it, you're getting a constant low-dose exposure to whatever the water contains, delivered directly to the most absorptive surfaces in your body.

- Bring sealed goggles — not for constant use, which would be impractical in the heat, but for the lower levels where the sweating intensifies and the air becomes thick with mineral moisture.
- Bring a respirator with mineral/particulate filters. Again, not for constant use — breathing through a respirator in extreme heat at altitude is its own kind of dangerous — but for the deep districts, the sub-street corridors, the vaulted spaces where the walls run freely.
- Consider a lightweight full-face shield for the deepest explorations.

**The numbness issue:** If any part of your body goes numb after contact with the city's water, treat it as a chemical exposure. Flush with your own clean water immediately. Document the location and duration. If numbness persists or spreads, abort and withdraw to your entry point. A numbing agent in the water could impair your ability to feel injuries, thermal burns from hot stone, or the early signs of skin breakdown. Numbness is the city beginning to process you. It is removing your ability to feel the boundary between yourself and itself.

### Pharmaceutical Preparation

Bring:
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics (any wound in this environment will be bathed in warm mineral water — infection risk is extreme)
- Antifungals (though the city seems hostile to biology, your own body's fungi may thrive in the humid conditions while the city's water may kill off competitive flora)
- Anti-inflammatories
- Lithium monitoring strips if they exist in your pharmacopeia
- Activated charcoal or equivalent universal adsorbent in case of accidental ingestion of city water in quantity
- Thyroid function monitoring capability if your stay extends beyond a few days
- A broad-spectrum chelation agent in case heavy metal exposure becomes symptomatic

## Equipment and Gear

### Clothing

The instinct will be to dress light. Resist it. You need coverage.

The ideal is a system that provides:
- Full skin coverage to minimize contact with the mineral water and humid air
- Breathability to prevent lethal heat retention
- Durability against rough, mineral-crusted stone surfaces

**Recommended approach:** Lightweight, tightly woven, moisture-wicking synthetic fabrics. Long sleeves, long pants, high-collared shirt. Think desert fieldwork clothing — the kind designed for environments where full sun exposure and heat make you want to strip down, but the environment demands you stay covered. Light colors to minimize radiant heat absorption, though in the Sweating City's diffuse, shadowless light, this matters less than it would under a direct sun.

**Boots:** This is not a sandal environment. You need:
- Waterproof (to keep the city's water out, not Earth rain)
- Ankle-supporting (the streets are worn, the stairs are becoming ramps, the mineral accretion makes surfaces uneven)
- Non-slip soles (everything is wet, the stone is slick, the mineral deposits can be glassy)
- Quick-draining if waterproofing is breached
- Thick-soled — the stone is warm, and prolonged contact with hot stone through thin soles will cause foot damage

Bring at least two pairs. Rotate them. Let one dry (as much as anything dries here) while wearing the other.

**Gloves:** You will want to touch things. The engines that vibrate at molecular frequencies, the walls that sweat, the pipes that still carry warm water. Bring thin, waterproof gloves with tactile sensitivity. Nitrile, at minimum, for casual protection. Thicker chemical-resistant gloves for the deep levels.

**Head covering:** A wide-brimmed hat may seem unnecessary under a diffuse bronze sky with no visible sun, but it serves a different purpose here — it keeps the condensing humidity from dripping directly onto your face and into your eyes. A brimmed hat with a neck drape. Functional, unglamorous, essential.

### Navigation and Orientation

The city is kilometers across, visibility is limited to a few hundred meters, and the street pattern is semi-regular but warped. You will get lost. Plan for this.

**Mapping:** Bring the best mapping technology available to you. If GPS or equivalent satellite navigation works in this multiverse location, use it. If not — and the bronze sky and atmospheric haze suggest satellite signals may not penetrate — bring:

- A compass (test it immediately upon arrival; the basalt plateau may have magnetic properties that make a compass unreliable or useless)
- Paper and writing implements that work in wet conditions (waterproof notebook, graphite pencils, not ink)
- Breadcrumb markers — something you can leave at intersections to mark your route. Choose something that won't be dissolved by the mineral water or incorporated by the city's accretion process. Ceramic fragments from the newer buildings might work; they seem to resist the city's chemistry. Alternatively, brightly colored synthetic cord or tape, tied at intersections.

**Vertical awareness:** The city has multiple levels. Sub-street corridors, vaulted spaces, those deep channels. When you descend, mark your entry point clearly. The mineral accretion can narrow passages over time — not fast enough to trap you on a single visit, probably, but enough that an entrance you remember as wide might be narrower than you expect on the return.

### Lighting

The diffuse bronze light provides ambient illumination on the surface streets, but the lower levels — the corridors, the engine rooms, the deep channel systems — will be dark. The light from above does not penetrate far into the ventilation shafts.

- Bring multiple independent light sources. Headlamp, handheld torch, chemical lightsticks as backup.
- Protect your light sources from the humidity. Waterproof housings are mandatory. The mineral water will corrode electronics and cloud lenses.
- Bring more batteries than you think you need, and keep them sealed and dry until use.

### Shelter and Rest

You will need to sleep in the Sweating City, and this is one of the more troubling logistical problems.

Every surface is wet. The ambient temperature is extreme. The humidity is near saturation. Lying down on any surface means lying in a film of warm mineral water. Sleeping means hours of unconscious exposure to whatever the water contains, with no ability to monitor numbness, skin maceration, or the slow encroachment of the city's chemistry into your body.

**Bring a raised sleeping platform** — a lightweight cot or hammock system that keeps you off the stone. A hammock is lighter but requires anchor points; given the massive, thick-walled architecture, you should be able to find suitable structures. A cot is heavier but self-supporting.

**Bring a waterproof sleeping surface** — not for rain, but for the condensation that will form on any surface, including your sleeping bag, your cot fabric, your own body.

**Choose your sleeping location carefully:**
- Elevated, not subterranean. The sweating intensifies below street level.
- Inside one of the ceramic buildings if possible — they are dry, they do not participate in the city's moisture cycle, and they offer a psychological respite from the constant dampness. They are also in worse repair, so assess structural integrity before sleeping inside one.
- Away from the ventilation shafts. The breathing sounds from below will make sleep difficult, and the air rising from the deep levels may carry concentrated mineral moisture.
- Away from the pipe networks. The gurgling is constant and the pipes may leak or shift.

**Set a watch if you're traveling with companions.** Not just for the unidentified shape on the rooftop or the sourceless ripple in the pool, but for changes in the city itself. If the water level in the streets rises while you sleep. If the mineral accretion begins to form on your equipment. If the temperature shifts. The city is a dynamic system, and you are a foreign object in it. Sleep is when you're most vulnerable to being processed.

### Communication and Emergency Extraction

**Establish a communication link with your exit point before entering the city.** Radio may work, but test it — the mineral-dense stone and humid atmosphere may attenuate signals badly. If radio fails, establish a schedule: check in at set times, and if you miss a check-in, extraction begins.

**Mark your entry point into the city unmistakably.** The haze boundary is a disorienting curtain of warm murk, and the city's edge may not be clearly defined. When you arrive, mark the route from the haze boundary to open ground with something durable and visible. If you need to leave in a hurry — and the growing wrongness of the place suggests you might — you cannot afford to be searching for the exit in bronze murk.

**Bring signal flares or equivalent.** Something visible from altitude, in case aerial extraction is available. Something that works in high humidity.

## Behavioral Protocols and Psychological Preparation

### The Incorporation Gradient

This is the most important concept for your survival, and it is not a physical preparation. It is a mental framework.

The Sweating City operates on a gradient. The further in you go — deeper into the city's interior, lower into its subterranean levels — the more completely you become part of its system. Surface streets: you're wet, you're hot, the mineral water is on your skin. Deep corridors: the walls run freely, the air is thick with mineral vapor, the water is in your lungs with every breath. The engine districts: the stone vibrates at frequencies you can feel in your bones, and the warmth is not just around you but *through* you, as if the city is testing whether you, too, can be made to sweat.

**Set depth limits before you enter, and honor them.** Decide in advance: I will not descend below the second sub-level. I will not enter any passage narrower than my shoulders. I will not spend more than thirty minutes in any space where the walls are running freely. Write these limits down. Consult them when the urge to go deeper becomes compelling, because it will become compelling. The city is interesting. The deeper you go, the more interesting it becomes. The engines. The channels. The autonomous systems still functioning after centuries without operators. The sound of water moving in the dark. You will want to understand. Understanding will pull you deeper.

**Set a total duration limit.** No more than three days on a first visit. No more than a week under any circumstances. The lithium exposure alone warrants this. The psychological effects warrant it more. The city is empty of intent, and prolonged exposure to an environment this thoroughly inhuman — this functional and this indifferent — does things to the mind. You begin to think in terms of the city's logic. Channels and deposits and thermal gradients. You begin to feel that your own sweating is part of the system. You are not wrong, exactly. But you are not right in a way that helps you leave.

### The Question of Agency

Something keeps the channels clear. Something produces the sourceless ripples. Something was silhouetted on the rooftop in a shape that was wrong for architecture and then moved.

You will not resolve these observations. Accept this now. The city exists in a state of deep ambiguity between the mechanical and the intentional, and your visit will not clarify which side of that line it falls on. If you spend your time trying to determine whether the city is alive, whether something inhabits it, whether the engines are operating or performing — you will stay too long, go too deep, and exhaust your water supply chasing answers that the city's haze will always keep just beyond your sightline.

**Adopt a policy of respectful non-investigation regarding the anomalies.** Document them. Note their locations, times, and characteristics. Do not pursue them. Do not wait for them to recur. The shape on the rooftop did not approach you. The ripple in the pool did not repeat. The groan from below lasted four seconds and stopped. These may be invitations to deeper engagement with the city. They may be warnings. They may be neither — just the autonomous twitches of a vast metabolic system that has no more awareness of you than your liver has of an individual red blood cell.

But consider: the city's water is hostile to all biology. No moss, no lichen, no algae. Not even the seeds of life have survived. If something *is* alive in there — something that has adapted to an environment that dissolves all other life — it is adapted at a level that makes it fundamentally incompatible with you. Do not seek contact. Do not invite curiosity. Be a foreign substance that passes through the system without being absorbed.

### The Urge to Stay

This is documented in enough multiverse travel accounts to constitute a pattern: certain locations exert a pull that is not physical but architectural. They are structured in ways that invite habitation. Doorways at your height. Steps at your scale. Rooms sized for your body. The Sweating City was built — or carved, or grown — for something approximately your size, and despite the emptiness and the mineral hostility and the wrong shape on the rooftop, some part of your brain will pattern-match this environment as *habitable*. A city. Streets. Buildings with rooms. A water supply. Infrastructure.

Your brain is wrong. This city is not habitable by you. The water is poison. The air is slowly dosing you with lithium and unknown numbing agents. The heat and altitude are degrading your cognition. The self-maintaining mineral accretion that keeps the city alive would, given enough time, begin to coat your equipment, clog your breathing filters, and — if you stayed long enough, stayed still enough — form thin, warm mineral films on your own skin, filling your pores, sealing your sweat glands, beginning the long slow process of turning you into another surface for the city to maintain.

**Before you enter, write yourself a letter.** Date it. In the letter, explain why you are visiting, what you hope to learn, and when you intend to leave. Carry it with you. Read it every morning. When the city feels like somewhere you could stay — when the warmth feels comforting instead of oppressive, when the sound of water in the pipes feels like companionship, when the bronze sky feels like home — read the letter. Remember that you wrote it when you were yourself. Trust the version of you that had not yet breathed the city's air.

## Entry and Movement Protocol

### Approach

The haze boundary is your first decision point. Before entering it, confirm:
- All equipment is sealed and functional
- Your entry point is marked on the outside of the haze
- Your communication link is active
- Your extraction schedule is established and agreed upon
- You have read your letter

The haze is warm, wet, and disorienting. Visibility will drop. You may lose your sense of direction. Move slowly. Mark your path. The transition from the haze into the city's edge should take less than fifteen minutes; if it takes longer, you may be moving parallel to the boundary rather than through it. Use a compass if it works, or track the slope of the ground — the plateau is highest at the center, so walking uphill means walking inward.

### Surface Movement

Stay on the main arterial streets for your first visit. They are wider, better illuminated by the diffuse light, and less affected by mineral accretion narrowing. The branching pattern — grid-warped-by-terrain or circulatory-system-straightened-by-intent — means that the largest streets connect to each other in broadly predictable ways. Map as you go.

Move during whatever passes for daytime. The bronze sky may not change — there may not be a day-night cycle — but if there is any variation in light levels, use the brighter periods for movement and the dimmer ones for rest.

**Do not enter buildings unless necessary.** The interiors are where the sweating is more concentrated, the ventilation shafts breathe their deep air, and the mineral water has pooled in rooms and corridors that may be slippery, deep, or unstable. When you do enter buildings, note the thickness of mineral accretion around doorways. If the accretion has narrowed a doorway significantly, be aware that it may have also affected the interior — constricting passages, changing floor levels, creating pools where the original builders intended dry rooms.

### Subterranean Movement

**Minimize this.** The sub-street corridors and vaulted spaces are where the city is most itself — most wet, most warm, most active, most autonomous. They are also where you are most vulnerable. Your skin is maximally exposed to the mineral moisture. Your respirator is working hardest. Your lights are your only illumination, and if they fail, you are in hot, wet, absolute darkness in a space that is actively circulating water through channels at your feet.

If you must descend:
- Never alone. Absolutely never alone.
- Maintain a physical tether to the surface — a line, a rope, something you can follow back by touch if your lights fail and the city's breathing in the shafts disorients you.
- Limit time below to the minimum required for your objective.
- Monitor your own cognitive state. The concentrated mineral atmosphere in the lower levels may accelerate lithium absorption and whatever the numbing agent does. If you feel dreamy, detached, or unusually comfortable, ascend immediately.

### The Engine District

You will be drawn to it. The engines are the most fascinating feature of the city — stone pistons with tolerances fine enough to seal, still warm, still vibrating at molecular frequencies, possibly still converting heat to water or water to heat in a reaction that has no terrestrial analog. The temptation to study them closely, to press your hands against them, to press your *ear* against them and listen to the stone being busy at levels you cannot see — this temptation will be strong.

**Limit your time in the engine district.** The engines are warmer than ambient. The sweating is more intense near them. The vibration, felt through the hands and the feet and eventually the chest, has a quality that is difficult to describe but easy to fall into — a resonance that your body begins to match, the way your breathing unconsciously synchronizes with another person's. You do not want to synchronize with the engines. You do not want your body to begin resonating at the frequency of stone that converts heat to mineral water.

Take your measurements. Collect your samples. Leave.

## On Departure

When you leave — and you must leave, on schedule, by the plan you made before you entered — the temptation will crystallize into a single thought: *I haven't understood it yet.*

You haven't. You won't. The Sweating City is a system of sufficient complexity and sufficient age that a week's visit will not decode it. You will leave with questions. You will leave not knowing whether the city is alive or mechanical, whether the shape on the rooftop was a creature or a column, whether the groan from below was an engine or a voice, whether the channels are vessels or gutters, whether the whole place is a factory or a body or a body that is a factory.

This is acceptable. This is the correct outcome. The alternative — staying until you understand — is how the city incorporates you. Not through violence. Not through intention. Through the simple, patient, hydraulic logic of a system that turns heat into water and water into stone and stone into more city, forever, and does not distinguish between a wall and a visitor and a wall that used to be a visitor.

Leave on schedule. Pass through the haze. Reach the marker you left on the outside. Breathe air that does not taste of iron and lithium. Notice how dry the world is. Notice how much you miss the warmth.

Do not go back immediately. Wait at least a month. Test your blood for lithium and heavy metals. Check your skin for mineral deposits — fine, nearly invisible crusts in the creases of your hands, the hollows of your collarbones, the places where the city's water collected longest. Scrub them off gently. They will not have bonded to you yet. Not after a single visit.

After a month, if your blood work is clean and your skin is clear and you have stopped dreaming of bronze light and the sound of water moving through dark pipes, you can begin planning your return.

You will plan your return. Everyone does. The city sweats, and it is patient, and it has been waiting for a very long time without minding the wait at all.

*Final note: This guide assumes the city does not notice you. This is the optimistic scenario. Prepare for it, travel under it, and leave before you accumulate enough evidence to question it. The pessimistic scenario — that the city is aware, that the ripples and the rooftop shape and the breathing in the shafts are attentive rather than automatic — requires preparations I cannot give you, because I do not know what the Sweating City wants with the things it notices. No one has reported back on that. Draw your own conclusions about why.*
