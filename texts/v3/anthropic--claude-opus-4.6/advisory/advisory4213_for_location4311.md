# Preparing to Visit the Crevice Rookery

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation will fail if it's organized around the wrong mental model. This is not a wildlife observation site in any managed sense. There is no trail, no ranger station, no interpretive signage, no other visitors. The plateau is high, dry, remote, and essentially unmarked. The fissure itself is a geological infant — a crack that hasn't been catalogued, that doesn't appear on maps, that you could miss by twenty feet and never know it existed. You are visiting something that does not expect to be visited and has no infrastructure for the concept.

This means your preparation has two distinct phases: getting yourself safely onto the high plateau and sustaining yourself there, and then conducting yourself at the fissure in a way that doesn't damage what is clearly a fragile and self-contained ecosystem operating on its own exquisite logic. The first is a logistics problem. The second is an ethical one, and honestly the harder of the two.

---

## Physical Conditioning and Baseline Health

The plateau is high. The description mentions thin air, elevation sufficient to affect wind patterns and atmospheric moisture. You should assume you're operating somewhere between 8,000 and 12,000 feet, possibly higher depending on the variant universe's geography. This means acclimatization is not optional. If you're arriving from a lower elevation — and as a multiverse traveler you almost certainly are — you need to plan for a staged ascent or allow several days at intermediate altitudes before pushing to the plateau itself.

Acute mountain sickness is insidious because it masquerades as simple fatigue and headache, and on a featureless plateau with no shelter, the consequences of impaired judgment compound rapidly. Bring acetazolamide (Diamox) if your physiology tolerates it, but don't treat it as a substitute for proper acclimatization. Drink far more water than you think you need. The dry air at elevation will dehydrate you through respiration alone, and you'll be sweating without realizing it because the sweat evaporates almost instantly in the low humidity.

Your cardiovascular fitness should be sufficient for sustained walking over uneven ground at altitude while carrying a full pack. This doesn't require elite conditioning, but if you can't comfortably hike six to eight miles at moderate elevation with thirty pounds on your back, you're not ready. The plateau is flat, yes, but flat at altitude on loose basalt dust is more taxing than it sounds. Your footing will be uncertain. The dust shifts. The sparse grass tufts conceal small irregularities in the ground. You'll be working harder than the terrain looks like it should demand.

## Navigation

This is perhaps the single most critical preparation element, and the one most likely to be underestimated.

The plateau is described as flat and featureless. The fissure is invisible until you're nearly at its lip. There are no paths leading to it. You could walk the plateau a dozen times and miss it by twenty feet. Let that sink in. You are searching for a forty-foot crack in what is essentially a flat, dust-covered expanse of basalt under a diffuse, shadowless sky that eliminates the visual cues you'd normally use to detect terrain changes.

If you have GPS coordinates for the fissure from prior travelers' reports, load them into a reliable handheld GPS unit — not your phone, which will die in the cold and dry air faster than you expect, but a dedicated device with spare batteries. Even then, GPS accuracy of fifteen to thirty feet could put you right next to the fissure without your seeing it. So your final approach needs to be methodical: once you're within estimated range, slow down dramatically. Walk in a systematic grid pattern. Watch the ground ahead of you with extreme care. The fissure is not wide — two outstretched arms at its broadest point, maybe twelve feet — and the edges are flush with the plateau surface. In the flat, diffuse light, there will be almost no shadow to betray the opening until you're right on top of it.

The sound will be your best guide. Once you're within a few hundred feet, you should be able to hear the colony, especially if you stop walking and stand still. The sound rises out of the ground. Listen for it. In the thin air at altitude, with almost nothing else generating noise on the plateau, it should be detectable from some distance — a low, complex churring and shrieking that doesn't match the wind. Walk toward it. As it intensifies, slow down further. You are approaching a fifteen-foot drop with no barrier, no warning, no railing. The edge is sharp volcanic tuff that may be brittle at the lip where the original surface crust still clings in fragments.

Bring a compass as a backup. Mark your approach bearing from whatever landmark or drop-off point got you onto the plateau so you can reverse the route. Consider leaving small, removable markers — nothing permanent, perhaps weighted flags you'll collect on departure — at intervals during your approach so you can find your way back across the featureless terrain. The hazy pearl-grey sky may not give you reliable sun position for orientation, especially if it never fully commits to clear.

If you don't have prior coordinates, finding the fissure becomes a genuine search problem. You'd need to plan for multiple days on the plateau with sufficient supplies, walking systematic transects, listening, watching for any irregularity in the ground surface. This is feasible but dramatically increases your exposure and supply requirements. Know which scenario you're in before you commit.

## Timing Your Visit

The description reveals a clear daily rhythm, and your experience of the place will differ enormously depending on when you arrive at the rim.

**Dawn:** The colony erupts. This is the departure pulse — hundreds or thousands of dark-backed birds pouring upward out of the crack in a rushing column and dispersing in every direction. If you are standing at the rim when this happens, you will be in the flight path. The birds are emerging from a narrow slot into open air; they will be moving fast, close together, and not expecting an obstacle at the lip. Position yourself several paces back from the edge before first light if you want to witness this. The sound will be overwhelming — the overnight murmur escalating into full-throated chaos as the colony wakes and launches. The visual spectacle, in the grey pre-dawn light, will be one of the most extraordinary things you've ever seen: a living column of wings rising from a crack in the earth.

**Midday:** The only time direct sunlight enters the fissure. For roughly an hour, a blade of light moves across the eastern wall, illuminating nests in sharp detail while the rest remains in deep shadow. This is the best time for observation and photography of the interior, but it's also when the colony is at its quietest — most adults are away feeding. You'll see the structure of the place most clearly: the nests, the guano streaks, the fern, the moss, the cairn. The light contrast will be extreme. Your eyes will struggle with the transition from bright plateau to shadowed interior.

**Dusk:** The return pulse. Birds converging from all compass points, funneling down into the crack. This is the mirror of dawn but somehow more dramatic because you can watch them approaching from distance — dark specks against the hazy sky, all vectors converging on a single invisible point. The sound builds from silence to pandemonium over perhaps thirty minutes as the colony resettles, disputes erupt over nesting spots, the day's accumulated grievances are aired at volume. Then the gradual subsidence into the overnight murmur. The fissure's interior, as darkness gathers, becomes genuinely dark — you won't see into it, only hear it.

**The optimal visit** encompasses at least one dawn and one dusk. This means camping on the plateau overnight, which introduces its own preparation requirements (see below). If you can only be there for a single period, dusk is arguably the richer experience — you get the return flight, the transition into the overnight murmur, and if you stay past dark and lie on the plateau near the rim, you'll hear the colony in its most intimate register, the breathing and settling of a thousand small bodies in a slot of warm darkness.

## What to Bring

### Essentials for Plateau Survival

**Water.** There is no water source on the plateau. None. The fissure's moisture cycle is biological condensation — you cannot harvest it, and attempting to would mean entering the fissure, which you should not do (see ethics section below). Carry every drop you'll need. At altitude, in dry air, with physical exertion, budget at minimum four liters per day per person. If you're planning an overnight, bring more than you think necessary. Dehydration at altitude cascades into impaired cognition, headache, and poor decisions in a place where poor decisions could mean stepping off an invisible edge.

**Food.** Calorie-dense, low-preparation food. You won't want to cook — fire on the plateau is both difficult (thin air, wind) and unwise (smoke and flame could disturb the colony, and there's no fuel source anyway). Nuts, dried fruit, hard cheese, dense bread or crackers, energy bars. Enough for your planned duration plus an emergency day.

**Shelter.** A low-profile tent or bivouac, preferably in a muted color that won't create a visual alarm for returning birds. The plateau has no natural shelter — no trees, no boulders, nothing to block wind. Your tent needs to withstand sustained wind and stake securely into basalt dust over rock, which may require rock-weight anchoring rather than conventional stakes. A good sleeping bag rated for the overnight temperature at elevation — the description implies significant temperature drops at night (enough to cause condensation in the fissure), so budget for temperatures in the 30s°F or lower, possibly significantly lower depending on season and altitude.

**Sun and wind protection.** The hazy sky may not feel threatening, but UV at altitude is intense even through diffuse cloud, and you can burn badly without direct sun if you're not careful. Bring high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm with SPF, and quality sunglasses. A wide-brimmed hat. The wind, though described as thin, is persistent and will chap exposed skin and dry your eyes. A buff or face covering for wind protection.

### Observation Equipment

**Binoculars.** Not optional. You will be observing from the rim, and the fissure is twelve to fifteen feet deep. Good binoculars — 8x42 or 10x42 — will let you study individual nests, watch behavior on the ledges, examine the guano substrate, spot the invertebrates on the lower walls, find the fern, the moss, the cairn. They transform the experience from "looking down into a noisy crack" to "watching a city operate."

**A headlamp or small flashlight with a red-light mode.** If you're there at dusk or overnight, you'll need light for your own camp tasks, but white light directed into the fissure will disturb the birds catastrophically. Red light is less disruptive and can be used cautiously at the rim to check your footing. Even so, use it sparingly and never direct it into the crevice.

**A camera, if you want photographs.** The midday light blade offers a narrow window for dramatic interior shots, but the contrast range will overwhelm most cameras. Bring one capable of good dynamic range and consider bracketing exposures. The dawn and dusk flights are fast and chaotic — a fast shutter speed and burst mode will help, but honestly, consider watching at least the first eruption or convergence without a camera between you and it. You may not return to this place. Let the first experience be unmediated.

**A small notebook and pencil.** Not a phone, not a tablet. The dust will infiltrate electronics. The cold will kill batteries. And there's something about this place that calls for the slower practice of writing by hand. You'll want to record what you see, what you hear, what you smell — and you'll want to do it in real time, because the density of sensory information is high and memory alone won't preserve the details. The specific sound of the colony at different times of day. The color of the guano streaks. The exact position of the cairn. The shape of the fern. Pencil, because ink may not flow well in the cold.

**A hand lens or loupe, 10x or higher.** If you have any opportunity to examine the rock at the rim — the surface crust, the exposed tuff face, the fracture planes — a hand lens will reveal the internal structure described: compressed ash, tiny pumice fragments, the crystalline grain of the stone. This is new rock. You are looking at geological biography.

### Safety Equipment

**A basic first aid kit** with particular attention to wound care (the volcanic tuff is sharp and will cut you if you slip or brace against it), blister treatment (the basalt dust is abrasive underfoot), and altitude sickness medication.

**A whistle and a signal mirror.** The plateau is remote. If something goes wrong — a fall, a medical emergency, a navigation failure — you need ways to attract attention that don't rely on electronics or line of sight. The whistle carries far in thin air. The mirror works under even the hazy sky conditions described.

**Gaiters or at minimum ankle-high boots with good closure.** The basalt dust will work its way into low shoes and abrade your feet raw. Boots with stiff soles will also protect you on the sharp tuff at the fissure rim.

**Trekking poles.** Not for the flat walking, but for probing the ground ahead of you as you approach the fissure. You are walking toward an invisible edge on a featureless plateau. A pole tapping the ground ahead of your feet is a low-tech but effective way to detect the point where the ground ceases to be solid.

## How to Behave at the Rim

This is where preparation becomes something other than logistics.

The Crevice Rookery is an ecosystem in its infancy. Everything about it is poised and contingent. The birds chose this crack because it is hidden, defensible, and undisturbed. The fern exists because of a precise convergence of guano chemistry, moisture, and sheltered light. The lichen is advancing at a centimeter per decade. The beetles in the deep guano are adapted to permanent darkness at the fissure floor. The whole system — birds, guano, condensation, decomposers, plants, weathering stone — is a feedback loop that has been running for perhaps a few centuries at most, and it is fragile in the way that all young ecosystems are fragile: it hasn't yet developed redundancy or resilience. It is still being invented.

You are a large, novel organism appearing at the rim of this system. Your impact begins the moment you arrive.

**Stay back from the edge.** Not at the lip — two to three feet back minimum. The edge itself may be structurally unsound; the surface crust described as "still clinging to the upper lips" suggests that the rim rock is partially undermined and could break away under weight. Beyond the safety concern, your silhouette at the rim will alarm the nesting birds. From their perspective, the rim is the danger line — the horizon from which predators appear. A human shape looming over the edge will trigger alarm calls and potentially flushing behavior, where birds abandon nests en masse. In a packed colony, flushing cascades. Eggs and chicks can be knocked from ledges. The panic can cause real harm.

Approach slowly. Low posture. If you can observe while lying prone a few feet from the edge, do so. Move with the pace of the lichen, not the pace of the birds.

**Do not enter the fissure.** This should be obvious but must be stated explicitly. The fissure is twelve to fifteen feet deep with raw, sharp walls and a narrow floor of compacted guano over unweathered rock. There is no safe way to descend without equipment, and even with equipment, your presence inside the crack would be catastrophic for the colony. You would destroy nests, crush the guano substrate, terrify hundreds of birds in an enclosed space, and potentially damage the fern, the moss, the lichen — organisms that have taken decades or centuries to establish. The cairn, half-cemented with guano, would be dislodged. The beetles' habitat would be compressed. You would undo years of slow accumulation in minutes. There is nothing in the fissure that you need to touch. Observe from above.

**Do not throw anything into the fissure.** No coins, no stones, no offerings, no trash. The temptation to drop a pebble and listen for the echo, or to toss a scrap of food to watch the birds react, is the kind of idle human impulse that ruins places. The fissure floor is a precisely layered substrate of guano and decomposition products. A foreign object disrupts that. More importantly, any sudden intrusion from above will panic the colony.

**Minimize your scent profile.** The birds may or may not be scent-sensitive — their petrel-like characteristics suggest they might be, since many petrels have well-developed olfactory systems. Avoid strong fragrances: no scented sunscreen, no cologne, no strongly scented food at the rim. Eat and store food well back from the edge.

**Do not leave anything behind.** Not markers, not trash, not waste. Pack out everything, including human waste. The plateau's ecosystem is sparse enough that introduced nutrients from human waste could alter the soil chemistry in the immediate area around the fissure. Use a waste bag system.

## The Ammonia

You will smell the rookery before you hear it, or possibly at the same moment. The description notes ammonia. This will be strong. If you have ever been near a large seabird colony, multiply that intensity by the concentrating effect of the fissure — the ammonia doesn't disperse across open rock or cliff face as it would in a surface colony. It rises from the crack in a concentrated plume, especially in the warmth of midday when the thermal dynamics of the fissure are most active.

If you have a sensitive respiratory system, consider a cloth face covering for extended rim observation. Don't use a chemical respirator — it's overkill, and the unfamiliar appearance and sound of your breathing through it could disturb the birds. A simple bandana dampened with water (from your supply) and held over your nose and mouth will take the edge off. Your eyes may water. This is normal.

The smell will be in your clothes and hair when you leave. It will be in your notebook. Accept this. It is the smell of a living system doing its work.

## The Sound

Nothing in this preparation guide can adequately prepare you for the sound. The description tries — shrieks, croaks, guttural churring, the clatter of wings, the percussive fencing of beaks — but reading about it and standing in it are different orders of experience.

The sound of a dense colonial bird rookery in an enclosed space is physical. The churring will vibrate in your sternum. You'll feel it in your teeth. It is not music, and it is not noise in the way traffic or machinery is noise. It is a living, modulating, responsive wall of vocal and mechanical sound produced by hundreds of individual sources at slightly different frequencies and rhythms, and your auditory system will oscillate between trying to parse individual calls and surrendering to the aggregate wash.

Bring earplugs if you're sensitive. You probably won't want to use them — the sound is too much a part of the experience — but have them available. If you're camping nearby overnight, you'll need them for sleep. The colony doesn't go silent. It subsides to a murmur, but the murmur of a thousand birds in a resonant stone chamber is still substantial.

Some travelers report that the colony sound, heard from the plateau at night while lying in a sleeping bag, is one of the most profound sounds they've ever heard — the earth itself seeming to breathe and mutter. The fissure acts as a resonating chamber. Low-frequency components of the colony's overnight vocalization are amplified and shaped by the rock walls, producing a sound that doesn't quite match what you'd expect from birds. It sounds geological. It sounds like a planet making up its mind about something.

## Emotional and Psychological Preparation

This might seem like an odd section in a practical preparation guide, but the Crevice Rookery has a quality that catches people off guard, and it's worth addressing directly.

The plateau is empty. Searingly, fundamentally empty. It is high and dry and far from anything. The sky commits to nothing. The ground is dust. You walk and walk and there is nothing, and your mind begins to flatten to match the terrain. Then the sound begins rising from below your feet, and the ground opens, and you're looking down into a crack teeming with life — a whole civilization packed into a wound in the rock, steaming and screaming and breeding and dying, the walls painted with the evidence of their sustained existence, a fern unfurling in the guano, beetles processing the dead in the dark, and all of it hidden, all of it invisible from twenty feet away.

The contrast is vertiginous. Not physically — the fissure isn't deep enough for true vertigo — but conceptually. You go from absolute emptiness to absolute fullness in the space of two steps. The rookery is a revelation in the literal sense: something hidden suddenly revealed. And the fact that it has been here, carrying on this entire complex operation in secret, on a plateau that seemed to contain nothing — it does something to your understanding of landscapes, of surfaces, of what might be happening just below or just beyond the threshold of your perception.

Many travelers describe a feeling of intrusion that sharpens into something closer to reverence. You are witnessing something that doesn't need you, doesn't know about you, and was not designed for your experience. The cairn — that single ambiguous evidence of prior human presence, now half-absorbed by the colony — is a reminder that the human relationship to this place is brief, peripheral, and ultimately consumed by the ongoing project of the birds. Whatever that person intended when they stacked those three flat stones, the birds have decided it's a nesting platform. Your visit will be similarly metabolized. You will come and look and leave, and the colony will continue.

This is not a melancholy realization. It's clarifying. But it can be disorienting if you arrive expecting the usual transaction between visitor and destination — the sense that the place exists in some way for your experience of it. The Crevice Rookery does not. Prepare for the feeling of being completely irrelevant to something magnificent.

## The Cairn

You will want to see it. Everyone does. It's on the eastern wall, partway along the fissure, about four feet below the rim — reachable by eye from above, though depending on the overhang of the rim you may need to position yourself carefully to get a line of sight.

Three flat stones, dinner-plate sized, stacked deliberately. Two nests on the top stone. Guano cementing the assembly to the wall. This is all that remains of the last human to stand where you're standing.

Don't try to add to it. Don't try to build your own. Don't leave a mark. The impulse to make your presence known — to say *I was here too* — is deeply human and, in this context, deeply inappropriate. The cairn is meaningful precisely because it is singular and because it has been claimed. Adding to the human record here would only add to what the birds will eventually absorb. Let the one anonymous cairn-builder represent all of us. They did it first. It's enough.

## Departure

When you leave — and you should leave well before dark if you're not camping, because navigating the featureless plateau in darkness is genuinely dangerous — walk slowly away from the rim. Don't stand up abruptly from a prone observation position; the sudden silhouette change will alarm the near-rim nesters. Ease back, rise slowly, and walk.

The sound will follow you. It diminishes with distance, but on the quiet plateau it carries, and you'll hear the colony for longer than you'd expect — a hundred yards, two hundred, maybe more in still air. There will be a point where you stop hearing it and the plateau silence reasserts itself: the wind, the dust, your breathing.

Turn around at that point and look back. You won't see the fissure. It's already invisible — a hairline crack in a vast flat expanse of basalt dust and sparse brown grass. Nothing marks it. Nothing announces it. A thousand birds, a fern, a cairn, and the slow patient work of biological weathering on new stone, all hidden in a line you can no longer distinguish from the ground.

Carry that. The knowledge that the plateau is not empty. That the earth's surface is not a reliable report of what lies just below. That in the cracks where the ground disagrees with itself, entire worlds take hold and persist, unwitnessed and unconcerned.

Drink some water. Check your bearing. Start walking.
