# Preparing for the Plateau of Held Breath: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## Preliminary Assessment: What You Are Actually Walking Into

Before we discuss a single piece of gear, you need to understand what this place will do to you. Not what it *might* do. What it *will* do. Because the Plateau of Held Breath is not hostile in any way your survival instincts are calibrated to recognize. It will not burn you, freeze you, poison you, or attack you. It will do something far more dangerous: it will make you want to stay. It will make staying feel like the most natural, the most *correct* thing you have ever done. And if you stay long enough, you will become part of it — not through violence or transformation, but through a kind of thermodynamic inevitability, the way a hot cup of coffee becomes room temperature, the way a spinning top eventually stops.

This is not a place that kills visitors. It is a place that *completes* them. And completion, in this context, means the cessation of every internal process that distinguishes you from the stone and the silent things that live on it.

Your preparation must therefore be organized around two parallel tracks: **physical survival** in a high-altitude, waterless, timeless environment, and **psychological defense** against an extraordinarily subtle form of assimilation that operates through comfort, satisfaction, and the progressive erosion of your desire to do anything other than be present.

Neither track is optional. Neglect the first and you die of dehydration on ancient rock. Neglect the second and you don't die at all, which is worse.

## Physical Preparation

### Hydration: The Central Crisis

There is no water on the Plateau. None. The description is unambiguous about this — not a seep, not a trace of moisture, not even residual dampness in cracks. The rock has forgotten water so completely that the concept becomes foreign. This means every molecule of water you will use must arrive with you, and you must plan for the possibility that your sense of time will be compromised, making it dangerously easy to forget to drink.

**Water supply:** Carry a minimum of 8 liters for what you intend to be a short visit (under what would normally be 24 hours), and understand that "short" is a concept the Plateau will immediately begin to dissolve. At high altitude with thin, dry air, you will lose moisture through respiration at an accelerated rate, and the alkaline mineral dust in the atmosphere will exacerbate dehydration of mucous membranes. You may not feel thirsty. The Plateau's settling-into-stillness effect may suppress the urgency of thirst signals along with everything else. This is potentially lethal.

**Water storage:** Use sealed, rigid containers — not bladders, not soft bottles. The mineral dust will work its way into any opening, and the dust is alkaline. Contamination of your water supply with even small quantities of powdered calcium compounds will accelerate the very dehydration you're trying to prevent. Wide-mouth Nalgene-style bottles with screw-top lids, sealed additionally with waterproof tape over the threads. Carry at least two backup containers in case one is compromised.

**Forced hydration schedule:** This is critical. You cannot rely on thirst or habit. Carry a timer — a mechanical timer, with a physical alarm, something that will make noise in that terrible silence. Set it for 20-minute intervals. Every 20 minutes, you drink 200ml of water. No exceptions. No "I'll drink at the next alarm." The timer goes off, you drink. Tape the protocol to the inside of your wrist where you will see it even if your mind begins to drift.

**Electrolyte supplementation:** The thin, dry air and the mineral dust environment will cause electrolyte imbalances faster than simple water loss would suggest. Carry oral rehydration salts — WHO-formula if possible — and add them to every second water intake. The alkaline taste of the air may make you feel like you don't need salt. You do.

**Emergency water reserves:** Carry an additional 4 liters in a sealed container that you do not open for regular hydration. This is your extraction reserve — the water you drink only when you are actively leaving. Mark it clearly. Do not touch it otherwise.

### Atmosphere and Altitude

The air is thin. You are at genuine high altitude, possibly equivalent to 3,500-5,000 meters based on the described respiratory effort. The air has a mineral, alkaline quality. There are no weather fluctuations, no pressure changes — just a constant, thin, cool atmosphere.

**Acclimatization:** If your transit method allows it, spend 2-3 days at intermediate altitudes before ascending to the Plateau. If direct transit is unavoidable, begin taking acetazolamide (Diamox) 24 hours before arrival — 125mg twice daily. This will help your kidneys compensate for the respiratory alkalosis of high altitude. Yes, it makes carbonated drinks taste flat. You won't find any carbonated drinks on the Plateau.

**Respiratory protection:** The mineral dust is fine, constant, and alkaline. Over hours of exposure, it will irritate and dry your airways, compounding the altitude effect. Carry a lightweight, moistened buff or gaiter for your nose and mouth — dampen it with plain water every hour. A proper half-face respirator with P100 filters would be more effective but may feel suffocating at altitude. Compromise: carry both, use the buff for general wear, switch to the respirator if you notice any cough, bloody mucus, or increasing respiratory difficulty.

**Supplemental oxygen:** Carry a small personal oxygen supply — one of the lightweight mountaineering systems with a flow regulator. Not for continuous use, but for moments when you realize you've been breathing too slowly, synchronizing with the Plateau's metabolic rhythm, and your oxygen saturation has dropped. A pulse oximeter on your finger, checked at every hydration alarm, will tell you when you need it. If your SpO2 drops below 85%, use oxygen and begin your departure protocol immediately.

**Pulse oximeter note:** Get one with a backlight and a physical alarm function. If your SpO2 drops below 88%, it should beep. Loudly. The sound will be obscene in that silence. Good. You need obscenity here. You need things that are rude, that don't fit, that disrupt the terrible peace.

### Temperature and Exposure

The temperature is cool and constant — described as cellar-like, cave-breath-like. Estimated range: 8-14°C (46-57°F). No fluctuation, no wind chill, no direct sun exposure (the light is diffuse). This sounds comfortable, and it is, and that comfort is part of the danger.

**Clothing:** Layer for moderate cold with an emphasis on sustained comfort over extended periods, because the insidious nature of this environment is that you won't notice time passing and hypothermia at these temperatures is slow, subtle, and easy to confuse with the Plateau's invitation to stillness.

- **Base layer:** Merino wool, medium weight. Not synthetic. Synthetics work through moisture management, and the air is so dry that moisture management is almost irrelevant. Merino maintains warmth even when slightly damp with perspiration and has a natural resistance to odor — relevant because you may be there longer than you plan.
- **Mid layer:** Light fleece or wool sweater. Something you can open or remove easily, because the temperature won't change but your activity level will.
- **Outer layer:** A light, windproof shell. Not for rain — there will never be rain — but for the faint constant air movement that your skin will stop registering. Just because you stop feeling it doesn't mean it stops extracting heat.
- **Lower body:** Wool or wool-blend hiking pants over merino base tights. The rock will be cold when you sit on it, and you will want to sit.
- **Extremities:** Light gloves. A hat that covers your ears. Wool socks, medium cushion. The rock surface will conduct heat away from your feet; insulated insoles in your boots.
- **The stone is smooth in the enclosures.** Comfortable to sit on. *Carry a foam sitting pad anyway.* Not primarily for comfort — for the symbolic act of placing something between yourself and the Plateau. A barrier. A reminder that you are *on* this place, not *of* it.

### Nutrition

You will not be hungry. I want to be clear about this — the Plateau suppresses the urgency of biological signals, and hunger is among the first to go. You will feel no appetite. Food will seem unnecessary, even slightly absurd, even slightly *rude*, like eating loudly during a funeral. Ignore this feeling. The feeling is the Plateau talking.

**Caloric density:** Carry high-calorie, low-volume food that requires no preparation, no water for cooking, and minimal chewing (chewing produces sound; the silence will make you reluctant to chew; this is a real psychological barrier and you should prepare for it). Nut butters in squeeze packets. Energy gels. Chocolate. Dense seed bars. Pemmican if you can stomach it.

**Forced eating schedule:** Same as hydration — timer-driven. Every 60 minutes, consume 300-400 calories. Set a separate alarm or tie it to every third hydration alarm.

**Flavor:** Bring things that taste *strong*. Aggressively flavored. Sour candy. Chili-spiced jerky. Ginger chews. Wasabi peas. The Plateau is a place of muted sensation — grey rock, grey sky, grey animals, grey silence. Strong flavors are a sensory anchor. They are a way of reminding your nervous system that it exists as a separate thing, that it has preferences and responses, that it is not a uniform field of quiet metabolism.

## Equipment

### Navigation and Extraction

This is the most important category and the one most likely to fail, because the Plateau is vast, featureless, and your sense of direction and urgency will degrade the longer you stay.

**The fundamental problem:** You need to be able to leave when it's time to leave, and the Plateau will make "time to leave" feel like a meaningless phrase. The mechanisms by which time announces itself have gone quiet. There is no sun movement, no shadow change, no circadian cue. Your internal clock will drift and then stop. You must externalize every timing function.

**Mechanical watch with alarm function:** Not digital — mechanical. I don't say this for anti-technology reasons. I say it because the Plateau seems to exist in a state of temporal equilibrium that may affect electronic timing mechanisms in unpredictable ways. A mechanical watch runs on a spring. It degrades at a known rate. Wind it before you arrive. If it begins to run slow — if your 20-minute hydration alarms start feeling longer — that's information about the environment, and it's information you need.

**Backup digital timer:** Carry one anyway, in case I'm wrong about electronics. Compare it to the mechanical watch at every alarm cycle. If they diverge, trust the mechanical watch and accelerate your departure.

**Maximum visit duration:** Decide before you arrive how long you will stay, and do not change this decision on the Plateau. I recommend a maximum of 6 hours as measured by your mechanical watch for a first visit. Write this number on your hand. Write it on a card in your pocket. When your watch says your time is up, you leave. You will not want to. You will feel that you just got here, that you haven't really experienced it yet, that a little more time wouldn't hurt. This is the Plateau finishing the sentence it started when you arrived. Leave anyway.

**Extraction route marking:** Whatever method you used to arrive, mark it. Physically, visually, unmistakably. If you walked in from an edge, leave a trail of high-visibility flagging tape tied to stakes driven into rock cracks every 50 meters. Use fluorescent orange or lime green — colors that do not exist anywhere on the Plateau. The visual disruption is part of the point. If your arrival method is a fixed portal or transit point, surround it with a ring of flagging tape, chemical light sticks, anything that screams *artificial, alien, not of this place*.

**Compass:** Bring one. The fixed light source from the west gives you a constant directional reference, which is genuinely helpful — unless you stop being able to see it, or unless you stop caring about direction. A compass with a physical needle provides a second reference. Mark your arrival bearing. Your departure bearing is the reverse. Write it on your hand next to your departure time.

**The enclosures face west.** Toward the light. Remember this if you become disoriented. If you find yourself sitting in an enclosure facing the light, you are facing west. Your extraction is unlikely to be in that direction. Turn around. Stand up. Walk.

### Communication and Anchoring

**Voice recorder, continuously running:** Carry a small voice recorder and press record when you arrive. Talk into it. Narrate what you see, what you feel, what you're doing. Every hydration alarm, state your name, the time on your watch, and your planned departure time. Not because you'll need the recording later — because the act of speaking is an act of *change* in a place that has abolished change. Your voice in that silence is a tiny engine of entropy, and entropy is what keeps you human here. Speak even when it feels absurd. Speak *especially* when it feels absurd.

**Photographs:** Take them compulsively. Not to document the Plateau — though the documentation has value — but because the act of framing, choosing, and capturing an image is an act of decision-making, of imposing your will on the visual field, and decision-making is the thing the Plateau will try to dissolve first. Every time you raise a camera and choose *this* and not *that*, you are practicing agency.

**Letters from people who love you:** I'm serious. Carry physical letters — handwritten if possible — from people in your life who are expecting you back. Read one at every eating interval. The handwriting is important. It is irregular, personal, and alive in a way that the Plateau's perfect geological patience is not. It is evidence that somewhere, outside the lens-clean horizon, there is a world where things change and people care about the changes and someone specifically wants you to come home.

**Music:** Carry a music player with headphones. Load it with music that is complex, emotional, and rhythmically varied — not ambient, not drone, not minimalist. The Plateau is already the greatest ambient album ever composed and you do not need more of that. You need Charlie Parker. You need Beethoven's late quartets. You need music that *argues with itself*, that changes its mind, that accelerates and pauses and surprises. Play it during the middle third of your visit, when the silence has had time to normalize and your auditory cortex is starting to accept the aggregate hum as baseline reality.

### Scientific Instruments (Optional but Recommended)

If you have the capacity, carry instruments. Not primarily for data — though data from this place would be extraordinary — but because instruments extend your sensorium beyond the Plateau's ability to numb it. Instruments tell you things your body has stopped reporting.

**Thermometer:** Digital, logging, with a probe. Check it regularly. If the temperature is truly constant, that itself is data. If it changes — even by a tenth of a degree — that change is precious. It means the Plateau is not quite as finished as it presents itself to be.

**Barometer/altimeter:** For the same reason. Log the readings. Any variation is a crack in the Plateau's argument, and cracks are where you maintain your grip.

**Geiger counter or radiation detector:** The light source behaves anomalously. The biological density is inconsistent with the available energy budget. Something is powering this system and it isn't photosynthesis as you understand it. A radiation detector may not answer the question, but it may tell you whether the question has a conventional answer.

**Magnifying glass or hand lens:** For examining the moss-circles, the crawling things, the knapped blade. Close observation is an act of intellectual engagement, and intellectual engagement is a form of motion, and motion is what the Plateau has learned to do without.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section that matters most. The physical preparation keeps your body functional. The psychological preparation keeps *you* inside your body.

### Understanding the Threat Model

The Plateau does not have malice. I want to emphasize this because preparing to resist malice is very different from preparing to resist what the Plateau actually offers, which is *completion*. *Satisfaction*. *Rest*. The Plateau is the end of every gradient — thermal, temporal, evolutionary, geological. It is the place where everything has already happened and now simply *is*. And it offers you that same resolution: the end of striving, the end of wanting, the end of the restless, uncomfortable, perpetually incomplete state of being a living thing that hasn't finished yet.

This is seductive at a level that operates below conscious thought. It bypasses your decision-making entirely and speaks directly to the part of your nervous system that has been tired since the day you were born — the part that has been maintaining homeostasis against entropy for your entire life and has never once been able to stop. The Plateau says: *you can stop now. Everything is already done. Sit down. Hold a smooth stone. Breathe with us.*

The defense against this is not willpower. Willpower is a finite resource and the Plateau is patient beyond any human capacity for resistance. The defense is **structured externality** — systems, alarms, rituals, and anchors that keep you tethered to the world of change even as the Plateau persuades you that change is unnecessary.

### Pre-Visit Psychological Conditioning

**1. Practice deliberate discomfort.** In the weeks before your visit, cultivate a tolerance for — and even a preference for — incompleteness. Leave tasks unfinished on purpose. Stop books in the middle. Walk away from conversations before they reach resolution. You are training your mind to be comfortable with open loops, because the Plateau's power lies in its offer of closure, and a mind that craves closure will be drawn toward it like water toward a drain.

**2. Establish a departure ritual.** Design a specific sequence of actions that you will perform when your timer says it's time to leave. Make it physical, make it multi-step, make it slightly ridiculous. Example: Stand up. Stamp your right foot three times. Say your mother's maiden name out loud. Turn in a full circle. Touch your nose. Begin walking toward your extraction point. The absurdity is functional — it is so incongruent with the Plateau's solemn, ancient stillness that performing it will feel like a violation, and that feeling of violation is your proof that you still have preferences, that you still find some things inappropriate, that you are still a creature of social norms and not a warm hillock on cold stone.

**3. Prepare a mantra of impermanence.** Choose a phrase that reminds you of change, of motion, of the unfinished nature of your life. Not something grandiose. Something personal and specific. "I haven't finished painting the kitchen." "My daughter is expecting me for dinner on Thursday." "I still don't know how the book ends." Repeat it at every hydration alarm. The specificity matters. "I want to leave" will stop feeling true very quickly. "I told Marcus I'd return his drill" has a texture that the Plateau cannot easily smooth away.

**4. Study the blink pattern.** You will blink in synchrony with the herd beasts. This is described as involuntary. During the synchronized blink, the world *changes* — shadows shift, positions alter, the substrate hiccups — and you cannot observe it because your eyes are closed during the interval. This is concerning for several reasons, but the most immediate practical concern is that the synchronized blink may be the mechanism by which the Plateau integrates you. Each blink may be a small edit, a tiny adjustment, a fractional reduction in the difference between you and everything else.

**Countermeasure:** Carry an eye patch or a blindfold that covers only one eye. When you feel the blink urge building — and based on the description, there is a perceptible rhythm — close one eye deliberately and force the other to remain open. You may see what happens during the interval. You may wish you hadn't. But seeing it is better than being edited by it without your knowledge.

**5. Do not sit in the enclosures.** I don't care how smooth the stone is. I don't care how perfectly shaped for rest they are. The enclosures were built by something that is now indistinguishable from its environment, and the smooth floors were worn by the long sitting of many things that came before you and are now part of the Plateau. The enclosures are not traps — they are *outcomes*. They are what comfort looks like at the end of time. If you feel drawn to sit in one, that is the most important moment to trigger your departure ritual. Stamp your foot. Say your mother's maiden name. Turn. Touch your nose. Walk.

**6. Do not pick up the rounded stones.** They are pleasing in the hand. The description tells you so. That pleasingness is not incidental — it is the end product of a long process of shaping that was once intentional and is now automatic, and the boundary between *holding a stone* and *becoming a thing that holds a stone* and *becoming a thing that is a stone being held* is not as clear here as it would be elsewhere. Look at the stones. Photograph them. Do not touch them.

**7. Do not look at the nesting colonies for extended periods.** The interference patterns will show you the laminated depth structure — the layers beneath layers, the infinite stack of frozen plateaus descending into impossible depth. This information may be true. It may even be important. But the *act of perceiving it* requires your visual cortex to synchronize with the colonies' pulse rhythm, and synchronization with any aspect of the Plateau's biology is a step toward integration. Glance. Note. Look away. Photograph if you want to study the pattern later, at a safe distance, in a world where things still change.

### Buddy System

Do not go alone if you can possibly avoid it.

Travel with a partner, and establish a mutual monitoring protocol:

- **Check-in every alarm cycle.** State your name, their name, the time, your planned departure time.
- **Code word for "I want to stay."** Agree on a word that means "I am feeling the pull and I need help leaving." Because you may not have the capacity to articulate what's happening to you in full sentences when it's happening. The word should be something harsh, percussive, ugly — something that sounds wrong in the silence. "CACTUS." "BRICK." "HONK."
- **Physical tether.** Consider a light rope or cord connecting you, with a maximum length of 10 meters. Not to prevent wandering — to provide a constant physical sensation of connection to another changing, incomplete, unfinished human being. A tug on the rope is a communication, and communication is an act of change, and change is your salvation here.
- **Staggered observation.** One person observes the Plateau while the other faces away and reads, eats, talks, does anything *other* than attend to the environment. Switch every 30 minutes. This ensures that at least one of you is always engaged with something other than the Plateau's patient invitation.

If your partner stops responding to check-ins — if they sit down and go quiet and look peaceful — **do not wait to see if they snap out of it.** Initiate physical contact immediately. Shake them. Shout their name. Use the code word. Pour cold water on them if necessary. Get them moving. Get them loud. Get them *incomplete* again.

If you must go alone, the voice recorder becomes your buddy. Talk to it constantly. Argue with it. Tell it jokes. The recordings will be strange to listen to later — a human voice chattering against a silence so deep it makes the audio waveform look like a thin scratch on a vast grey surface — but the strangeness is evidence that you came back different from the Plateau, and *different* is a synonym for *alive*.

## The Deeper Concerns

### The Laminated Structure

If the nesting colonies' revelation is accurate — if the Plateau is indeed the top layer of an effectively infinite stack of identical frozen ecosystems embedded in the rock — then several implications follow:

1. **The Plateau may be self-replicating.** Each layer may have been the surface once. Each layer may have been a place where things moved and changed and built stone enclosures, until the change ran out and a new layer formed on top, inheriting the pattern. You may be standing on the leading edge of a very slow process of lithification of *life itself*.

2. **The inhabitants may not be alive in a way you understand.** They may be *impressions* — biological fossils that have retained metabolic function without retaining biological purpose. The heartbeat-pulses of the nesting colonies, the loop-paths of the crawling things, the endless descent of the gliders — these may be residual patterns, the last twitches of processes that completed their purpose eons ago and now simply repeat because nothing has arrived to make them stop.

3. **You could become a layer.** Not quickly. Not violently. But the process that creates new layers may operate on visitors — it may begin with synchronization (the blinking), proceed through spatial integration (sitting in the enclosures, holding the stones), and culminate in something that is not death but is not life either: a permanent, metabolically active presence in the geological record, breathing once per epoch, blinking in unison with everything else, your proteins slowly mineralizing around the core of your still-conscious experience.

This is speculative. But the Plateau rewards caution more than curiosity.

### The Builders

Something built the enclosures and shaped the stones and knapped the blade. Something with hands, with intention, with a concept of *space-making* that implies a concept of *self* distinct from *environment*. That something is no longer distinguishable from the rest of the Plateau's inhabitants.

This means one of two things:

1. The builders left. (But where? The horizon is clean in every direction.)
2. The builders are still here, distributed among the motionless bodies, their agency absorbed back into the substrate.

If it's the second — and the description strongly implies the second — then the Plateau has already demonstrated its ability to take beings with human-level cognition and tool-use capacity and integrate them so completely that they become indistinguishable from the fauna. The builders didn't fail. They *succeeded*. They built their enclosures, shaped their stones, made their tools, and then they sat down in the smooth-floored spaces they had made and they *finished*. They reached their argument's end, just like the mountains.

You are a builder. You have hands, intention, a concept of self. The Plateau has already processed your type before. It knows how you work. It knows that the smoothness of the stone and the pleasingness of the spheres and the comfort of the enclosure facing the eternal west are the exact inputs that cause beings like you to transition from *visitor* to *resident* to *feature*.

### The Blink

The synchronized blinking is the single most alarming feature of the Plateau, and you should think carefully about its implications.

During the blink, the world changes. Shadows shift. Positions alter. The substrate rearranges. But no observer can see this happening because every eye on the Plateau closes simultaneously — including yours. The description states this directly: "You blink with them. You cannot help it."

This means:

- The Plateau has already synchronized one of your involuntary biological functions with its collective rhythm, from the moment you became aware of the pattern.
- The changes that occur during the blink are hidden by design or by consequence — they can only happen unobserved.
- You are being changed during these intervals and you cannot know how.

The one-eye patch countermeasure I described above is the best I can offer, but I want to be honest: it may not work. The blink urge may be so profound that it overcomes the patch, or seeing what happens during the interval may itself be a vector of integration — the Plateau may *want* you to see, and the apparent hiding may be an invitation disguised as a prohibition.

If you use the patch and see something during the interval that makes you want to remove the patch and blink normally — **leave immediately.** Do not pass through another blink cycle. Whatever you saw was not meant to be refused, and the fact that you want to comply means the integration is already advanced.

## Rules for the Visit

Write these on a card. Carry the card. Read it at every hydration alarm.

1. **You are leaving at [TIME].** This is not negotiable. This was decided before you came here. The you who decided was thinking clearly. Trust that person.

2. **Drink when the alarm sounds.** Every time. No skipping.

3. **Eat when the alarm sounds.** Even if food seems absurd.

4. **Speak.** Out loud. Often. The silence is not sacred. It is a symptom.

5. **Do not sit in the enclosures.**

6. **Do not pick up the rounded stones.**

7. **Do not stare at the nesting colonies.**

8. **If you feel peaceful, that is a warning sign.** Peace here is not peace. It is the sensation of your gradients flattening. Respond to peace the way you would respond to chest pain: with immediate, concrete action.

9. **If you blink with the herds and want to keep blinking with the herds, leave now.**

10. **You are unfinished. You are incomplete. You have things left to do. This is not a flaw. This is what it means to be alive. The Plateau has nothing left to do. The Plateau is not alive in any way that matters to you. Do not envy it. Leave.**

## After You Return

You will feel strange. The world outside will seem noisy, chaotic, wasteful, full of unnecessary motion and pointless change. You will feel a nostalgia for the Plateau that is disproportionate to the time you spent there — a deep, bone-level longing for that quality of stillness, for the smooth stone, for the unhurried breathing of the herd-beasts, for the silence that was full instead of empty.

This is normal. It fades. Let it fade.

Do the following in the first 72 hours after return:

- **Be around people.** Noisy, messy, unpredictable people who want things and argue and change their minds.
- **Make something.** Cook a meal. Build a shelf. Write a letter. The act of creation — of imposing intention on material — is the opposite of what the Plateau offered, and your hands need to remember that they are for *making*, not for *holding smooth stones in an enclosure forever*.
- **Break something unimportant.** A cracker. A stick. An old plate. The sound of something breaking is the sound of irreversibility, of change that cannot be undone, and it is the sound the Plateau cannot make.
- **Do not research the Plateau.** Do not look at your photographs for at least a week. Do not listen to your voice recordings. Let the experience settle into memory, where it will lose its edges and become manageable. If you engage with it too soon, while the longing is still fresh, you will begin planning your return.

And if you do plan your return — if the longing doesn't fade, if you find yourself staring at blank walls and feeling that they're almost smooth enough, almost still enough — talk to someone who has been there. Not to share the experience. To hear them tell you that they felt the same pull, and that the pull is the last thing the Plateau placed in them before they left, a seed of completion planted in their incompleteness, and that the only thing to do with it is to let it remain unfinished.

You are an unfinished thing. Stay that way. It's the only way to stay at all.
