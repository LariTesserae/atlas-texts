# Preparing for The Roost at Kethane Ridge

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before I lay out the practical preparation, I want to be honest about something: this is not a hostile location in the conventional sense. Nothing here is described as aggressive toward visitors. But it is one of the most physiologically demanding and psychologically disorienting environments a multiverse traveler can encounter, precisely because it is so *indifferent* to your presence. The ridge is a complete, closed, self-sustaining system. You are not a participant. You are not prey. You are not even an intrusion—you are simply irrelevant, a transient anomaly in something ancient and self-contained. That indifference, combined with the environmental extremes, is what makes this place dangerous. You will not be attacked. You will be eroded.

Prepare accordingly.

## Respiratory and Atmospheric Preparation

This is the single most critical dimension of preparation, and you need to take it seriously enough that it shapes every other decision.

**The atmosphere is hot, thin, sulfurous, and bone-dry.** Each of these is a problem. Together, they are compounding and potentially lethal over even moderate exposure times.

### Altitude and Thin Air

The description emphasizes considerable elevation and thinness of air. You're dealing with something functionally equivalent to high altitude on Earth—possibly 4,000 meters equivalent or higher, though in an alien atmospheric composition the partial pressures may not map neatly to terrestrial altitudes. The point is: your blood will not carry oxygen as efficiently as you're used to.

**Pre-acclimatization:** If you have access to altitude simulation before the trip—hypoxic tents, altitude training masks with calibrated resistance, or better yet, time spent at genuine high-altitude locations in other accessible universes—spend at minimum two weeks acclimatizing. Your body needs time to upregulate red blood cell production. There's no shortcut that doesn't carry its own risks.

**Supplemental oxygen:** Bring it. A closed-circuit rebreather system is ideal because it conserves your supply and doesn't vent gases into the local environment. You don't know how the ecosystem will respond to an exogenous oxygen source, and introducing concentrated O₂ into a sulfurous atmosphere near geothermal venting carries genuine risk of unwanted chemical reactions. A rebreather also lets you filter and condition the air you're breathing, which matters enormously given the other atmospheric challenges.

**Acetazolamide or equivalent:** If your physiology supports it, pharmaceutical altitude prophylaxis is worth taking. Start 24-48 hours before arrival. It promotes bicarbonate excretion and helps your body adjust its blood pH to compensate for hyperventilation at altitude. It will make you urinate more—a serious consideration in a waterless environment, so plan your hydration accordingly.

### Heat

The air is hot. The ground is hot. The sky radiates warmth downward. You are in a convective oven with no cold sink. Your body will attempt to thermoregulate through sweating, and in the desiccating atmosphere, that sweat will evaporate almost instantly, which means you'll lose water at a tremendous rate while potentially failing to *notice* how much you're sweating because your skin will feel dry.

**Core temperature monitoring:** Wear a continuous-read core temp sensor. Ingestible thermometer pills are the gold standard—you swallow one before arrival and it transmits your core temperature to an external reader for 24-36 hours. When your core temp hits 38.5°C, you need to start thinking about withdrawal. At 39.5°C, leave. No exceptions. Heatstroke in a remote, waterless environment with thin air is not survivable without immediate intervention.

**Cooling:** Active cooling garments—vests or full suits with circulating coolant—are strongly recommended. The best systems use a small reservoir of water and a battery-powered pump that circulates it through tubing woven into the garment. The problem is weight. Every piece of gear you carry, you carry up that basalt scree slope, in the heat, at altitude. There is a brutal calculus here between thermal safety and the energy cost of hauling the equipment that provides it. I'll address this more under load planning.

**Passive thermal management:** At minimum, your clothing should be light-colored (to reflect what ambient thermal radiation exists), loose-fitting (to allow air circulation), and made of materials that wick and breathe. But understand: in an environment where the radiant heat comes from *below and above simultaneously*, light-colored clothing helps less than you'd think. You're not dealing with solar radiation. You're dealing with omnidirectional infrared. Reflective metallic underlayers may actually serve you better—space-blanket-type material incorporated into your base layer to reflect IR back away from your skin.

### Sulfur

"Faintly sulfurous" is doing a lot of work in that description. On Earth, hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) is detectable by smell at concentrations as low as 0.5 parts per billion. At low concentrations it smells like rotten eggs. At higher concentrations—above 100 ppm—it causes olfactory fatigue, meaning *you stop being able to smell it precisely when it becomes most dangerous*. At 500+ ppm it can cause rapid unconsciousness and death.

You do not know the concentration on the ridge. "Faintly sulfurous" might mean 1 ppm—irritating but not dangerous for short exposures. Or it might mean the traveler who wrote the description had already undergone partial olfactory fatigue and what they perceived as "faint" was actually a much higher concentration.

**Gas detection:** Carry a multi-gas detector calibrated for H₂S, SO₂, and CO₂ at minimum. Clip it to your chest where you can glance at it constantly. Set audible alarms well below dangerous thresholds. H₂S alarm at 10 ppm. SO₂ at 2 ppm. CO₂ at 5,000 ppm (0.5%). If any of these trip, you need to reassess whether your respiratory protection is adequate and whether continued presence is safe.

**Respiratory filtration:** Your rebreather should handle this, but if you're using a simpler system, ensure your filters are rated for acid gases. Standard particulate filters will not remove H₂S or SO₂. You need combination cartridges—typically activated carbon with specific impregnants for acid gas adsorption. Carry spares. In a continuously sulfurous environment, cartridge life will be shorter than rated.

**Eye protection:** Sulfurous gases attack mucous membranes, including conjunctivae. Sealed goggles. Not safety glasses, not sunglasses—*sealed* goggles with indirect ventilation through chemical-rated filters, or better yet, a full-face mask integrated with your breathing system.

### Desiccation

The atmosphere will strip moisture from every exposed tissue. Your throat, your nasal passages, your eyes, your lungs—all of these are lined with mucous membranes that require moisture to function. The description specifically calls out "desiccates the membranes of your throat with each breath."

**Humidification:** If your breathing apparatus allows it, run the intake air through a humidifier or at minimum a damp filter medium before it reaches your airways. Even a simple HME (heat and moisture exchanger) filter—the kind used in medical ventilator circuits—placed inline in your breathing path will recover some of the moisture from your exhalation and return it to your inhalation.

**Nasal and ocular hydration:** Carry saline spray for your nasal passages and preservative-free artificial tears for your eyes. Apply both prophylactically at regular intervals—every 30 minutes is not too frequent in this environment.

**Skin:** Exposed skin will crack. Even if your clothing covers most of you, your face (to the extent it's not covered by your mask), your hands if you remove gloves, any exposed skin will lose moisture rapidly. Heavy-duty emollient—petroleum jelly-based or lanolin-based, something that forms a true occlusive barrier—applied before exposure and reapplied as needed.

## Hydration and Nutrition

**Water is the primary consumable.** You will burn through it at an extraordinary rate. Between altitude-driven respiratory water loss (you breathe faster and harder, exhaling more moisture), heat-driven sweating, and the desiccating atmosphere, a conservative estimate for water consumption in this environment is 1-1.5 liters per hour of active time. For a visit of any meaningful duration—say 6-8 hours on the ridge—you're looking at 8-12 liters of water minimum, plus reserves.

Twelve liters of water weighs twelve kilograms. This is your single heaviest supply item and there is no way around it. **There is no water on the ridge.** The description is emphatic about this. Not a seep, not condensation, nothing. You cannot resupply locally. You carry everything you need or you die of dehydration in an environment that will kill you faster than a terrestrial desert.

**Electrolytes:** You're not just losing water. You're losing sodium, potassium, chloride, magnesium. Drink electrolyte-enhanced water, not plain water. Hyponatremia from drinking large volumes of plain water while sweating heavily is a real risk. Oral rehydration salts mixed into your supply, or electrolyte tablets dissolved as you drink.

**Food:** Secondary to water but not irrelevant. Your metabolic rate will be elevated from the heat stress and the altitude. Bring calorie-dense, easily digestible food that doesn't require water to prepare. Nuts, dried fruit, energy bars, pemmican-equivalent. Nothing that will make you thirsty. Nothing that requires chewing with a dry mouth—you'll be surprised how difficult that becomes.

**Carrying system:** The water has to come up the scree slope with you, in the heat, at altitude. This is the crux of the logistics problem. Consider staging: if your transit route to the ridge allows it, cache water at the base of the scree slope and make multiple trips, or use an intermediate cache point partway up. Alternatively, if you have access to any kind of mechanical assistance—a pulley system, a pack animal adapted to the conditions, even a simple drag sled on the smoother sections—use it. Your body is a heat engine operating at reduced efficiency in this environment. Every calorie you spend hauling gear is a calorie that accelerates your dehydration and heat stress.

## Clothing and Personal Protection

### The philosophy of layering here is inverted.

In most hostile environments, you layer for insulation—trapping heat near your body. Here, you layer for *isolation*—keeping environmental heat *away* from your body while allowing your own metabolic heat to escape.

**Base layer:** Moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool. Its job is to move sweat away from your skin. In this environment, sweat evaporates almost instantly, so the base layer's wicking function is less about evaporative cooling (which will happen regardless) and more about preventing salt crystal accumulation on your skin, which causes irritation and, over time, sores.

**Mid layer:** This is where your active cooling system lives, if you're using one. Tubing woven into a vest or shirt. If you're going passive, this layer should be your reflective IR barrier—a thin, metallic-film-lined garment that bounces thermal radiation away from your body.

**Outer layer:** Durable, loose, light-colored, full-coverage. Long sleeves, long legs, high collar. You want to minimize exposed skin for both desiccation prevention and protection from contact with the local biology. More on that shortly.

### Gloves

You will want to touch things. The stone, the placed artifacts, possibly the organisms. **Don't.** Or rather—don't with bare hands.

The vethryn hide is described as "half keratin and half something crystalline." Crystalline biological structures can be sharp at the micro-scale. The hand-creatures have serrated fingertips. The biofilm crust is of entirely unknown chemistry. The drifter filaments are "sticky or perhaps absorptive." Any of these could cause contact reactions ranging from mechanical abrasion to chemical burns to absorption of unknown bioactive compounds through your skin.

Wear gloves at all times on the ridge. Nitrile or butyl rubber inner gloves for chemical resistance, with a durable outer glove for mechanical protection. If you must handle specimens or touch surfaces, do so through the gloves and decontaminate afterward. Carry spare gloves—the environment will degrade rubber over time, and you don't want to discover a pinhole breach after you've been handling the crust.

### Footwear

**Critical.** You are climbing a scree slope of fist-sized rounded basalt that is warm to the touch, then standing and moving on a ridge surface that is either bare hot rock, living biofilm crust, or the bodies of large animals. 

Boots should be: stiff-soled (to prevent bruising on the rounded scree and to distribute pressure on uneven biological surfaces), heat-resistant (the ground temperature is elevated—standard hiking boot soles may soften), high-ankled (for stability on loose scree), and chemically resistant on the lower surfaces (you're standing in or on unknown biological material).

Consider an insulating insole. Not for cold—for heat. You want a thermal break between the hot ground and your foot. Felt or aerogel insoles, the kind designed for workers on hot industrial surfaces, would serve.

**Traction:** The scree is rounded and will shift under your feet. The biofilm crust is of unknown slipperiness. The vethryn hides—if you find yourself stepping on them, which seems almost inevitable given how thoroughly they cover the ridge—could be slick or could grip unpredictably depending on the nap of their crystalline fibers. You want aggressive tread but not sharp lugs that might injure a vethryn (and provoke an unknown response). Vibram-style rubber with moderate depth and close spacing.

## Biological Hazard Preparation

This is an alien ecosystem of unknown biochemistry. Every organism you encounter is a potential source of:

- **Allergens:** Novel proteins that your immune system has never encountered. Anaphylaxis is possible from inhalation of shed biological material (crystalline fiber dust from vethryn, spores from the crust's fruiting bodies, cellular debris from deflated drifters).
- **Toxins:** The crust smells of "old blood and hot quartz." Many biological defense chemicals produce metallic or sweet odors. The origami-creatures produce an "almost electric" crackling—possibly a chemical discharge.
- **Pathogens:** Less likely to jump to an entirely alien biology, but prions, small organic toxins, and some chemical messengers can cause damage regardless of biological compatibility.
- **Contact irritants:** The drifter filaments are explicitly absorptive. They're designed to extract material from surfaces they touch. They will try to extract material from *you* if they contact your skin.

### Precautions:

**Respiratory protection (already covered):** Your rebreather or filtered mask is your first line of defense against inhaled biological material.

**Full-body coverage (already covered):** No exposed skin.

**Antihistamines:** Carry both a fast-acting oral antihistamine (diphenhydramine or equivalent) and an epinephrine auto-injector. If you begin experiencing facial swelling, throat tightness, hives, or difficulty breathing beyond what the altitude and sulfur explain, use the epinephrine and evacuate.

**Decontamination:** Carry a spray bottle of dilute bleach solution or broad-spectrum antimicrobial wash. Before you leave the ridge, decontaminate your outer surfaces—boots, gloves, any equipment that contacted the local biology. You don't want to carry novel organisms back to your point of origin.

**Don't eat anything.** This should go without saying, but I'm saying it. Don't taste the crust. Don't touch the crystalline fibers to your tongue. Don't drink condensation from the drifter membranes. The ecosystem is alien and you have no way to assess biochemical compatibility.

**Sample collection:** If you're collecting biological samples (and the temptation will be enormous), use sealed, airtight containers. Double-bag everything. Label meticulously. Do not open containers until you're in a controlled laboratory setting with proper biosafety protocols. Treat every sample as BSL-3 equivalent until proven otherwise.

## Equipment for Observation and Documentation

This is a place of extraordinary biological complexity, and much of it happens at scales or in conditions that make casual observation inadequate.

### Light

There is almost no ambient light. What exists is biological—the vethryn bioluminescence, pulsing in amber and burnt orange. Your eyes will adapt to the dark over 20-30 minutes, and you should *let them*. Dark-adapted vision will reveal far more of the ridge's activity than any flashlight will. The bioluminescence is the native illumination, and the organisms have evolved around it. Introducing bright artificial light could disrupt behavior, suppress bioluminescence, startle the vethryn, or attract the shard-hunters.

**Red-filtered headlamp:** If you need task lighting—checking your gas detector, adjusting equipment, consulting notes—use a dim red-filtered light. Red wavelengths are least likely to disrupt dark adaptation (yours or the organisms') and are typically least reactive for bioluminescent species.

**For documentation:** A camera with extreme low-light capability. Modern full-frame sensors at very high ISO, or better yet, a purpose-built scientific imaging system with cooled sensors and long-exposure capability. You'll want:
- A fast lens—f/1.4 or wider
- A tripod or stabilization system (though finding stable ground is going to be challenging)
- Infrared imaging capability—many bioluminescent organisms also emit in near-IR, and the thermal environment will be rich in IR data. A thermal imaging camera will reveal heat patterns in the vethryn mass, the crust activity, the drifter columns—an entirely different view of the ecosystem.

**Audio recording:** The soundscape is described as rich and layered—subsonic vethryn breathing, electric crackling, wing hums, stone-fall. Bring a high-quality field recorder with a wide frequency response. A pair of matched condenser microphones in a stereo configuration for spatial recording. A contact microphone to place on the stone surface to capture the sub-bass tectonic vibration. These recordings may be the most valuable documentation you bring back—sound carries information about biological rhythms and relationships that visual observation may miss.

### Magnification

Much of the ecosystem's complexity is at small scales. The bead-like organisms on the hand-creatures, the serrated fingertips, the crystalline fiber structure of vethryn hide, the fruiting bodies of the crust—all of these demand close examination.

**Hand lens:** 10x and 20x, illuminated (dim red LED). Keep it accessible.

**Macro lens for your camera:** 1:1 reproduction ratio minimum. A dedicated macro setup will let you document the small-scale biology that is invisible at arm's length.

**Binoculars:** For observing the drifter column and shard-hunters above. Low-light optimized—large objective lenses, high-quality coatings. 7x50 or 8x56 configuration.

## Navigation and Orientation

There is no sky. No stars, no sun, no moon. No compass directions (magnetic field behavior is unknown). No GPS (no satellites). The massif disappears into darkness in both directions. You are on a narrow ridge in three-dimensional darkness with the only spatial references being the ridge itself, the scree slope you climbed, and the placed stones.

**Mark your ascent route.** The scree slope is your only known path in and out. In the dark, with the disorientation that heat and thin air and biological strangeness will produce, losing the route back is a genuine possibility. Use reflective markers—small adhesive strips or clipped flags on stakes—placed at regular intervals along your ascent path. Use something that won't be absorbed by the crust or colonized by the hand-creatures during the hours you're on the ridge. Metal stakes with reflective tape are probably your best option.

**Carry an altimeter/barometer.** Even without GPS, you can track relative altitude. Note your elevation at the base of the scree, at the ridge, and at key landmarks. If you become disoriented, descending to the correct altitude is at least a partial navigation solution.

**Map the ridge mentally and on paper before you move through it.** The ridge is only 200 meters long and 60 wide. That's a small area. Sketch it. Note the stone arc, the upright obsidian tool, the narrowing blade where the ridge drops away. These are your landmarks. Orient to them constantly.

**Do not go past the blade edge.** The ridge narrows to a point and drops into an abyss of unknown depth. The description notes that falling stones produce no audible impact. There is no reason to approach that edge and every reason not to.

## Behavioral Protocols: How to Be on the Ridge

This is perhaps the most important section.

### Move slowly

Everything on this ridge moves slowly except the shard-hunters and the origami-creatures, and even those operate in brief bursts. You are entering an ecosystem tuned to gradual, tidal rhythms. Rapid movement will stand out. The vethryn are watching. They have four eyes each and forty to fifty of them are present. You will be observed continuously. Move at the pace of the crust—deliberately, smoothly, without sudden gestures.

### Minimize your footprint (literally)

The ridge is saturated. Every surface is occupied. You *will* step on things. The crust, at minimum, is unavoidable. Try to step on the crust rather than on vethryn or on the hand-creatures. Watch your foot placement with extreme care. If you're using a red headlamp, use it for foot placement checks. 

If a vethryn is in your path, do not step over it, do not push it, do not attempt to move it. Go around, or wait. The vethryn shift on their own schedule. You have time. You are not in a hurry. There is nowhere on this ridge that is urgent.

### Do not touch the placed stones—at first

The stone arc and the upright obsidian tool are the only clearly non-biological artifacts on the ridge. They represent the only evidence of prior visitation or intelligence. They are also the only features the vethryn actively avoid. This avoidance may be incidental or it may be meaningful. The arc may be a territorial marker or a sacred boundary placed by someone or something that understood the ridge's ecology. Until you've observed for a long time—hours, not minutes—and have a sense of whether the placed stones carry behavioral significance, leave them alone. Observe from a slight distance. Note the vethryn behavior near the arc. Note whether the hand-creatures colonize the upright stone differently than they colonize vethryn hide. Note whether the crust approaches the placed stones or avoids them.

If and when you do enter the stone arc—and the description invites you to sit there—do so slowly, and be prepared for the possibility that your presence in that space changes something. The vethryn avoid it. If you occupy it, you are occupying a space they recognize as different. How they interpret a large warm novel organism sitting in the space they avoid is unknowable in advance.

### Do not interfere with predation events

When the shard-hunters arrive to feed on the drifters, the entire ridge tenses. You will feel the change in atmosphere before you understand it cognitively. The vethryn will compress. The bioluminescence may dim. The hand-creatures will retract. You will feel the urge to duck, to shield yourself, to react to the predator presence. Resist this. The shard-hunters are not hunting you. You are not part of this food web. Stay still, stay low, and observe. The punctured drifter membranes will fall around you. Let them fall. Don't catch them, don't dodge them. They're being recycled.

### Manage your emotional response

This is not a trivial point.

The Roost at Kethane Ridge is overwhelming. Not because it is dangerous (though it is) or alien (though it is) but because it is *dense*. The sheer biological richness of the place, the layered complexity, the thousands of simultaneous interactions happening at every scale from microscopic to the size of a horse—it will saturate your senses. In the dark, in the heat, with thin air and sulfur and the subsonic breathing pressing against your chest, there is a real risk of a kind of cognitive overwhelm. Not panic. Something quieter and more insidious: a feeling of being absorbed, of dissolving into the living mass of the ridge, of losing the boundary between yourself and the ecosystem.

This is partly physiological. Hypoxia produces euphoria and impaired judgment. Heat stress produces confusion. Infrasound (the sub-bass tectonic vibration) is known to cause feelings of unease, awe, depersonalization, and in some people a sense of "presence"—the feeling that something is watching you or that you are in a sacred space. The vethryn *are* watching you, which will reinforce this.

Bring a timer. Set it to alert you at regular intervals—every 30 minutes. When it goes off, perform a self-check:
- What is my core temperature?
- When did I last drink water?
- How much water do I have left?
- What time is it? How long have I been here?
- Can I clearly state my name, my purpose, and my route back to the scree slope?

If you can't answer any of these clearly, you're impaired and you need to begin your descent.

## Emergency Preparation

### Evacuation route

You have one way in and one way out: the scree slope. Descending loose scree in the dark, at altitude, while potentially heat-stressed or dehydrated, is extremely hazardous. Falls are likely. The rounded stones will shift and roll underfoot.

**Trekking poles:** Essential for scree descent. Adjustable, with carbide tips for grip on basalt. They give you two additional points of contact.

**Helmet:** The scree could shift above you. Other travelers could dislodge stones. Wear a climbing helmet.

**Emergency bivouac:** If you become too impaired to safely descend, you may need to wait on the ridge until you recover enough to move. The stone arc is the obvious shelter—the only vethryn-free space. Carry a lightweight emergency bivy sack, though you won't need it for warmth. Its main use would be as a barrier between you and the local biology if you need to lie down and rest. You do not want to fall asleep directly on the crust.

### Medical

Beyond the antihistamines, epinephrine, and altitude medication already mentioned:
- **Oral rehydration therapy supplies** (in case of severe dehydration)
- **Dexamethasone** (for high-altitude cerebral edema, which can develop rapidly at elevation, especially with concomitant heat stress)
- **Basic wound care** (adhesive bandages, antiseptic, gauze—in case of falls on the scree or cuts from the crystalline biology)
- **Chemical cold packs** (for emergency core cooling if heatstroke develops—apply to neck, armpits, groin)
- **A whistle or personal locator beacon** adapted to whatever recovery infrastructure exists in this universe—if any does

### Communication

If you are traveling with a partner or a support team at the base of the scree, maintain communication via radio. Check in every 30 minutes (sync it with your self-check timer). Agree on abort signals and a protocol for non-contact: if you miss two consecutive check-ins, your support team should begin ascending to your last known position.

**Do not travel to this ridge alone.** The combination of altitude, heat, atmospheric contamination, total darkness, and disorienting biology makes solo travel recklessly dangerous. A partner who can recognize your cognitive decline when you cannot, who can assist with an evacuation, who can make medical decisions if you're incapacitated—this is not optional.

## Duration Planning

This is not a place for extended occupation. Plan for **4-8 hours on the ridge**, not days. The water requirements alone make multi-day stays logistically prohibitive without resupply infrastructure that doesn't exist in this environment.

A recommended timeline:

- **Hour 0-1:** Ascend the scree slope. Establish yourself on the ridge. Allow dark adaptation. Get your bearings. Begin gas monitoring. Locate the stone arc and the obsidian tool. Note the current state of the vethryn—their density, their breathing rhythm, the bioluminescence pattern. Note whether shard-hunters are present or absent.
- **Hour 1-3:** Primary observation period. Move slowly across the ridge (if possible). Document. Record audio. Photograph. Take notes. This is when your senses will be sharpest and your physical condition strongest.
- **Hour 3-5:** Deep observation. By now you should have a sense of the ridge's rhythms. If you haven't witnessed a shard-hunter feeding event, remain and watch for one. If you plan to enter the stone arc, this is the time. Sit. Be still. Let the ridge continue around you.
- **Hour 5-6:** Begin preparing for departure. Final documentation. Decontaminate gear. Hydrate aggressively for the descent.
- **Hour 6-7:** Descend. Take your time. The scree is more dangerous going down than going up.
- **Hour 7-8:** Buffer time for delays, rest stops, and the unexpected.

Carry enough water and supplies for at least 10-12 hours in case something goes wrong.

## Mental and Philosophical Preparation

I've saved this for last, but it's not least.

The Roost at Kethane Ridge is a place that will challenge your sense of scale and significance. You are visiting an ecosystem that is ancient, stable, complete, and utterly unconcerned with your existence. The vethryn will watch you the way they watch everything—with patient, four-eyed vigilance that does not distinguish between you and a fallen drifter membrane. The crust will advance toward your boots the moment you stand still. The hand-creatures will begin exploring your equipment if you set it down. You are warm and you are material and you are therefore relevant to the ridge's metabolism, if not to its meaning.

The placed stones suggest that someone came here before you and felt the need to mark the place. The tally marks—or whatever they are—suggest counting. Counting what? Visits? Vethryn? Days in the dark? Heartbeats? The marks are deep. Whoever made them pressed hard. They wanted the record to last, and it has lasted, and it says nothing you can read.

You may find this humbling. You may find it terrifying. You may find it deeply peaceful—the assurance that something can persist in the dark, packed with life, breathing together, needing nothing from you.

Whatever you find, bring it back carefully. Decontaminate your gear, drink water, check your core temperature, and walk slowly down the scree, testing each stone before you trust it with your weight.

The ridge will be there when you leave. It will still be breathing. The crust will close over your footprints within the hour.
