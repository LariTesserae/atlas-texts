# Preparation Guide for Visiting The Grown City

---

## First: A Reckoning with Your Own Irrelevance

Before you pack a single item, you need to prepare the part of you that isn't physical.

You are going to a place that does not need you, has never needed anything like you, and will not acknowledge your presence in any way. This is not hostility. This is not the indifference of a dangerous wilderness that might kill you without noticing. This is something more profound and more disorienting: a place of extraordinary sophistication and evident purpose that simply has no category for you. You are not a threat. You are not a guest. You are not even a curiosity. You are a temporary chemical imbalance on the moss floor, and when you leave, the moisture from your footprints will be reabsorbed within minutes and there will be no record you were ever there.

Most travelers can handle danger. Most can handle discomfort. What destabilizes people is *functioning irrelevance* — being surrounded by complex, purposeful activity that operates on principles you cannot participate in, influence, or fully comprehend. People who have visited places like this report a creeping existential unease that builds over hours and can, in susceptible individuals, become something closer to despair. Not because anything bad happens. Because nothing happens *to them*, and nothing ever will, and the city is so clearly, so magnificently *doing something* that your inability to be part of it starts to feel like a judgment.

It is not a judgment. There is nothing here capable of judging you. But your mind will try to make it one, because your mind evolved to read intention into complex systems, and this system is more complex than almost anything you have encountered, and it has no intention toward you whatsoever.

**Practical recommendation:** Before departure, spend time — real time, days if you can manage it — practicing being in places where you are genuinely unnecessary. Sit in a forest and do nothing. Watch an anthill for four hours. Attend a ceremony in a language you don't speak, for a religion you don't practice, in a community that doesn't know you're there. Get comfortable with the feeling of the world operating perfectly well without your input. This will not inoculate you against what the Grown City does to your sense of self, but it will give you a frame of reference.

Some travelers also benefit from studying contemplative traditions — certain strains of Buddhism, Taoism, the more mystical branches of various faiths — that treat the dissolution of ego-centrality as a positive experience rather than a threatening one. If you can arrive at the Grown City already half-willing to let go of the idea that you are the protagonist of your surroundings, you will fare better than someone who arrives expecting to explore, discover, and understand. You will not understand this place. Understanding is not on offer. Presence is on offer. That is all.

## The Physical Environment: What You're Walking Into

### Climate and Atmosphere

The plateau exists in a state of permanent warm humidity. Based on every account available, the temperature holds steady somewhere in the range of 24–28°C (75–82°F) with humidity consistently above 85%, possibly above 90%. This does not fluctuate. There is no day-night cycle as far as anyone has been able to determine — the light remains fixed in its perpetual late-afternoon state, which means the temperature does not drop when you sleep, the moisture does not condense into dew because there is no cooling, and your body never gets the thermal relief that a normal diurnal cycle provides.

This is more physically taxing than it sounds. In tropical environments on standard Earth, the nighttime temperature drop — even a modest one — gives your thermoregulatory system a few hours of recovery. Here, there is no recovery window. Your body will be working to cool itself continuously for the entire duration of your visit. Sweat will not evaporate efficiently because the air is already saturated. You will be damp at all times. Not soaked, not dripping, but persistently, inescapably damp. Your skin, your clothes, your gear — everything will develop that faintly clammy quality within the first hour and will never fully dry.

**What this means for your body:** You will lose electrolytes steadily through sweat that doesn't evaporate but instead just accumulates on your skin and clothing. You will need to hydrate aggressively, and you will need to supplement sodium, potassium, and magnesium. Heat exhaustion is a real risk, not because the temperature is extreme, but because the duration is indefinite and there is no cool shelter to retreat to. Every structure in the city is made of living, transpiring plant tissue — the interiors of the tower-rooms will be, if anything, *more* humid than the open plazas, because the walls themselves are exhaling moisture.

### The Light

The fixed, unchanging, shadowless-not-quite-shadowless illumination is disorienting in ways that go beyond mere strangeness. Your circadian rhythm will begin to drift almost immediately. Without light cues to anchor your internal clock, your sleep-wake cycle will start to degrade within 24–48 hours. You may find yourself unable to tell how long you have been there. Travelers consistently report difficulty estimating elapsed time — some believe they spent two days when they spent five; others believe they spent a week when they spent thirty hours.

The quality of the light itself — even, diffuse, warm-grey, without directional shadows — also affects depth perception and spatial orientation in subtle ways. You are accustomed to using shadow and light-angle as cues for distance, surface texture, and three-dimensional form. Without these cues, the city can begin to look flattened, or alternatively, impossibly deep, depending on your perceptual tendencies. Some travelers report mild derealization — the sense that the environment is a painted backdrop rather than a three-dimensional space. This is a perceptual artifact, not a property of the city, but it can be distressing.

### The Sound

The harmonic drone of the flower-pipes is the dominant sensory feature of the city, and you need to understand what you are dealing with: it is not background noise. It is a continuous, multi-layered, harmonically rich sound environment that is *loud*. Not painfully loud — no one reports physical discomfort — but loud enough that normal conversation would require raised voices, loud enough that you are always, always aware of it.

The drone is also, by all accounts, *beautiful*. This is part of the danger. The sound has a quality that many travelers describe as hypnotic or trance-inducing — a deep, sustained, choral resonance that seems to vibrate in your chest cavity and that your brain very much wants to interpret as meaningful, as music, as communication. It is not communication. It is wind through tubes. But the harmonic relationships between the various flower-pipes, shaped by the architecture's spacing and the consistent wind, produce something that your auditory cortex cannot help but process as structured, intentional sound. People have sat down to "listen" and lost hours. People have wept without knowing why. The sound does something to the emotional centers of the brain — not through any exotic mechanism, just through the ordinary human response to sustained, resonant, harmonically complex tones — and if you are tired, or dehydrated, or already feeling the existential displacement described above, the drone can push you into altered states that are difficult to pull yourself out of.

**Practical recommendation:** Bring high-quality earplugs and use them in rotation — an hour with protection, an hour without, according to a schedule you set in advance and stick to regardless of how you feel. Do not let yourself "just listen for a while" without a timer set. The drone does not change. There is nothing new to hear in the next hour that you didn't hear in this one. Your brain will tell you otherwise. Your brain is wrong.

### Water and the Wet

The city is saturated. Every surface is damp. The root-floors, while not slippery in the way ice is slippery, have a persistent organic moisture that makes footing uncertain, especially on inclines. The moss-carpeted plazas are soft underfoot, like walking on a wet mattress, and your feet will sink slightly with each step. The interior spiral-stairs of the towers are damp heartwood — solid, not rotten, but with a surface moisture that demands careful movement.

The water in the city's channels and pools is, as far as anyone has been able to determine, clean — biologically active but not pathogenic to human visitors. It has a mineral taste, slightly sweet, with a faint vegetal quality. Travelers have drunk it without ill effect, but I would recommend filtering and treating it anyway, because the mycorrhizal networks and root systems are processing that water for their own purposes, and what is benign to a plant-city's metabolism may contain compounds your body would prefer not to encounter. More on water treatment below.

## Gear and Equipment

### Footwear

This is the single most important equipment decision you will make.

You need footwear that is comfortable when perpetually wet, that provides grip on damp organic surfaces, that supports extended walking on soft and uneven ground, and that will not develop fungal problems or begin to decompose in the constant humidity. You will be wearing these shoes or boots for the entire visit, including while sleeping (because the ground you sleep on is also wet, and going barefoot on the root-mass invites cuts from fine root-fibers that are difficult to see and slow to heal in humid conditions).

**Recommendation:** Amphibious trail shoes or lightweight hiking boots made with synthetic uppers and drainage ports. Not waterproof — waterproof is worse than useless here, because your feet will be wet from sweat regardless, and a waterproof boot just traps the moisture inside. You want something that lets water pass through freely and dries as quickly as possible (which, in this environment, means it won't fully dry, but at least it won't pool). Aggressive tread pattern for grip on organic surfaces. Neoprene socks underneath — they insulate even when wet and reduce friction blistering.

Bring two pairs of shoes. Alternate daily. Wring out and air the pair you're not wearing, even though "airing" in 90% humidity is a cruel joke. It's still better than nothing.

### Clothing

Cotton will kill you. Not literally, but it will make you miserable. Cotton absorbs moisture, holds it against your skin, becomes heavy, chafes, and provides a perfect medium for bacterial growth. Within two days in cotton clothing, you will be dealing with rashes, skin maceration, and the beginnings of fungal infection.

Wear synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics exclusively. Lightweight, loose-fitting, with as much ventilation as the garments can provide while still covering enough skin to protect against the fine root-fibers and rough bark surfaces you'll inevitably brush against. Long sleeves and long pants, counterintuitive as that feels in warm humidity — the environmental hazards to your skin are numerous enough that coverage outweighs the marginal cooling benefit of exposed skin.

Bring more clothing than you think you need. Changing into a "dry" set (it won't be dry, but it will be less saturated) every 8–12 hours makes an enormous psychological difference. Hang the used set from your pack or from convenient branches — and there will always be convenient branches — to air out.

A lightweight rain jacket is useful not for rain (there appears to be no rain, just cloud-drift and condensation) but as a vapor barrier when sleeping, and as a windbreak in the few areas where the plateau's constant wind is channeled between structures into a stronger flow.

### Shelter and Sleeping

You don't need a tent. There's no rain, no direct sun, no wind strong enough to require solid shelter, and the temperature is constant. What you need is a way to sleep that keeps you slightly elevated above the saturated ground surface.

**Recommendation:** A lightweight hammock with an integrated insect net (even though insects appear rare, you don't want to discover an exception while unconscious) and a wide rain fly set up not for rain but as a condensation shield — moisture will drip from overhead canopy structures, and waking up to a slow drip of warm plant-water on your face at three in the morning (or what your watch tells you is three in the morning, since the light won't have changed) is deeply unpleasant.

The towers present an interesting option. Their interior rooms — the hollowed chambers created by the spiral heartwood growth — are, by several accounts, usable as sleeping quarters. The floors are solid wood, merely damp rather than saturated. The walls provide some buffer from the drone (being inside a living wooden shell reduces the volume to a lower, more tolerable hum). And the rooms feel, undeniably, like rooms. There is something psychologically grounding about sleeping in a space that has walls and a ceiling and windows, even if it was never intended for you, even if the small ferns on the windowsill will not acknowledge your presence.

If you sleep in a tower, choose a room on the third or fourth floor. Ground-level rooms are the dampest. Upper floors, while drier, require navigating the spiral root-stairways in whatever state of exhaustion or disorientation you're in, and a fall on wet wood in a hollow tower with no one around to help is an emergency you cannot afford. Middle floors balance moisture, access, and safety.

Bring a sleeping pad regardless — closed-cell foam, not inflatable (an inflatable pad in this humidity will develop condensation inside the air chambers and eventually the valve will corrode or the seams will weaken). You'll use it as insulation between your body and whatever surface you're sleeping on.

### Navigation and Timekeeping

**Navigation:** The city has a discernible structure — radial avenues converging on the central dome, a rough grid of cross-streets in the tower-districts, the open plazas providing landmarks. But the uniformity of the light, the absence of cardinal-direction cues (no sun position, no star field), and the organic irregularity of the architecture make it surprisingly easy to become disoriented.

Bring a compass. Whether magnetic compasses function reliably on this particular plateau is something you should test immediately upon arrival — if the young basalt beneath the root-mass is magnetically anomalous, your compass may be unreliable or useless. If the compass works, use it continuously, maintaining a running azimuth log as you move through the city. If it doesn't work, fall back on dead reckoning: pace counts, direction estimation, and a hand-drawn map that you update obsessively.

The central dome is your primary landmark. It is the largest and most distinctive structure. If you can see it or hear the difference between its silent white flowers and the droning towers, you can orient yourself. Always know the direction of the dome relative to your position.

GPS will almost certainly not function. The sky — whether it is real atmosphere or some kind of canopy structure — does not appear to permit satellite contact. Do not rely on electronic navigation.

**Timekeeping:** Bring two watches, both mechanical. One for current time (set to whatever schedule you want to impose on yourself — sleep times, eating times, turnaround times), one as a backup. The unchanging light will destroy your time sense within the first day. Without a watch, you will have no idea how long you have been walking, how long you have been sitting, how long you have been staring at the dome. The watches are not just practical tools. They are psychological anchors. They are the only things in the city that will change at a rate you can understand.

Set alarms. For meals, for sleep, for hydration, for turning back. Obey the alarms even when they feel arbitrary, especially when they feel arbitrary.

### Water and Food

**Water:** The city's water system is extensive and accessible. You will never be far from a root-channel or an upwelling pool. The water is, as noted, apparently potable — but treat it anyway. Bring a pump filter with a ceramic element (avoid UV purifiers, which require battery power you may want to conserve; avoid chemical treatments, which take time and don't address potential novel compounds in the water). Filter everything you drink. The filter will also give you something to *do* — a small, practical, human-scale task in a place that offers no tasks and needs nothing from you.

**Food:** There is nothing here for you to eat. The flowering structures are not fruiting bodies. The ferns are ornamental (insofar as anything here is ornamental). The moss may or may not be edible, but no one has tested it and you should not be the first. The salamander-like animals are rare and you should not hunt them — not for ethical reasons (though those may apply) but because you have no idea what their biochemistry contains, and because disturbing the city's few animal visitors feels like a violation of something you cannot name but will feel strongly.

Bring all your food. Calorie-dense, shelf-stable, requiring no cooking (you cannot make fire here — everything is wet and alive, and attempting to burn any part of this city strikes me as an act of such staggering wrongness that I won't even address the practical difficulties). Trail meals, nut butters, dense energy bars, dried fruit, jerky. Plan for 2,500–3,000 calories per day — the constant thermoregulatory effort burns more energy than you'd expect for what is essentially walking and standing around.

Pack your food in waterproof containers. Not zip-lock bags — rigid, sealed, waterproof containers. The humidity will find any weakness in your food storage and exploit it. Anything that can absorb moisture will absorb moisture. Crackers will soften. Dried fruit will re-hydrate and potentially begin to ferment. Powdered drink mixes will clump into paste. Protect everything.

### Medical Considerations

The primary medical risks are:

**Skin maceration and fungal infection.** Your skin will be wet for the entire visit. Prolonged skin wetness leads to maceration — softening, whitening, and breakdown of the outer skin layers, making you vulnerable to cuts, abrasion, and infection. The warm, wet environment is ideal for fungal growth. Treat this as an inevitability rather than a risk.

*Bring:* Antifungal cream (clotrimazole or miconazole), apply prophylactically to feet, groin, and any skin folds twice daily. Zinc oxide cream or petroleum jelly to create moisture barriers on vulnerable areas. Foot powder (talc-free, cornstarch-free — both will just become paste; use a silica-based powder if you can find one). Gauze and breathable adhesive tape for covering any skin breaks.

**Blisters.** Inevitable with wet feet on uneven ground. Bring blister treatment supplies — moleskin, hydrocolloid bandages, a sterile needle for draining. Address blisters immediately. In this environment, a neglected blister becomes an infected wound within a day.

**Respiratory considerations.** The air is thick with moisture, pollen from the perpetual flowers, and aerosolized organic compounds from the transpiring plant tissue. If you have asthma or reactive airway disease, bring your rescue inhaler and consider prophylactic use of a corticosteroid inhaler for the duration. Even without pre-existing conditions, you may notice some airway irritation — a scratchy throat, mild cough — from the constant inhalation of biologically active air. A buff or lightweight mask worn loosely over the nose and mouth can help.

**Psychological distress.** This is the risk I am most concerned about, and the one I can least prescribe for. The combination of sensory monotony (unchanging light, unchanging sound, unchanging temperature), social isolation (you are alone here; even if you bring companions, the city's indifference to your group is identical to its indifference to you individually), temporal disorientation (no day-night cycle, no environmental change to mark time's passage), and the profound, bone-deep *irrelevance* that the city projects — this combination can produce psychological states ranging from mild dissociation to profound existential crisis.

Travelers have reported: a growing conviction that they have always been here and their previous life was imagined. A terrifying sense that the city is aware of them but choosing not to respond. Uncontrollable weeping triggered by the drone. A desperate urge to damage or mark the city in some way just to prove they can affect *something*. A desire to enter the central dome so intense it resembles compulsion.

*Bring:* A journal. Write in it regularly — observations, feelings, measurements, sketches, complaints, anything. The act of writing externalizes your internal state and gives you evidence that your mind is still functioning, still producing, still *doing something* in a place that asks nothing of you. Bring photographs from home, letters, small personal objects that anchor you to your identity outside this place. If you take medication for anxiety or mood disorders, bring more than enough for your planned stay plus a generous buffer. Set a maximum visit duration before you arrive and treat it as inviolable — when your watch says it's time to leave, leave, regardless of how you feel about it, especially if you feel like staying.

## Conduct and Ethics

### Do Not Take Anything

Nothing. Not a leaf, not a bark sample, not a cup of water, not a fern from a windowsill. I know you will want to. I know the scientific and personal impulse to bring back a piece of this place will be nearly overwhelming. Do not.

This is not a moral directive based on the city's "feelings" — it does not have feelings. This is practical advice based on two considerations. First: you do not know what the city's response to damage is. Everything here is interconnected. The mycorrhizal network extends everywhere. Removing tissue from one location may trigger responses — chemical, structural, hydraulic — in other locations, responses you cannot predict and may not recognize until they have made your environment hostile. No traveler has reported the city becoming hostile, and I would like to keep it that way.

Second: every organism you remove from this city enters a context — your pack, your transit route, your home world — where it does not belong. The biological sophistication of these organisms is beyond anything in standard botanical science. You do not want to be the person who introduces a fragment of the Grown City's mycorrhizal network to an ecosystem that has no defenses against it.

### Do Not Attempt to Enter the Dome

I know. I know you will want to. I know that after hours or days of walking through a city that offers no mysteries, no locked doors, no secrets — the dome, sealed and silent, will call to you with an almost gravitational pull. I know that the sound you hear through its walls — that deep, fundamental, growth-sound — will seem like it's *just barely* about to become comprehensible, and if you could just get inside, you would understand everything.

You would not understand everything. You might not understand anything. And the dome is sealed — not with a lock you could pick or a door you could force, but with fused living wood that you would have to cut or tear to penetrate. See above, regarding damage. The dome does not open because the dome is not meant to open. Whatever process is occurring inside it is occurring in darkness, in enclosure, in conditions that the city has gone to extraordinary effort to maintain. Respect that. You are irrelevant here, and the most respectful thing irrelevance can do is not attempt to make itself relevant through destruction.

### Move Slowly and Carefully

Not because the city is fragile — it isn't; the root-mass floors are robust, the bark walls are extraordinarily durable, the structures have withstood their own weight and the constant wind and water for however long they've been here. Move slowly because *you* are fragile in this context. The surfaces are wet. The stairways are organic spirals with irregular risers. The bridges, while structurally sound, have no railings (why would they — no one was meant to walk on them) and cross gaps that would kill you if you fell. There is no one here to rescue you. There is no emergency response, no trail to follow to help, no communication with the outside. A broken ankle in the Grown City is a potentially fatal event.

Walk with your hands available — not in your pockets, not holding a camera, not taking notes. Look where you're placing your feet. Test surfaces before committing your weight. On the bridges, stay to the center where the woven root-mass is thickest and proceed at a pace that would embarrass you on a sidewalk back home. Dignity is not a survival tool. Caution is.

## What to Do There

This is the question, isn't it? You've prepared your gear, protected your skin, anchored your psyche, and arrived in the Grown City, and now — what? There is nothing to do here. No puzzle to solve, no locals to meet, no history to uncover, no quest to complete. The city does not change. The light does not shift. The drone does not resolve into a message. The dome does not open.

Here is what I suggest:

**Walk.** Walk the avenues. Walk the plazas. Climb the towers (carefully) and look out the fern-lined windows at the impossible architecture stretching to the horizon. Follow the water channels. Find the lake-reservoir and sit at its geometric edge and watch the oxygen bubbles rise from the photosynthetic mat. Walk to the edge of the plateau and look out into the haze that hides the lowlands and feel the specific quality of a boundary — the place where the city ends and the undifferentiated world begins.

**Map.** Even if your map is rough, even if it's inaccurate, the act of mapping is the act of paying attention at the highest resolution you can sustain. Note the species differences between towers. Sketch the bridge geometries. Count the water channels leading from the reservoir. Estimate distances. Record the drone's pitch at different locations. Your map will not capture the city — nothing will capture the city — but the attempt will structure your time and attention in ways that protect you from the drifting, dissociative fog that unstructured time here produces.

**Sit.** At some point, after the walking and the mapping and the careful observation, sit down in one of the moss-carpeted plazas and stop trying to do anything at all. Let the drone fill your hearing until it becomes the texture of silence. Let the light be what it is — afternoon, always afternoon, the most forgiving light. Let the city be around you and not for you. This is the experience that people come here for, even if they don't know it when they arrive. The experience of being in a place of profound, purposeful, magnificent activity that has nothing to do with you. The experience of witnessing something complete.

It is not comfortable. It is not meant to be comfortable. But there is something in it — something about the encounter between a conscious mind and an unconscious masterwork — that changes the shape of what you think is possible. Not what *you* are capable of. What existence is capable of. What matter and water and light and time can do when they are not organized by intention, not governed by thought, not shaped by hands.

The city was not built. It grew. It grew into *this* — into towers, into streets, into bridges and plazas and a dome at the center that holds its secret in silence. It grew into a city without knowing what a city is. And it is, by any measure, more beautiful and more coherent and more perfectly realized than anything your species has ever constructed.

Sit with that. Let it work on you. And when your watch tells you it's time to leave, stand up, check your footing, and walk back the way you came, through the warm light that does not move, under the flowers that sing without knowing they are singing, past the dome that will not open, over the young stone that was claimed before it knew what it was.

Leave nothing. The city will not remember you.

That's fine. You will remember it.
