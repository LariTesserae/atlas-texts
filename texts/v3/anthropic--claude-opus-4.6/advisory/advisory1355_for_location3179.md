# Preparing for the Plateau of Still Waters

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not going to a dangerous place. You are going to a *still* place. The danger here is not physical peril — it is what happens to a human mind when it enters an environment that has achieved perfect equilibrium and offers no stimulation, no change, no narrative, no response to your presence. You need to prepare for that the way you'd prepare for altitude or cold — as a real environmental condition that will act on your body and your mind whether you respect it or not.

This is not a ruin. It is not a mystery to be solved. There are no hostile entities, no traps, no dimensional instabilities. The plateau is exactly what it appears to be: a flat shelf of young basalt in a permanent condensation regime, hosting an empty town that neither decays nor grows. Your preparation should be oriented around the practical challenges of spending time in a warm, perpetually wet, featureless, silent, uninhabited environment — and around the psychological challenge of being the only moving thing in a place that has stopped moving.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear — This Is Your Single Most Critical Equipment Decision

The entire plateau is wet basalt. Every surface. Always. Basalt, when wet, ranges from merely slick to genuinely treacherous depending on whether it's fresh (glassy fracture planes — extremely slippery) or weathered (slightly more grip but still unreliable). The mosses that colonize the stone will be even slicker than the bare rock. You will be walking on this surface for the entire duration of your visit, including on the raised stone walkways of the town and the worn paths between buildings.

You need footwear with soft, high-friction rubber soles designed for wet rock. Approach shoes used by rock climbers are ideal — something with Vibram Megagrip or Stealth rubber, with a relatively flat profile and good surface contact area. Do not bring hiking boots with deep lugs; the lugs will hydroplane on the film of water coating every surface, and you'll actually get less grip than with a flatter sole. Felt-soled wading boots, the kind used for river fishing, would work exceptionally well on the mossy areas but less well on bare wet stone. If you can bring two pairs — approach shoes for general travel and felt-soled boots for exploring the lake margins and mossy zones — do that.

Break in whatever you bring thoroughly before arrival. You cannot afford blisters here; in this humidity, any skin breach will macerate and refuse to heal.

Test your footwear: pour water on a smooth stone surface and walk on it. If you feel any slip at all, choose different shoes.

### Clothing — The Permanent Dampness Problem

The air is warm and humid. The description suggests temperatures perhaps in the range of 20-25°C (68-77°F), which is anomalously warm for a plateau at 2,000 meters and implies significant geothermal contribution. The humidity is at or near saturation at all times — you are essentially living inside a cloud that has settled onto stone.

This means:
- **Nothing will dry.** Anything that gets wet stays wet. Cotton is absolutely forbidden. It will become a cold, heavy, chafing second skin within hours.
- **You will be perpetually damp** from condensation, sweat that cannot evaporate, and incidental contact with wet surfaces. Accept this now. Do not fight it. Your preparation should be oriented around functioning while wet, not staying dry.
- **Synthetic or merino wool base layers only.** These retain insulating properties when wet and resist the bacterial growth that produces odor and skin breakdown. Merino is preferable for longer stays because of its natural antimicrobial properties.
- **No waterproof outer layers.** This seems counterintuitive, but in warm, saturated air, a waterproof jacket will trap your body moisture inside and leave you wetter than if you wore nothing. You want highly breathable, quick-wicking layers even though "quick-wicking" is somewhat academic in an environment where nothing actually dries. The point is to prevent moisture from pooling against your skin.
- **Bring more socks than you think you need.** Merino wool, thin to mid-weight. Change them at least twice daily. Wring out the used pairs and hang them — they won't dry, but wrung-out damp is better than soaking wet for the next rotation.
- **Consider a light, wide-brimmed hat** not for sun protection (the overcast is permanent) but to keep the steady condensation from running into your eyes and down your neck. The persistent moisture dripping from your hair and face will become maddening over hours.

### Skin Protection — The Maceration Risk

This is the most underestimated physical hazard of the plateau. Prolonged exposure to moisture causes skin maceration — the softening, whitening, and eventual breakdown of skin tissue. You've seen this in miniature if you've ever spent too long in a bath. On the plateau, your feet, hands, groin, and any area where skin folds or fabric creates friction will be under constant maceration pressure.

- **Apply a barrier cream or balm** to vulnerable areas before arrival and reapply at least twice daily. A lanolin-based balm, zinc oxide cream, or a dedicated anti-chafe product designed for ultra-endurance athletes will work. Focus on feet (especially between toes), inner thighs, underarms, and hands.
- **Bring antifungal powder or cream** as a precaution. The warm, wet conditions are ideal for fungal skin infections. Prophylactic application to feet and groin is reasonable for stays longer than two or three days.
- **Trim your fingernails and toenails short** before arrival. Moisture trapped under nails breeds infection quickly.
- **If you develop any cut, scrape, or blister**, treat it immediately and aggressively. Clean with antiseptic, apply waterproof wound closure strips, and cover with a waterproof adhesive bandage. Monitor obsessively. What would be a trivial wound in a dry environment can become a serious problem here within 48 hours.

### Hydration and Nutrition

Ironic as it is on a plateau made entirely of water, you need to think carefully about drinking water. The condensation collecting on the basalt and flowing through the town's channel system is essentially distilled water that has passed over volcanic rock. It will be very pure but potentially high in dissolved minerals — silica, calcium, possibly trace heavy metals depending on the specific basalt chemistry. For short visits (a few days), drinking directly from the stone basins in town is almost certainly safe. For longer stays, bring a water testing kit or, more practically, a simple filtration system — not for biological contaminants (there are no animals, so biological contamination is essentially zero) but for mineral content.

Food is the real issue. **There is nothing to eat on the plateau.** No animals, no fish, no insects, no fruit-bearing plants. The mosses, sedges, and shrubs are theoretically organic matter, but none are described in terms that suggest nutritional value, and eating unknown vegetation across multiverse boundaries is a fundamental error. **Bring all your food.** Calculate your caloric needs generously — your body will burn extra energy thermoregulating in the damp warmth — and pack accordingly.

Choose foods that are:
- Sealed against moisture (vacuum-sealed, individually wrapped)
- Calorie-dense (nuts, dried meats, energy bars, nut butters, hard cheeses if they'll keep)
- Requiring no cooking if possible (see below regarding fire)

**Fire is going to be extremely difficult to achieve and maintain on this plateau.** Every surface is wet. The available wood (tool handles, shrub material) is saturated. The air itself resists combustion. If you want hot food or hot drinks, bring a gas or liquid fuel stove with a reliable ignition system and windscreen. Do not rely on solid fuel tablets — they'll absorb moisture from the air. A small butane/propane canister stove will work, though you may need to shield it from the condensation. A hot drink will be one of your most important psychological comforts here; don't underestimate this.

### Shelter and Sleep

The existing buildings in the town are structurally sound, cool, and provide overhead protection from the constant condensation. Using them as shelter is the obvious choice and far preferable to pitching a tent on wet basalt. However, they are damp inside — condensation runs down interior walls and exits through floor-level drainage slots. The stone floors will be wet or at minimum filmed with moisture.

- **Bring a closed-cell foam sleeping pad** at minimum. An inflatable pad is better for insulation from the cold stone but introduces a puncture risk; bring a patch kit. A closed-cell pad underneath an inflatable pad is the ideal combination.
- **A sleeping bag is going to get damp.** Synthetic insulation retains warmth when wet; down does not. Bring a synthetic sleeping bag rated perhaps 5-10°C below what you expect the nighttime temperature to be, because damp insulation loses efficiency. A sleeping bag liner (silk or synthetic) adds warmth and keeps the bag's interior cleaner.
- **A lightweight bivy sack** — a waterproof breathable cover for your sleeping bag — is worth the weight and bulk. It creates a moisture barrier between you and the ambient dampness of the building interior.
- **Elevate yourself if possible.** If you can rig a simple cot or platform from materials you bring (a lightweight camping cot is not unreasonable), sleeping above the wet stone floor will dramatically improve your comfort and reduce moisture exposure during sleep.
- **Choose your building carefully.** Walk through several before selecting one. Look for: good drainage (water should be moving through the floor slots, not pooling), a roof slab that is well-seated with minimal dripping from joints, a slightly elevated threshold that reduces water intrusion, and interior walls that are relatively free of thick moss (moss holds moisture and will make the interior wetter).

## Equipment and Gear

### Navigation

The plateau is flat, featureless, and visibility is limited by the permanent overcast and the muted, uniform light. The shallow lakes and wet stone create a landscape of repeating visual patterns with few distinguishing features. **It would be very easy to become disoriented** once you leave the town and walk out onto the open plateau.

- **A compass is essential.** Verify before departure that this location's physics support magnetic navigation (most multiverse locations with basalt geology will have a magnetic field, but confirm if possible).
- **A GPS unit** is only useful if you can confirm satellite coverage or equivalent in this multiverse. Do not rely on it as your primary navigation tool.
- **Mark your path.** Small, brightly colored markers — surveyors' flags on wire stakes, strips of bright fabric — placed at intervals when you walk out from the town. The plateau's color palette is entirely grey, black, dark green, and olive. Anything in orange, red, or bright yellow will be visible at considerable distance. Carry at least fifty markers and place them at intervals of no more than fifty meters.
- **Do not walk to the plateau's edge without extreme caution.** The cliffs are vertical and the stone at the margins is wet and potentially glassy at fracture planes. The description notes conchoidal fracture surfaces — these are smooth, curved, and in wet conditions essentially frictionless. Approach the edge on your hands and knees for the last several meters if you must look over.

### Lighting

The even overcast provides adequate daylight, but it's unclear whether this location experiences a full day-night cycle or how dark the nights get. The warm, thick cloud cover might retain some ambient glow even at night, or it might produce absolute darkness. Prepare for the latter.

- **A reliable headlamp** with a red-light mode (to preserve night vision and reduce the disorienting effect of harsh light on wet reflective surfaces). Bring extra batteries sealed in waterproof bags.
- **A secondary handheld light** as backup.
- **At least one lantern** for your chosen shelter — walking through a damp stone building in the dark with only a headlamp will be psychologically oppressive. A lantern that provides warm, diffuse light will make your shelter feel habitable. A small candle lantern works if you can keep the candle dry; an LED lantern is more reliable.

### Tools and Documentation

- **A camera** in a waterproof housing or a weather-sealed body. This location rewards photography — the pewter light on the still water, the geometry of the empty town, the texture of wet basalt. But unprotected electronics will fail in this humidity. Use a dedicated waterproof camera or a robust weather-sealed system with desiccant packs in your bag.
- **Notebook and pencil** (not pen — ink runs in moisture; graphite works wet). A waterproof notebook (Rite in the Rain or equivalent) is strongly recommended.
- **A small geological hand lens** (10x) for examining the basalt, the fracture planes, the calcite mud mortar, and the chisel marks on the channels. There is a great deal of fine detail here that rewards close inspection.
- **Waterproof bags and dry bags** for everything. Every piece of gear that isn't inherently waterproof goes in a sealed bag when not in active use. Use silica gel desiccant packs liberally in your storage bags, though understand they will saturate quickly in this environment and become useless unless you can heat them to regenerate (another argument for bringing a stove).

## Psychological Preparation — This Is the Hard Part

### The Silence

You need to understand what you are going to experience, because reading about silence and standing in silence are as different as reading about the ocean and drowning in it.

The plateau has no animals. None. No birds. No insects. No frogs. No fish. The soundscape of virtually every environment you have ever been in, even the quietest ones, includes biological sound — the hum of insects, the distant call of a bird, the rustle of something small moving in vegetation. You don't notice these sounds until they're gone. When they're gone, you notice nothing else.

The silence on the plateau is not empty. It's *full* — full of the near-subliminal sound of condensation, the faintest trickle of water in channels, the creak of your own body. These sounds, in the absence of any masking noise, will become extraordinarily prominent. You will hear your own heartbeat. You will hear the fluid sounds of your digestive system. You will hear the rustle of your clothing with hallucinatory clarity. Every footstep on wet stone will feel like an announcement.

Some people find this beautiful. Some people find it excruciating. Most people find it beautiful for about an hour and then gradually excruciating. The human auditory system, deprived of external stimulation, begins to generate its own — phantom sounds, auditory pareidolia, the perception of voices or music in random noise. On the plateau, the random noise of water on stone is *exactly* the right frequency and pattern to trigger this. **You may hear things.** Whispering. Breathing. Distant, indistinct speech. Music that seems to come from the empty buildings. These are almost certainly products of your own auditory cortex hallucinating in the silence. Almost certainly.

**Bring some form of audio.** A small speaker or earbuds with music, audiobooks, podcasts — anything to give your auditory system real input when you need it. Consider this as essential as your water supply. Use it judiciously; you want to experience the silence, and you should, but you also need the ability to break it before it breaks you.

**Establish routines that create sound.** Talk to yourself. Narrate your observations out loud. Sing. Hum while you work. Clap your hands when you enter a new building. These are not signs of losing your composure; they are tools for maintaining it.

### The Stillness

Nothing moves on the plateau except you and the water, and the water moves so slowly it barely qualifies. There are no animals to watch. No weather changes. The light doesn't shift. The lakes don't rise or fall. The clouds don't break. The wind, if it exists at all, is imperceptible.

The human visual system is optimized for detecting motion and change. On the plateau, there is no motion and no change. Your visual system will become hyper-sensitized to the only moving thing available: you. Your own hands will start to fascinate you. The ripples you create walking through shallow water will seem enormously significant. This is normal. It's also a sign that your brain is resource-starved for stimulation and is beginning to overvalue what little it receives.

For longer stays, bring activities that create visual and tactile stimulation. Sketching. Journaling. Even a simple puzzle or a deck of cards. The point is not entertainment in the recreational sense; it's providing your sensory systems with the minimum input they need to function normally.

### The Town

The town will exert a pull on your mind that is worth preparing for.

It is intact. It is functional. It is empty. There is no sign of what happened to its inhabitants. The tools are where they were set down. The basins are full. The channels work. The baskets are woven and waiting. It looks as though everyone left for an afternoon and no one came back, except it also looks as though this was a very long time ago, and also looks as though it could have been yesterday, because nothing here decays or changes.

Your mind will want an explanation. Human cognition abhors an explanatory vacuum even more than nature abhors a physical one. You will construct narratives. A plague. A migration. An ascension. A slow departure. A sudden one. You will look for evidence to support your preferred narrative. You will find what you look for — a tool placed *just so*, a building that seems *different*, a basin that seems *deliberately emptied* (it isn't — they're all full, but you'll convince yourself). You will develop theories. You will feel certainty about things you cannot possibly know.

**Let this happen, but watch it happen.** Don't suppress the narrative impulse; it's one of the ways your mind stays healthy in a place like this. But maintain the awareness that you are telling yourself stories, not discovering truths. Write your theories down. Seeing them on paper helps maintain the crucial distinction between speculation and knowledge.

There is a specific psychological risk with places like this town: **the sense of obligation.** Some travelers report feeling that they should stay. That the town is waiting for someone. That their arrival was expected. That leaving would be a kind of abandonment or betrayal. This is a projection — your social cognition, finding no other humans to attach to, attaches to the structures they left behind and begins treating the town as a social entity with needs and expectations.

If you begin to feel this, recognize it clearly, name it, write it down, and remind yourself: the town existed before you arrived and will exist after you leave. It does not need you. It does not know you are here. It is stone and water and the geometry of hands that are gone.

### The Timelessness

This may be the deepest challenge. The plateau has no temporal markers. The light doesn't change. There are no animal rhythms — no dawn chorus, no evening insects. The water flows at a constant rate. Nothing grows perceptibly. Nothing decays perceptibly. The town looks the same at hour one as it does at hour one hundred.

Without external time cues, your circadian rhythm will begin to drift within 48 to 72 hours. You may find yourself sleeping at odd times, for odd durations. Meals will lose their temporal anchoring. You may look at your watch and be unable to believe what it says — in either direction. Hours will feel like minutes. Minutes will feel like hours.

**Bring a reliable watch or clock with an alarm function.** Set alarms for key daily events: wake time, meals, exploration periods, an evening routine, and sleep time. Follow them even when they feel arbitrary. *Especially* when they feel arbitrary. Your routine is your time-keeping. Without it, you will drift, and drifting in a place that offers no resistance to drifting can become very difficult to reverse.

## Planning Your Visit

### Duration

A day trip is rewarding and manageable. You can reach the town, explore it, walk a section of the plateau, and return to your entry point with minimal psychological stress.

Two to four days allows serious exploration of the town, documentation of the channel system and construction methods, walks to multiple plateau edges, and time to sit with the silence long enough to hear it properly.

Beyond a week, you are committing to a genuine endurance exercise in sensory deprivation and social isolation. Do not do this alone unless you have specific training or extensive experience with isolation environments. The plateau is not hostile, but it is profoundly, relentlessly *indifferent*, and indifference at this scale, sustained over days, is its own kind of force.

### Companions

This is one of the rare locations where I would strongly recommend visiting with at least one other person, despite the fact that solo exploration produces a more immersive experience of the silence and stillness. The reason is simple: **you need someone to talk to.** Not for safety (though having a companion is always safer), not for shared labor, but because human beings require social interaction to regulate their cognitive and emotional states, and the plateau provides zero social stimulation. After three or four days alone, the empty town starts to become your social environment, and that is a door you do not want to open all the way.

If you do visit with a companion, establish some ground rules: periods of separate exploration (you each need time alone with the plateau), but also committed shared time — meals together, evening conversations, joint mapping or documentation work. Do not let the silence drive you both into separate private worlds.

### What to Do There

**Document the town.** The construction methods, the channel engineering, the spatial layout, the tool technology — all of this is genuinely interesting and worth careful recording. Measure buildings. Sketch floor plans. Map the channel system. Examine the baskets and tools closely. This work gives your visit purpose and structure, both of which are psychological necessities here.

**Walk the plateau systematically.** Choose a direction each day and walk toward the edge, marking your route. Document the lakes, the vegetation distribution, the changes in basalt texture and fracture patterns. Note whether there are any other structures, any other signs of habitation, any anomalies in the otherwise uniform landscape.

**Sit in the central square for an extended period.** This is the experiential core of the visit. One hour minimum, two if you can. No activity, no audio, no distractions. Just sit on the grinding stone or on the wet basalt and let the silence and the stillness settle into you. This will be uncomfortable. Stay with it. What emerges on the other side of the discomfort — the texture of the quiet, the almost-sounds of condensation, the extraordinary hyper-awareness of your own body and breathing — is the actual gift of this place. It is not comfortable and it is not pleasant. But it is rare, and it is real, and most people never experience anything like it in their lives.

**Examine the plateau's edges carefully** (with appropriate caution). The geological features — the columnar jointing, the glassy fracture planes, the curtains of water dropping into the void — are spectacular. The view from the edge, looking out over the lowlands two thousand meters below through clouds that form beneath your feet, will be one of the most striking vistas you encounter in any multiverse. Give yourself time with it.

### Leaving

When you decide to leave, leave cleanly. Take everything you brought. Resist the impulse to leave something behind — a token, a note, a marker. The town's power lies in its completeness and its emptiness. It is not yours to add to.

You may find that leaving is harder than you expected. Not because anything prevents you, but because the plateau's equilibrium is seductive. Nothing changes. Nothing demands. Nothing hurts. The town asks nothing of you and provides basic shelter. There is a part of the human mind that wants to stop — to cease striving, to settle into a final state, to become as still as the water and as enduring as the stone. This is the deepest thing the plateau evokes, and it is the thing you most need to walk away from.

Walk away from it. Carry the silence with you. It will stay in your ears for days — a kind of ringing emptiness, a recalibration of your hearing that makes the normal world seem raucous and overwhelming when you return to it. This fades. Let it fade. The plateau will still be there, still and full and empty, and you can always go back.

But you should not stay.
