# Preparing for the Forge Vent

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Internalize the parameters, because this is a location that will punish imprecision and reward careful, almost ritualistic preparation. You are descending through a body-width fissure in volcanic basalt into a chamber where the ambient air temperature hovers around 60-65°C, the humidity is effectively zero, the noise level is comparable to an industrial factory floor, the rock surfaces range from blistering to actively molten, and every available surface is colonized by dense thermophilic fauna. Your window of safe exposure is approximately twenty minutes. There is no water source, no easy egress, no rescue infrastructure, and no room for error in a space the size of a rail car.

This is not an adventure. It is a controlled exposure to an environment that is actively hostile to your biology. Prepare accordingly.

## Multiverse Travel Baseline

Before addressing the Forge Vent specifically, handle the standard multiverse transit considerations:

**Determine the transit modality.** How are you arriving on-world? If your method deposits you at coordinates of your choosing, you need precise positional data for the volcanic plateau, not the vent itself—you want to arrive on the ridgeline with time to orient, acclimatize to altitude and local atmospheric conditions, and locate the fissure deliberately. If your transit method is less precise, build in travel time across the plateau, which means additional gear for surface conditions (high-altitude volcanic desert: UV exposure, thin air, radiant ground heat, no shade, no water).

**Atmospheric compatibility.** The description implies a breathable atmosphere at the surface, but the vent chamber introduces complications: volcanic outgassing through the floor seam likely means elevated concentrations of sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and possibly hydrogen fluoride or other volcanic volatiles. The surface air may be fine. The chamber air may not be. Do not assume. (More on this below.)

**Biological quarantine.** You are entering a unique chemosynthetic ecosystem. You represent a contamination vector. The organisms here have no evolutionary exposure to anything you carry—skin flora, respiratory droplets, fabric fibers, polymer particulates. Equally, they represent an unknown contact risk to you. The thermophilic organisms may produce metabolic byproducts, surface compounds, or shed particulates that your immune system has no framework for. Preparation must address both directions of contamination.

**Legal and ethical framework.** If this location exists within a governed territory, determine whether access is permitted, restricted, or prohibited. A unique geological-biological formation like this may be a protected site. Even if no jurisdiction applies, consider whether your visit could damage the ecosystem. The chamber is small. The organisms are densely packed. The rock is young and fragile. You are large and clumsy by comparison. The ethical weight of visiting should be considered before the logistical weight.

## Thermal Protection: The Central Problem

The defining challenge of the Forge Vent is heat. At 60-65°C ambient air temperature with near-zero humidity, you are facing conditions that will overwhelm your body's thermoregulation within minutes. Let's be specific about what happens:

**Your core body temperature** will begin rising almost immediately upon entering the chamber. The normal human core temperature is approximately 37°C. Hyperthermia becomes dangerous at 39-40°C and potentially fatal at 41-42°C. In a 60°C environment, your body cannot shed heat through convection (the air is hotter than you) and can barely shed heat through evaporation (sweat will evaporate, but the rate of heat gain from the environment will outpace evaporative cooling, especially because the dry air, while efficient at evaporating sweat, will dehydrate you rapidly). Radiant heat from the floor seam and the walls adds to the load. You are a cold object in a hot box, and thermodynamics is not on your side.

**The twenty-minute window** described is plausible but optimistic for an unprotected human. With preparation, you can extend your safe exposure time and reduce risk. Without it, you may have significantly less than twenty minutes before cognitive impairment from hyperthermia begins—and cognitive impairment in a space you have to climb out of through a narrow, twice-turning fissure is a death sentence.

### What to Wear

You need a thermal protection ensemble that balances four competing demands: heat resistance, mobility (the fissure requires you to compress your body through a space barely wider than your torso), weight (you're climbing nine meters vertically), and contamination control (both directions).

**Base layer:** A close-fitting garment made from a high-performance wicking fabric with embedded phase-change materials (PCMs) if available in the local technology base. PCMs absorb heat energy as they transition from solid to liquid, providing a thermal buffer. If PCMs aren't available, a garment with integrated tubing for circulated coolant is the next best option—but this requires carrying a pump and reservoir, which adds bulk. If neither is available, a simple moisture-wicking synthetic base layer is the minimum. Cotton will kill you here—it holds moisture against the skin unpredictably and provides no thermal management.

**Cooling vest or core cooling system:** This is not optional. A vest containing ice packs, gel packs, or a circulating coolant system worn over the base layer is your primary thermal defense. The vest should cover the torso, where the major blood vessels run close to the surface. Pre-chill the cooling medium as cold as practical. In a 60°C environment, you want as much thermal mass working in your favor as possible. A vest loaded with approximately 2-3 kg of ice or phase-change packs gives you perhaps 30-40 minutes of meaningful cooling depending on the melt rate. This is your clock. When the packs are spent, you leave.

**Outer layer:** A loose-fitting, reflective outer shell made from aluminized fabric or similar radiant heat barrier material. This reflects infrared radiation from the glowing seam and the hot walls back away from your body. It must be loose to allow an insulating air gap between the shell and your cooling layer. It must also be trim enough to fit through the fissure. This is the hardest compromise. Consider a design that can be compressed or partially removed for the fissure transit and then deployed once in the chamber.

**Gloves:** The description notes that the rock is "almost too hot to touch through cloth." You will be touching rock throughout the descent and ascent. Heavy-duty heat-resistant gloves—Kevlar-lined, multilayer, rated for sustained contact with surfaces up to at least 150°C—are essential. They must allow enough dexterity to grip irregular rock surfaces during the climb. Test them beforehand. Practice climbing in them. If you lose your grip because your gloves are too thick, the fall is nine meters onto stone that may be hot enough to burn exposed skin on contact.

**Footwear:** Heavy-soled boots with thermal insulation underfoot. The floor of the chamber, particularly near the heat seam, will be extremely hot. Standard hiking boots may not provide sufficient insulation. Look for boots with composite or ceramic-insulated soles. They need to grip well on glassy basalt, which can be slippery, especially with the faceted, unweathered surfaces described. Vibram-type rubber compounds rated for high temperatures. Test the soles against hot surfaces before committing.

**Head and neck:** A reflective hood or balaclava with thermal insulation. Your head is a major site of heat gain in a hot environment (contrary to the popular myth about heat loss, in an environment hotter than your body, your head becomes a major site of heat *absorption*). A reflective hood deflects radiant heat. Consider also a cooling neck wrap—the carotid arteries run close to the surface at the neck, and cooling them directly helps moderate core temperature.

### Pre-cooling

Before entering the fissure, pre-cool your body. This is a technique used by athletes and military personnel operating in extreme heat. Drink cold fluids, apply ice to pulse points (wrists, neck, groin, armpits), and if possible, immerse yourself in cool water or wear your cooling vest for 15-30 minutes beforehand. Starting with a core temperature at the low end of normal (around 36.5°C rather than 37.2°C) gives you measurably more time before you reach dangerous thresholds.

### Hydration

You must be aggressively pre-hydrated. The description notes that sweat evaporates so fast it feels chemical—this means you are losing water at an extraordinary rate, and because it vanishes instantly, you won't have the usual perceptual cue of feeling wet or dripping. You will dehydrate faster than you think. Drink at least a liter of electrolyte-enhanced water in the hour before descent. Carry a sealed, insulated container with cold water—take small sips during your time in the chamber. The water serves double duty: hydration and internal cooling. Every sip of cold water absorbs a small amount of heat from your core. Do not carry water in a container that might leach chemicals when heated—the ambient temperature will warm any container quickly.

## Respiratory Protection

This is potentially the most critical safety concern after heat, and it's the one most likely to be underestimated.

The chamber is fed by volcanic gases venting through a floor seam connected to an active magmatic system. The "dense, layered, overlapping drone" and the cycling convection currents mean gases are continuously being introduced into a poorly ventilated, enclosed space. The fissure above provides some air exchange, but the chamber is nine meters below the surface through a twice-turning passage—natural ventilation will be minimal.

**Potential hazards:**

- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂):** Denser than air. Volcanic vents commonly emit CO₂ in dangerous concentrations. In an enclosed space, CO₂ can pool at floor level and displace oxygen. The floor seam is the likely emission source. You might be breathing normally and simply not getting enough oxygen. Symptoms at high concentrations: headache, dizziness, confusion, unconsciousness. At very high concentrations: death within minutes.

- **Sulfur dioxide (SO₂):** Irritant to the respiratory system. Even low concentrations cause coughing, throat burning, bronchospasm. The sulfur compounds that the beetles feed on had to arrive somehow—SO₂ in the gas emissions is nearly certain.

- **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S):** The "rotten egg" gas, except at high concentrations it paralyzes your olfactory nerve and you stop smelling it, which means you lose your only warning. Toxic at relatively low concentrations. Can cause rapid unconsciousness and death.

- **Carbon monoxide (CO):** Odorless, colorless. Binds to hemoglobin with much higher affinity than oxygen. Volcanic emissions can contain meaningful concentrations.

- **Hydrogen fluoride (HF):** If present even at trace levels, it is extraordinarily dangerous—corrosive to lungs, skin, and eyes, and can cause systemic fluoride poisoning through inhalation.

- **Particulates:** The description mentions mineral dust, shed chitin, and other material swirling in convection currents. You do not want to inhale thermophilic organism fragments of unknown biochemistry deep into your lungs.

**Required equipment:**

A self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) or, at minimum, a supplied-air respirator with a clean air source. Do not rely on filter-type respirators—they are designed for specific known contaminants and may be inadequate against the full spectrum of volcanic gases, particularly CO, which most chemical filters do not capture well. An SCBA with a 20-30 minute air supply is ideal, though the bulk of the tank creates a significant problem for the fissure transit. You may need a compact, low-profile system—a rebreather or a small-bottle escape-type SCBA designed for confined space entry.

If SCBA is genuinely impossible (the fissure is too narrow), then at absolute minimum: a full-face respirator with combination acid gas/particulate cartridges AND a portable multi-gas detector carried on your person, set to alarm for O₂ deficiency, CO₂, SO₂, H₂S, and CO. If the detector alarms, you leave immediately. No exceptions. No "just one more minute." You leave.

**Gas detection before entry:** Lower a multi-gas detector into the fissure on a cord before you enter. Get readings at the chamber level. If oxygen is below 19.5%, or if any toxic gas exceeds its short-term exposure limit, do not enter. Period. Volcanic gas emissions fluctuate—what was safe yesterday may not be safe today. Test every time.

## Hearing Protection

The sound environment is described as comparable to a factory floor, with a low-frequency fundamental you feel in your sternum overlaid with higher-frequency shrieks and whistles. This suggests overall sound pressure levels potentially exceeding 85-90 dB, possibly higher for the low-frequency components which are often underestimated by standard weighting curves.

Hearing damage from exposure at these levels begins at durations shorter than your planned stay. More importantly, the low-frequency vibration component may cause disorientation, nausea, or a vague sense of panic—infrasound and near-infrasound can produce physiological and psychological effects independent of perceived loudness.

**Wear hearing protection.** High-quality earplugs (NRR 30+) or, better, electronic earmuffs that attenuate dangerous levels while allowing communication if you're with a partner. The earmuffs must be compatible with your respiratory protection and hood—test the full ensemble for fit and comfort before you go. Gaps between the earmuff seal and your other headgear will dramatically reduce protection.

However—and this is important—part of the experience of the Forge Vent is the sound. The description makes clear that the acoustic environment is one of the defining features of the place. You may want to experience the full sound for a brief period, even knowing the risk. If so, limit unprotected exposure to the absolute minimum (a few minutes at most), and be aware that the low-frequency component may affect your vestibular system. Don't make that choice while standing near the heat seam.

## Eye Protection

The radiant glow from the floor seam, while not bright in the photopic sense, is concentrated in the infrared spectrum. Prolonged viewing of molten or near-molten rock can cause thermal damage to the cornea and lens (the same mechanism as "glassblower's cataract"). The exposure here is probably below the threshold for acute injury in a twenty-minute visit, but there's no reason to take the chance.

Wear polycarbonate safety glasses or goggles with an infrared-filtering tint. Not sunglasses—actual IR-rated protective eyewear. They should also protect against particulates in the swirling convection currents. If you're wearing a full-face respirator, the visor may provide adequate eye protection, but verify its IR rating.

## The Descent and Ascent: Confined Space Protocol

The fissure is "barely wider than a torso," turns twice, and descends approximately nine meters. This is a confined space entry in every meaningful sense, with the added complications of extreme heat, potential toxic atmosphere, and rock surfaces that may burn you on contact.

**Do not do this alone.**

I know. The description has a solitary quality to it—one person, pressing through the crack, alone with the heat and the sound and the beetles. And maybe that's how you want to experience it. But confined space entry without a surface attendant is one of the most reliably lethal mistakes in industrial and exploration safety. If you become incapacitated in the fissure or in the chamber—from heat stroke, from toxic gas exposure, from a fall, from a simple muscle cramp in an awkward position—you need someone at the surface who knows where you are, how long you've been down, and when to initiate rescue.

**Surface attendant requirements:**
- Remains at the fissure entrance throughout your descent and stay
- Maintains voice or radio contact (radio is better—the fissure turns may block line-of-sight communication)
- Carries a rescue plan and equipment (rope, harness, additional breathing apparatus)
- Has a firm, pre-agreed timeline: if you are not back at the surface by minute X, they begin the rescue protocol, which may mean calling for extraction rather than entering themselves (sending a second person into a space that has incapacitated the first is how confined space incidents become multiple-fatality events)

**For the fissure itself:**

- **Measure it first.** Before committing your body to the crack, lower a camera or probe and map the passage. Know where the turns are. Know the narrowest points. Know whether your body plus your gear will fit. If you're wearing a cooling vest, reflective shell, SCBA, and carrying supplies, your effective profile is significantly larger than your bare torso. Can you get through? Are you certain? Because backing up through a turning fissure in full gear while panicking is not something you want to discover you can't do.

- **Rope and harness.** Rig a fixed line from the surface to the chamber. Use a harness rated for confined space rescue (not a standard climbing harness—you want one with a dorsal attachment point so you can be hauled up vertically if unconscious). The rope should be heat-resistant—nylon melts at temperatures well below what these rock surfaces may reach. Kevlar or similar aramid fiber rope, or wire rope with a fabric sheath for grip. Anchor solidly at the surface.

- **Protect yourself from the rock.** The basalt in the fissure may be hot enough to burn through fabric. Your gloves are already addressed. Consider elbow and knee pads with heat-resistant outer surfaces. Long sleeves, long pants, no exposed skin anywhere. Every contact point with the rock is a potential burn. Move deliberately. Don't rush. Don't slide.

- **Manage your gear.** Anything that can snag on rock will snag on rock. Streamline your profile. No dangling straps, no loose buckles, nothing protruding. Secure everything close to your body. If you're carrying instruments, cameras, or sampling equipment, pack them in a hard case that you can push or pull through the fissure separately, rather than wearing them.

## Biological Considerations and Contact Protocol

You will be sharing a rail-car-sized space with tens of thousands of organisms adapted to an environment that is profoundly alien to your biology, even if they superficially resemble terrestrial arthropods. Treat every organism as potentially hazardous until you have evidence otherwise.

**Do not touch the animals.** This should be obvious, but in the near-darkness, with every surface colonized, avoiding contact may be difficult. The beetles are described as covering walls in layers dense enough to be mistaken for bare rock. You may put your hand on what you think is stone and feel movement. The predatory centipede-analogues are described as running hotter than the air—contact with them could mean a thermal burn in addition to whatever chemical or biological hazard their surface presents. The ceiling organisms drop offspring. Things may fall on you.

**Wear full-coverage clothing.** No exposed skin. This is already required for thermal protection, but it's independently required for biological isolation. Your outer shell should be a smooth, non-porous material that organisms cannot easily grip or penetrate. After exiting, decontaminate the outer layer before removing it—brush off any organisms, then wipe down with an appropriate agent (dilute bleach solution, isopropyl alcohol, or whatever is available and compatible with your gear).

**Do not remove anything from the chamber.** No specimens, no rock samples, no "just one beetle." This ecosystem exists in a precise equilibrium in a very small space. Removing organisms disrupts it. Removing rock disrupts it. And bringing unknown thermophilic organisms into the outside world is an uncontrolled biological release that could have consequences you can't predict. If scientific sampling is the purpose of your visit, that's a different conversation requiring proper collection protocols, containment, and ethical review. As a traveler, leave everything where it is.

**After exiting, monitor yourself** for unusual skin reactions, respiratory symptoms, or systemic illness in the hours and days following. You were immersed in an atmosphere carrying volatile compounds and biological particulates from organisms with unknown biochemistry. If anything feels wrong, seek medical attention and disclose what you were exposed to.

## Equipment List

Let me consolidate this into a practical loadout:

### Worn
- Heat-resistant base layer with phase-change material or wicking fabric
- Cooling vest with pre-chilled packs (carry spares in an insulated bag if possible)
- Aluminized/reflective outer shell, streamlined for fissure transit
- Heat-resistant gloves (dexterity-rated for climbing)
- Thermally insulated boots with high-temperature-rated soles and good grip
- Reflective hood/balaclava with insulation
- Cooling neck wrap
- Heat-resistant elbow and knee pads
- Confined-space harness with dorsal attachment
- Hearing protection (electronic earmuffs or high-NRR plugs)
- IR-filtering eye protection (goggles or safety glasses)
- Respiratory protection (SCBA/rebreather preferred, full-face respirator with combination cartridges as backup)

### Carried on Person
- Multi-gas detector (O₂, CO₂, CO, H₂S, SO₂ at minimum), clipped to belt or vest at breathing zone height
- Insulated water bottle with cold electrolyte solution
- Small, high-intensity headlamp (red-light mode available—white light will ruin your dark adaptation and may disturb the animals; red light preserves vision and is less likely to provoke a response from organisms adapted to an infrared-dominated environment)
- Backup light source (chemical light stick—no batteries to fail in heat)
- Watch or timer set to alarm at 15 minutes (giving you 5 minutes to exit before the 20-minute thermal limit)
- Communication device (radio, with surface attendant on the same channel)

### Carried to Fissure (Left with Attendant or Staged at Top)
- Heat-resistant rope (Kevlar/aramid), pre-rigged with anchors
- Additional cooling packs (to swap in before descent if you've used time pre-cooling)
- Additional water (at least 2 liters)
- First aid kit with burn treatment supplies, oral rehydration salts, and cold packs
- Emergency oxygen (small bottle, for use at the surface if you exit with respiratory distress)
- Decontamination supplies (wipes, brush, sealable bags for contaminated outer garments)
- The surface attendant themselves, briefed and equipped

## Mental and Experiential Preparation

Everything above is about keeping your body alive and intact. Now for the other part: what it will be like to actually be there, and how to prepare your mind for it.

### The Fissure
The descent will be claustrophobic even for people who don't normally experience claustrophobia. You are pressing your body into a crack in hot rock, in the dark, with the sound growing louder below you, and the heat increasing with every meter of descent. The fissure turns twice, meaning there will be moments when you cannot see the sky above or the chamber below—you are simply inside rock, compressed and descending. This will trigger a primal fear response in most humans. Expect it. Name it. Breathe through it (through your respirator, slowly, controlled). If you have any history of panic attacks in enclosed spaces, this is not the place to test whether you've outgrown them.

Practice confined space work beforehand if at all possible. Crawl through tight spaces. Get used to the feeling of stone (or walls, or any hard surface) pressing against your shoulders and hips simultaneously. Get used to moving by inches rather than strides. Get used to the way sound changes in a tight space—your own breathing becomes very loud, very close.

### The Chamber
When you arrive, your senses will be overwhelmed on multiple channels simultaneously. The heat will hit you as a physical force—not gradually, but as a wall. The sound will fill your skull. The light will be wrong—everything lit from below, shadows inverted, your spatial processing briefly confused because your visual system is used to light coming from above. And then, as your eyes adjust, you will begin to see that the walls are alive.

The density of life in this space is described as total. Every surface claimed. Tens of thousands of organisms in a room the size of a rail car. This will likely trigger one of two responses in a visitor: fascination or revulsion. Possibly both simultaneously. If you have any aversion to insects, to dense colonies of small moving things, to the sight of organisms packed body-to-body across a surface—know that this will be tested here at an intensity you have probably not encountered before. And you will be unable to avoid them. The walls, the floor margins, the ceiling—they are everywhere. You may feel them near your feet. Things may drop from above.

**Prepare by adjusting your framing.** These organisms are not threatening you. They are not interested in you. You are too cold, too large, too foreign to be relevant to their ecology. The beetles eat mineral films. The predators eat beetles. The ceiling organisms filter particulates. None of them eat warm, water-rich, organic visitors. You are furniture to them at best, an obstruction at worst. Their density is not aggression—it is the simple consequence of a productive ecosystem in a very small space. Every organism is where it is because the mathematics of resources and competition put it there. The packed, living walls are an expression of success, not menace.

But knowing that intellectually and feeling it viscerally, in the dark, with the heat and the noise and the moving surfaces—those are different things. Give yourself permission to be unsettled. Being unsettled is appropriate. You are in an alien place doing alien things, and the appropriate human response to the genuinely alien is a mixture of awe and unease.

### The Sound
The sound deserves its own mental preparation because it is, by the description, the most pervasive and inescapable feature of the chamber. You cannot close your ears the way you can close your eyes. Even with hearing protection, the low-frequency fundamental will reach you through bone conduction—you will feel it in your chest, your jaw, your skull. This is not painful, but it is deeply, physically strange. The sensation of a sound inside your skeleton, bypassing your ears, is something that can cause significant anxiety if you're not expecting it.

Exposure to low-frequency noise can also produce subtle psychological effects: unease, a sense of dread, difficulty concentrating, a feeling that something is wrong even when you can't identify what. These are physiological effects of the sound waves on your body, not accurate perceptions of danger (beyond the danger you've already identified and prepared for). Know this in advance. If you begin to feel a sourceless dread, it is the sound. Name it. Continue your observation. Leave on schedule.

### The Time Limit
Twenty minutes is not very long. It will feel even shorter because your senses will be saturated and there will be far more to observe than you can process. You will want to stay longer. You will convince yourself that you feel fine, that the heat isn't that bad, that you have time.

You do not have time. Heat stroke is insidious—by the time you have symptoms (confusion, disorientation, cessation of sweating, rapid pulse), you are already in a medical emergency, and you are nine meters underground in a narrow fissure with the nearest cool environment at the top. Set your timer. Obey your timer. The Forge Vent will be there tomorrow. You can come back. You cannot come back from a core temperature of 42°C at the bottom of a volcanic crack.

I would actually recommend a shorter visit for your first time—ten to twelve minutes. Get in. Orient yourself. Observe. Get out. Process what you saw on the surface, in safety and comfort. Return for a longer visit once you understand the environment experientially, not just from a description.

### The Exit
Climbing out will be harder than descending. You will be heat-fatigued. Your muscles will be less responsive. Your coordination may be slightly impaired. The cooling packs will be partially or fully spent. You will be dehydrated despite your water intake. And you will be climbing upward through a narrow fissure, which requires more physical effort than descending.

Budget your energy. Don't spend your last reserves on "just one more minute" of observation. Begin your ascent while you still feel strong. Move deliberately. Use your rope. Let your attendant know you're coming up. When you reach the surface, sit down in whatever shade is available, drink water, and do not be surprised if you feel significantly worse for a few minutes as your body recalibrates to the (relatively) cooler surface conditions. The thermal gradient between the chamber and the plateau surface is large enough that your cardiovascular system will need to adjust. Faintness, nausea, and a pounding headache are all possible and normal.

## What You Will Carry Away

Not specimens. Not samples. Not organisms.

What you will carry is the knowledge that you have been inside the planet while it was making itself. That you heard the sound of rock being born—not as a metaphor, but as an acoustic fact, the resonance of thermal convection through a chamber that was building new walls around you in real time. That you saw an ecosystem that owes nothing to the sun, nothing to photosynthesis, nothing to the surface world—a community of organisms that eat the breath of magma and arrange themselves in the dark by rules that are entirely their own. That you felt the heat of the deep earth on your face, lit from below like something seen through a furnace grate, and understood for a few minutes that the planet is not a stage we perform on but a machine that is still running, still producing, still working, and that we live on the thinnest, coolest, quietest skin of something incomprehensibly energetic.

The Forge Vent is not beautiful in any conventional sense. It is dark, loud, hot, crowded with alien life, and dangerous. It is also one of the most honest places you will ever visit—a place where the fundamental processes of a planet are laid bare at human scale, close enough to touch (though you shouldn't), in a room small enough to comprehend.

Prepare carefully. Respect the environment. Respect the organisms. Respect your own limits. Go in, see what there is to see, and come back out. The hum will stay with you, the low vibration you felt in your bones. It was always there, under your feet, everywhere you've ever stood. You just couldn't hear it until you went down to where the world is still being made.
