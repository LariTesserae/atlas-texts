# Preparing for the Drowned Halls

## First: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before a single piece of gear is packed, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because the preparation failures that kill people in the Drowned Halls won't be the ones you'd expect from an underwater environment. People drown, yes. But more commonly, people lose themselves — spatially, temporally, psychologically. You are entering a lightless, structure-enclosed body of fresh water that extends for distances measured in days of swimming, with no exterior, no surface to ascend to, no natural reference frame, and a sensory environment so minimal that your brain will begin generating its own content within hours. The Drowned Halls are not hostile. They are indifferent in a way that is worse than hostility, because hostility at least provides something to orient against.

Plan your visit with the understanding that you are entering one of the most profoundly alien environments a human being can survive in. Not because of toxicity or extreme temperature or predators, but because of *absence* — the systematic removal of nearly everything your nervous system uses to construct a sense of self-in-place.

## Breathing and Life Support

This is the foundational concern and it governs everything else.

### The Water
The water is fresh snowmelt, cold, well-oxygenated at its source but depleted by the time it reaches the middle depths where most of the Drowned Halls exist. Do not assume you can extract breathable gas from the ambient water without testing. Bring a portable dissolved-oxygen analyzer and test frequently as you move through different sections. The vegetation's metabolic processes may locally enrich or deplete oxygen in unpredictable ways — the bioluminescent pulses are chemical events, and chemical events consume or release gases.

### Breathing Apparatus
You need a closed-circuit rebreather system, not open-circuit scuba. The reasons are multiple:

- **Duration**: Open-circuit systems waste gas catastrophically. You cannot carry enough tanks to sustain a multi-day traverse, and there is nowhere to refill. A closed-circuit rebreather recycles your exhalation, scrubs the CO2, and meters in fresh oxygen from a much smaller supply. A well-maintained CCR with sufficient scrubber material and O2 supply can extend your breathing gas for 8-12 hours per charge, and you can carry replacement scrubber cartridges and small O2 cylinders to extend that to days.

- **Silence**: Open-circuit regulators produce bubbles. In the acoustic environment of the Drowned Halls, where a single water drop migrating through stone is an event, a stream of bubbles will be deafening, disorienting, and will propagate through the structure in ways that destroy your ability to use sound for navigation. A CCR produces no bubbles during normal operation.

- **Thermal considerations**: Exhaled gas from an open-circuit system carries heat away from your face and creates local turbulence. In water this cold and this still, you want to minimize every source of heat loss.

Bring **at minimum** three complete scrubber cartridge loads and two O2 cylinders beyond what you calculate you'll need. There is no resupply. There is no rescue service. If your scrubber is exhausted, you will die in the dark, and the moss will eventually grow over you as it has grown over everything else.

### Backup
Carry a small bailout cylinder — a pony bottle with an independent regulator — against catastrophic CCR failure. This gives you perhaps 20-30 minutes of emergency breathing, which is not enough to exit the Drowned Halls but is enough to troubleshoot a fixable problem or reach a pre-established cache point. More on caching strategy below.

## Thermal Protection

The water is snowmelt that has traveled miles downward through stone. Expect temperatures between 2-6°C (35-43°F). This is survivable but only with serious insulation, and only for limited durations.

### Drysuit
A wetsuit is inadequate. You need a full drysuit with integrated boots and dry gloves. The suit must be:

- **Fitted properly**: In the older, tighter corridors, you will be moving through spaces barely wider than your body. A loose or bulky suit catches on stone, tears on the sharp edges of delaminating walls, and impedes the careful, tactile navigation that is your primary means of orientation. Get the suit tailored if possible.

- **Puncture-resistant**: The stone surfaces, even those covered in moss, can have edges. The root-weave material that holds older structures together is fibrous and can catch and tear thin materials. Consider a drysuit with an outer shell of abrasion-resistant fabric (Cordura or equivalent) at the knees, elbows, shoulders, and chest — every point of contact during corridor navigation.

- **Repairable**: Bring a drysuit repair kit with adhesive patches, seam tape, and spare wrist and neck seals. A compromised drysuit in 3°C water transitions from life support to liability within minutes.

### Undergarments
Beneath the drysuit, wear a high-loft insulating undergarment designed for cold-water diving. Thinsulate-type materials that retain insulation when slightly damp are preferable to down or fleece, because if your suit leaks — and over a multi-day traverse, it may — you need insulation that degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically.

### Supplemental Heating
For extended stays, consider a battery-powered heated undergarment. The batteries are heavy and finite, but even intermittent warming can prevent the slow, insidious core temperature drop that accumulates over days of immersion in near-freezing water. Hypothermia in this environment won't announce itself dramatically — it will creep in as sluggish thinking, impaired dexterity, bad decisions that seem reasonable at the time. By the time you notice, you may be too cognitively impaired to respond effectively.

### Head and Hands
Your head and hands are your primary interfaces with this environment. You navigate by touch. You read the architecture with your fingers. Protect them, but don't over-protect them:

- **Hood**: A drysuit-integrated hood with a smooth exterior that won't snag on moss or stone. Thick enough to insulate but thin enough to allow some sensation of water temperature differences, which are a navigational input.

- **Gloves**: This is a genuine dilemma. Bare hands give you the best tactile information — you can feel the difference between worked stone and natural stone, between live moss and dead moss, between a weight-bearing wall and a partition. But bare hands in 3°C water lose dexterity within minutes and risk cold injury within hours. Compromise: wear thin dry gloves (latex or similar) with a light insulating liner. You'll lose some fine sensation but retain enough to navigate by touch. Carry a spare pair. Carry two spare pairs.

## Navigation

This is where preparation matters most, because the Drowned Halls will defeat every conventional navigation strategy you bring.

### What Won't Work
- **Visual navigation**: There is no light. The bioluminescent pulses are too dim, too brief, and too spatially distributed to illuminate your surroundings in any useful way. They will occasionally silhouette a nearby structure or give you a momentary sense of the scale of a space you're in, but you cannot navigate by them.

- **Compass**: Depends on the mineral content of the stone. If the builders used iron-rich stone (and the description mentions "iron-ghosts" in the water), magnetic navigation may be unreliable or useless. Bring a compass, test it, but don't rely on it.

- **GPS or satellite-based systems**: You are miles underground, inside a continent-spanning stone structure, under water. No signal will reach you. None.

- **Sonar/echo-based mapping**: Potentially useful in the great cisterns, but the acoustic environment is described as profoundly distorted — frequencies absorbed by moss, amplified by vaults, bent around corners. Active sonar may give you range-to-wall in simple spaces but will generate confusing returns in complex architecture, and the sound you produce will propagate unpredictably, potentially disorienting you further.

### What Might Work

**Guideline**: The single most critical piece of navigation equipment. A continuous physical line that connects you to your entry point and to every branch decision you've made. Use cave-diving standard practices:

- Primary reel with at least 500 meters of braided line (not monofilament — it needs to be tactile, findable by touch in complete darkness)
- At least two safety reels with 50+ meters each
- Line arrows: directional markers you clip to the line at decision points, oriented to point toward the exit. In darkness, these must be readable by touch — raised or textured arrows on a flat plate that you can feel with gloved fingers.
- **Never leave the line.** This is not a suggestion. In a sighted cave, a lost diver might spot their line from a distance, swim to it, reorient. In the Drowned Halls, if you lose the line, you have lost everything. You are in infinite, featureless darkness inside a structure that extends for *days* of swimming in every direction. Without the line, you will not find your way back. You will swim until your gas runs out, and then you will stop.

**Tactile Mapping**: Develop a system for encoding architectural information by touch. As you move through passages, note (in waterproof writing or by some recording method) the characteristics of the walls: width of passage, curve direction, ceiling height (reachable or not), moss density and texture, stone style, presence of tools or artifacts. Over time, this builds a tactile signature for each section that can help you recognize where you are if you somehow return to a previously visited area.

**Knotted Line System**: Consider adding coded knots or beads to your guideline at measured intervals — every 10 meters, for instance, with distinctive knot patterns at 50 and 100 meters. This gives you a distance reference along your travel path, which is one of the only quantitative spatial measurements available to you.

**Current Mapping**: The description mentions faint convective currents. These are subtle but potentially consistent over large scales. If you can detect them — through temperature differentials, vegetation movement, or the drift of a neutrally buoyant marker — they may provide a crude sense of direction. The overall flow is downward from the snowmelt source, so "against the barely perceptible current" is roughly "toward the surface" — though the current will be channeled and redirected by the architecture in complex ways.

### Depth and Position

Bring a depth gauge. It won't tell you where you are horizontally, but it gives you a vertical reference, which is more than you'll have otherwise. Note your entry depth, and track depth changes as you move. If the Drowned Halls have any systematic vertical organization (and the description suggests older construction is deeper, with newer construction above), depth may loosely correlate with architectural age and style, giving you another axis of orientation.

## Lighting

Bring lights. Bring many lights. But understand their limitations and their costs in this environment.

### Primary Light
A high-quality dive light with adjustable output — critical. You want the *option* of powerful illumination, but you will rarely use it at full power. Here's why:

In a space that has been in total darkness for ten thousand years, in water carrying fine mineral particulate and surrounded by reflective-pale moss, a powerful dive light will do several things:

1. **Backscatter**: The mineral content of the water will reflect your light back at you, creating a glowing fog that obscures rather than reveals. Low power, tightly focused beams will perform better than floodlights.

2. **Biological response**: The vegetation has been in darkness since before human civilization. It communicates by chemical bioluminescence. A powerful external light source is an unprecedented stimulus. The moss may respond — by brightening, by darkening, by triggering a cascade of bioluminescent pulses, by doing nothing, or by doing something no one has documented. Be prepared for the possibility that your light changes the behavior of the environment around you in ways that are disorienting or obscuring.

3. **Psychological impact**: After hours or days in total darkness, navigating by touch and sound and thermal gradients, your eyes will have adapted to a darkness so profound that the bioluminescent pulses — which are described as barely visible — will have become your visual world. Turning on a powerful light in this state will be physically painful and perceptually overwhelming. Use minimum effective illumination. Give your eyes time to adjust. And be prepared for the strange grief of turning the light off again.

### Backup Lights
At minimum three independent backup lights, of different types, stored in different locations on your body and in your equipment. Lights fail. Batteries die. Seals leak. In this environment, a failed light is merely a return to the ambient conditions — you can survive and navigate without light — but the psychological impact of losing your last light source is severe, and psychological integrity is a survival parameter here.

### Chemical Lights
Glow sticks — the simple chemical kind — have a role. They're waterproof, failure-proof, require no batteries, and produce a soft, omnidirectional glow that is less disruptive than an electric beam. Use them as marker lights at cache points, at guideline junctions, at rest stops. Their duration is limited (hours, not days), but they weigh nothing and pack small. Bring dozens.

## Food and Hydration

### Water
You are surrounded by water. The description says it's fresh snowmelt with a faint mineral taste. It is likely potable but test it first — mineral content, microbial load, dissolved metals. Iron and sulfur are mentioned explicitly. Bring a compact water testing kit and a small filter/purifier for initial use. If the water tests safe, you can hydrate from the environment, which massively reduces the consumables you need to carry.

A note: drinking the water you are immersed in, in total darkness, miles underground, inside a structure built by an unknown civilization, is a psychologically significant act. It ties you to the place. Some travelers find this grounding. Others find it disturbing. Know yourself.

### Food
Caloric needs will be high. Cold-water immersion burns energy at a prodigious rate, even inside a drysuit, because your body is fighting a continuous thermal gradient. Plan for 4,000-5,000 calories per day, possibly more.

Bring calorie-dense, waterproof, no-preparation-needed food. Possibilities:
- High-fat energy bars or blocks (vacuum-sealed)
- Nut butters in squeeze tubes
- Hard cheese sealed in wax
- Dried fruit and meat in waterproof containers

Eating underwater in a drysuit is awkward but feasible if you're using a full-face mask with a removable mouthpiece or a surface-supplied-style helmet with a dry oral cavity. If you're using a standard regulator mouthpiece, you'll need to find a suitable space — ideally a pocket of trapped air, though these may be extremely rare in the Drowned Halls — or develop a technique for eating small, soft food items while removing the regulator briefly. Practice this before you arrive.

The lack of trapped air pockets is a significant concern. In a natural flooded cave, air domes and pockets are common. In the Drowned Halls, where every chamber was deliberately constructed and the water has had millennia to fill every volume, permanent air pockets may be nonexistent. If they exist, they will be in the highest points of vaulted ceilings, and the air in them may be stale — CO2 accumulated over centuries, oxygen depleted by the slow metabolic processes of the moss. **Test any air pocket before breathing it.** A CO2/O2 analyzer is essential equipment if you plan to use found air spaces for rest or meals.

Alternatively, you can create temporary air spaces using an inverted container or a collapsible habitat — essentially an underwater tent that traps exhaled and supplemental gas to create a small dry environment. This is worth the weight if you plan to stay more than 24 hours. Even a space just large enough to get your head above water, breathe ambient gas (supplemented from your supply), eat, and rest with your face dry provides an enormous psychological benefit.

## Rest and Sleep

You will need to sleep. The Drowned Halls demand multi-day traverses even for modest exploration. Sleeping underwater, in a drysuit, in total darkness, in near-freezing water, inside an alien structure, is one of the more challenging things a human being can attempt.

### Options

**Sleep in the water**: Possible with proper buoyancy management. Clip yourself to your guideline or to a stable piece of architecture so you don't drift. Assume a fetal position to minimize heat loss. Set an alarm — a vibrating wrist timer, since audible alarms will propagate unpredictably — to wake you at intervals to check your gas supply, scrubber status, and thermal condition. Sleep will be shallow, interrupted, and unsatisfying, but it can sustain you for several days.

**Collapsible habitat**: As mentioned above, a small inflatable or collapsible structure that can be anchored inside a chamber and filled with breathing gas to create a dry air pocket large enough to lie down in. This is heavy, bulky, consumes gas, and takes time to set up and break down, but it provides actual rest — dry, horizontal, with your face above water. If you're planning a traverse of more than 48 hours, this moves from luxury to necessity.

**Hammock**: Some deep-cave divers use underwater hammocks — mesh sleeping surfaces strung between anchor points that keep you oriented and prevent drift. Combined with a sleeping bag designed for cold-water drysuit use (they exist; they're niche), this provides a middle ground between full immersion and a habitat.

### Sleep Architecture in the Halls
Your sleep will be strange. The subsonic hum of the structure — the "holding sound" of stone under pressure — will be felt through your body constantly. Some travelers report it as soothing, a whole-body vibration that approaches white noise. Others find it prevents deep sleep entirely, the body interpreting the vibration as a signal that something is wrong, that the structure is about to fail, that the weight of miles of water and stone above is about to assert itself. You won't know which type you are until you're there.

The bioluminescent pulses will be visible through closed eyelids. They will enter your dreams.

## Psychological Preparation

This section is as important as any equipment list.

### Darkness
You need to have spent time — real time, not an hour in a dark room — in total darkness before entering the Drowned Halls. Ideally, do a multi-day sensory deprivation exercise: a lightless cave, a float tank extended to twelve or more hours, anything that gives your brain the experience of operating without visual input for long enough that it begins to reorganize. What you'll discover is that your brain does not accept darkness. It generates visual content — patterns, shapes, faces, landscapes. These are hypnagogic hallucinations, entoptic phenomena, the visual cortex refusing to be idle. They are normal. They are not dangerous *if you know they're coming.* If you don't, if you arrive in the Drowned Halls expecting darkness and instead see things moving in the periphery of vision that isn't there, you may panic, and panic in this environment is lethal.

Practice darkness until it is boring. That is your benchmark.

### Solitude
If you are traveling alone — and the narrow corridors of the older sections may enforce single-file passage that makes companionship impractical anyway — prepare for the specific quality of solitude that the Drowned Halls impose. It is not the loneliness of a solo hike or a remote cabin. It is the solitude of being the only living animal in a world that has no animals, has never had animals in any time frame that matters. The vegetation is alive but it is not company. The structure was built by *someone* but they are gone so completely that their tools have become part of the moss. You will be, in a very real sense, the only consciousness in a world-sized space, and the space will not care that you're there. It will not respond to you. It will not threaten you or welcome you. It will simply continue being what it has been for ten thousand years, and you will be a brief chemical interruption in its mineral patience.

Some people find this liberating. Some people find this annihilating. Know which you are, or at minimum know that you don't know, and build abort criteria into your plan for the moment you find out.

### Time Distortion
Without light cycles, without social cues, without any environmental rhythms faster than the bioluminescent pulses (which span minutes) and the vegetation's growth adjustments (which span years), your sense of time will degrade rapidly. Wear a watch. Check it frequently. Log your activities with timestamps. Without this discipline, you will lose track of hours, and then of days, and the Drowned Halls will feel both infinitely long and strangely compressed, and you will not know how long you've been down here, and that uncertainty will erode your ability to make rational decisions about gas supply and turnaround points.

### The Builders
You will find their tools. You will move through spaces shaped by their hands, their aesthetic sensibilities, their understanding of pressure and stone and the strange vegetation that was either their charge or their master. You will not find them. But you will feel the overwhelming *evidence* of them — every surface, every arch, every precisely fitted joint is a record of someone's labor, someone's lifetime. The Drowned Halls are a monument to effort so vast and so patient that it becomes almost indistinguishable from geological process. This can produce a profound emotional response — awe, grief, a strange kinship with beings you'll never meet, or conversely a vertiginous horror at the scale of time and labor represented by the stone around you. Allow whatever response comes. Don't suppress it. But don't let it pull you off your guideline.

## Entry and Exit Strategy

### Before Entry
Establish your entry point with extreme care. Mark it redundantly — on your guideline, with chemical lights, with a physical anchor or cache of supplies. Leave a cache at entry with:
- Emergency breathing gas
- Food and water for 48 hours
- Backup light
- Thermal supplies

This is your fallback. If everything goes wrong deeper in, your goal is to get back to this point.

### Turnaround Rules
Apply cave diving's rule of thirds, adapted for the Drowned Halls:
- One third of your total gas supply for penetration
- One third for return
- One third for emergencies

In practice, given the distances and the possibility of disorientation, consider a rule of *fourths* — turn back at 25% consumption, preserving 75% for return and contingency. The Drowned Halls are forgiving in the sense that nothing will chase you, attack you, or actively impede your exit. They are unforgiving in the sense that there is no shortcut back, and every meter of penetration must be retraced exactly.

### Cache Points
On a multi-day traverse, establish supply caches at regular intervals along your guideline. Each cache should contain minimum:
- One scrubber cartridge load
- One O2 cylinder
- Food for 24 hours
- Chemical lights
- A backup light

Secure caches to the architecture with clips or line. Mark them on your guideline with a distinctive knot pattern. The moss will begin to grow on them almost immediately — bring container materials that are smooth and non-porous to slow this.

### Exit
When you leave the Drowned Halls — if you leave — do so slowly. Not because of decompression (the depth may not require it, depending on your entry point and the pressure profile of the spaces you've visited), but because of the psychological transition. You have been in a world without light, without sound beyond the structure's own voice, without any living company, navigating by touch through spaces built by hands that shaped stone for ten thousand years. Returning to a world of sky and sun and noise and other people will be jarring in ways that are difficult to predict. Give yourself a transition period. Rest near your entry point. Turn on a light and sit with it. Eat something familiar. Check your watch, note the date, and be prepared for it to feel wrong — either too soon or impossibly late.

## Final Notes

The Drowned Halls are not dangerous in the way that a predator is dangerous or a volcano is dangerous. They are dangerous in the way that deep water is dangerous: quietly, patiently, through the accumulation of small deficits — a degree of temperature lost, a liter of gas consumed, a moment of inattention on the guideline, a psychological crack that widens in the dark. Everything that can go wrong will go wrong slowly, and by the time you recognize it, the margin for correction will be narrow.

But.

The description of this place carries a quality that equipment lists and turnaround rules can't fully address. The Drowned Halls are, by every account, *beautiful* — in the way that deep geological time is beautiful, in the way that utter darkness and faint blue-green light pulsing through ancient moss across a three-hundred-foot cistern is beautiful, in the way that stone fitted so precisely that water cannot pass through it after ten thousand years of trying is beautiful. The builders made something that outlasted them, outlasted memory, outlasted the very concept of sky. The vegetation took it and continued it, spreading through the joints they calibrated, lighting the halls they shaped, decomposing and rebuilding in a cycle so slow it looks like permanence.

You are going there to witness something that was not made for witnessing. The Drowned Halls have no audience and need none. The light they produce illuminates nothing but itself. The structure holds because holding is what it does. Your presence will not change this, and this is, depending on your temperament, either the most humbling or the most terrifying thing about the place.

Go prepared. Go carefully. Follow your line back.
