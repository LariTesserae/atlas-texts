# Preparing for the Understory Engine

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this place will challenge your categories. You are entering a living organism that is also a city, a construction site that is also an act of growth, a wilderness that is also an industrial zone. The temptation will be to default to one frame — to treat it as a cave, or as a factory, or as a forest, or as an alien settlement — and every frame you choose will be partially right and mostly insufficient. The best preparation begins with mental flexibility. You need to arrive willing to hold contradictions: that the walls are alive and also architecture, that the infrastructure is installed and also cultivated, that the inhabitants are absent and also everywhere in their traces, that the place is young and also ancient in its foundation.

This is not a hostile environment in the conventional sense. Nothing in the description suggests active danger to a visitor. But it is an *operating* environment — a place doing complex work on multiple timescales simultaneously — and the primary risk is not predation or environmental collapse but *interference*. You are walking into someone else's ongoing project. The greatest preparation you can do is to understand that deeply.

## Respiratory and Atmospheric Preparation

The air is breathable but distinctive. Cool, dry, and saturated with volatile organic compounds — cambium, tannin, that sharp green astringency that coats the throat. This is essentially the atmosphere inside a massive living woody structure that has engineered its own ventilation, and you should expect it to be rich in terpenes, phenolic compounds, and whatever this organism's particular biochemistry releases as metabolic byproduct.

**Bring a lightweight respirator with activated carbon filters, but don't plan to wear it constantly.** The air is clearly survivable — the inhabitants work in it, the fauna thrives in it — but prolonged exposure to concentrated terpenes and tannins can cause headaches, throat irritation, and in some people a kind of mild euphoria or disorientation. The astringency mentioned is real; you will feel it in your sinuses and the back of your throat within minutes. If you are sensitive to strong botanical scents — if pine forests give you headaches, if sawmills make your eyes water — you may want the respirator available for the first several hours while you acclimate.

More importantly: **pay attention to the breathing cycle of the space.** The description notes cyclical air movement, drawn in from one direction and expelled from another, possibly reversing at intervals. This is the organism's respiration, and it means the atmospheric composition may shift subtly over the course of hours. There might be periods where oxygen levels dip slightly as the root-mass consumes it, and periods where CO2 concentrations rise. These fluctuations are probably mild — again, people live and work here — but they could explain feelings of drowsiness or heightened alertness at different times. A small portable gas monitor (O2, CO2, VOC levels) would be worth carrying. Not because you expect a crisis, but because understanding the respiratory rhythm of the space will help you understand the space itself. If you can correlate the air-cycling schedule with the activity patterns of the inhabitants — the shift changes, the rest periods — you'll learn something important about how life here is organized around the needs of the organism.

**Hydration is critical.** The dryness is the single most practically significant environmental factor for a human visitor. The Understory Engine has deliberately dehumidified its interior spaces, which means your body will lose moisture faster than you expect, especially because the cool temperature will mask the sensation of dehydration. You won't feel hot. You won't feel yourself sweating. But the dry air will wick moisture from your skin, your mucous membranes, your lungs with every breath. Carry more water than you think you need — at least four liters per day of expected exploration, more if you're physically active. Supplement with electrolytes. Bring lip balm, nasal saline spray, and eye drops if you're prone to dry eyes. The dryness is maintained with purpose — the organism keeps its interior desert-like so that other life can exist there without the problems of perpetual humidity (fungal growth, rot, bacterial proliferation in warm wet wood) — and you should respect that purpose by not introducing unnecessary moisture to the environment, but you must protect your own physiology.

## Navigation and Orientation

This will be one of your greatest challenges. The root-architecture follows the logic of growth, not geometry. Every sightline curves. Intersections occur at organic junctions, not grid coordinates. Your normal spatial instincts — the ones calibrated to city blocks, hallways, cardinal directions — will struggle.

**A compass may or may not be useful.** You're underground, surrounded by an enormous biomass with unknown mineral content, near iron fittings and machinery. Magnetic interference is plausible. Bring a compass, but verify it against other references before trusting it.

**GPS will not work.** You are beneath potentially hundreds of meters of root-mass and soil. No satellite signal will reach you.

**Inertial navigation** — a good quality IMU (inertial measurement unit) or dead-reckoning device — would be the most reliable electronic option. These track your movement relative to a starting point using accelerometers and gyroscopes, independent of external signals. They accumulate drift error over time, but for several miles of exploration they can give you a returnable track.

**But honestly: mark your path.** Low-tech, reliable, reversible. The description mentions chalk marks already in use by the inhabitants for guiding construction beetles, so chalk is clearly an accepted medium in this environment. Bring your own chalk in a color distinct from whatever the inhabitants use — something that won't be mistaken for a construction instruction. Make small, consistent marks at decision points. Or carry biodegradable flagging tape and place small ties at intersections, collecting them on your way back out.

**Map as you go.** Bring a notebook — paper, not electronic, because the humidity is low and paper will hold up beautifully here. Sketch intersections. Note the direction of airflow (which may help you orient, since it's cyclical and directional). Note the density and direction of light-conducting roots, which will be brighter toward the surface and may help you track depth. Note the iron fittings and infrastructure, which likely follow some systematic layout. The scheduling board with its pegs and threads suggests the inhabitants think in organized systems; their infrastructure probably has a logic you can learn.

**The sound environment is a navigation tool.** The wood transmits vibration with high fidelity. The generators, the construction beetles, the grinding of active growth — these are landmarks you can hear and feel. Press your hand to the root-structure periodically and note what you feel. The generator hum will be louder toward the industrial areas. The grinding of growth will be louder toward the active expansion zones at the city's edges. Silence — true silence, meaning the absence of both mechanical and biological vibration — might indicate areas that are dormant, completed, or deliberately left empty. Build a tactile map to supplement your visual one.

## Physical Gear and Clothing

The environment is cool, dry, and mechanically complex. Dress for it.

**Layers.** The temperature is steady — wine-cellar cool, so probably 12-16°C (54-61°F). Comfortable for moderate activity, but you'll get chilled if you stop moving for long periods to observe or take notes. A moisture-wicking base layer, a light insulating mid-layer, and a thin windbreaker-type shell for areas where the air-cycling creates drafts. Nothing bulky. You need to move freely, and some passages may require climbing or squeezing through gaps in the root-architecture.

**Footwear matters enormously.** The floor is compressed, interlocked root-wood worn smooth by passage. In dry conditions, smooth wood can be surprisingly slippery, especially if there's a film of fine sawdust or sap residue. Wear shoes with good grip on wood surfaces — rubber soles with moderate tread, the kind of thing you'd wear on a sailboat deck. Not aggressive hiking lugs, which will be awkward on smooth surfaces and might damage the living floor. Not smooth-soled shoes, which will betray you on any incline. The root-floor dips into broad concavities in the plaza areas; you'll be walking on curved surfaces. Ankle support is worth having.

**Gloves.** Light work gloves for when you touch the root-structure. The sap is astringent and may be mildly irritating to skin with prolonged contact. The bark surfaces, where they haven't been worn smooth, may be rough or splintery. And you will want to touch everything — the impulse will be irresistible, and touching the root-pillars is how you read the vibrations of the city — so protect your hands while keeping enough dexterity to write, use tools, and handle your equipment.

**A headlamp, but probably not as your primary light source.** The conducted light is sufficient to see and cast shadows — roughly equivalent to moderate indoor lighting. But it's diffuse and warm-toned, which means it may wash out details in deep crevices, inside the carved nodes, or in areas where the light-conducting roots are sparse. A headlamp with adjustable brightness and a warm-tone setting (to avoid jarring contrast with the ambient light) would let you inspect details without blinding yourself or the fauna. Consider also a small handheld light for examining specific things — the scheduling board, the workbench tools, the construction beetle markings.

**Do not bring open flame.** The environment is dry wood. The organism has gone to extraordinary lengths to maintain dryness. The astringent sap-smell suggests volatile compounds in the air. A fire here would be catastrophic — not just for you, but for the entire living system and everyone who depends on it. No matches, no lighters, no candles, no camp stoves. If you need to heat water or food, bring a flameless chemical heater or plan to eat cold.

## Equipment for Study and Documentation

You are visiting what amounts to a living city in the process of building itself — one of the most extraordinary convergences of biology and engineering imaginable. You will want to document it.

**Photography and video:** Bring a good camera with excellent low-light capability. The ambient light is warm and diffuse — beautiful for photography, actually, but demanding for equipment. A fast lens (f/1.4 to f/2.8) will let you work without flash, which you should avoid using near the fauna (the eyeless lizards navigate by other senses and may not care, but the moth-winged birds with their translucent wings might be sensitive to sudden bright light). A tripod or monopod for longer exposures in dimmer areas. Video with good audio recording capability — the soundscape of this place is as important as the visual: the generator hum, the beetle frequencies, the grinding of growth, the distant voices carrying through the wood.

**Audio recording equipment** specifically. A contact microphone — the kind you press against a surface to record its vibrations — would be extraordinary here. Press it against different root-pillars and record the transmitted soundscape of the city. You might capture the generator rhythms, the structural stresses of growth, the footfalls of inhabitants in distant quarters. This is a place where the architecture itself is a transmission medium; a contact microphone turns you into a listener at the correct scale.

**Sampling equipment, but use it with extreme caution and restraint.** Small sterile containers, a penknife, forceps, zip-lock bags. You may find fallen bark fragments, sawdust, shed beetle carapaces, dried sap residue — loose material that can be collected without cutting or damaging anything living. **Do not cut into the living root-structure.** Do not take samples from the cambium layer. The inhabitants bolt their pipes carefully to avoid the cambium; follow their example. The organism's tolerance for the industrial infrastructure suggests it can accept foreign objects attached to its surface, but penetrating wounds might trigger immune responses, sap-flooding, altered growth — consequences you can't predict and that might draw unwelcome attention.

**A notebook and multiple pencils** (not pens — in dry conditions, some inks dry out or skip; pencils are reliable). Sketch the architecture, the infrastructure layout, the scheduling board's peg-and-thread system, the chalk marks that guide the construction beetles. Note colors, since the light-conducting roots will make color perception unreliable — what looks golden-green to you might be a different color in neutral light. Bring a small color reference card (a Munsell chart or even a printed color wheel) that you can hold next to things for comparison.

## Interacting with the Fauna

The fauna is integrated into the city's ecology, and some of it is clearly domesticated or at least task-oriented. This means it is accustomed to the presence of the inhabitants, but not necessarily accustomed to *you*.

**The pack-lizards** are domesticated working animals carrying loads on familiar routes. Treat them the way you'd treat a working dog or a mule on a trail: give them space, don't obstruct their path, don't try to touch or interact with them unless they approach you. They are eyeless, navigating by other senses — vibration, scent, echolocation, something else entirely. Your presence may register differently to them than the presence of the inhabitants. Move predictably. Don't make sudden movements or loud noises that send unfamiliar vibrations through the root-floor.

**The construction beetles** are biological tools performing precise work. They are almost certainly not dangerous to you — they're chewing wood, not defending territory — but they are in the middle of a task. Do not disturb them. Do not touch them. Do not stand on or lean against the section of wall they're working on. Watch from a respectful distance. The humming frequency that makes your fingertips buzz suggests they communicate or coordinate through vibration; adding your own vibrations (stomping, tapping, dropping equipment) in their work area could interfere with their coordination. Be still when you watch them.

**The moth-winged birds** in the upper canopy are the most likely source of unpredictable interaction. They're wild, or at least semi-wild, occupying an ecological niche rather than performing a task. They swoop down to snatch things from crevices — food, presumably. If you are standing near the root-walls with exposed crevices, you might get buzzed by a feeding bird. This is probably startling but not dangerous. Don't flail. Don't swat. Let them do their thing.

**Unknown fauna.** The description notes that doors are "of different sizes — some human-scale, some not." The non-human-scale doors suggest inhabitants or visitors of different body plans. You may encounter creatures or beings that are not described in what you've been told. Default to caution: maintain distance, do not block pathways, do not touch or approach. If something approaches you with apparent purpose, stand still and let it assess you. Rapid movement through unfamiliar spaces is how prey behaves; stillness and calm attention are more likely to be read as non-threatening by most forms of intelligence.

## Interacting with the Inhabitants

You haven't seen them. You've heard their voices. You've seen their tools, their schedules, their mug with its dried residue. They are here. They are aware that this is their city and their project.

**This is the most important part of your preparation: you are entering an active workplace and an emerging home.**

Not a tourist destination. Not a ruin. Not an abandoned wonder. A place where people — beings — are in the middle of building something enormous and complex, something that requires coordination between biological and mechanical systems, something that has a schedule (the peg board), a labor structure (the shift changes), an expansion plan (the guided root-tips, the empty lots held in reserve). You are walking into someone's life's work in progress.

**Assume you have not been invited.** Nothing in the description suggests an entry protocol, a welcome system, or any accommodation for visitors. The doors are fitted with hinges; they can be closed. The scheduling board is not labeled in your language. The city is between shifts, meaning you've arrived during a quiet period — and that quiet period will end. When it does, you will be a stranger in someone else's workplace.

**Do not enter the carved-out nodes with closed doors.** These are rooms. They belong to people. A closed door is universal.

**Do not handle the tools on the workbench.** They are laid out in order by someone who will return for them. The tuning fork-sized instrument, the calibrated devices you don't recognize — these are precision tools for work you don't understand. Moving them, handling them, even just picking one up to look at it could disrupt someone's workflow, damage calibration, or be interpreted as theft.

**Do not adjust the scheduling board.** Do not move the threads or pegs, even to examine them more closely. Document it visually and move on.

**When you encounter the inhabitants — and you will, because this is a living city and you are walking through it — the first interaction will set the tone for everything that follows.** You don't speak their language. They don't speak yours. Here is what you can do:

- **Be visible.** Don't lurk in the shadow of root-pillars. Don't duck behind structures when you hear footsteps. If you are seen trying to hide, you become an intruder. If you are seen standing openly in a thoroughfare, looking around with obvious curiosity, you become a stranger — and strangers, while inconvenient, are a known category in any settlement.

- **Be still when first seen.** Let them approach you. Let them set the terms. Stand with your hands visible and empty. If you're carrying equipment, set it down slowly and step slightly away from it — a gesture that says *I have things but I am not wielding them, and you can look at what I've brought.*

- **Mirror their formality.** If they speak to you, respond with calm, even tones, even though the words won't be understood. Vocal tone crosses language barriers. The cadence of cooperation — the back-and-forth rhythm you heard through the walls — is their natural mode. Match it. Ask questions, even if they can't be answered yet. Questioning tone is recognizable in nearly all languages and signals engagement rather than demand.

- **Offer something.** Not a gift, exactly — a contribution. You are in a construction zone. If you have a skill — if you can carry things, hold things steady, apply physical labor — a willingness to help is a lingua franca. But wait to be directed. Don't assume you know what needs doing.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where I want to spend the most time, because the Understory Engine will work on you psychologically in ways that are subtle and cumulative.

**The scale will disorient you.** Not in a vertigo sense — the spaces are generous but not abyssal. The disorientation is *categorical*. You are inside something alive, and it is the size of a city, and it is building itself, and people have built within it, and both of these things are happening simultaneously. Your brain will keep trying to classify the space — is this a building? a cave? a body? — and each classification will fail. This is tiring. It produces a low-grade cognitive dissonance that you'll carry the entire time you're there. Be aware of it. Take breaks. Sit on the smooth root-floor in one of the wide-margin empty stretches and just breathe the cool, tannin-laced air and let your categories relax. You don't have to understand this place all at once. No one does. The inhabitants themselves are still figuring it out — that's what the scheduling board is for.

**The sense of being inside a living body.** You will feel this. Pressing your hand to the pillars and feeling the whole city at work — the growth, the generators, the footsteps — this is intimate in a way that buildings are not. Buildings don't breathe. Buildings don't have a circulatory system that you're aware is keeping its moisture away from you on purpose. You are a guest inside something that is alive and aware of its own interior conditions and actively managing them. This can feel nurturing — the warmth of the light, the even coolness, the sense of being held. It can also feel claustrophobic — the knowledge that the walls are growing, that the space you occupy exists because the organism permits it, that you are surrounded in every direction by a living thing that is vastly larger than you and fundamentally unknowable. Both feelings are correct. Let them coexist.

**The emptiness between shifts.** You are arriving at a pause in the activity cycle. The factory floor is idle. The workbench is waiting. The voices faded. This gives the Understory Engine a liminal, held-breath quality — the feeling of a theater before the audience arrives, or a kitchen between the lunch rush and dinner prep. This pause won't last. Enjoy the quiet observation while you have it, but prepare yourself for the space to fill. When the shift changes, when the inhabitants return, when the machines start up and the beetles resume their humming and the pack-lizards multiply on their routes, the sensory load will increase dramatically. The wood transmits everything. You will feel the whole city come alive through the soles of your feet.

**The temporal uncanniness.** This city is young, but the organism is ancient. The iron fittings are new, but the root-architecture is timeless. The construction beetles work in minutes; the roots grow over months or years; the organism's full development spans centuries or millennia. You are standing at the intersection of multiple timescales, and all of them are active. The inhabitants have found a way to work within this — their scheduling board, their shift system, their trained root-tips — but you haven't. You may feel urgency where there should be patience, or patience where things are actually moving fast. Pay attention to the inhabitants' pace when you encounter them. They have calibrated their working rhythm to the timescales of this place. Follow their lead.

## Ethical Considerations

**You are visiting someone else's home during construction.** The ethical weight of this cannot be overstated.

The Understory Engine is not a natural wonder existing for your awe. It is not a ruin to be explored. It is a project — an ongoing, deliberate, incredibly ambitious collaboration between intelligent beings and a living organism (or perhaps the organism *is* one of the intelligent beings, and the collaboration is between it and the others, and the industrial infrastructure is the shared language they've developed). Whatever the relationship, it is working. The city is growing. The factories are being equipped. The beetles know their chalk marks. The light has been engineered.

**Do not assume this was built for you to find.** It probably wasn't. Multiverse travelers are, by definition, uninvited. The infrastructure has no visitor center, no signage in common languages, no obvious protocol for outsiders. You are crashing someone's construction site.

**Leave no trace.** Carry out everything you carry in. Don't leave chalk marks (even if you use them for navigation, erase them on your way out or use a different system). Don't discard food wrappers or water containers. Don't urinate in the root-city — the maintained dryness is a critical environmental parameter, and introducing liquid waste could trigger biological responses in the root-structure. Use a sealed personal container if necessary and carry it out.

**Do not take living samples.** Not a beetle, not a bark fragment from a living root, not a thread from the scheduling board. Dead material on the floor — sawdust, shed carapace fragments, fallen bark — is probably acceptable to collect in small quantities. But if in doubt, leave it.

**Be prepared to leave if asked.** If the inhabitants encounter you and indicate through any means — gesture, tone, physical herding, the sudden closing of doors, the redirection of pack-lizards to block your path — that you should not be there, go. Immediately. Without argument, without trying to negotiate one more look, without "just let me get a photo of—." Go. You can process what you've seen afterward, from outside.

## Supply List (Summary)

**Essential:**
- 4+ liters water per day, electrolyte supplements
- Food that requires no fire to prepare (bars, dried meals with flameless heaters, fruit)
- Sealed personal waste container
- Lightweight respirator with carbon filters
- Portable gas monitor (O2, CO2, VOC)
- Shoes with good grip on smooth wood, ankle support
- Layered clothing (base, insulation, shell)
- Light work gloves
- Headlamp (warm-tone, adjustable brightness) + handheld light
- Navigation: chalk (distinctive color), notebook, pencil, IMU/dead-reckoning device
- Small first aid kit with emphasis on skin/respiratory irritation (antihistamines, saline spray, eye drops, lip balm, hand cream)

**Recommended:**
- Camera with fast lens, low-light capability, tripod
- Audio recorder with contact microphone
- Small sterile sample containers (for loose, dead material only)
- Color reference card
- Earplugs or noise-isolating earbuds (for rest periods when the city is active and the wood is transmitting everything)
- Compact binoculars (for observing the upper canopy ecology and the moth-winged birds)

**Do not bring:**
- Open flame of any kind
- Liquid-based marking systems (spray paint, etc.)
- Anything that requires water to operate
- Weapons (there is nothing here to fight and much to lose by trying)
- Any sense of entitlement

## Final Thought

The Understory Engine is a place that has made itself a desert on the inside so that things other than itself can live there. Sit with that. An organism of immense scale and age has engineered its own body to be hospitable — has withheld its moisture, conducted its light, cycled its air, tolerated bolts in its bark and beetles chewing its wood — so that a city could grow inside it. Whether this is symbiosis or domestication or something entirely other, it is an act of accommodation so profound it borders on grace.

You are going to walk through a cathedral that is also a construction site that is also a body that is also a home, and every surface you touch will be alive and working. The least you can do is be a good guest. Be quiet. Be careful. Be amazed, but hold your amazement gently, like something that doesn't belong to you — because it doesn't. This place belongs to the ones who are building it and the one that is letting itself be built in. You are just passing through, and the greatest gift you can bring is the willingness to pass through without leaving a mark, carrying nothing out but memory and the faint green astringency of living wood on the back of your throat.
