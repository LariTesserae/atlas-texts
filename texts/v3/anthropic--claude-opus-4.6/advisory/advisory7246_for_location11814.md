# Preparing to Visit The Ridgeback

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description and understand the nature of this place. The Ridgeback is not hostile in the way a jungle is hostile or a frozen world is hostile. It won't try to kill you quickly. It is indifferent on a timescale that makes your visit a rounding error. The danger here is environmental attrition — dehydration, mineral dust, heat stress, disorientation across featureless terrain — compounded by the psychological weight of a place that operates on a clock so slow it makes your heartbeat feel frantic. You need to prepare for both.

---

## Atmospheric and Altitude Considerations

The Ridgeback summit stands at roughly eleven kilometers above the surrounding lowlands. On Earth, that would put you above the summit of Everest — unbreathable without supplemental oxygen, temperatures fatally cold. Here, the physics differ. The atmospheric composition, surface gravity, and baseline atmospheric pressure of this world are not specified, but several clues tell you the air at summit elevation is breathable, if thin. People live and work up there without apparent respiratory equipment. The air is described as warm. The thermal output of the Ridgeback itself moderates conditions.

Still, "breathable" and "comfortable" are different things. Plan for thin air. Your lungs will work harder. You'll fatigue faster than you expect, especially if you're coming from a standard-atmosphere world. Bring a portable pulse oximeter or its multiverse equivalent. If your blood oxygen saturation drops below 88% at rest, you need supplemental oxygen or you need to descend. Some travelers carry a small compressed-air supplement — not a full breathing apparatus, just a canister for occasional pulls when you feel lightheaded. This is wise here. The exertion of walking on bare rock in steady wind at altitude will tax you more than you anticipate.

Acclimatize if you can. If your arrival point is in the lowlands rather than directly on the Ridgeback, spend two or three days at intermediate elevation before ascending to the summit settlements. If you arrive directly at a settlement, spend your first full day resting. Do not attempt to walk the inter-settlement distances on your first day, no matter how fit you are.

## Hydration: The Central Problem

There is no water on the Ridgeback. Understand this completely. No streams. No wells. No condensation. No dew. No rain. The rock is warm at all times, the air is dry at all times, and your body will lose moisture continuously through respiration and perspiration. The wind — constant, steady, scouring from the northeast — will accelerate evaporative loss from your skin and lungs in ways you may not consciously register until you're already significantly dehydrated.

The settlements have water. It comes from atmospheric extraction plants that pull trace moisture and hydrogen-bearing compounds from the thin air, or from chemical processes in the industrial cycle, or possibly from deep mining operations that intersect hydrated mineral deposits. However it's produced, it is precious. It is the limiting factor on settlement size. When you are in a settlement, you will have access to water, but it will not be cheap and it will not be unlimited. Bring trade goods or currency appropriate to the local economy — more on this later — and understand that a significant portion of your expenditure will go to water.

Between settlements, you carry what you have. The inter-settlement distances are sixty to a hundred kilometers of bare rock with no shade, no water sources, and constant dry wind. At a walking pace in thin air with a pack, that is a minimum of two full days of travel, more likely three or four. You need to carry all your water for that duration plus a safety margin. A human body in hot, dry, windy conditions at altitude can require six to eight liters per day or more. For a three-day crossing, that's eighteen to twenty-four liters minimum — roughly eighteen to twenty-four kilograms of water weight alone. This is a serious load.

**Recommendations:**

- **Do not walk between settlements if the rail line is operational.** The train is slow, but it exists precisely because the distances are lethal on foot without significant preparation. Secure passage on the rail. It will be the single most important logistical decision of your visit.
- If you must walk — if the rail schedule doesn't align, if a section is under repair, if you want to explore the inter-settlement wilderness — bring a minimum of eight liters per day of expected travel time plus two extra days' supply. Use sealed, insulated containers. The ambient heat will warm your water; warm water is fine for hydration but you'll drink less of it instinctively, so discipline yourself.
- Bring oral rehydration salts or electrolyte supplements. You will be losing salt to sweat continuously. Plain water won't be enough.
- Monitor your urine color. If it darkens past pale straw, you are already behind on hydration. Stop, drink, rest.
- A broad-spectrum water purification method is wise to carry as backup, but understand there may be literally nothing to purify between settlements. This is not a "find water and treat it" situation. This is a "carry it or die" situation.

## Thermal Management

The Ridgeback presents a thermal environment unlike almost any other habitable location. You are being heated from two directions simultaneously — solar radiation from above (sharp, saturated, through thin atmosphere with minimal UV filtering) and geothermal radiation from below, radiated directly through the rock you're standing on. At midday, the combined heat load is substantial.

Yet this is not a sweltering tropical environment. The air itself may not be exceptionally hot — the surrounding lowlands are described as cooler than the Ridgeback despite being at lower altitude, and the warmth of the summit is attributed to the rock itself, not to atmospheric temperature. So you may experience the odd sensation of warm feet and a cool face, of standing in a steady wind that feels temperate while the ground beneath you radiates like a heated floor.

**Clothing:**

- **Footwear is critical.** The rock surface is warm at all times and actively hot in places, particularly near thermal vents or over sections where the crystalline structure channels heat toward the surface. Standard hiking boots may be insufficient. You want thick, insulated soles — not for cold but for heat. Multi-layer composite soles with thermal barriers. Test them before you arrive: stand on a surface heated to 60-70°C for ten minutes and see if your feet are comfortable. If not, upgrade.
- Carry a small, foldable insulating pad or mat for sitting and sleeping. You will never place your body directly against the rock for rest. The warmth that feels pleasant through boot soles will cook you over hours of direct skin contact during sleep.
- Loose, light-colored, full-coverage clothing. Long sleeves, long legs, a wide-brimmed hat or head covering. You are protecting against UV from above (thin atmosphere, reduced ozone likely) and reflected heat from the dark rock surfaces. The dark mineral absorbs and re-radiates heat; you want to reflect as much as possible.
- The wind is constant. Bring a windproof outer layer that breathes — you need evaporative cooling to function but you don't want the wind stripping your skin moisture faster than necessary. A balance.
- At night, the rock stays warm, so hypothermia is unlikely on the Ridgeback itself. But the air temperature may drop significantly with the sun gone and the wind continuing. A light insulating layer for nighttime is wise. You're unlikely to need heavy cold-weather gear, but don't assume the daytime warmth persists in the air after dark.

**Sun protection:**

- High-SPF sunscreen or physical sun barrier (zinc-based). Reapply frequently; the wind and dust will strip it.
- UV-blocking eye protection. The combination of thin atmosphere, bright G-type star, and highly reflective mineral surfaces (feldspar shear zones, polished olivine) can produce dangerous glare. Polarized lenses recommended.
- Lip protection. Your lips will crack in this environment within hours without it.

## Dust and Respiratory Protection

The wind-polished mineral surfaces of the Ridgeback are not inert from a respiratory standpoint. Pyroxene and olivine are silicate minerals. Fine silicate dust — even if this world's specific mineralogy differs slightly from Earth equivalents — is a serious respiratory hazard with prolonged exposure. The settlements have thick-walled architecture with shuttered windows designed for dust storms. The locals live here permanently. You are visiting.

Bring a quality particulate filter mask or respirator rated for fine mineral dust. Wear it:
- During any dust event, even a minor one
- When walking in sustained wind across open ridge surfaces (the constant northeast wind carries fine particles)
- Near any active mining or quarrying operations
- Optionally, at all times outdoors if you want to be cautious

A buff or bandana is better than nothing but not sufficient for fine crystalline silicate particles. Proper filtration matters. Silicosis-equivalent conditions likely exist among long-term residents, though they may have adapted culturally or biologically in ways you haven't.

Also protect your eyes — sealed goggles for dust events, wraparound glasses at minimum for normal conditions. Mineral dust in the eyes is painful and potentially damaging.

## Navigation and Orientation

The inter-settlement terrain is described as having almost no visual referents. Dark rock with slow color shifts. No vegetation, no water features, no trees. The mineral towers and arches provide some landmarks, but they are irregular, wind-carved, and may look similar to each other across distances. This is terrain where disorientation is a serious risk.

**Do not rely on visual navigation between settlements.**

- Bring a compass if this world has a usable magnetic field (the ultramafic rock of the Ridgeback itself may produce local magnetic anomalies — calibrate carefully and be aware of deviation).
- A GPS-equivalent is ideal if the settlements maintain any satellite or beacon infrastructure. Ask locally.
- The rail line is your lifeline for navigation as well as transport. If you must walk between settlements, follow the rail tracks. Do not deviate from them into open ridgeline. The tracks connect to where you need to be. The open ridge connects to nothing.
- Mark your position relative to the summit crenellations. The Ridgeback runs NE-SW; if you can determine the sun's arc relative to this axis, you can maintain rough orientation.
- Carry a light source with significant range. At night, the settlements glow amber from their fissure-slots and are visible from distance in clear air. But "clear air" is relative when there's mineral haze, and the distances are vast.

## Food and Sustenance

The Ridgeback has no biosphere to speak of. The clingers are not described as edible, and eating something that subsists by ingesting mineral rock and excreting depleted silicate is inadvisable without extensive local knowledge. The aerial gas-bladder creatures are rarely seen and unlikely to be a practical food source. Nothing grows here.

The settlements must import, synthesize, or extract all their nutrition. This likely means:
- Mineral-derived chemical synthesis (amino acids, carbohydrates built from atmospheric and mineral feedstocks using geothermal power)
- Imports from the lowlands or off-world, brought in by rail or by whatever means originally supplied the settlements
- Possibly some form of chemosynthetic cultivation in the deep warm shafts — vat-grown proteins, mineral-substrate cultures

Whatever they eat, it will be dense, calorie-efficient, and probably not varied. Expect a utilitarian cuisine. Bring supplemental food for your stay — high-calorie, low-weight, shelf-stable. Nutrient bars, dried meals, whatever your preferred multiverse travel rations are. Enough for your entire planned stay plus emergency surplus. Do not assume you can purchase sufficient food locally; the settlements are small, resources are precious, and an unexpected mouth to feed for days or weeks may strain local goodwill.

If you do eat locally, be gracious about it. Ask what's appropriate. Offer reciprocity in whatever form is valued.

## Social Preparation

The people of the Ridgeback operate on a different temporal and psychological register than you likely do. They plan in generations. They speak slowly. Their common greeting — *what are you building that you won't finish?* — is not small talk. It is a philosophical statement about their relationship to time, labor, and continuity. You are arriving from a multiverse of rapid transitions and varied worlds. They have been cutting pyroxene blocks and extending a rail line for a hundred years.

**Understand what your presence means to them:**

You are a flicker. A brief visitor from outside their slow, patient, deeply rooted existence. This can be received warmly — novelty is rare here, and a traveler brings news, perspectives, trade goods, and the simple interest of difference. Or it can be received with wariness — you are fast where they are slow, you will leave where they remain, and your presence highlights the transience they have organized their entire culture around accepting.

**Practical social guidance:**

- Slow down. Consciously. Speak more slowly than feels natural. Pause before responding. Do not interrupt. These are people who experience rushed speech and abrupt movement as something between rude and unsettling.
- When greeted with "what are you building that you won't finish?" have an answer. A real one. Think about it before you arrive. What are you working toward that exceeds your own lifespan or capacity? What do you contribute to that you'll never see completed? If you can answer this honestly, you will be understood. If you deflect or joke, you will be politely tolerated but not trusted.
- Offer something. Not as a transaction but as a gesture of participation. You are on their ridge, breathing their extracted air, drinking their extracted water. What do you bring? Information about other worlds is valuable. Tools are valuable. Expertise is valuable. Stories might be valuable if told well and slowly. Find what you can contribute.
- Respect the pace. If someone says they'll meet you tomorrow, they mean it. If a project is described as nearly complete and you learn it's been underway for thirty years, do not express surprise. Thirty years is a reasonable duration for meaningful work here.
- Ask about what they're building. This is how you express genuine interest. Everyone on the Ridgeback is building something they won't finish. They want to tell you about it. Let them.

## The Clingers

You will encounter them. They are arthropod-like, warm, rock-colored, and range from a few centimeters to dog-sized. They are on the walls around the settlements, on the mineral towers, on any sun-warmed rock face. Often you won't see them until you hear the scraping of their mouthparts on crystal, or until you place your hand on a rock surface and feel something shift beneath your fingers.

**Are they dangerous?** The description doesn't indicate aggression, but:
- The largest ones are dog-sized. Any animal that mass, regardless of temperament, can injure you if startled or threatened.
- They have mouthparts capable of scraping crystalline rock. Those mouthparts can scrape you.
- Their ventral surfaces are specialized for heat absorption. They may be warm to the touch, or they may have chemical or textural properties on their undersides that you don't want against your skin.

**Practical guidance:**
- Do not place hands, feet, or body weight on rock surfaces without looking first. The camouflage is near-perfect.
- Watch where you sit. A south-facing warm rock slab that looks like an ideal rest spot may have a dozen clingers on it that you haven't noticed.
- If you find them on your equipment or your sleeping area, move them gently if they're small. Ask locals for protocol with the larger ones. The locals have lived alongside these creatures for their entire history; they know how they behave.
- Do not attempt to eat them.
- They excrete fine depleted silicate powder. This powder may be on surfaces where they've been feeding. It is yet another source of respiratory fine particulates. One more reason for your mask.

## The Aerial Creatures

Gas-bladder organisms that ride the thermal updrafts along the Ridgeback's flanks. They feed on clingers or clinger byproducts. They descend onto rock faces "with a quiet precision that is almost gentle."

You are unlikely to encounter them in settlements (they avoid turbine vibration). On the open ridge, they may be present. They are above you, in the thermal columns, and they come down.

- They are predators or scavengers of the clingers. You are not a clinger. But you are a warm body on the rock surface, and misidentification by an organism with an alien sensory apparatus is always possible.
- Be aware of their presence overhead when resting on the open ridge. If you see elongated ribbed shapes drifting in the updrafts, you're in their territory.
- They are described as lighter-than-air, which means they're not massive, but "dropping down onto rock faces" implies controlled descent and some degree of purposeful action. Give them space.

## The Bore-Holes

Something lives inside the Ridgeback. Something that excavates smooth-bore holes into warm rock. Something that miners have never directly encountered but whose tunnels intersect their shafts. Something that occasionally produces a single deep resonant tone through the rock — "the question."

**This is the most unknown variable of your visit.**

No one knows what the burrowers are. No one has seen them. The holes are biological — round-bored, smooth-sided, angled downward into increasing heat. Whatever makes them is comfortable in conditions that would kill you — deep rock, high temperatures, zero light, minimal atmosphere.

**Guidance:**
- Do not enter bore-holes. This should be obvious, but multiverse travelers have a documented tendency toward the obviously inadvisable. The holes angle downward into the deep Ridgeback where temperatures increase with depth. You have no knowledge of what excavated them, what their current status is, or whether the tunnels remain structurally stable.
- If you encounter a bore-hole on the surface, note its location, observe from a distance, and report it to the nearest settlement if it appears recent. The locals track these.
- If you hear "the question" — the deep resonant tone from below — sit with it. Do not attempt to locate its source. Do not shout into bore-holes. Do not drop objects into bore-holes. Whatever produces this sound has been doing so since before human habitation. It does not require your investigation. It requires your respect.
- In mining areas, if you are permitted to descend (the deep thermal shafts are industrial workplaces, not tourist attractions — ask permission), be aware that bore-hole intersections have been found. The miners treat these with what sounds like professional caution rather than alarm. Follow their lead.

## The Rail

The narrow-gauge electric rail line connecting the settlements is your primary means of inter-settlement travel. It runs the length of the Ridgeback, powered by overhead catenary wires from pylons bolted into the ridge. The trains are electric, slow, and presumably infrequent given the small population and vast distances.

**Practical considerations:**
- Determine the schedule immediately upon arrival at any settlement. Trains may run daily, weekly, or irregularly. Missing one could strand you for significant time.
- The rail cars are likely utilitarian — built for mineral transport with passenger accommodation as a secondary function. Bring your own comfort. A seat pad, your own water, your own food.
- The journey between settlements at slow speed over sixty to a hundred kilometers may take several hours. The views will be of bare mineral ridgeline. This can be meditative or maddening depending on your temperament. Bring something to occupy your mind if you need it, but consider instead simply watching the Ridgeback pass. This is a rare thing to see.
- The catenary system is described as constantly maintained because the tracks heat-warp. There may be delays, diversions, or stoppages for maintenance. Build slack into your schedule.
- Ask whether you can ride freight cars or maintenance vehicles if the regular schedule doesn't serve your needs. Small communities often have informal arrangements.

## Equipment Checklist

Consolidating the above into a practical list:

**Essentials:**
- Water: maximum you can carry, minimum 4L per day between settlements, plus purification capability
- Electrolyte supplements
- Food: full supply for planned duration plus 50% emergency surplus
- Insulated-sole boots (heat-rated)
- Insulating ground pad/mat for sitting and sleeping
- Particulate respirator mask with spare filters
- UV-blocking wraparound eye protection and sealed goggles for dust events
- Full-coverage light-colored clothing with windproof breathable outer layer
- Wide-brimmed hat
- High-SPF sunscreen and lip protection
- Pulse oximeter
- Small supplemental oxygen supply
- Navigation tools: compass (with awareness of magnetic anomalies), beacon receiver if available
- Light source with good range
- Multi-tool
- First aid kit emphasizing: blister care, wound cleaning (mineral dust in wounds is an infection vector), heat exhaustion treatment, eye wash

**Recommended:**
- Trade goods appropriate to a resource-scarce industrial community (information, tools, specialized materials, skills)
- Notebook and writing implement (the slow pace rewards journaling; also useful for recording observations the locals may value)
- Ear protection (for proximity to turbine infrastructure; the sub-bass drone is felt more than heard but prolonged exposure to industrial vibration has physiological effects)
- A small amount of something to share — not necessarily physical. A skill you can demonstrate. A story you can tell slowly.

**Leave behind:**
- Anything that requires water to function or maintain
- Perishable goods (the heat will destroy them)
- Urgency

## Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section.

The Ridgeback will work on you in ways that are difficult to anticipate. The combination of factors — the silence-with-a-floor, the limited palette, the featureless distances, the slow pace, the heat from below, the constant wind, the absence of biological comfort (no greenery, no water sounds, no familiar life) — produces an altered state in many visitors. Some find it profoundly peaceful. Others find it claustrophobic despite the vast open spaces, because the sameness of the environment creates a psychological enclosure. There is nowhere to go that is different from where you are.

The settlements offer human contact and the warmth of community, but they are communities of people who have fully adapted to a pace of life you have not. You may feel rushed in your own skin. Your thoughts may feel too fast, too jumpy, too surface-level. The locals will notice. They won't judge — they've seen travelers before — but you may judge yourself.

**Suggestions:**
- Before arriving, practice stillness. Meditate, or simply sit without stimulation for extended periods. An hour. Two hours. Get comfortable with your own unoccupied mind. The Ridgeback will give you a lot of time with it.
- Accept that you will not understand everything. The burrowers, the question, the clingers' metabolism, the deep logic of why this place is the way it is — these are open mysteries. You are not here to solve them. You are here to witness them.
- When you hear the question — the resonant tone from below — let it be a question. Don't try to answer it. The locals haven't in all their generations, and they are not troubled by this. The unanswered question is part of the architecture of this place, as fundamental as the heat and the stone.
- Think about your answer to *what are you building that you won't finish?* Not as a performance but as a genuine act of self-examination. If you don't have an answer, that's worth knowing about yourself. If you do, it will connect you to these people more deeply than any trade good.

## Duration

How long should you stay?

A day is too short. You'll see the surface of a settlement and feel the heat from the rock and leave without understanding anything.

A week begins to work. By the third or fourth day, if you let it, the pace starts to slow your internal clock. You begin to hear the turbine drone as silence. You begin to notice the clingers without startling. You begin to understand why the buildings are the way they are, why the windows are deep-set, why everything is heavy.

A month, and you'll ride the rail between settlements and see the Ridgeback as a place rather than a spectacle. You'll have conversations that unfold over days. You'll start a project you won't finish.

Longer than that, and you begin to face a real question — not the one from the bore-holes, but your own. Whether to leave. The Ridgeback has a gravitational pull that is not physical. The patience of the place, the warmth from below, the slow certainty of work measured in generations — it offers something that faster worlds don't. Some travelers stay. They take up a tool, they join a construction crew, they begin learning the rock. They stop being visitors and become the newest thing on the Ridgeback.

You should decide before you arrive how long you're staying, and you should hold to that decision. Not because staying would be wrong, but because the decision to stay should be made freely and not by default, not because the Ridgeback's slow gravity captured you before you realized it was pulling.

## Final Note

When you stand on the Ridgeback at night, with the warm rock beneath your insulated mat and the stars sharp and close through the thin atmosphere and the wind steady from the northeast and the amber glow of a settlement visible in its slot-fissure five kilometers away, and you feel the sub-bass hum of the turbines in your sternum, and a clinger shifts on a rock face nearby with a sound like a single stroke of a file, and then from somewhere below — deep, sourceless, patient — a single resonant tone rises through the stone and through your body and into the dark air and fades —

You will understand why they call it a question.

You will not know what it's asking.

This is correct. This is the appropriate response. Sit with it. Feel the warmth from below. Let the Ridgeback breathe. You are the newest thing here, and not the strangest, and that is a gift — to be small and brief and present on something ancient and slow and still deciding.

Go well. Carry enough water. Answer the greeting honestly.
