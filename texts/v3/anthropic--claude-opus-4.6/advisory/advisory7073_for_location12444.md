# Preparing for the Bilge Quarter

---

## Understanding What You're Getting Into

Before anything else, sit with this: the Bilge Quarter is not hostile. It is not welcoming either. It is *indifferent* in the way that a reef is indifferent, or the way the inside of a functioning machine is indifferent. It will not try to kill you, but it will not accommodate you, and the difference between those two things is narrower than you'd like.

You are going to spend time — possibly a long time — fully submerged in warm, biologically active water, inside a structure so dense with construction that you will rarely see more than a few meters in any direction. You will navigate by touch, by memory, and by the dim halos of widely-spaced industrial lamps. The pressure won't be extreme (the depression isn't ocean-deep; you're looking at maybe twenty to forty meters of water overhead at most, depending on where in the Quarter you go), but the *enclosure* will be total. There is no surface to swim up to. There is no air pocket waiting around the corner. The entire volume of this place is water, and has been for decades, and everything in it — the people, the kelp, the machinery, the architecture — has adapted to that fact. You have not adapted. You are visiting. Plan accordingly.

This is not an adventure dive. This is not a flooded cave system you're passing through. You will be *living* inside this water for the duration of your stay — sleeping in it, eating in it, moving through it. The Bilge Quarter has no tourism infrastructure, no visitor center, no decompression lounge. You arrive wet and you stay wet until you leave.

## Breathing: The Central Problem

The most immediate concern is obvious: you need to breathe, and there's no air here.

**Do not rely on a single system.** Whatever breathing apparatus you bring, bring redundancy. The Bilge Quarter's environment is corrosive in subtle ways — the warm water is mineral-rich, biologically active, and carries dissolved compounds from the deep machinery that you probably can't fully characterize in advance. Standard dive equipment will function, but its lifespan will be shortened. Seals degrade faster. Filters clog with bioite particulates. Metal components develop surface corrosion within hours.

**Recommended approach: closed-circuit rebreather with chemical scrubbers, plus at minimum two independent backup gas supplies.** The rebreather is your primary — it's quiet (important; more on this later), efficient with gas, and gives you the longest operational window. The backup supplies are for when the rebreather's scrubber medium gets contaminated by something in the local water, which it will, eventually. Plan for scrubber cartridge replacement at twice the rate you'd normally expect. Bring four times as many cartridges as you think you need.

Some multiverse travelers have reported success with bio-adapted gill systems — semi-organic membrane units that extract dissolved oxygen directly from water. If you have access to one and experience using it, this is arguably the ideal solution for the Bilge Quarter, as it eliminates the gas supply problem entirely. But be aware: the dissolved oxygen content here is managed by the deep machinery's pumping cycle, and it fluctuates. The water *breathes*, as the description notes, on a cycle of hours. During the low phase of that cycle, dissolved oxygen drops. If your gill system can't compensate, you'll feel it as a slow, creeping air-hunger that builds over twenty or thirty minutes. Have a backup gas supply even if you're gill-equipped.

**Under no circumstances plan to find or create air pockets inside the Quarter.** The architecture remembers air but no longer requires it. Some older structures have sealed internal spaces that might theoretically be drained, but the inhabitants haven't maintained these as breathable environments, and any trapped gas in such spaces is likely toxic — a stale stew of machinery off-gassing, bacterial metabolism, and chemical byproducts that's been accumulating in an unventilated pocket for decades. Cracking one open would be worse than useless.

## Thermal Regulation

The water is blood-warm. This sounds comfortable. It is not.

Blood-temperature water is insidious because your body cannot shed heat into it. In cooler water, you lose heat and need insulation. In water that matches your core temperature, you're thermally neutral — which means any metabolic heat you generate from movement, exertion, or even digestion has nowhere to go. Over hours, this creeps up on you. You get warm. Then warmer. Then you develop a low, persistent headache and a vague nausea that you might mistake for anxiety or claustrophobia but is actually the early onset of hyperthermia.

**You need active cooling.** A standard wetsuit will make this worse by trapping additional heat. A drysuit is marginally better for keeping the biologically active water off your skin (advisable for other reasons), but it still won't solve the heat problem. What you want is a suit with an integrated cooling loop — a thin-tube network circulating fluid against your skin, with a small heat-exchange unit that dumps your excess thermal energy into the ambient water slightly faster than the ambient water puts it back. This sounds paradoxical — cooling yourself into water that's the same temperature as you — but the engineering is straightforward. The key is that moving water carries heat more efficiently than still water, and the heat exchanger can create a localized temperature differential sufficient to draw heat off your body, even if it's only shunting that heat a few meters away.

Bring spare pumps for the cooling loop. The warm, mineral-rich water will scale up the heat exchanger's external surfaces within a day or two. Plan to clean or replace the exchanger element regularly.

If you can't source active cooling, your fallback is behavioral: move slowly, rest often, avoid exertion, and periodically find areas near the deep machinery's water intake pipes, where the cycling action creates slightly cooler microcurrents. The inhabitants know where these are. Watch where they rest.

## The Water Itself

Let's talk about what you're submerged in.

This is not clean water. It's not filthy either — the deep machinery cycles and presumably filters it to some degree — but it is *biological*. It carries dissolved minerals, organic particulates from the kelp and the bioite crust, metabolic waste products from the fauna (the eels, the rust-colored filter organisms, whatever else lives in the crust layers), trace compounds from corroding infrastructure, and whatever the deep machinery puts into it or fails to fully remove.

**Skin contact:** Prolonged immersion will affect your skin. Within the first day, expect your skin to soften and wrinkle beyond normal water-wrinkling — the warm mineral content accelerates maceration. Within two to three days, you may develop a faint rash, particularly in skin folds and areas where your suit's seals create friction. This is likely a combination of mineral irritation and early colonization by the ubiquitous microorganisms that form the bioite crust on every surface. They will try to colonize you. You are a surface.

**A full drysuit with sealed extremities is strongly recommended.** Gloves, boots, hood — everything sealed. Minimize exposed skin. Where your face is exposed (around your breathing apparatus), apply a thick barrier cream, medical grade, something designed for extended water exposure. Reapply whenever you can.

If you're using a bio-adapted gill system mounted on your neck or torso, the interface zone where the gill meets your skin will be particularly vulnerable. Keep the surrounding area treated with antifungal and antimicrobial agents. Check it every few hours.

**Ingestion:** Do not swallow the water. This seems obvious, but over a multi-day stay in full submersion, some water ingestion is nearly inevitable — clearing your mask, adjusting your mouthpiece, eating (yes, you'll have to eat; see below). The water probably won't poison you acutely, but the microbial load is unknown, the mineral content is uncharacterized, and whatever the deep machinery is extracting from or adding to the water is a complete unknown. Carry broad-spectrum gut prophylactics and take them daily. If you develop gastrointestinal symptoms, don't assume they're from the food.

**Eyes:** If you're not wearing a full-face mask, use sealed goggles. The water will irritate unprotected eyes within hours, and the particulates may cause corneal micro-abrasions that you won't feel initially (the warm water acts as a mild analgesic on surface tissues) but that will become problematic later. If your vision starts to blur or halo — beyond the natural murkiness of the environment — flush your eyes with clean water from your carried supply immediately.

## Pressure Considerations

The Bilge Quarter sits in a depression, and you enter from above, descending through the water column. The depth isn't extreme — probably equivalent to a moderate recreational dive at the deepest occupied levels, and substantially shallower in the upper structures. But you'll be at depth for *far longer* than a normal dive. This changes the calculus dramatically.

**Nitrogen narcosis** is unlikely at these depths on standard breathing mixtures. **Oxygen toxicity** is similarly unlikely unless you're breathing enriched mixes, which you shouldn't need to.

**Decompression** is the real concern. If you're breathing compressed gas at depth for days, your tissues will be thoroughly saturated. Coming back up will require an extremely slow ascent with extended decompression stops, or you'll need access to a decompression facility at the surface (or wherever you exit to). Plan this before you go down. Know how long you intend to stay and calculate your decompression obligation accordingly. For stays of more than a few days at even moderate depth, you may be looking at many hours of staged decompression on ascent.

Alternatively, if your breathing system uses a non-nitrogenous gas mixture or a bio-extraction method that doesn't involve inert gas loading, decompression may be simplified or eliminated. Know your equipment and plan accordingly.

## Navigation

There are no streets. There are no maps — or if there are, they're maintained by the inhabitants in a format you may not be able to access or interpret. You will navigate by feel.

**This is harder than you think.** The Bilge Quarter is three-dimensional in a way that surface environments are not. The gaps between buildings run horizontally, vertically, and at angles. You can move up, down, sideways, through openings that might be doors or might be structural failures. The kelp obscures surfaces and makes identical-looking passages out of structurally different ones. The lighting is sparse and inconsistent. You will get disoriented. Accept this now.

**Recommended navigation aids:**

- **A reel and line.** Old-fashioned, reliable, doesn't corrode quickly. Tie off at your entry point and run line behind you as you move. This is your lifeline back. Use it. Don't be cavalier about it. In the blackout zones between lamp-pools, your reel line may be the only thing that keeps you from becoming permanently lost in a maze of identical-feeling ferrocite walls.

- **Tactile markers.** Small clips or tags you can fix to surfaces (guide-cables, pipe runs, structural elements) at decision points. Carry them in a pouch on your harness where you can find them by touch alone. Mark your turns. Mark dead ends so you don't re-enter them.

- **A compass or inertial positioning unit.** A compass may not be reliable — the ferrocite construction and the machinery below will create significant magnetic anomalies. An inertial unit is better, if you have one. It won't give you a map, but it will give you a vector back to your entry point, and in this environment, that's worth everything.

- **Follow the pipes.** The pipe networks are the closest thing to a navigational grid. Major pipe runs generally follow the primary axes of the architecture — they route from the deep machinery upward and outward through the structure. If you can identify a major pipe run and follow it, you'll at least be moving in a consistent direction. Some pipes are labeled, though the markings are degraded. Learn whatever coding system you can decipher.

- **Follow the inhabitants.** If you see someone moving through the Quarter with purpose, they know where they're going. Follow at a distance if they'll tolerate it. Don't crowd them in the narrow gaps — there isn't room, and it's presumably rude.

## Equipment and Gear

Beyond breathing and thermal management, here's what to bring:

**Lighting.** Bring your own lights, at least two, with the understanding that they will not illuminate much. The water attenuates light severely — particulates scatter it, the kelp absorbs it, and the general murkiness limits even a powerful beam to a few meters of useful visibility. A focused, narrow-beam light is more useful than a flood — it lets you see what's directly ahead without creating a useless orb of scattered glow. But also carry a dim, wide-angle light for general orientation. And note: the inhabitants may not appreciate bright lights. They've adapted to near-darkness. A blazing beam in one of the narrow seams between buildings is the equivalent of someone shining a floodlight through your bedroom window. Be judicious.

**A knife or multi-tool.** The kelp is everywhere and will snag your gear, your line, your limbs. You'll need to cut yourself free at some point. Make sure the blade is ceramic or high-grade stainless — regular steel will corrode in this water within a day. The multi-tool is for everything else: tightening fittings on your equipment, prying open stuck hatches if you need to enter structures, scraping bioite off your gear.

**Food and water.** Yes, water. You're surrounded by water you shouldn't drink. Carry sealed containers of clean drinking water. For food, you need things that can be consumed underwater — this is awkward but not impossible. Nutrient pastes in squeeze tubes, similar to what astronauts or technical divers use. High-calorie, low-bulk, sealed against contamination. You'll eat by bringing the tube to your mouth, removing your mouthpiece briefly (if using a regulator) or feeding it under your full-face mask's seal, squeezing the paste in, and resealing. Practice this before you go. Fumbling with food in warm murky water while hovering in a kelp-choked gap between two buildings is not the time to discover you can't work the tube seal with gloves on.

**A slate or writing surface.** Communication underwater is primarily visual — hand signals, written messages. Carry a wrist-mounted slate with a stylus. Useful for communicating with your own party and potentially with inhabitants, though they may use a communication system you can't easily participate in (vibration-based signaling through the structures, for instance, given the way sound behaves here).

**Spare parts for everything.** Seals, o-rings, hose clamps, valve components, scrubber cartridges, battery packs, bulbs or LED modules. The environment eats equipment. Corrosion, bioite fouling, mineral scaling — everything degrades faster here than you're used to. Your maintenance schedule should be aggressive. Check all seals and connections at least twice daily. Clean the bioite off your equipment's moving parts before it calcifies. Replace anything that shows early signs of failure — in this environment, early signs become late-stage failure faster than you expect.

## The Inhabitants

You will see them. They will see you. Beyond that, very little is certain.

They are human, or were, or are something that grew out of human and has not finished growing into whatever it's becoming. They move with the slow deliberation of people conserving energy in a resistant medium — which is exactly what they're doing. They pull themselves along guide-cables that you can feel strung between anchor points throughout the Quarter, a three-dimensional web of transit routes that they navigate with the unconscious ease of a commuter on a familiar subway. Their movements are efficient, minimal, almost hypnotic to watch — there's no wasted motion, no flailing, no unnecessary expenditure of energy against the water.

You probably can't tell much about their physical appearance in the dim light and murky water. You'll get impressions: that they're dressed in close-fitting garments (loose clothing catches on everything in this environment), that some of them carry tools or containers, that their skin may have an unusual texture or coloration (bioite accumulation? adaptation? both?). You might notice that they seem comfortable in a way that goes beyond mere adaptation — that the water is not something they're enduring but something they're *part of*, the way you are part of the air you breathe.

**Approach with humility and patience.** You are a visitor in an environment that is foreign to you and native to them. You are clumsy where they are graceful. You are dependent on machinery for what they accomplish with their bodies. You are loud — your breathing apparatus makes noise, your movements are ungainly, you bump into things. In a place where the prevailing sound is a deep subsonic hum and the prevailing movement is slow and intentional, you are conspicuous.

Don't assume they speak any language you know, or that they communicate primarily through sound at all. The water compresses and distorts speech. They may use hand signals, written messages, tactile codes (touching specific patterns on each other's arms or hands), or vibration-based communication transmitted through the structures. Watch how they interact with each other before you try to interact with them. Mirror their communication modes as best you can.

**Don't touch what you can't identify.** The pipes, the valves, the machinery access points — these are the circulatory system of the Quarter. Turning the wrong valve or pulling the wrong cable could affect water circulation, temperature, filtration, or any of the other critical systems that make this place livable. The inhabitants know which pipes are active and which are defunct, which valves are functional and which have been welded shut and bypassed. You don't. Keep your hands to yourself unless you're told otherwise.

**Respect the kelp.** This is not decoration. It's not an inconvenience. The kelp is part of the ecosystem — it may be part of the food chain, part of the water filtration system, part of the bioluminescent lighting. The inhabitants have clearly maintained a relationship with it, clearing it where necessary and tolerating it everywhere else. Don't hack through it indiscriminately to clear your path. Part it gently, pass through, let it close behind you.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most travelers underestimate the Bilge Quarter.

The physical challenges are manageable with good equipment and planning. The psychological challenges are deeper (pun acknowledged, not intended) and harder to prepare for.

**Claustrophobia.** The Quarter is *tight*. You are enclosed on all sides — by water, by walls, by kelp, by pipes. You cannot see the sky. You cannot see the bottom. Your visual range is a few meters at best. You are navigating by touch in near-darkness through gaps barely wide enough for your body. If you have any tendency toward claustrophobia, this environment will find it and magnify it. Test yourself honestly before you go. Spend time in confined underwater spaces — flooded tunnels, wreck interiors, restricted cave passages. If you can't maintain calm in those environments, the Bilge Quarter will be very difficult for you.

**Temporal disorientation.** There is no daylight cycle here. There is no visible clock (or if there is, you probably can't read it). The ambient conditions — light, temperature, sound — are nearly constant. The only rhythmic variation is the slow breathing of the water cycle, and that operates on a scale of hours that you'll have trouble perceiving consciously. You will lose track of time. Bring a watch. Check it often. Maintain a deliberate schedule of sleep, waking, eating, and equipment maintenance, or you will drift into an unstructured haze that compounds every other psychological challenge.

**The hum.** You will feel the deep machinery's vibration in your bones from the moment you arrive, and it will not stop. It's sub-audible, which means you can't tune it out the way you'd tune out a constant noise — it bypasses your ears and registers directly in your skeleton, your sinuses, your viscera. Most people find it merely odd at first. After several hours, some find it soothing — a kind of full-body white noise. Others find it increasingly oppressive, a sensation that the place itself is vibrating *through* you, that you are losing the boundary between your body and the structure. Both responses are normal. Neither is a reason to panic. If the hum becomes distressing, focus on something else — a task, a navigation problem, the texture of a wall under your gloved hand. Give your brain something to organize itself around that isn't the vibration.

**The slowness.** Everything here is slow. Movement through water is slow. Navigation is slow. Communication is slow. The kelp moves slowly. The inhabitants move slowly. The water cycles slowly. You will feel an urge to move faster, do more, push through — this is your surface-dwelling instincts rebelling against an environment that operates at a fraction of your normal pace. Resist this urge. Moving fast in the Bilge Quarter wastes energy, generates heat you can't shed, disturbs the kelp and the water column, makes noise, and increases the risk of equipment snag or damage. Slow down. Match the pace of the place. You will accomplish less per hour than you're used to, and that is correct.

**The aliveness of the environment.** This may be the most difficult thing to prepare for, because it operates below the threshold of easy articulation. The Bilge Quarter is not a ruin. It's not a machine. It's not a habitat. It's something that has been all of these things and has, through decades of continuous occupation and adaptation, become an *organism* — not metaphorically, but in the way that a coral reef is an organism, or a city is an organism. The kelp reaches toward light and warmth. The mineral deposits form patterns that look like something organized. The water feels thicker in some passages, as though the space itself has variable density. The machinery responds to conditions you can't measure.

You will begin to feel, after a day or two, that the Quarter is aware of you. Not watching you — awareness without eyes, without intent as you understand it. Just... registering your presence the way a body registers a splinter. You are a foreign object in a living system, and the system notices, and begins — very slowly, very patiently — to respond. The kelp filament reaching for your sleeve is the most literal expression of this, but it's not the only one. You may notice the water temperature shifting slightly in the passages you frequent. You may notice the bioluminescence brightening faintly near your habitual resting spot. You may notice that the gaps you use regularly seem — seem — marginally wider than they were, as though the bioite crust has thinned where you pass.

This could be confirmation bias. It could be coincidence. It could be the normal metabolic response of a biological system to a new input. Or the Quarter could be accommodating you, slowly, the way it accommodates everything, incorporating you into itself piece by piece.

This is unsettling. It should be unsettling. Hold the unsettlement without letting it become panic. You are visiting a place that is alive in a way that doesn't map neatly onto your categories of alive and not-alive. It's not trying to consume you. It's trying to *include* you, because that's what it does — that's what decades of continuous adaptation have made it. A system that includes what enters it.

The kelp will grow on you if you stay still. Don't stay still too long.

## Specific Logistical Recommendations

**Duration of stay:** Plan for no more than three to five days on your first visit. This gives you enough time to orient yourself, observe the inhabitants, explore a meaningful portion of the Quarter, and begin to understand its rhythms — without staying so long that equipment degradation, psychological fatigue, or the cumulative effects of the water begin to pose serious problems. You can always return.

**Entry and exit:** Identify your entry point clearly and mark it unmistakably. Consider installing a small anchor beacon — something that emits a low-frequency pulse you can home in on from elsewhere in the Quarter. Make sure it's secured against drift and against kelp overgrowth (it will start to be colonized immediately). Plan your exit during a high phase of the water cycle, when dissolved oxygen is at its maximum and the current from the machinery's pumping is at its strongest — this will make the ascent marginally easier and ensure your breathing system has the best possible gas supply during the decompression process.

**Buddy system:** Do not visit alone. Bring at least one other person, preferably someone with technical diving experience and strong composure in confined, low-visibility environments. Maintain physical contact or tethered connection at all times when moving through blackout zones. Agree on emergency signals in advance — light patterns, tug codes on the tether, whatever works. In this environment, a minor equipment failure that would be trivially manageable with a buddy can become life-threatening when you're alone, disoriented, and unable to call for help through water that smothers sound.

**Medical kit:** Carry a sealed waterproof medical kit with the following, at minimum: broad-spectrum antibiotics (you're immersed in a biologically active soup and any skin break is an infection risk), anti-inflammatory medications, topical antifungals, antihistamines (in case of reaction to local biologicals), wound sealant that cures underwater, and a sedative (in case of panic — yours or your buddy's. It happens. The environment is psychologically intense. No shame in needing chemical assistance to regain composure).

**Documentation:** If you want to record what you see, use a sealed camera system designed for extended underwater use in low-light conditions. Night-vision or infrared capability will be more useful than a powerful flash — a flash will illuminate a sphere of particles and kelp fragments and give you a photo of glowing murk. A sensitive low-light sensor will capture the actual visual character of the place: the amber lamp-halos, the green bioluminescence, the dark shapes of buildings looming just at the edge of perception.

## A Final Note

The Bilge Quarter will be there when you arrive, and it will be there when you leave, and in between it will not have changed in any way you can easily measure. But you will have changed. The experience of sustained immersion in a living, breathing, self-maintaining environment that operates at a pace and a scale and a logic different from anything on the surface — this changes the body doing the experiencing. Not dramatically. Not dangerously. But the way a few days in deep wilderness changes you, or a few days at sea: you come back with a different relationship to enclosure, to darkness, to the feel of walls under your hands, to the idea that a place can be alive without being a creature.

You may find, afterward, that you think about the hum. Not often. Not distressingly. Just — sometimes, in quiet moments, you'll become aware of a vibration in the silence around you, a bass-note buried in the foundations of whatever building you're standing in, and you'll feel a brief, inexplicable kinship with it. As though the world everywhere is machinery beneath living crust, and you just happened to spend a few days inside one of the places honest enough to admit it.

The kelp filament on your sleeve, if you didn't brush it off, will have died and dried by the time you're back on the surface. It'll look like a thread of dark paper. Keep it if you want. It won't grow back in air.

But it tried.
