# Preparing for a Visit to The Growing City

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a place. You are visiting a *process*. The Growing City is not a destination in the way that even the strangest multiverse locations are destinations. It is an event that happens to look like a city, an ongoing act of becoming that occupies a valley the way a thought occupies a mind — temporarily, intensely, and with an internal logic that may not translate. Your preparation must account for the fact that the environment is not hostile, not welcoming, not indifferent in the way that weather is indifferent. It is *busy*. You are entering something that is concentrating, and your primary challenge will be existing inside that concentration without becoming part of it.

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal and Moisture Management

The cold here is not extreme but it is *persistent* and *wet*. This is the kind of cold that kills slowly, through attrition, by pulling heat out of you through damp fabric hour after hour. The condensation is constant and universal — it will coat you, your gear, the inside of your lungs. This is not rain; it cannot be waited out. It is the city's exhalation, the ambient moisture of a system that sweats as it works.

**Base layers:** Merino wool, full coverage, top and bottom. Not synthetic — synthetics will develop a biofilm here. The spores in the mortar, the wet breath of the factories, the biological activity saturating every surface — synthetic fabrics will become colonized. You'll pull off a polyester base layer after eight hours and find a thin green fuzz establishing itself in the weave. Merino resists this, not perfectly but meaningfully. Bring three sets minimum and rotate them, hanging damp ones to dry in whatever shelter you establish (more on shelter below). Wool continues to insulate when wet, which matters here more than in almost any other environment because you *will* be wet.

**Mid layers:** Wool fleece or dense wool knit. Avoid down — it will become waterlogged within hours and the spore load in the air will infiltrate it. A soaked down jacket in this environment becomes a terrarium. Dense wool fleece gives you warmth even when damp, and it can be wrung out and re-worn without catastrophic loss of insulation.

**Outer layer:** This is critical. You need something that breathes but repels the condensation film. A hard shell with a DWR coating will bead water initially but the coating will degrade fast — the city's moisture isn't pure water. There's something in it, some mineral or biological component that attacks hydrophobic coatings. Waxed canvas is your best option. Heavy, yes. Old-fashioned, yes. But wax can be reapplied in the field, it handles the mineral-laden condensation without degrading, and it has the structural rigidity to resist the ferns. Yes, the ferns — more on that shortly. A long waxed canvas jacket, mid-thigh at least, with a deep hood and storm flap closures rather than zippers. Zippers will become fouled with mineral deposits and moss within a day.

**Hands and feet:** Wool glove liners under waxed leather gauntlets. Your hands will be touching surfaces constantly, whether to steady yourself in the shifting streets or to push away encroaching vegetation, and the surfaces here are not inert. The pipes carry unknown fluids. The mortar weeps. The bricks arrive pre-colonized. Leather forms a barrier that fabric does not. For feet: heavy leather boots, full-grain, with a Goodyear welt (stitched, not glued — adhesives fail in this moisture). Treat them with a heavy application of beeswax-based waterproofing before arrival and bring material to retreat them every twelve hours. Gaiters are non-negotiable — the condensation pools in the basalt depressions along the streets and you'll be wading through ankle-deep standing water in the lower alleys.

**Head protection:** A wool balaclava under the waxed hood. The cold is lung-cold; breathing warm air through wool will help. Also: the corvids watch. Having less exposed skin means fewer surfaces for their compound eyes to catalogue. I'll discuss why this matters later.

### Respiratory Considerations

The air carries spores. This is not speculative — the mortar carries spores, the mosses release them continuously, and the pale exhalation from the factory smokestacks is almost certainly a sporulating medium. You are breathing in the city's reproductive material with every inhalation.

Bring a respirator. Not a dust mask — a proper half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and organic vapor cartridges. The organic vapor component matters because of the oily, resinous substance sweating from certain pipes. That pine-resin-and-machine-grease aroma indicates volatile organic compounds of unknown composition, and inhaling them deeply in enclosed alley spaces could have effects ranging from mild intoxication to serious pulmonary sensitization. Wear the respirator whenever you're inside the city proper. When you're at the valley rim, standard observation distance, you can probably breathe freely, but monitor yourself for any unusual taste or sensation in your throat.

Bring backup filters. Many backup filters. The moisture will clog P100 media faster than in a dry environment — expect to change filters every four to six hours rather than the rated duration. Pack at least twelve sets for a multi-day visit.

### Nutrition and Water

Do not eat anything that grows here. I realize this should be obvious, but the vegetation is extraordinary, the horsetails glow, and there's a particular quality to the sphagnum moss that might, in certain light and a certain exhaustion-induced state of mind, look appetizing in the way that very fresh, very green things sometimes do. Do not eat it. The biological systems here are integrated with the construction process in ways you don't understand. The beetles carry minerals. The mosses feed on the memory of walls. The entire ecosystem is a single organism or something so close to one that the distinction is academic, and introducing any part of it into your digestive system is an unknown risk you cannot afford.

Bring all your own food. High-calorie, low-moisture, sealed. The cold and the damp will burn through your energy reserves faster than you expect — your body will be working hard just to stay warm. Dense caloric foods: nuts, hard cheeses sealed in wax, dried meats, chocolate, dense grain bars. Nothing that requires cooking unless you bring a fully self-contained stove system, and even then, be cautious about open flame inside the city. The city is not obviously flammable — basalt and iron don't burn — but the mosses might react to fire in ways you can't predict, and attracting the attention of the corvids through anomalous thermal activity is inadvisable.

Water: bring it. Sealed containers, metal, with screw-top closures that can be tightened against the condensation. The condensation itself is almost certainly not safe to drink — it carries the city's biological material. The pooling water in the basalt depressions is habitat for the salamanders and probably for organisms you can't see. Your water supply is your lifeline. Calculate generously — in cold, damp environments you often don't feel thirsty but your body is still losing moisture through respiration and exertion. Three liters per day minimum, more if you're active.

If your visit will exceed what you can carry in water, bring a reliable pump-action filtration system with a ceramic element *and* UV sterilization. The ceramic will catch particulates and spores; the UV will handle anything biological that passes through. Even then, I'd only filter the condensation runoff as a last resort. The standing pools are off limits entirely.

## Equipment

### Navigation and Orientation

The city moves. This cannot be overstated. It is migrating eastward across the valley, reforming as it goes, and the internal layout is in constant flux. Streets widen and narrow. Walls advance and retreat. A landmark you noted an hour ago may have been disassembled and its materials redeployed elsewhere. Conventional navigation — landmarks, mental maps, even GPS (which assumes a static terrain) — will fail you.

**Compass:** Bring one. A good baseplate compass, liquid-filled, declination-adjustable. The valley has a clear east-west axis defined by the direction of the city's migration, and the compass will at minimum let you orient yourself relative to that axis. If you need to retreat, you go west — toward the dissolving edge, toward open ground. If you want to see what's coming, you go east — toward the growth front. North and south take you toward the valley walls, which appear to be stable geology and represent your nearest truly fixed ground.

**Marking system:** Do not mark the city itself. Do not scratch arrows on walls, do not tie cord to railings, do not attempt to blaze a trail. You don't know what the construction system considers an error, and you don't want to find out whether the city corrects errors. Instead, use your own body as the reference point. Carry a watch (mechanical — quartz may be affected by the subsonic hum, and the clock towers' habit of showing different times suggests temporal effects that electronic timepieces might be sensitive to). Track your movement by time and compass bearing. Keep a written log: "Entered at south rim, bearing 045 for twelve minutes, bearing 090 for seven minutes." This is crude but it works even when the streets reorganize around you, because it tells you your net displacement vector relative to the valley, not relative to the city.

**Elevation awareness:** The valley is shallow but the city creates its own topography through layered construction. The grated floors revealing subterranean gears suggest there are levels beneath the street surface. Do not go down. The city's construction activity in the substrata is invisible to you and you have no way to predict when a space might be reformed, filled in, or repurposed. Stay at or above street level.

### Shelter

You need somewhere to sleep that the city will not absorb.

This is the core logistical problem of an extended visit. Every surface in the city is active — constructing, deconstructing, being colonized. You cannot pitch a tent on the valley floor inside the city because the floor is part of the system. You cannot lean against a wall because the wall might advance. The alleys breathe.

**Option one: Valley rim.** Establish a base camp on the valley rim, outside the city's current footprint, and make day trips in. This is the safest approach. The rim appears to be stable ground — older geology, or at least geology that isn't currently participating in the city's construction cycle. Pitch a freestanding tent (not staked into the ground if you can help it, in case the ground turns out to be less stable than it looks). Weight it with rocks from outside the valley. Keep your camp upwind of the city if wind exists here; the spore load downwind may be significant.

The drawback: the city moves eastward. If your camp is on the eastern rim, the city is coming toward you. If your camp is on the western rim, the city is retreating from you and your day trips become longer each day. If your camp is on the northern or southern rim, you have a stable perpendicular relationship with the migration axis, which is ideal.

**Option two: Persistent structures.** The account notes that certain structures persist through the churning — factories, clock towers, wider arterial streets. If you can identify a persistent structure and establish that it isn't going to be disassembled during your visit, you could shelter inside it. This is riskier but offers the advantage of direct, extended observation. A clock tower might work — they seem to have a supervisory role in the city's construction rhythm, and their persistence suggests they're load-bearing elements of the overall pattern. But you would be inside a clock tower that strikes non-hours and prompts shifts in the surrounding architecture, surrounded by corvids that watch everything with compound insect eyes. Your sleep will not be restful.

**Option three: A portable hard shelter.** A bivouac system with a rigid shell — think a one-person aluminum or fiberglass capsule, the kind used in extreme mountaineering or polar expeditions. Something the city cannot easily integrate because its materials are foreign to the local construction palette. The city builds with cast iron, basalt, brick, and mortar. It seeds its structures with biological material. A smooth-walled aluminum pod offers nothing for moss to grip, no mortar joints for ferns to colonize, and no structural connection points for iron girders to click into. You would be a foreign body the city might route around rather than incorporate. But "might" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence.

I recommend Option One for most visitors. Stay on the rim. The city is fascinating but it is not meant for you and its tolerance for foreign bodies is untested.

### Observation Tools

**Binoculars:** High-quality, fog-proof, with a wide field of view. You'll want to watch the construction process from distance before entering the city. The macro-scale patterns — the eastward migration, the assembly-disassembly cycle, the persistence of certain structures — are only visible from above.

**Hand lens or loupe:** The small-scale processes are as important as the large-scale ones. The beetles carrying chalk. The mortar weeping spores. The bricks arriving pre-furred with moss. A 10x or 20x hand lens will let you examine these without getting your face dangerously close to active surfaces.

**Notebook and pencil:** Not a phone, not a tablet, not any electronic device you care about. The subsonic hum may or may not affect electronics, but the condensation certainly will, and the spore-laden atmosphere is not something you want inside a charging port. A waterproof field notebook (Rite in the Rain or equivalent) and a mechanical pencil. Document everything. Sketch the construction sequences. Note the clock tower chimes and what they seem to trigger. Record the beetle traffic patterns. This information may be invaluable for understanding the city's logic, and your notes may be the only record — photographs, as the account notes, would make the city look ordinary. The wrongness doesn't photograph. It must be described.

**Sample containers:** Sealed glass vials with screw caps and silicone gaskets. If you choose to collect samples — moss, mortar, a mineral fragment, condensation — do so carefully and seal them immediately. Do not carry open biological material from this environment on your person any longer than necessary. Double-bag sealed vials in zip-lock bags. Triple-bag if you're collecting anything from the factories.

Regarding collection: think carefully about whether you should. The ecosystem is integrated. Removing a component might not be noticed, or it might be. The beetles have purposes. The mosses have roles. Taking a brick might be the equivalent of removing a cell from an organism — trivial in isolation, informative for study, but an act that registers in a system that tracks its own inventory. The corvids are watching. The city remembers its previous configurations. It may notice what it's missing.

## Behavioral Preparation

### How to Move

Slowly. Predictably. With intention.

The city is concentrating. The silence is the silence of focus. You are entering a workspace during an active project, and the appropriate behavior is the same as it would be in any workshop where complex, delicate operations are underway: move deliberately, don't touch what you don't need to touch, give the work space.

Walk at a steady pace. Do not run. Do not suddenly reverse direction. The streets are breathing — widening and narrowing — and sudden movement might put you in the path of a wall that's advancing its scheduled six inches. The city's construction operates on a rhythm set by the clock tower chimes. Learn the rhythm. If a chime triggers a reconfiguration, stop walking. Stand still. Let the shift complete around you. Then assess whether your path is still open. If it isn't, reroute. Do not try to squeeze through a narrowing gap. The city does not know you're there, or if it does, it doesn't care, and it will close a passage with the same unhurried inevitability with which it opens one.

### How to Handle the Corvids

The corvids are the closest thing this environment has to an interface. They watch. They may direct. One tilted its head at a misaligned beam and the beam adjusted — correlation or causation, the distinction barely matters for your practical purposes. Treat the corvids as supervisors. You do not challenge supervisors. You do not interfere with their work. You do not stare at them (compound eyes process visual attention differently than vertebrate eyes, and sustained direct gaze may register as something you don't intend). You do not feed them, touch them, or attempt to communicate with them.

If a corvid watches you, continue what you were doing at the same pace. Do not freeze — freezing is prey behavior, and while there's no evidence the corvids are predatory, adopting a prey posture in front of an unknown intelligence is never advisable. Do not speed up — haste is anomaly, and the city's systems seem designed to address anomalies. Just continue. Be boring. Be predictable. Be the equivalent of background noise in the city's concentrated silence.

If a corvid tilts its head at you and something in your vicinity adjusts — a wall shifts, a pipe reroutes, a section of floor tilts — move with the adjustment, not against it. The corvid may be routing around you the way it routes around any obstacle. Accept the routing. Go where the city makes space for you.

If multiple corvids gather and watch you simultaneously from multiple vantage points, leave that area. Not quickly. Just redirect your walk. Multiple supervisory entities focusing on a single point suggests that point has become a problem, and you do not want to be a problem.

### How to Handle the Vegetation

The vegetation is part of the city. This is essential to internalize. It is not ornamental. It is not incidental. The bricks arrive pre-furred. The mortar carries spores. The moss grows along the memory traces of old foundations. The biological and the mechanical are one system, and when you touch the moss, you are touching the city in a way that is at least as intimate as touching its brickwork.

Do not pull, tear, or uproot any vegetation. If a fern frond is blocking your path, go around it. If moss is growing on something you want to examine, examine it with the moss on. The one-meter ferns unfurling between bricks with "muscular slowness" — those are structural. They may be load-bearing in ways that make no engineering sense but perfect biological sense. The bioluminescent horsetails in the deep alleys are clearly specialized organisms with a role in the ecosystem that is not yet understood. Leave them alone. Admire them from a respectful distance.

If vegetation begins to grow on you — on your boots, your jacket, the surface of your pack — remove it promptly but gently. Brush it away. Don't let it establish. You are not a substrate. The waxed canvas outer layer should resist colonization better than most materials, but the wool underneath, if exposed, may be vulnerable. Check yourself regularly, the way you'd check for ticks in a forest. The city builds on everything. Don't let it build on you.

### How to Handle the Streets

The streets breathe. The account describes this directly: walls advancing and retreating, alleys narrowing and widening. This is not a metaphor. You are walking through a structure that is actively reconfiguring itself on a timescale of minutes.

Practical implications:

- **Always know where your nearest wide space is.** The arterial streets — the wider, persistent ones — are your safety corridors. They may shift in detail but they persist as features. In the narrower alleys, you are inside the city's experimental tissue, the substrate it tries and discards. Don't linger in narrow alleys.

- **Keep your arms close to your body.** Don't lean on walls. Don't brace yourself in doorways. Don't stand with your hand against a surface. A wall that advances six inches while your hand is pressed against its neighbor will crush your hand.

- **Listen for the ratcheting clicks and metallic sighs.** These are the sounds of construction and deconstruction. If you hear them intensifying near you, the local geometry is changing. Find a wider space and wait.

- **Watch the moss.** The account says the mosses on the walls "ripple like fur" during a reconfiguration. The vegetation is an early warning system. If the moss on a nearby wall begins to move — rippling, peeling, rolling itself inward — that wall is about to change. Step away from it.

- **Never enter a structure that is partially formed.** The building described as "a third complete" with a nascent clock tower is not a ruin you can explore. It is a construction site. It is actively assembling. Walking into it would be like walking into a factory machine during operation. The iron girders click into place with mechanical force. The bricks cascade in waves. Being inside that process when it's happening around you is not survivable.

### How to Handle the Factories

The factories are the city's organs. They are where structures originate before emerging and extending into the urban fabric. They are persistent, which means they are important — possibly the most important elements of the entire system.

Observe them from outside. Look through the open bay doors if you can do so from a safe distance. Note what enters and what exits. The structures that emerge are visible. But what about inputs? The factories must be processing something — the raw basalt, the pale mineral intrusions, the city's own deconstructed western edge. There may be a material cycle: disassembled buildings on the west are somehow transported (through the substrata? via the beetles? through channels you can't see?) to the factories on the east, processed, and re-emitted as new structures. Understanding this cycle would be a significant insight.

But do not enter a factory. The interior of a factory in this city is the interior of an organ in an organism you don't understand. The factory exhalation — "something that isn't quite smoke" — is being produced by processes inside. The gears visible through grated floors suggest machinery below the surface. The warm pipes, the vibrating pipes, the oily-resinous pipes — these are the city's circulatory system, and they converge on the factories. You don't go inside a heart to study how it beats.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most travelers underestimate what's required.

### The Silence

The quiet will work on you. Human beings are wired to find silence in populated-looking environments deeply unsettling — it triggers the same alarm response as walking into a village and finding it empty, which in our evolutionary past meant plague, raid, or predator. Your hindbrain will be screaming that something is wrong, that the absence of human noise in a human-shaped environment means danger.

It does mean something, but not danger exactly. It means you are irrelevant. The city doesn't need you. It doesn't notice you, or if it notices you, it categorizes you as it categorizes a beetle or a fern — a small biological element that may or may not serve a function. This is psychologically difficult for humans to sustain. We are accustomed to being the most significant thing in our immediate environment. Here, you are not. You are less significant than a brick. The brick has a place in the pattern. You don't.

Prepare for this by practicing being unimportant. Meditate on it, if meditation is part of your practice. Sit with the idea that you can exist in a space that has no use for you, that assigns you no role, that would continue exactly as it is whether you were present or not. The city is the silence of concentration, and you are the silence of being overlooked.

### The Ordinariness

The flat, democratic daylight. The fact that a photograph would look like an overcast Wednesday in an industrial town. The mundanity. This is, in some ways, harder to handle than the alien. The human mind can process the alien — it fires up the novelty circuits, dumps adrenaline, engages fully. But the *almost-normal* slides under the defenses. You'll have moments, especially if you're fatigued, where you forget where you are. Where the city just looks like a city and you reach for a door handle to see what's inside, or lean against a warm wall because you're cold, or think "I should find a pub" because the architecture suggests a pub should be nearby. These moments of normalization are dangerous. The city looks like a human artifact but it is not one. Every surface is active. Every structure is temporary. The door you reach for might be in the process of becoming a wall. The warm wall you lean against might be warm because it's about to extrude a new pipe.

Maintain a practice of periodic reality-checking. Every fifteen to twenty minutes, stop. Look around. Actively identify three things that are wrong — three things that would not be present in a normal city. The building assembling itself three streets over. The beetles carrying chalk in formation. The corvid watching you with its compound eye. Name the wrongness. Keep it in focus. Do not let the ordinary daylight persuade you that you are somewhere ordinary.

### The Temptation to Find Meaning

You will want to understand what the city is *for*.

This is the deepest psychological trap. Humans are pattern-completing animals. We see a city and we infer inhabitants, economy, purpose. We see a factory and we infer products, markets, supply chains. We see clock towers and we infer timekeeping, schedules, shared social coordination. The Growing City presents all the forms of purpose without any of the content, and your mind will itch to fill in the gaps. You'll start hypothesizing. You'll think: it's preparing for inhabitants who haven't arrived yet. You'll think: it's a machine and the city-form is just the shape the machine happens to take. You'll think: the corvids are the inhabitants and the city is their art, or their language, or their body.

Maybe one of these is true. Maybe none of them. The point is that you don't know, and you should be comfortable not knowing for the duration of your visit. Premature pattern-completion in an environment this complex will cause you to see confirmations everywhere and miss the data that doesn't fit your theory. Worse, it may cause you to act on your theory — to try to interact with the "inhabitants," to try to "help" the construction, to try to find the city's "center" or "brain" or "purpose." These actions have unpredictable consequences in a system whose logic you haven't cracked.

Observe. Record. Withhold judgment. Leave the meaning-making for after you've departed, when you're sitting somewhere safe with your waterproof notebook, reviewing your sketches and your compass-and-time navigation log, and you can hypothesize without risk.

### The Compound Eyes

This will haunt you. I want to prepare you for it.

A corvid — a bird-shaped thing with the proportions slightly wrong, the head too large, the wings too small — will land near you and look at you, and its eye will be faceted. Compound. An insect's eye in a vertebrate's skull. Every facet will catch the flat daylight and every facet will be aimed at you, and you will feel — not fear, exactly, but *exposure*. The sense that you are being seen in a way you are not built to be seen. A compound eye doesn't focus. It samples. It takes in the whole visual field simultaneously, each facet a tiny independent image, the composite a mosaic of you from every angle at once. When a corvid looks at you, it isn't looking *at* you. It is looking at everything near you and you are part of that everything, and you can't tell where its attention is directed because its attention is directed everywhere.

This is deeply uncomfortable for primates. We are wired to read gaze direction. We use it to assess threat, intention, social status. A compound eye gives you nothing. You can't read it. You can't tell what it's thinking. You can't tell if it's thinking. You can only see yourself reflected in a hundred tiny curved mirrors, each showing a slightly different version of your face.

Prepare for this by accepting that you will be watched and that you will not understand the watching. Carry that acceptance the way you carry your compass and your water — as essential equipment for surviving the visit.

## Departure Preparation

### Decontamination

When you leave the city, before you return to your home location or any other multiverse destination, you must decontaminate. The city seeds itself as it builds. Spores in the mortar. Pre-furred bricks. Vegetation that doesn't compete with construction but integrates with it. You have been walking through a self-propagating system, breathing its air, touching its surfaces, accumulating its biological material on every piece of your equipment.

At your rim camp, before packing out:

1. **Strip outer layers.** Remove the waxed canvas jacket, boots, gauntlets, gaiters. Inspect every surface. Brush off any visible biological growth — moss, lichen, fern spores, anything green or grey or yellow-sulfur. Use a stiff brush, not your hands.

2. **Inspect inner layers.** Check wool garments for colonization, especially in creases, cuffs, and where layers overlapped and trapped moisture.

3. **Inspect equipment.** Every pocket, every seam, every grommet, every zipper (if you were foolish enough to bring zippers). The beetles are small. The salamanders are translucent. The spores are invisible. Be thorough.

4. **Seal or destroy contaminated items.** If any piece of gear is visibly colonized and you cannot clean it to your satisfaction, seal it in a heavy plastic bag and either dispose of it at a designated multiverse waste facility or burn it outside the valley (well away from the city — you don't want to find out whether the smoke carries viable spores back to it).

5. **Personal decontamination.** Wash your skin and hair with clean water and soap. Pay attention to your hands, face, and any areas where condensation accumulated. Check your nostrils and ear canals — the spores are fine enough to deposit there. If you have a saline nasal rinse, use it.

6. **Quarantine your samples.** If you collected anything, it remains in sealed glass vials, double-bagged, and does not come out until you are in a controlled laboratory environment equipped for containment of unknown biological material. The city builds itself from its own biological substrate. Any sample you carry is, in a very real sense, a seed.

### Mental Decompression

After departing, give yourself time before your next destination. The Growing City is not traumatic in the conventional sense — nothing attacks you, nothing chases you, nothing threatens you directly. But it is *uncanny* in the deepest meaning of that word: the familiar made strange, the homely made homeless. Your mind has spent the entire visit pattern-matching against "city" and failing, and the cognitive dissonance accumulates like the condensation — slowly, persistently, soaking in.

You may find, in the days after your visit, that ordinary cities look wrong to you. That you watch buildings and wait for them to move. That you listen for the hum. That you look at pigeons and check their eyes. This fades, but it takes time. Don't rush it. Sit with it. Write about it. Review your notes. Let the experience metabolize at its own pace, the way the mosses grow along the memory traces of old foundations — slowly, feeding on what was there before.

## Final Considerations

I want to be honest about something: I don't know if this preparation is sufficient. The Growing City is not documented well enough for anyone to guarantee safe passage. Every recommendation I've made is based on inference from a single detailed observation — the description you've been provided. I've extrapolated from what the city does to what it might do to *you*, but the city's attitude toward human visitors is unknown. It may be indifferent. It may be accommodating, the way the corvid seemed to reroute a wall for the observer. It may be incorporative, in the worst sense, given enough time — another beetle carrying chalk, another fern rooting in a crack, another small biological element slotted into the pattern.

The safest visit is a short one. Go to the rim. Watch from above with binoculars. Take notes. Leave before sunset (you don't know what the city does in the dark, and neither do I, and the absence of that information should terrify you more than anything I've described). Come back another day if you need more.

If you go in, go in light. Go in quiet. Go in with the understanding that you are visiting something alive — not alive like a forest, where life is distributed and competitive and chaotic, but alive like a single organism, coordinated and purposeful and vast. You are a foreign cell in its tissue. Whether it treats you as neutral, useful, or something to be encapsulated and walled off depends on factors you cannot control and may not even perceive.

Move slowly. Watch the moss. Don't stare at the corvids. And when a clock tower strikes a tone that isn't an hour and the street begins to breathe around you — stand still, stand quiet, and let the city do what it's doing.

It was doing it before you arrived. It will be doing it long after you leave. The only question is whether you're still standing in the gap when the next brick clicks into place.
