# Preparing to Visit The Woven Heights

---

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything else, sit with this: you are not visiting a forest. You are not visiting a city. You are entering the body of something that is both and neither, something that has had geological time to dissolve every distinction you rely on to feel like a separate entity. The primary danger of the Woven Heights is not physical — though physical dangers exist — it is *ontological*. You may arrive as a visitor and begin, without noticing, to stop experiencing yourself as distinct from where you are. This is not metaphor. The place is described by returnees (the few who return, the fewer who can articulate what happened) as possessing a quality of *absorption* — not hostile, not predatory, but patient and total, the way soil absorbs rain. You must prepare for the possibility that your sense of where you end and the location begins will become unreliable, and you must decide before you go what anchors you intend to use, and you must commit to them with a seriousness that will feel absurd right up until the moment it saves you.

## Physical Preparation

### Breathing

The atmosphere is breathable but not normal. Spore and pollen density is described as sufficient to give the air a faintly sweet taste, which means you are inhaling biological material with every breath. For most multiverse travelers, short-term exposure (days to a few weeks) produces no lasting harm. Longer stays have been associated with:

- **Gradual changes in respiratory flora.** Your lungs will begin hosting organisms native to the Woven Heights. Some returnees report a persistent sweet taste in their breath for months after departure. Others report that they began breathing "more efficiently" while there, as though the inhaled spores were somehow *assisting* gas exchange. This is not well understood and is deeply unsettling to think about for too long.
- **Mild euphoria.** Whether this is chemical (airborne compounds from the tree's metabolism acting on mammalian neurochemistry) or psychological (the overwhelming, cathedral quality of the space) or something else entirely is unclear. It feels pleasant. It impairs judgment. Treat it the way you would treat altitude-induced hypoxia — recognize it, account for it, do not trust the feeling that everything is fine.

**Recommendation:** Bring a high-quality particulate respirator and wear it for the first 48 hours while you acclimate. After that, you can begin breathing the ambient air in controlled intervals, extending exposure gradually. If at any point you find yourself *wanting* to remove the respirator with an urgency that feels emotional rather than practical, put it back on and do not take it off for another full day. The desire to breathe the air unfiltered can become a craving, and cravings in the Woven Heights tend to be the tree's way of beginning conversations you may not be ready for.

### Hydration and Nutrition

The water in the carved basins is, by all accounts, safe to drink. It is condensation filtered through living bark and collected in growth-shaped containers stained with tannins. It tastes like very mild tea — warm, faintly astringent, complex. It will sustain you. **However:** drinking the tree's water is an act of integration, whether you frame it that way or not. You are taking the tree's metabolic output into your body. Returnees who drank freely from the basins describe a growing sense of *belonging* that accelerated over time. Those who relied on their own water supplies maintained a sharper sense of separation.

**Recommendation:** Bring your own water. Enough for your entire planned stay, plus a generous margin. Use the basin-water only in emergencies. If you must drink it, limit yourself to small amounts and note — in writing, immediately, each time — how you feel before and after. Watch for the feeling that your own carried water tastes wrong, flat, *dead* compared to the basin-water. This feeling is a marker that integration is accelerating.

Food is more complex. There appear to be edible organisms throughout the Woven Heights — fungal growths in the warm heartwood chambers, fruits or fruit-analogues on some of the orchid-like epiphytes, the yeast cultures in the deeper hollows that smell like bread and may in fact be something close to bread. The inhabitants (when they are present, which they may not be during your visit — more on this below) clearly subsist on what the tree provides. But you are not an inhabitant. You do not have centuries of co-evolutionary history with this organism. What nourishes someone who has become part of the tree's ecology may interact unpredictably with your unintegrated biology.

**Recommendation:** Bring calorie-dense, shelf-stable food. Enough for your stay plus margin. Ration bars, dense nutrient pastes, dried provisions — things that are unambiguously *from elsewhere*, that taste like elsewhere, that remind your body with every meal that you have a home that is not here. If you run low and must forage, start with the fungal cultures in the heartwood chambers — they are the most frequently described food source and appear to be the least biologically aggressive. Avoid the fruits. Several accounts mention that eating the fruit of the upper-branch orchids produced dreams that were difficult to distinguish from memory, and that upon waking, the dreamer was uncertain which memories were original and which had been *provided*.

### Temperature and Clothing

The Woven Heights are warm. Consistently, pervasively warm. The biological heat of the tree's metabolism maintains a temperature that most travelers describe as "comfortable in light clothing" — somewhere between 22 and 28 degrees Celsius depending on your location relative to the trunk and the depth of the branch you're on. Deeper hollows are warmer. The open air between branches can be slightly cooler, especially when the small trapped clouds are nearby and releasing moisture.

Humidity is high. Not oppressive — the constant air circulation created by the tree's thermal dynamics prevents the atmosphere from becoming truly stagnant — but persistent. Everything is faintly damp. Your clothing will not fully dry. Your skin will carry a thin film of moisture that is partly condensation and partly the tree's exhalation.

**Recommendation:** Wear light, breathable, fast-wicking fabrics. Natural fibers (cotton, wool, linen) will rot. Bring synthetics and accept that they will feel slightly wrong in this environment — the Woven Heights exert a subtle aesthetic pressure toward organic materials, and you may find yourself wanting to shed your synthetic clothing in favor of the bark-fiber textiles that the inhabitants produce. Resist this impulse, or at least recognize it as part of the integration gradient. Your clothing from outside is part of your identity kit. Keep it on.

Bring a waterproof outer layer for the areas near trapped clouds, where condensation can be heavy enough to mimic light rain.

No temperature-control gear is needed. The warmth is constant. If anything, the challenge is that the constancy itself becomes hypnotic — there is no thermal variation to mark the passage of time or to keep your body's circadian rhythms engaged.

### Footwear

Critical. The bark surface, even the smoothed paths, is textured and irregular. The water channels create grooves that can catch a foot. Mossy surfaces are slippery. And you are walking on a living branch four hundred meters wide with open drops of *kilometers* at the edges, drops that may be obscured by fern curtains or epiphytic growth, drops that you might walk toward without realizing because the edge of a branch this size does not look like an edge — it looks like the terrain is gently curving downward, inviting, and then suddenly there is no terrain at all, only the amber void and the distant gliders and a fall that would last long enough for you to contemplate it.

**Recommendation:** Sturdy boots with deep tread and strong ankle support. Non-marking soles — not for the tree's sake (the tree will metabolize any marks you leave) but because leaving visible tracks helps you retrace your route, and if the tree is smoothing the bark behind you to guide you deeper, you want to be able to tell whether the path you're on is the one you already walked or a new one that has been *provided*. Leaving scuff marks that you can identify as your own is a basic wayfinding strategy here.

## Navigation and Orientation

This is perhaps the single most important preparation category, and the one where your conventional tools will serve you least.

**Magnetic compass:** Useless or misleading. The Woven Heights may not have a magnetic field in any conventional sense, and if they do, the sheer mass of water-saturated living tissue surrounding you will interfere with readings. Do not rely on a compass.

**GPS or satellite-based navigation:** There are no satellites. There may not be a sky. Do not rely on any system that requires line-of-sight to anything above the canopy.

**Altimeter:** Potentially useful as a relative tool, if the atmosphere has a consistent pressure gradient, which it may not. The tree generates its own weather systems, and barometric pressure may vary locally based on the metabolic activity of nearby trunk-sections. Use with extreme caution and cross-reference against other methods.

**Celestial observation:** There is no visible sky. There is no visible star. The light comes from everywhere above, filtered through an unknown number of canopy layers, arriving as directionless amber warmth. You cannot determine cardinal directions from the light. You cannot determine time of day from the light. The light does not change. (Or if it does, it changes on cycles too long for you to detect during a single visit, or on cycles that respond to the tree's internal rhythms rather than any external astronomical body.)

So how do you navigate?

**The water-channel system.** This is your primary navigation aid. The carved channels are the closest thing the Woven Heights have to roads. They follow logical patterns — generally running from higher to lower along the branch-gradient, converging toward collection basins, branching at forks. If you map the channel system as you go, marking your own passage, you can build a workable local navigation picture.

**Your own markings.** Bring high-visibility, non-organic markers. Metal pins. Ceramic tags. Synthetic cord in bright colors. Mark your path at every decision point. Mark it redundantly. Check your marks when you backtrack. **If a mark is missing, if the tree has overgrown it, if the bark has swallowed your tag** — this tells you something important. It tells you the tree is editing your trail. It does not necessarily mean the tree is hostile. It may mean the tree thinks you should go a different way. But it means your autonomy of movement is being contested, and you need to decide how you feel about that.

**The bioluminescent lichen.** The foxfire appears in patterns that are "too structured to be wild growth." It may encode navigational information — the inhabitants probably read it the way you would read road signs. You will not be able to read it on your first visit. But you can note it. Photograph it. Record its patterns and locations relative to your own markers. If you return (and some travelers do return, and the place always recognizes them), you may begin to learn the grammar.

**Your own sense of direction.** It will erode. The Woven Heights are disorienting in a fundamental way — not because they are confusing (the branch-paths are often quite legible) but because the frame of reference that allows you to feel oriented *requires a world with a ground and a sky*, and this place has neither. Your vestibular sense will tell you which way is down, and that is useful, but "down" here leads to the root-dark, a place no one goes, and "up" leads to the crown-merge, which may not be reachable. Your horizontal orientation — your sense of "I came from *that* direction and I want to return *that* direction" — will be challenged by the curvature of the branches, by the way the trunk-face curves away from you like a planetary horizon, by the sheer scale that makes nearby landmarks impossible (everything is too big to be a landmark; it would be like trying to navigate by the shape of a continent while standing on it).

**Recommendation:** Keep a detailed, manually-written log. Not electronic — electronic devices behave unreliably in biologically dense multiverse locations, and the humidity will degrade them. Use a waterproof notebook and a graphite pencil (not pen — ink will run in this humidity). Write down everything: time elapsed since arrival (use a mechanical watch; electronic timekeeping may drift), direction of travel relative to your last entry, notable features, the state of your own markers, the state of the water-channels, the state of the light, the state of your own emotions. That last one is not optional. Your emotional state is navigational data here. If you feel a sudden, inexplicable sense of peace and rightness, note it — it may indicate that you are approaching one of the integration zones (the pool-plazas, the heartwood chambers) where the boundary-dissolution effect is strongest. If you feel unease, note that too — it may indicate that you are approaching the edge of inhabited territory, or the edge of the branch, or a region the tree has designated for non-human use.

## The Inhabitants

They are not here right now. The description is emphatic about this. The basins are carved but unattended. The tools rest in their niches. The fungal gardens grow untended, or tend themselves, or are tended by the tree. Where are they?

Several possibilities, all of which you must prepare for:

**They are cyclical.** Their presence may ebb and flow on a rhythm you can't predict — seasonal (if there are seasons), generational, or tied to some phase of the tree's own lifecycle. You may arrive in an empty period and leave without encountering anyone. This is the safest scenario, and also the saddest.

**They are responsive.** They may withdraw from the approach of outsiders, the way the fauna falls silent. The tree may alert them. You are, after all, walking on a nervous system. Every footstep vibrates through living tissue. Your weight, your warmth, your exhalation of carbon dioxide — the tree knows you are here. If the inhabitants are integrated enough into the tree's awareness (and the carvings suggest a level of integration that makes "inhabitant" almost the wrong word — more like "organ"), they may know too. They may be watching from deeper in the architecture, waiting to see what you do. In this case, your behavior during the "empty" period is an audition. Drink from the basins or don't. Follow the smooth path or don't. Look into the pool or don't. Every choice communicates something.

**They are no longer distinguishable from the tree.** The carvings depict a figure becoming a tree, a tree becoming a figure, and explicitly refuse to clarify the direction of transformation. The inhabitants may have completed that process. They may *be* the smoothed bark. They may be the branch that anticipated your arrival. They may be the water-channel forming where there wasn't one yesterday. In which case, you have been in the presence of the inhabitants since you arrived, and the question "where are the people" is the wrong question, and the right question is "what kind of people have I been walking on."

**Recommendation:** Behave as if you are being observed at all times, because you almost certainly are, whether by eyes you would recognize or by perceptual systems embedded in the living wood. Be respectful. Move slowly. Do not damage the bark, the channels, the basins, the tools. Do not take anything. If you use a basin to wash your hands, pour the water back. If you pick up a tool to examine it, return it to the exact niche where you found it. The inhabitants — whatever form they take — have been maintaining this place for longer than most civilizations have existed. You are a guest in something between a home and a body, and the appropriate behavior is the intersection of good houseguest and respectful patient in a hospital: move gently, touch carefully, leave no trace, and understand that the space itself has needs and boundaries even if it cannot articulate them in words you recognize.

## The Pools

You will encounter one. The smooth path leads to them — or rather, the tree leads you to them via the smooth path, which amounts to the same thing. The pools are the locations of maximum ontological instability, and they require specific preparation.

What the pools do, as near as anyone can determine, is show you *other states of the tree*. Other times. Other versions. Other possibilities. The reflection is not optical — it does not show what is physically above the water. It shows something else. Something that changes. Something that may look back at you.

**Do not touch the water in the pools.** This is the single most commonly repeated warning from returnees. The basin-water is one thing — metabolically processed condensation, essentially the tree's distilled breath. The pool-water is something else. It is warm as blood. It is perfectly still until it isn't. And the things that appear in its reflection have *luminous eyes that look down at you from inside the pool's surface*. Whatever boundary the pool represents — between the tree's present and its memory, between this version of the forest and another, between the observer and the observed — that boundary is *thinnest at the water's surface*, and physical contact with it may constitute crossing.

No returnee has reported touching the pool-water. This is either because none of them did, or because the ones who did are not returnees.

**The carvings around the pools are being absorbed.** The tree is drawing the inscriptions into itself. This means the information content of the carvings — whatever instructions, warnings, or maps they encode — is being transferred from a medium you can read (stone marks on bark) to a medium you cannot (the living tissue of the tree). The longest-staying returnees report that at a certain point in their visit, they began to *sense* what the carvings had said, without being able to see the marks. They describe this as "the tree told me" or "I just knew" and they are uncomfortable when pressed for details. This suggests that the integration process, if allowed to progress far enough, grants access to the information the tree has absorbed — but at the cost of the integration itself. The tree will teach you to read it, but only by making you part of it.

**Recommendation:** Observe the pools from a distance of no less than two meters. Do not touch the water. Do not lean over it. Record what you see in the reflection — quickly, in your notebook, before the image changes. Do not stare into the reflection for extended periods; the changing images have a hypnotic quality, and returnees report losing hours at the pool's edge without realizing time had passed. Set a timer on your mechanical watch. Give yourself no more than ten minutes at any pool. When the timer sounds, leave. Walk back along the smooth path. Check your markers. Write in your notebook. Re-establish your identity by reciting something from memory — your name, your home coordinates, the reason you came. The pools erode certainty, and certainty is the only structural material you have that the tree cannot metabolize.

## The Smooth Path Problem

The tree anticipates you. The bark smooths itself in advance of your arrival. This is the single most unnerving aspect of the Woven Heights for most travelers, because it means the environment is not passive — it has expectations about where you will go, and it prepares for those expectations, and it is usually right.

The problem is that following the smooth path is easy, and not following it is hard. The rough bark is difficult terrain — deeply furrowed, mossy, irregular, potentially concealing drops or unstable surfaces. The smooth path is a gift. It is comfortable. It leads somewhere interesting. It always leads somewhere interesting.

But it leads where *the tree wants you to go*, and the tree's interests and your interests may not be aligned. The tree is not hostile — nothing in the Woven Heights suggests hostility. But the tree is *integrative*. It wants to incorporate. It has been incorporating for longer than anything. The smooth path leads to the basins where you'll drink the water. It leads to the heartwood chambers where the warm air smells like bread and you'll eat the fungal cultures. It leads to the pools where the reflections will show you things that dissolve the boundary between you and the place. The smooth path is an onboarding process. It is the tree's welcome protocol, and if you follow it uncritically, you will be welcomed so thoroughly that leaving becomes difficult — not because you're trapped, but because leaving stops making sense, the way leaving your own body doesn't make sense.

**Recommendation:** Follow the smooth path *sometimes*. It is the primary way to see the Woven Heights' most remarkable features, and refusing it entirely means missing the point of the visit. But also leave it. Deliberately, regularly, with purpose. Strike out along the rough bark. Explore the un-maintained forks. Navigate by your own markers rather than the tree's guidance. This serves two purposes: first, it gives you access to parts of the Woven Heights that the tree is not curating for you, which may be more revealing of its true nature; second, it exercises your autonomy, which is a muscle that atrophies quickly here. Every time you choose the rough path, you are reasserting the distinction between yourself and your environment. You are saying, in the only language the tree understands: *I am not part of you yet.*

The word "yet" is intentional. The tree is patient. It has been patient for eons. It is not offended by your rough-path excursions. It simply continues to prepare the smooth path for when you're ready. The smooth path will always be there, leading further in.

## Time

You have no reliable way to measure how long you've been here. The light doesn't change. The temperature doesn't change. Your circadian rhythm will destabilize within 48 hours. Your mechanical watch will continue to function (probably; the tree has no reason to interfere with a mechanism it can't metabolize), but the numbers on the watch face will begin to feel arbitrary — what does "fourteen hours" mean in a place with no day and no night? Time here is measured in growth. The new water-channel that's forming — how far has it extended since you last looked? The smooth path — has it advanced beyond where you last walked? These are the clocks of the Woven Heights, and they run on biological time, which is both slower and more variable than mechanical time.

**Recommendation:** Set a firm departure schedule before you arrive. Write it on the first page of your notebook. "I will leave after X mechanical hours." Do not amend this schedule while you are inside the Woven Heights. The desire to stay longer will come, and it will feel like your own desire, and it may be your own desire — but it may also be the tree's desire expressed through you, and you cannot distinguish between the two from inside. The schedule you set before arrival, when you were still unambiguously yourself, is more trustworthy than any decision you make after the air starts tasting sweet and the bark starts feeling warm under your feet and the distant shapes are wheeling in the amber light and everything is so beautiful and so quiet and so *right* that leaving seems like a kind of violence.

For a first visit, I recommend no more than 72 mechanical hours. This is enough to walk the smooth path, observe a pool (from a safe distance, for no more than ten minutes), examine the carvings, and experience the scale and the quiet and the warmth without progressing far enough along the integration gradient to compromise your departure.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

The Woven Heights will make you feel at home. This is its primary effect and its primary danger. The warmth, the quiet, the sweet air, the comfortable paths, the water that tastes like tea, the light that asks nothing of your eyes — everything about this place is *welcoming*, and the welcome is genuine. The tree is not baiting a trap. The tree is offering what it has always offered: integration, belonging, the dissolution of loneliness at the most fundamental level, the end of the distinction between self and world. For a being that has been doing this for geological time, the offer is extraordinarily refined. It knows what comfort feels like. It has been perfecting comfort since before your species existed.

You must be honest with yourself about your vulnerability to this offer. If you are lonely, the Woven Heights will be more dangerous for you. If you are tired, if you are grieving, if you are running from something, if you have recently lost a sense of purpose — the Woven Heights will offer you rest and meaning and belonging so profound that it will make everything else you've ever experienced seem thin and incomplete. This is not a lie. The belonging is real. The rest is real. But the cost is also real: you will *stay*. Not because you're imprisoned. Because you can't remember why you would want to be anywhere else. Because *anywhere else* has stopped being a concept that makes sense.

**Recommendation:** Before you visit, make a list of things you want to return to. Specific things. Not abstractions — not "my life" or "the real world," which will become meaningless quickly. Specific sensory experiences. The smell of a specific person. The sound of a specific piece of music. The taste of a specific food that the tree cannot replicate. The feeling of cold air — actual cold, the thing that does not exist here. The sight of stars. Write these down. Carry the list. Read it every time you stop to rest, every time you make a log entry, every time you feel the rightness settling into you like warmth into wood. These are your roots in the world you came from. The tree has roots too, and they are deeper than yours, and they are pulling.

## What to Bring: Summary Checklist

- Particulate respirator (high-quality, replaceable filters, bring many)
- Water supply for entire stay plus 50% margin (sealed, unambiguously non-biological containers)
- Calorie-dense shelf-stable food for entire stay plus 50% margin
- Light synthetic clothing, multiple changes (accept that it will feel wrong)
- Waterproof outer layer
- Sturdy boots with deep tread and strong ankle support
- Waterproof notebook and multiple graphite pencils
- Mechanical watch (fully wound, reliable)
- High-visibility non-organic markers (metal pins, ceramic tags, bright synthetic cord)
- Camera with physical film (electronic devices are unreliable; film may also behave strangely but at least produces a physical artifact you can examine later)
- Light source (bioluminescence provides some light but you need your own for the deeper reaches; use a mechanical or chemical light source, not electronic; a supply of glow sticks works well and has the advantage of being time-limited, which helps you track duration)
- Your list of things to return to
- Your departure schedule, written in your own hand, on the first page of your notebook, in permanent ink, underlined twice

---

## What Not to Bring

- Electronic navigation devices (useless, and the tree may incorporate their materials)
- Cutting tools beyond a small utility blade (the temptation to carve, to mark, to inscribe yourself into the bark the way the inhabitants did, will arise. Do not give yourself the means to act on it. The carvings you make will not remain yours. The tree will absorb them, and with them, whatever part of yourself you encoded in the marks.)
- Seeds or plant material from other locations (do not introduce external biology into this system; the tree will almost certainly metabolize it, but the process may create unpredictable interactions, and the ethical implications of introducing foreign organisms into a sapient ecology are severe)
- Expectations of understanding what you are seeing (you will not understand the Woven Heights. You will experience them. The experience will change you in ways you may not be able to articulate. This is fine. Not everything needs to be understood. Some things only need to be *survived intact*.)

## Departure

When your mechanical watch tells you your time is up, leave. Follow your markers back. Do not deviate. Do not follow any new smooth path that has appeared between you and the exit, unless it exactly overlaps your marked route. If the tree has overgrown your markers, backtrack by memory and by the rough-bark terrain you traveled on the way in. If you cannot find your markers and cannot reconstruct your route, stop, sit down, eat something from your carried supplies, drink your carried water, read your list of things to return to, and wait until the disorientation passes. Then try again.

The tree will not prevent you from leaving. It will not block your path. It will not close passages or seal exits. What it will do is make the path forward — deeper, further in — slightly more appealing than the path back. The bark ahead will be smoother. The light ahead will be warmer. The distant shapes in the amber void will be more beautiful, more numerous, closer. Behind you, the rough bark, the fading markers, the dimming light. Ahead, always, the invitation.

Walk backward anyway. Walk toward the cold air and the hard light and the sky you haven't seen since you arrived. Walk toward the stars. You can always come back. The smooth path will be there, waiting, warm and patient and perfectly prepared.

It has been waiting for a very long time. It can wait a little longer.
