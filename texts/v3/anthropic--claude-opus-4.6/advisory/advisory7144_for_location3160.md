# Preparing for the Risen Shelves

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into — Or Rather, Swimming Into

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this is not a dive. Not in any sense you understand from recreational or even technical diving on baseline Earth. You are not descending to visit a site and then returning to air. You are entering a place where air is irrelevant, where the entire civilization has organized itself around its absence, and where you will need to exist — for however long you stay — as a body in warm saltwater at roughly forty meters depth, surrounded by people for whom this is simply home. Your preparation needs to account not only for the physical challenge of sustained underwater existence but for the psychological reality of living without a surface, without sky, without the reassuring knowledge that "up" leads to breath. You need to prepare your body, your equipment, your mind, and — perhaps most importantly — your sense of time.

---

## Breathing: The Central Problem and Its Solutions

At forty meters in saltwater, you are at roughly five atmospheres of pressure. Standard scuba becomes a logistics nightmare for any stay longer than an hour or two — nitrogen narcosis, decompression obligations, gas consumption. If you are planning to spend days or weeks (and you should, because this place reveals itself slowly, and rushing it would be like trying to read a book by flipping through it at arm's length), you need a long-duration breathing solution.

**Rebreather systems** are your starting point, but not the recreational closed-circuit units you might be familiar with. You want a fully closed-circuit system with redundant oxygen sensors, CO2 scrubber cartridges in quantities sufficient for your planned stay plus a generous margin, and a diluent gas mixture appropriate for the depth. At forty meters, a trimix or heliox diluent — helium replacing much of the nitrogen — will keep narcosis manageable and reduce decompression stress. Carry enough scrubber material. Then carry more. Then figure out if you can source or fabricate additional scrubber material in situ (calcium hydroxide is the usual absorbent; volcanic mineral deposits might offer chemical analogues, but do not rely on this without testing). Your rebreather is your life. Bring spare parts for every component that can fail. Bring two units if you can manage the transport.

**Alternative or supplementary breathing approaches** are worth researching for your specific multiverse-travel capabilities. Some travelers report access to gill-analogue biomodifications, osmotic membrane masks that extract dissolved oxygen from sufficiently warm, oxygen-rich water, or enchanted/technological artifacts that simply provide breathable gas indefinitely. If you have access to any of these, use them — but bring the rebreather as backup regardless. The water here is warm and fed by hydrothermal activity, which may mean variable dissolved oxygen levels and potentially elevated concentrations of hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, or other volcanic gases near the vents. Whatever you breathe through needs to handle that variability.

**Breathing psychology** is its own preparation. You will be breathing through an apparatus continuously, with no option to simply surface for a lungful of air. The sound of your own breathing — the click and hiss of a rebreather loop, the faint mechanical rhythm — will become your most constant companion. Practice this. Before you go, spend extended periods on a rebreather in controlled conditions. Not hours. Days, if possible. Sleep on it. Eat around it. Let the apparatus become invisible to your consciousness so that when you arrive at the Shelves and everything else is strange, at least the act of breathing is something your body has made peace with.

## Pressure and Decompression

If you plan to remain at depth for an extended period and then return to surface pressure (whatever surface exists in this world), you will accumulate a massive decompression obligation. Weeks at forty meters on any gas mix will saturate your tissues completely. Your ascent, when it comes, will require days of staged decompression stops — or access to a hyperbaric facility, or saturation diving protocols with a slow, controlled pressure reduction.

Plan this before you leave. Know your exit strategy. If your method of multiverse travel can simply return you to your origin point instantaneously at ambient pressure, then you need to transition to surface pressure gradually *before* departing, or have a pressurized environment waiting on the other side. Decompression sickness does not care about dimensional boundaries. Your blood will still fizz if you drop from five atmospheres to one without adequate off-gassing.

Consider adopting **saturation diving principles**: once your tissues are fully saturated with inert gas (which happens after roughly 24 hours at a stable depth), additional time at that depth adds no further decompression obligation. You simply owe the same lengthy decompression whether you stay for two days or two months. This is actually liberating — it means you can stay as long as you want without the obligation growing. But it means your departure will always require the same careful, multi-day ascent.

Carry **dive tables or a dive computer** rated for saturation exposures, even if you think you won't need them. Carry **emergency oxygen** for treating decompression illness, though administering pure O2 at depth introduces its own toxicity risks. Know the signs — joint pain, skin mottling, dizziness, visual disturbances, confusion. Know them so well that you can recognize them in yourself even when your thinking is compromised.

## Thermal Considerations

The water is described as warm, heated by volcanic activity. This is a gift — cold is the silent killer in prolonged immersion, and warmth means you can likely operate in a lighter exposure suit than deep diving would normally require. However, "warm" is relative and variable.

**Bring a flexible thermal protection system.** A thin wetsuit or dive skin for the upper shelves, where the water will be at its coolest (merely warm rather than hot), and the option to strip down further as you descend toward the thermal vents. Near the vents themselves, the water may be extremely hot — not boiling (the pressure at depth raises the boiling point), but potentially hot enough to scald. Treat the vent zones the way you would treat an open fire: useful, essential even, but dangerous if you get too close without understanding the thermal gradients.

Monitor your core temperature. Prolonged immersion in warm water can lead to hyperthermia just as insidiously as cold water leads to hypothermia. If the ambient water is, say, 32-35°C (quite warm for ocean water but plausible with geothermal input), your body's ability to shed metabolic heat through the water is reduced. You may find yourself overheating during physical exertion — swimming between shelves, helping with construction, exploring. Hydration becomes critical. Yes, you are surrounded by water, but it is saltwater, and you are losing fresh water through respiration and possibly through your skin depending on osmotic gradients. Bring or produce fresh water. The thermal vents might offer a distillation opportunity if you can rig a condenser, but don't count on it.

## Vision and Light

The light at the Shelves is described as thin, greenish, perpetual dusk — attenuated sunlight filtered through cloud, water column, and a living canopy. Your eyes will adapt, but slowly, and never completely. The bioluminescence provides supplementary illumination, but it is dim, blue-green, and non-directional. You will exist in a world without shadows as you know them, where forms emerge gradually from a luminous murk rather than being crisply delineated.

**Bring a dive light, but use it sparingly and thoughtfully.** A bright artificial light in this environment will be blinding to adapted eyes — yours and the inhabitants'. It will also disrupt the bioluminescent organisms, which dim in response to external light sources. Use a red-filtered or very low-intensity light for tasks that require precision (equipment maintenance, reading instruments, medical situations). Otherwise, let your eyes adapt. This takes time — full scotopic adaptation in these conditions might take 30-45 minutes, and even then you won't have the acuity you're used to. Accept this. The Shelves are not meant to be seen with full clarity. The permanent dusk is not a limitation but a quality of the place, like the silence of a library or the warmth of a kitchen. Fighting it with artificial light is possible but would be like shouting in a meditation hall — technically permitted but profoundly at odds with what's actually happening.

**Bring a mask with a wide field of view and anti-fog treatment.** Fogging is your enemy in low-light conditions. If your mask fogs, you lose what little contrast your adapted eyes can detect, and the world becomes a featureless green murk. Consider a full-face mask integrated with your rebreather — it protects your entire face, allows verbal communication through integrated comms if you have a buddy, and eliminates the jaw fatigue of biting a traditional mouthpiece for days on end.

## Movement and Buoyancy

At forty meters, in warm saltwater, wearing whatever equipment you've brought, you need to be neutrally buoyant and completely comfortable in three-dimensional movement. This is not negotiable. The Shelves are a vertical environment — multiple tiers, rope bridges that serve as pull-lines, dwellings at different heights. The inhabitants swim between levels with the ease of walking. You will not have that ease, not at first, but you can minimize the gap.

**Master your buoyancy before you arrive.** Trim your weighting so that you hang motionless in the water column without effort, without finning, without holding your breath. Every unnecessary movement you make kicks up the fine volcanic silt that covers every surface, and that silt takes "long, patient minutes to settle." You will announce yourself as a clumsy outsider every time you stir up a cloud. Practice frog kicks, flutter kicks with minimal amplitude, helicopter turns — the small, precise movements that technical divers use in silty environments. Practice hovering in place. Practice moving backward. Practice stopping instantly.

**Consider your fins carefully.** Full-blade fins give you power but encourage large movements that generate silt. Split fins or short paddle fins might give you finer control. The inhabitants almost certainly use no fins at all, relying on adapted swimming technique — watch them, learn from them, but don't expect to match their efficiency without their lifetime of practice.

**Carry a buoyancy compensation device** but keep it minimal. In a stable depth environment where you're not ascending and descending through large pressure changes, you won't need the range of a full BCD. A small wing or bladder, enough to fine-tune your trim, is sufficient. The less gear you carry, the less drag you create, the less silt you disturb.

## The Volcanic Silt: A Persistent Companion

This deserves its own section because it will affect everything you do. The fine volcanic silt covering the shelves is described as clouding when disturbed and taking minutes to settle. This means:

- **Every surface you touch produces a small eruption of particles.** Resting your hand on a ledge, setting down a piece of equipment, brushing against a wall — all of these create localized clouds that obscure vision and, in an enclosed space like one of the dome dwellings, could take a very long time to clear.

- **Entering a dwelling requires deliberation.** You cannot simply swim in through the entrance. You must approach slowly, arrest your momentum before reaching the threshold, and glide in with minimal water displacement. Practice this.

- **Your equipment needs to be streamlined and secured.** Nothing dangling, nothing loose. Every hose, clip, and accessory tucked close to your body. Dangling objects catch on tithe-weed, bump against surfaces, and create the silt clouds you're trying to avoid.

- **You may want to adopt the inhabitants' movement patterns.** If they've been here long enough to develop a culture, they've certainly developed movement habits optimized for silt management. Watch how they approach surfaces, how they settle onto a shelf, how they enter and exit structures. Mimic them.

## Communication

You are entering a community that lives and communicates entirely underwater. Sound propagates differently in water — faster, farther, but with different frequency characteristics. The inhabitants likely communicate through a combination of low-frequency vocalizations (which travel well in water), gesture, touch, and possibly the manipulation of bioluminescent organisms (triggering the blue glow of the filament mosses as a signaling mechanism).

**You will not be able to speak to them in any conventional sense when you arrive.** Even if you share a language (unlikely), the mechanics of underwater speech through a rebreather mouthpiece or full-face mask produce sounds that are distorted, tinny, and probably outside the frequency range the inhabitants have optimized their communication for.

Prepare by:

- **Learning basic underwater communication gestures** from your own diving tradition (okay, stop, up, down, problem, help), but be aware these may mean nothing to the inhabitants.

- **Bringing a waterproof writing surface** — a slate, wax tablet, or similar — for attempting written communication if the inhabitants have a written system. Given their level of material culture (weaving, carving, pottery, the mineral-deposition library), they very likely do. Observe their carved objects and woven tokens for recurring symbols.

- **Being prepared for a long, patient, non-verbal period of relationship-building.** You are a large, strange, equipment-encrusted creature arriving in a quiet community. Move slowly. Don't approach people directly at first. Settle somewhere visible but non-intrusive — an unoccupied shelf edge, perhaps — and simply exist in their space. Let them come to you. They will. Curiosity is as universal as gravity, and rather more reliable in this particular environment.

- **Listening.** Not just for words but for the sonic environment. The bass hum of the vents, the creak of tithe-weed, the *tik tik tik* of carving, the bell-ring of the cup organisms. These sounds constitute the acoustic landscape of daily life here. Learning to hear them — really hear them, to distinguish the normal background from something unusual — is a form of fluency.

## Nutrition and Sustenance

You cannot eat conventionally while on a rebreather, and even with a full-face mask, underwater eating requires specific techniques and foods. Plan for:

- **Nutrient-dense, compact food supplies** brought from your point of origin. Energy gels, paste-based rations, anything that can be consumed through a feeding port in a full-face mask or during brief mask-off moments in a sheltered location. Calculate your caloric needs generously — swimming constantly, even in warm water, burns significant energy, and the pressure at depth affects metabolism.

- **Gradual integration of local food sources.** The inhabitants eat cultivated algae, young tithe-weed stalks, and likely the small shrimp-like organisms. All of these are potentially edible by a human body, but "potentially" is doing heavy lifting in that sentence. Introduce local foods slowly, in small quantities, watching for digestive distress or allergic reaction. The algae cultivated near the thermal vents may contain unusual mineral concentrations — high sulfur, heavy metals, or exotic trace elements from the volcanic chemistry. Your kidneys and liver will be the arbiters of what you can tolerate.

- **Water.** I cannot stress this enough. You need fresh water. Dehydration underwater is insidious because you don't feel thirsty the way you do in air — the constant immersion suppresses the psychological sensation of thirst even as your body loses water through immersion diuresis (the pressure-induced increase in urine production that every diver experiences). Bring a water supply. Bring purification capability. Investigate local fresh water sources — in a world-spanning ocean with no described land, fresh water may be exceedingly rare or entirely absent in liquid form, though condensation on the underside of the surface canopy organisms or chemical extraction from the tithe-weed's cellular fluid might be possible.

## Equipment Maintenance and Repair

Your equipment is your life support. In an environment with no metal, no fire, and no air, your ability to repair and maintain your gear depends entirely on what you bring and what you know.

**Bring:**
- Complete spare parts kit for your rebreather: O-rings, sensors, batteries, scrubber cartridges, mouthpiece components, hose clamps.
- Backup breathing system (a small bailout cylinder with an appropriate gas mix, at minimum).
- Tools: wrenches, screwdrivers, pliers — whatever your specific equipment requires for field maintenance. Stainless steel or titanium, to resist corrosion.
- Silicone grease for O-rings and seals.
- Spare mask and mask straps.
- Repair patches for your exposure suit.
- Waterproof containers for anything that doesn't tolerate immersion (certain electronics, spare batteries, medical supplies).
- Zip ties. Many zip ties. They fix everything temporarily and some things permanently.
- Several good dive knives or shears, for cutting tangled tithe-weed or line.

**Know:**
- How to perform a complete strip-down and rebuild of your rebreather underwater. Practice this at home in a pool until you can do it blind, because you may need to do it in near-darkness with numb fingers while stressed.
- Basic field-expedient repairs using local materials. Tithe-weed fiber cord could substitute for some lashings or straps. The volcanic glass (obsidian-like but softer) could be knapped into cutting tools if your metal ones fail. The mineral silt, mixed with bioluminescent moss extract, might produce a workable adhesive or sealant — but test this before you need it.

## Medical Preparation

You are entering an environment with no medical infrastructure compatible with your physiology, no evacuation route faster than however long it takes you to ascend safely and transit back through the multiverse, and a set of medical risks that are unique and cumulative.

**Specific risks to prepare for:**

- **Oxygen toxicity.** At five atmospheres, the partial pressure of oxygen in your breathing mix must be carefully managed. Too high, and you risk central nervous system oxygen toxicity — seizures, without warning, underwater. This is rapidly fatal if your mouthpiece is lost during the seizure. Know your oxygen limits. Monitor your PO2 constantly. Set conservative alarms on your rebreather.

- **Carbon dioxide buildup.** A failing scrubber canister leads to CO2 accumulation in the breathing loop. Symptoms: headache, shortness of breath, confusion, panic. If you feel any of these, switch to bailout gas immediately and troubleshoot the rebreather only after you're breathing clean gas.

- **Infection.** You are immersed in warm saltwater full of microbial life from another world. Any break in your skin — a scrape against the rough volcanic rock, a cut from a volcanic glass edge, a blister from equipment rub — is an entry point for organisms your immune system has never encountered. Bring broad-spectrum antibiotics, antifungals, and antiseptic solution. Keep wounds clean and covered, though "clean" is a relative concept when you're living in an ocean. Consider prophylactic antibiotic use if you sustain any significant wound.

- **Ear and sinus issues.** Extended immersion means your ears and sinuses are permanently exposed to pressure. Ear infections (otitis externa) are almost inevitable over a long stay. Bring drying drops (a mix of alcohol and acetic acid), antibiotic ear drops, and decongestants for sinus management.

- **Skin maceration.** Your skin will soften, wrinkle, and eventually begin to break down from continuous water exposure. This is not just cosmetically unpleasant — macerated skin tears easily, heals poorly, and invites infection. The warm water accelerates the process. Consider a full-body exposure suit that minimizes direct skin contact with the water, and treat any areas of breakdown promptly with barrier cream and waterproof dressings.

- **Psychological stress.** This is not optional to prepare for. You will be in a dim, enclosed, alien environment with no access to air, sky, or the usual markers of human comfort — for days, weeks, perhaps longer. The bass hum of the vents will be incessant. The green twilight will become oppressive at times. The absence of a day-night cycle will disrupt your circadian rhythm. Bring sleep medication if you are prone to insomnia. Bring a journal and a waterproof way to write in it — the act of recording your experience provides psychological anchoring. Bring music if you have a waterproof playback device, though you may find that after the first few days, you prefer the sounds of the Shelves to anything you brought with you.

## What to Bring as Gifts and Trade Goods

You are entering a community with a specific material culture. They work with tithe-weed fiber, volcanic glass, bone-like material, volcanic silt clay, and bioluminescent organisms. They lack metal, synthetic materials, and anything that requires fire or air to produce.

**Potentially valuable gifts:**

- **Cordage.** Synthetic rope or cord, especially anything stronger and more durable than tithe-weed fiber. Nylon, Dyneema, Spectra — these would be extraordinary materials to people who weave all their structural cordage from plant fibers. Even a few hundred meters of paracord could be transformative.

- **Cutting tools.** A good stainless steel knife would be a wonder in a community that works with chipping volcanic glass. Bring several. They will be treasured, and offering one is a gesture of profound generosity.

- **Needles.** Metal sewing needles and sailmaker's needles. They use bone needles. Metal ones would be sharper, thinner, more durable. A small tin of assorted needles costs you nothing and could earn you deep goodwill.

- **Light sources.** Small, long-lasting, low-intensity chemical light sticks or waterproof LED lights with adjustable brightness. Not to replace the bioluminescence but as a complementary tool. Let the inhabitants decide how and whether to use them.

- **Nothing that disrupts the mineral-deposition process.** Do not bring anything that could chemically interfere with the slow crystalline growth that forms the heart of their cultural practice. No strong acids, no chelating agents, no materials that leach chemicals you can't predict. When in doubt, keep your synthetic materials away from the central structure.

## Approaching the Central Structure — The Mineral Library

This is the heart of the place, and you need to prepare for it the way you would prepare for entering a sacred space in any culture — which is to say, with humility, attention, and no assumptions.

The structure where the mineral deposits grow in branching columns, slowly encasing the objects placed among them, is simultaneously a library, a temple, a clock, and a negotiation with geological forces. It is the community's way of participating in the process that created the plateau itself. They are not merely living on the rock; they are collaborating with it, offering it shapes to grow around, patterns to incorporate, memories to mineralize.

**Do not touch the crystalline branches without invitation.** The description notes that inhabitants pause, touch a branch, listen. This is a practiced interaction with specific cultural meaning. Touching casually, touching out of curiosity, touching to see what it feels like — all of these would be, at best, gauche and at worst, deeply offensive. Watch first. Watch for a long time. If you are eventually invited to touch, or to place an object of your own among the branches, understand the weight of what is being offered: they are asking you to contribute something to a process that will outlast every person currently alive, something that will become part of the geological record of this world. Choose your offering with care. Something small, something meaningful, something that has a shape worth preserving. A key, maybe, or a coin, or a small carved figure — an object that carries information about where you came from, so that when it is eventually encased beyond recognition, the mineral record holds a ghost of a world that is not this one.

## Preparing for the Strangeness

The description mentions several phenomena that don't fit neatly into physical explanations: thermal plumes carrying shapes that move with apparent purpose, rock that grows overnight in response to the presence of structures, light that shifts direction without apparent cause, bioluminescent organisms that dim simultaneously as though responding to a unified signal.

You have two options. You can try to explain these things — volcanic convection patterns creating coherent fluid structures, piezogenic mineral growth accelerated by pressure from buildings, bioluminescent organisms linked by chemical signaling networks in the water. These explanations may be correct. They may even be complete.

Or you can accept that you are on an alien world where a volcanic plateau is building itself into existence while a community of adapted humans builds itself into the plateau, and that the boundary between geological process and living intention may not be as clear here as it is where you come from. The inhabitants' response to the thermal-plume shapes — a hand turned palm-outward, a brief pause — suggests acknowledgment without fear, recognition without worship. They see these phenomena regularly. They have integrated them into daily life. They do not seem to find them threatening or even particularly remarkable. Take your cue from them.

**Prepare your mind by practicing radical acceptance.** Meditate, if that is your practice. Or simply spend time before your departure sitting with uncertainty — with the knowledge that you are going somewhere that operates by rules you don't fully understand, and that your job is not to understand them but to be present within them, to move carefully, to pay attention, and to allow the place to be what it is without needing it to be what you expected.

## Time

There is no day-night cycle. The light shifts without reference to solar position. The thermal vents pulse in patterns that may constitute a local sense of time, but you won't understand those patterns for weeks, if ever.

**Bring a watch.** A reliable, pressure-rated dive watch or computer that tracks elapsed time. Not because you need to maintain a schedule but because without some external time reference, your perception of duration will drift alarmingly. Humans need temporal anchors. Without them, depression and disorientation set in. Use your watch to impose a routine on yourself: eat at regular intervals, sleep at regular intervals (even if you don't feel tired — the warm water and dim light will make you feel perpetually drowsy), check your equipment at regular intervals, write in your journal at regular intervals.

But also — and this is the harder instruction — be prepared to let go of clock time as your primary way of understanding when things happen. The village runs on vent-pulse time, current-shift time, tithe-weed-growth time, mineral-deposition time. Some of these cycles are measured in minutes. Some in weeks. Some in generations. As you acclimate, you may find that your watch becomes less important than the rhythm of the *tik tik tik* of someone carving nearby, or the slow sway of the tithe-weed curtains, or the periodic brightening and dimming of the bacterial blue in your dwelling's walls.

## Final Preparations: What to Carry in Yourself

Bring patience. Bring more patience than you think you need, and then more beyond that. This is a place where the volcanic rock grows in centimeters, where the mineral library encases objects over years, where the architecture merges with the geology over decades. Everything here is in the process of becoming. Nothing is finished. You are visiting a work in progress, and the work is being done on a timescale that makes human urgency look absurd.

Bring curiosity without the need to catalog. You will see things you cannot name or explain. Resist the urge to immediately categorize them. Sit with them. Let them be unnamed for a while. The cup-organisms that ring like bells — you don't need to know what phylum they belong to. The light that shifts from nowhere — you don't need a physical explanation before you can appreciate what it does to the color of the rock. The thermal plume shapes that move with purpose — you don't need to decide whether they're alive before you can acknowledge them.

Bring respect for the inhabitants' relationship with their environment. These people are not struggling against an alien world. They are not surviving. They are *living* — building, cultivating, creating art, negotiating with geological processes, raising children (presumably), maintaining a community with shared practices and a collective memory encoded in slowly mineralizing crystal. Their technology is simple. Their situation is not. Do not mistake the absence of metal for the absence of sophistication. Their understanding of the volcanic rock's behavior, of the water's currents and chemistry, of the ecosystem's cycles, is certainly deeper than anything you will achieve during your visit. Learn from them if they'll teach you. Watch them if they won't.

Bring the willingness to be changed. Every place you visit in the multiverse leaves a residue — in your memories, in your habits, in the way you subsequently perceive your home world. The Risen Shelves will leave you with a particular residue: a sense of slowness, of patience, of the permeability of boundaries you thought were solid (between built and geological, between plant and settlement, between intention and natural process). You may find, after you return, that you move more carefully. That you pay more attention to the surfaces beneath your hands. That you listen, sometimes, for a bass hum in the ground, the sound of a planet still making itself, still not finished with any of it.

Pack your equipment. Check it twice. Check it again. Then go — slowly, carefully, with your hands open and your expectations set aside — into the warm green twilight, into the hum, into the place that is still deciding what it wants to be, and offer it the courtesy of not deciding for it.
