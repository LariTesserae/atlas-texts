# Preparing for the Drowning Tiers

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Let it settle. Because the primary danger of the Drowning Tiers is not any single environmental hazard — it's the *totality*. Every system you rely on to maintain your body, your sanity, and your sense of self as a discrete organism will be under simultaneous, patient siege. Nothing here will attack you. Everything here will accumulate on you.

This is not a hostile environment in the way a volcano or a war zone is hostile. It is an indifferent environment that operates on principles fundamentally incompatible with human comfort, human timescales, and human boundaries between self and surroundings. You must prepare not to fight it but to *persist* inside it for whatever duration you intend to visit, and you must set that duration in advance, because the Tiers will not give you clear signals that it's time to leave. The degradation is too gradual. You'll adjust. You'll keep adjusting. You'll never fully arrive. That line from the description is not poetry. It's a warning.

## Thermal Protection: The Central Problem

The cold described here is not extreme in the way polar expeditions face extreme cold. It's worse, in a specific way. Polar cold is dry, and wind-driven, and your body can fight it through metabolic heat generation and proper insulation. The Drowning Tiers cold is *wet*, *still*, and *conductive*. The saturated atmosphere means any insulation that relies on trapped air — down, fleece, most synthetic fills — will become waterlogged and useless within hours. The lack of wind means convective drying never occurs. The moisture on your skin doesn't evaporate. It accumulates. This is the description telling you that your body's primary thermoregulation mechanism (sweating and evaporative cooling, and the reverse — keeping dry to stay warm) is completely nonfunctional here.

**What to bring:**

- **Closed-cell insulation only.** No down. No fibrous fills. Think neoprene, think wetsuit technology, but modified. You want a full-body layering system built on the assumption that every outer surface will be permanently wet and every layer must retain thermal value when saturated. Closed-cell foam undergarments beneath a sealed outer shell.

- **A sealed outer shell.** Not waterproof in the breathable membrane sense — those rely on vapor pressure differentials that won't exist in a saturated atmosphere. Fully sealed. Vulcanized seams. Think drysuit, not rain jacket. Accept that you will be in a closed system and manage your internal moisture with wicking base layers and desiccant packs against your skin. You will be uncomfortable. The alternative is hypothermia.

- **Heated elements.** Battery-powered or chemical heating wired into your base layer, concentrated at the core, wrists, neck, and feet. Your body will lose heat continuously through conduction with the wet stone if you sit, lean, or touch anything, so your gloves and boot soles need thermal barriers — rigid insulating layers that prevent direct contact with the terrace floors and walls. Consider heated insoles. Consider never sitting directly on the stone.

- **Multiple redundancy.** Bring a second full thermal system packed in a waterproof compression bag. If your primary system is compromised — a torn seal, a failed heating element — you need to change immediately. Hypothermia here won't announce itself with shivering and dramatic symptoms. It will arrive the way everything here arrives: slowly, patiently, by degrees, and by the time you notice, your cognitive function will already be degraded enough that you may not care.

**Thermal monitoring:** Wear a continuous-read core temperature sensor — an ingestible pill transmitter or a tympanic probe — and set an alarm for any drop below 36.5°C. Do not trust your subjective sense of how cold you are. The slow conductive cooling will feel like nothing for a long time, and then it will feel like everything, and the transition will be blurry.

## Respiratory Protection: Breathing the Soup

The atmosphere is described as dense, soupy with moisture. This presents several concerns:

- **Aspiration.** You may literally be breathing in fine water droplets with every breath. Over hours, this can lead to a form of near-drowning — fluid accumulation in the lungs, progressive impairment of gas exchange. It won't feel like drowning. It'll feel like a slight tightness, then a cough, then fatigue, then confusion. In a place where confusion already creeps in through other channels, this is extremely dangerous.

- **Mineral content.** The water here is mineral-heavy, leaving crystalline deposits on every surface. You will be breathing those minerals. Whatever trace metals catalyze the chemiluminescence in the basalt — and whatever biochemistry the organisms contribute to the atmospheric moisture — will be entering your lungs.

- **Biological load.** The description notes organisms so small and numerous that their bioluminescence reads as a property of the environment. You cannot see them individually. They are in the air. They are in the moisture. You will breathe them.

**What to bring:**

- **A full-face respirator with sealed positive-pressure air supply.** Not a filter mask — the particulates here may be biological, may be mineral, may be something with no earthly analogue, and you cannot assume any filter medium will catch what needs catching. Bring a closed-circuit rebreather system with a substantial CO₂ scrubbing capacity, or, better, a portable air supply with enough capacity for your planned visit duration plus 50% margin.

- **Backup air.** Compact emergency bottles, enough for several hours. If your primary system fails, you need time to get out.

- **Seal integrity.** The respirator must seal against the same drysuit-style outer shell protecting your body. Any gap between face seal and suit seal is a pathway for the moisture to enter. Full integration. Test it before you arrive. Test it again after you arrive, before you descend to the lower tiers.

- **Anti-fog measures.** The temperature differential between your face and the respirator visor, in this saturated atmosphere, will produce immediate and permanent fogging on any untreated surface. Hydrophobic coatings on the inside, continuous airflow across the visor from your breathing system, and a manual wipe mechanism you can operate with gloved hands.

If you choose not to bring respiratory protection — if you want to breathe the place, to know what it's like — then limit your exposure to the upper terraces only, for no more than an hour, and have someone monitoring your oxygen saturation continuously. Accept that you may develop a cough that lasts weeks. Accept that you may inhale organisms whose lifecycle in human lung tissue is unknown. Accept that the mineral deposits that coat every surface here may begin to coat the inside of you.

## Sensory Management: The Sound

This is where most travelers underestimate the Drowning Tiers.

The sound is not background noise. It is described as the organizing principle of the city. It is structured, layered, pervasive, and physically felt — the deep thrumming of the armored creatures transmits through the stone, up through your feet, into your bones. The clicks. The keening. The wet static. The polyrhythmic interplay of dozens of groups producing a city-wide composition that never resolves, never reaches a cadence, never stops.

Prolonged exposure to this kind of immersive, low-frequency, structured-but-nonhuman sound environment will produce effects that range from disorientation to nausea to altered states of consciousness to profound psychological distress. The low frequencies especially — the chest-resonant thrumming, the vibrations transmitted through the stone — can affect heart rhythm, breathing patterns, and vestibular function. You may feel seasick on solid ground. You may feel a compulsion to match the rhythms with your own movements, your own breathing, and you should be cautious about this impulse because you do not know what synchronizing with the city's operating frequency actually does.

**What to bring:**

- **Active noise-canceling in-ear monitors with passive isolation.** Custom-molded. Good enough to reduce the ambient by 30+ dB. You will still hear everything — the sound is too pervasive and too low-frequency to fully block — but the reduction from overwhelming to merely loud is the difference between functioning and not functioning.

- **Variable attenuation.** You want the ability to dial your hearing protection up and down. There will be moments when you need to hear the full sound — to assess your surroundings, to notice changes in the creatures' behavior that might indicate danger or interest in you — and there will be long stretches when you need the relief of partial silence.

- **Bone-conduction awareness.** Understand that ear protection does nothing against vibration transmitted through the stone into your skeleton. Your boot soles should include vibration-dampening layers. When standing on the terraces, consider standing on a portable insulating pad. The thrumming will still reach you, but attenuated. This matters more than you think. Hours of continuous bone-conducted infrasound will exhaust you in ways that feel like depression rather than fatigue.

- **Your own sound.** Bring music. Bring recorded human voices. Bring something to play in your ear monitors during rest periods that anchors you to human patterns of rhythm and melody. The Tiers' soundscape is coherent and hypnotic and deeply, deeply alien, and after enough hours immersed in it, human music starts to sound strange instead. Notice when that shift happens. It's a useful marker for how deep the place has gotten into you.

- **A recording setup.** Bring high-quality field recording equipment with low-frequency extension down to at least 5 Hz, because what's happening here acoustically is extraordinary and unprecedented and you owe it to every researcher who will never visit to bring back the best recording you can. Binaural microphones at head height plus a contact microphone pressed against the stone floor. The two recordings together will capture both the airborne and solid-transmitted components of the sound.

## Vision and Lighting

There is almost no light. What exists is chemiluminescent, faint, and possibly partly biological — emitted in deep blue spectra that sits at the ragged edge of human perception. Your eyes will adjust continuously and never fully arrive. This is significant. Permanent semi-dark-adaptation means your pupils will be dilated the entire time, which means any sudden bright light source — including your own, if you're careless — will be agonizing and will reset your adaptation, leaving you effectively blind for minutes.

**What to bring:**

- **Night vision.** Not image-intensification (there may not be enough photons for it to amplify). Thermal imaging is your primary modality here. The armored creatures are warm. The walls have thermal signatures. The organisms have metabolic heat. A thermal overlay on a heads-up display in your visor will give you a readable map of the living environment that your naked eyes can never achieve in this darkness.

- **Supplemental lighting: red and deep red only.** Dim. Diffuse. Headlamp with a deep red filter turned to its lowest setting. Red light preserves dark adaptation better than any other visible wavelength, and in an environment dominated by blue bioluminescence, red light will provide contrast without washing out what's already visible. Keep it dim. You are a guest in a lightless world and the organisms here may be sensitive to wavelengths they never encounter.

- **UV capability.** Bring a small UV torch. The mineral deposits may fluoresce dramatically under ultraviolet, and the biological organisms almost certainly will. This could reveal population densities and distributions invisible to the naked eye. Use sparingly — you don't know what UV does to organisms that have never evolved a response to it.

- **No flash photography.** The impulse to photograph the bioluminescent lower tiers will be overwhelming. Use long exposures, high-ISO sensors, tripods, and patience. A camera flash in this environment is an act of violence against every photosensitive organism on the terrace, which may be all of them. It may also provoke responses from the armored creatures whose sensory apparatus you do not understand.

## Surface Contact and Contamination

Everything is wet. Every surface supports life. The water is mineral-heavy and deposits crystalline material on everything it touches. You will be touched by all of this continuously.

The contamination risk is bidirectional. You risk contaminating yourself with organisms and minerals whose interaction with human biochemistry is unknown. You also risk contaminating the Tiers with human microbiota, skin cells, exhalations, and chemical traces from your equipment. In an ecosystem this dense, this closed, and this apparently ancient, introducing foreign biology could be catastrophic. Or it could be irrelevant — the organisms here metabolize stone, and may find your biology as alien and indigestible as you find theirs. But you don't know.

**What to bring:**

- **Full contamination protocol, both directions.** Your drysuit outer shell is already your primary barrier. Ensure it's made of materials that won't leach plasticizers, dyes, or other chemicals into the water. Ensure your boot soles won't shed microplastics on the terrace floors. Ensure every piece of equipment that touches any surface is clean, inert, and retrievable — nothing left behind.

- **Sample collection kit.** Sterile vials, sterile swabs, a portable cold-storage unit. You will want samples of the water, the mineral deposits, the wall-clinging organisms, the air. Take them carefully, take small amounts, and label everything with precise location data because the chemical and biological composition likely varies dramatically between upper and lower tiers.

- **Decontamination for egress.** When you leave, you need to decontaminate everything before returning to your point of origin. The organisms here live in saturated darkness on mineral chemistry. Most of them probably can't survive in a normal atmosphere. But "probably" isn't good enough when you're talking about organisms that metabolize basalt. Bring UV sterilization, chemical wash solutions, and sealed containers for your suit and equipment to go through decon before anything goes home with you.

- **Do not touch anything with bare skin.** Not the walls, not the water, not the creatures. The mineral deposits leave crusts on everything they contact. Your skin is a surface. The organisms that scrape and metabolize those crusts may not distinguish between stone and you.

## Psychological Preparation: The Hardest Part

The Drowning Tiers will make you feel dissolved.

This is not metaphorical. Every aspect of the environment works to erode the boundaries between you and it. The moisture accumulates on your skin and won't leave. The sound enters through your skeleton and synchronizes your breathing. The darkness eliminates your visual horizon and collapses your sense of space. The cold creeps inward by degrees. The organisms are so numerous and so small that the environment itself seems alive — because it is — and you are inside something living, something vast, something that doesn't notice you or notices you only as a temporary thermal anomaly, a brief warm spot on a terrace that will be re-colonized by clinging creatures within hours of your departure.

The scale of time here will disorient you. The city is growing out of the mountain over months and years. The armored creatures patrol routes that may not repeat for days. The wall emerging from the cliff face twenty meters away looks like stillness but isn't. You are operating on seconds and minutes inside a system that operates on seasons and centuries, and the felt experience of this mismatch is a particular kind of loneliness — not the loneliness of isolation, because you are surrounded by more life per square meter than almost any environment you've ever visited, but the loneliness of irrelevance, of being the wrong speed, the wrong temperature, the wrong kind of pattern in a place that is entirely composed of patterns that are not yours.

**How to prepare:**

- **Meditation practice.** Specifically, practices that strengthen your sense of bodily boundary and self-continuity. Body scan meditations. Breath counting. Anything that gives you a reliable internal anchor when external reference points dissolve. Start practicing weeks before your visit.

- **Set a firm departure time and enforce it mechanically.** A timer on your wrist that vibrates when it's time to leave. Not an alarm — you don't want to add human sound to this place — but a physical signal against your skin. When it goes off, you leave. No extensions. No "just a little longer." The Tiers reward lingering with deeper immersion, and deeper immersion is not your friend past a certain threshold.

- **Buddy system.** If at all possible, do not visit alone. Bring someone whose judgment you trust and establish a mutual monitoring protocol: regular check-ins at set intervals where you ask each other simple cognitive questions. What's your name. What time did we arrive. What's our departure time. How do you feel. If either person can't answer clearly, or answers with a dreamy vagueness, or says "I feel fine" with an intonation that sounds like the sentence doesn't quite mean anything anymore — you leave. Both of you. Immediately.

- **Journal.** Bring a waterproof notebook and a pencil (not a pen — ink won't flow reliably in this moisture). Write observations at regular intervals. The act of writing in human language is an anchoring behavior. It forces you to translate the experience into your own cognitive framework rather than letting the place's framework subsume yours. Note how your handwriting changes over the hours. It will.

- **Expect grief.** This sounds strange, but many visitors to environments of this nature and scale report a sadness that arrives without obvious cause — a mourning for something they can't name. It may be the recognition that this vast, ancient, self-constructing world is utterly complete without you. It may be the sound, which has tonal qualities that mimic the frequencies humans associate with lamentation. It may be the cold, which your mammalian brain interprets as abandonment at a preverbal level. Whatever it is, it will come, and it will feel true, and you should let it move through you without believing it is the whole truth. You will feel like the smallest and most temporary thing in this place. You are. That's not a reason to despair. It's just a fact, and facts can be survived.

## Practical Logistics

**Food and water:** Bring everything you need. There is nothing metabolically compatible for you here, and the local water will deposit minerals in your kidneys that no earthly biology is equipped to process. Sealed pouches, drinking tube routed inside your respirator. High-calorie, high-fat foods — your body will be burning enormous calories maintaining core temperature even with active heating.

**Waste management:** Full containment. Nothing from you enters this ecosystem. Sealed collection bags. Catheter systems for longer visits, unpleasant as they are. This is non-negotiable.

**Navigation:** The tiers are dense, the geometry is non-Euclidean in feeling if not in fact, and every surface looks similar in the dark. Bring a mapping system — LIDAR if you can manage it, inertial navigation at minimum. Mark your entry point with something you can find by thermal imaging. Dropping physical markers is contamination.

**Duration:** For a first visit, I would recommend no more than six to eight hours on the upper terraces. Do not descend past the third tier. The lower tiers are denser in organisms, louder, warmer from collective metabolic heat, and the bioluminescent glow — which sounds beautiful and is — reportedly becomes hypnotic in a way that isn't just aesthetic but neurological. The rhythms down there are more complex. The creatures are more varied. The pull to go deeper is real. Resist it until you've completed at least two upper-terrace visits and understand your own reactions well enough to predict them.

**Communication:** Expect radio interference from the mineral-dense basalt and the pervasive vibration. Hardline tether to your entry point if you need guaranteed communication with a support team outside. Don't rely on wireless anything.

## What to Bring for the Soul

This section has no equipment list.

Go to the Drowning Tiers with reverence. What is happening there — the city that builds itself through sound and heat and vibration, the ecosystem that is its own architecture, the mountain slowly becoming something it was not — is among the most extraordinary things any traveler will ever witness. It is older than you. It will outlast you. It doesn't need you to see it in order to be magnificent, but you need to see it in order to know that this kind of magnificence exists.

Go with humility. You are a warm, soft, temporary thing visiting a place made of stone and patience. The armored creatures will sense you — they can feel thermal anomalies, and you are the warmest thing on your terrace by a wide margin. They may approach. They may alter their rhythms in your presence. They may ignore you. Whatever they do, stay still. Observe. Do not attempt to interact, communicate, or test hypotheses about their intelligence by introducing stimuli. You are a guest in someone's home. The home has been under construction for longer than your species has existed. Behave accordingly.

Go with curiosity, but hold it on a leash. The desire to understand this place — to map the water system, decode the rhythms, catalog the organisms, determine whether the armored creatures are truly the architects — is good, is human, is worthy. But don't let the analytical impulse shield you from the raw experience. There will be a moment, probably on the second or third hour, when you stop trying to classify and just *stand there*, in the dark, in the cold, in the sound, and feel the moisture collecting on the surface of your suit, and watch a thin sheet of mineral water creep across the terrace floor toward the spiraling channel that will carry it down to the glowing depths, and hear the rhythms of two creature-groups merge and separate and merge again, and you will feel something that isn't grief and isn't joy and isn't fear. It's the feeling of being inside something alive and knowing it. Let that happen. That's what you came for.

Go knowing you will be changed. Not by anything dramatic — the Tiers don't transform, they accumulate. But you will leave with a recalibrated sense of time, a heightened sensitivity to the sounds of your own world, a tendency to notice moisture on surfaces that you never noticed before, and a deep, persistent awareness that somewhere, right now, in permanent darkness on a world that has never known the sun, a mountain is slowly becoming a city, and the city is full of singing, and the singing is the city, and none of it needs you, and all of it is beautiful, and the water keeps flowing down.
