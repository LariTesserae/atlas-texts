# Preparing for the Reliquary on the Spine of Eth-Kalaam

---

## First: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are visiting a location that is not hostile in the way a predator is hostile. It is hostile in the way that a vacuum is hostile, in the way that deep ocean pressure is hostile—indifferently, structurally, as a consequence of what it *is*. The mountain does not want to kill you. The mountain does not want anything. The room at the top does not want anything either, or if it does, what it wants operates on timescales and along axes that make your survival a matter of complete irrelevance to it. This is not comforting. This is the preparation.

You need to internalize, before you go, that you will be standing inside a functioning artifact of unknown provenance that manipulates fundamental physical forces—gravity, spatial geometry, possibly the structure of matter itself—and that this artifact has no user interface, no safety protocols, no signage, no railings, and no emergency exits beyond the spiral seam you entered through. You will be a mouse inside a running engine whose purpose you cannot determine. The engine has been running for longer than you can verify. It shows no signs of stopping or starting. You cannot turn it off. You may not be able to tell if it notices you.

That said: other people have been there and returned. The hand-wear on the passage walls, the ancient piton below—these are evidence that the room has received visitors and that those visitors survived long enough to leave by the way they came. This is the best safety data you have. It's not much.

## The Approach: Getting to the Base of Eth-Kalaam

### Multiverse Transit Considerations

Depending on your method of arrival, you may materialize anywhere from the lowland desert floor to somewhere on the range itself. If your transit method allows targeting precision, aim for the base of the Eth-Kalaam massif rather than the summit. There are several reasons for this:

**Acclimatization.** Even if you could arrive at the peak, the atmospheric conditions there are extreme enough that sudden exposure could incapacitate you. The air is thin—think high-altitude mountaineering thin, possibly thinner—and while the volcanic heat means you won't face hypothermia, altitude sickness doesn't care about temperature. It cares about partial pressure of oxygen, and that will be dangerously low at the summit. You need time to acclimatize. A few days at intermediate elevations, if you can find stable camp sites on the lower ridgelines, will reduce your risk of pulmonary or cerebral edema significantly.

**Reconnaissance.** You need to see the mountain before you climb it. The range is described as tectonically young, edges "sharp as theory"—this means unstable rock, knife-edge ridgelines, scree fields on steep grades, and the real possibility of rockfall or spontaneous collapse of features that look solid but are geologically infantile. Study the route from below. Look for the path of least insanity.

**Baseline mental state.** The room at the top contains perceptual anomalies—the floor that shifts with attention, the lattice that operates in geometries your eyes can't track, the sourceless sound. You want to arrive at the room in the best cognitive condition possible, which means not arriving already hypoxic, dehydrated, exhausted, and rattled from a bad transit. Give yourself time to settle into the local reality before you challenge its upper limits.

### What the Lowlands Offer

Almost nothing. The description suggests a pre-ecological landscape—mineral desert, no soil, no vegetation. The seasonal river in the distant valley might offer water for a few weeks a year; if you're lucky enough to arrive during that window, you can resupply there. If not, you carry everything.

There is no local population evident. No settlements, no roads, no infrastructure. You are operating in true wilderness, the kind that hasn't been wilderness long enough for that word to even apply—it's just *un-lived-in space*, raw geology that hasn't been touched by biology yet. No one will find you if something goes wrong. No one will come looking.

## Physical Preparation and Gear

### Atmospheric Protection

**Supplemental oxygen is non-negotiable.** The summit atmosphere is described as "thin enough to taste: metallic, adrenal." That metallic taste is a real physiological phenomenon associated with breathing air at very low pressure—your mucous membranes dry out, trace minerals in your tissues become perceptible, and the low oxygen triggers adrenaline responses that give everything a buzzy, electric quality. You are operating at the edge of what human lungs can extract oxygen from.

Bring a closed-circuit or semi-closed-circuit oxygen system. Not just bottled O₂ with a mask—you want something that recirculates and enriches your breathing air, because you'll be at altitude for an extended period (the climb, the time in the room, the descent) and carrying enough bottled gas for all of that in a place with no resupply is a weight problem you don't need. A rebreather-style system with CO₂ scrubbers and a modest oxygen supply, supplemented by what you can pull from the ambient air, is more sustainable.

**Important caveat about the room itself:** The air inside the chamber is described as thicker, almost humid. The semi-permeable walls concentrate moisture inward, and it's possible they concentrate atmospheric gases as well. You may find that breathing inside the room is easier than breathing on the approach—the room may, intentionally or incidentally, create a livable microatmosphere. Do not count on this. Bring your own air. But be aware that you might not need it once inside, and that wearing a mask and rebreather apparatus might interfere with your ability to perceive what the room is doing. You'll have to make a judgment call on-site about when to transition to ambient breathing, if you do at all.

**Bring a pulse oximeter.** Clip it to your finger and check it obsessively. If your blood oxygen saturation drops below 80%, you are in danger. Below 70%, you are dying. The symptoms of severe hypoxia include euphoria, impaired judgment, visual disturbance, and hallucinations—and you are about to enter a room that already contains things that challenge perception. You absolutely must be able to distinguish between "the lattice is doing something impossible to my vision" and "my brain is shutting down from oxygen deprivation." The oximeter is your reality anchor for this specific variable.

### Thermal Management

The environment is paradoxical: extreme altitude that should be freezing but isn't, because volcanic heat radiates upward from below. You're dealing with "punishing, airless warmth"—hot, thin air, which is a dehydration machine. Hot air makes you sweat. Thin air makes you breathe faster and harder, losing moisture with every exhalation. You will dehydrate with alarming speed.

**Water.** Carry more than you think you need. Then carry more than that. A minimum of six liters for the summit push and time in the room, with additional caches at lower camps. The room's moisture might technically be drinkable—condensation from stone that was restructured at the molecular level into unknown crystal lattices is... not something I'd recommend drinking without analysis, but in an emergency, it's liquid water. The water lens on the ceiling is off-limits; disturbing it seems extraordinarily inadvisable for reasons I'll address later.

**Clothing.** Light, breathable, moisture-wicking layers. UV protection is critical—at this altitude, with thin atmosphere and no cloud cover, solar radiation will be extreme. Long sleeves, a wide-brimmed hat or hood, UV-blocking eye protection. The interior of the room is lit only by dim, filtered light through the translucent walls, but the approach will be fully exposed. Expect to be managing both overheating (from the volcanic warmth and exertion) and the risk of radiant heat on exposed skin.

**Don't bring heavy insulation.** This isn't an Everest-type cold-altitude scenario. Your enemy is heat and dryness, not cold.

### Climbing Gear

The mountain is young, steep, and unstable. You're climbing what amounts to a recently-extruded volcanic massif with minimal erosional smoothing—expect:

- **Loose rock everywhere.** Handholds that look solid may not be. Test everything before you weight it.
- **Knife-edge ridgelines.** Exposure on both sides. Bring a low-profile harness, dynamic rope, and a selection of passive protection (nuts, hexes, cams) for self-belaying on the worst sections. There are no fixed routes, no bolts, no anchors except that single ancient piton thirty meters below the summit seam, which is centuries old and rusted and should be treated as a historical artifact rather than reliable protection.
- **The scree fields** will be ankle-breaking terrain on steep grades. Gaiters. Stiff-soled boots with good ankle support. Trekking poles for the lower approaches.
- **The spiral seam** at the top is barely shoulder-width. You will need to remove any large pack and push it through separately, or drag it behind you on a cord. The passage is a meter deep through restructured stone that is uncomfortably warm to the touch. Wear gloves for the passage—light, heat-resistant gloves. The stone hums at a subsonic frequency you'll feel in your teeth and behind your eyes; this is unpleasant but apparently not harmful based on the evidence of prior visitors.

Plan your load ruthlessly. Every kilogram you carry is a kilogram you haul up a technically demanding climb in thin, hot air. You need oxygen, water, minimal food, climbing hardware, scientific instruments (more on this below), and little else.

## Scientific and Observational Equipment

You are visiting what may be the most significant anomalous artifact accessible in this branch of the multiverse. The instinct to document everything is correct. But understand your limitations: the phenomena in this room may not be fully capturable by instruments designed for conventional physics. Bring what you can. Record what you can. Accept that some of what you observe may only be storable in the deeply unreliable medium of human memory.

### Baseline Instruments

- **A reliable mechanical watch or chronometer.** Not digital—you don't know what the room's emissions might do to electronics. Bring a mechanical backup for every digital device you carry. Time your visit. Note when events occur (pool dimples, phantom water drops, lattice reconfigurations). Even if the intervals seem random, recording them might reveal patterns over multiple visits or when correlated with other data.

- **A compass, both magnetic and gyroscopic.** The lattice operates in spatial geometries that may not correspond to three dimensions. If the room is warping local space, a compass might show anomalies—precession, drift, inconsistent readings between the magnetic and gyroscopic instruments. This is both a data source and a safety tool: if your gyroscopic compass starts disagreeing with your magnetic compass by more than a few degrees, local spacetime may be non-Euclidean in ways that could affect your ability to navigate back out through the spiral passage.

- **A thermometer with fine resolution, capable of reading to 0.1°C or better.** Map the thermal environment. The lattice emits heat that feels "granular, pixelated"—see if the thermometer confirms this as actual temperature fluctuation rather than a perceptual artifact. Take readings at multiple distances from the lattice, from the pool, from the weightless stone. See if the ambient temperature inside the room is uniform or if there are gradients that correspond to the three objects.

- **A small precision scale, mechanical.** For the weightless stone. Confirm that it truly registers zero. Then: does it have inertial mass? Push it gently. Does it resist acceleration in proportion to its apparent volume and density, as a normal basalt rock of that size would? If it has inertial mass but no gravitational mass, that is a profound physical finding. If it has neither, that is a different and possibly more alarming finding.

- **A hand lens or jeweler's loupe.** For examining the restructured stone surfaces, the crystal lattice, the floor material, the condensation patterns. Molecular-level phenomena may not be visible at this resolution, but gross crystal structure might be.

- **A small sample container with inert lining (glass or PTFE).** If you can bring yourself to take a sample of the dark liquid in the floor pool—and I'm not sure you should, for reasons I'll discuss—you'll want to contain it without it contacting reactive materials. Bring a separate container for condensation water from the walls, which is probably safer to sample and could be analyzed for unusual dissolved minerals or compounds.

### Recording Devices

- **A film camera, manual, fully mechanical.** No batteries, no electronics. If the room disrupts electronic devices, a mechanical camera will still function. Bring high-speed black-and-white film—the interior lighting is very low (dim light filtered through semi-translucent stone, concentrated by the water lens), and you'll need film that can handle long exposures. A small, stable tripod that can sit on the concave floor. Photograph everything. Multiple exposures of the lattice from the same position, minutes apart, to capture its reconfiguration.

- **A digital camera and/or video recorder as backup**, understanding it might malfunction. If it works, the data density is worth having. If the floor's iridescence truly shifts with attention rather than viewing angle, video from a fixed, unattended camera might show a static surface—which would confirm the effect is observer-dependent rather than objective. This is an important experiment.

- **A notebook and pencil.** Graphite on paper. The oldest recording technology. Write down everything. Your impressions, your timeline, your instrument readings, your emotional state. The room may affect cognition—the lattice demonstrably derails attention, directing your gaze to the water lens when you try to perceive its non-three-dimensional aspects. Record when this happens. Note what you were looking at, what you were thinking about, and where your attention was redirected to. This is data about how the room interacts with observers.

- **A voice recorder, mechanical if possible (spring-wound dictaphone).** For capturing the phantom water-drop sound and the subsonic hum, if they register on equipment. Also for narrating your observations in real-time, which is faster than writing and lets you keep your eyes on what you're describing.

### Specialized Instruments (If Available)

- **A Geiger counter or dosimeter.** The restructured stone, the lattice, and especially the dark liquid pool—you want to know if any of them are radioactive. The "hunger" with which the pool absorbs light is concerning; verify it's not also emitting ionizing radiation.

- **An infrasound detector or very-low-frequency microphone.** The subsonic hum is below hearing but felt in the body. Characterize its frequency and amplitude. Is it constant? Does it modulate when the lattice reconfigures? Does it change when you're in the room versus when an unattended instrument records it?

- **A magnetometer.** The lattice is crystalline and warm. Ferromagnetic or piezoelectric effects are plausible. Map the local magnetic field inside the room and look for anomalies centered on the three objects.

- **A small, bright light source (chemical lightstick, not battery-powered).** Shine it at the pool surface. The description says the liquid doesn't reflect. Test this. Does it absorb all wavelengths equally? Is the absorption total or merely very high? Hold the light at different angles. If it truly reflects *nothing*, that's a surface with effectively zero albedo across the visible spectrum, which is itself an extraordinary material property.

## Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

This is the section that matters most, and the one that no amount of gear can substitute for.

### The Attention Effects

The room contains at least two phenomena that directly interact with human cognition:

1. **The floor's attention-dependent iridescence.** The surface appears different in direct versus peripheral vision, and the shift correlates with attention, not angle. This means the room is either (a) exploiting a quirk of human visual processing (peripheral vision uses different retinal cells and different neural pathways than foveal vision, and it's possible a material could look different to rods versus cones in a way that *seems* attention-dependent but is really just optical), or (b) the material is actually responding to observation in some way that implies the room can detect where you're looking. Option (a) is unsettling. Option (b) is much more unsettling. You cannot currently distinguish between them without controlled experiments—hence the fixed-camera test described above.

2. **The lattice's attention-derailing effect.** When you try to perceive the non-three-dimensional aspects of the lattice geometry, your attention is redirected. Your gaze slides to the water lens. Your "train of thought is neatly derailed." This is described as something that happens *to you*, not something you choose. It may be a perceptual defense mechanism—your visual cortex encountering geometry it cannot process and defaulting to a more parseable stimulus—or it may be something the lattice actively does. Either way, it means that sustained observation of the lattice's most anomalous properties may be difficult or impossible for an unassisted human observer.

**Preparation:** Practice sustained attention and meditative focus before your visit. Not because meditation will let you overpower whatever the lattice does—it probably won't—but because a disciplined mind will *notice* when it's being redirected. You want to be able to say "my attention just jumped from the lattice to the water lens and I didn't choose that" rather than simply experiencing the redirect seamlessly and never noticing it happened. Metacognitive awareness—thinking about your own thinking—is your best tool here.

Consider bringing a companion, if one is available and willing. Two observers can watch each other. "You were staring at the lattice and then you went blank for about four seconds and then you were looking at the ceiling" is the kind of report that a solo visitor will never be able to generate about themselves.

### The Isolation and the Quiet

The description emphasizes the curated quality of the silence—not absence of sound but the specific removal of biological frequencies. Everything you hear is geological or atmospheric or anomalous. There are no sounds of life. You will be the only living thing on an entire mountain, possibly in an entire mountain range, possibly for a large radius in all directions. The landscape is pre-biological. Not post-apocalyptic, not abandoned—*pre-life*. Nothing has ever lived here.

This is a flavor of loneliness that most humans never experience. Even in Earth deserts, there are insects, bacteria, the residue of biological processes in the soil. Here, you're standing on rock that has never been touched by anything alive. The psychological weight of this can be significant. Some travelers report a feeling of *illegitimacy*—a sense that they are the intrusion, that their biological processes (breathing, sweating, shedding skin cells, exhaling CO₂) are a form of contamination in a sterile space. This feeling is not entirely irrational. You *are* introducing biological material into a pre-biotic environment. Whether this matters depends on factors you can't assess.

**Preparation:** Spend time, before your visit, in the most lifeless environments you can access. High deserts. Volcanic fields. Cleanrooms, even. Acclimate yourself to the absence of biological company. Notice how it feels. Notice what your mind does to fill the void—the phantom sounds, the pattern-matching in random textures, the low-grade unease. These responses will be amplified at the summit of Eth-Kalaam. Know them in advance so they don't surprise you.

### The Objects and the Temptation to Interact

You will want to touch things. This is human. You will want to put your hand in the dark pool. You will want to touch the lattice rods. You will want to pick up the weightless stone.

**The pool:** Don't. Not initially. Not until you've observed it extensively, taken readings, tested it with a light source, and assessed it for radiation and other emissions. The liquid is described as "hungry" for light. If it absorbs electromagnetic radiation that aggressively, it may absorb other forms of energy—thermal, chemical, biological. Putting your hand in a substance that demonstrably violates normal material properties, with no understanding of its nature, in a room with no medical support at the top of an inaccessible mountain, is a risk with no bounded upside and potentially unbounded downside. If you eventually decide to interact with it, use a probe first. A wooden stick. A strip of cloth. See what happens to them.

**The lattice:** The description says it's warm, that its radiant heat has a granular texture your skin can distinguish. This implies it's safe to be near and possibly safe to touch, or at least that previous visitors' proximity to it didn't cause observable harm. But the rods are sliding through their intersection points, reconfiguring over time. If you're touching a rod when it moves, what happens? Does it move through your finger? Around it? Does it stop? You don't know the answer. Approach with caution. Proximity first. Then brief contact with a non-vital body part (back of the hand, not fingertips—you need your fingertips for climbing down). Note what you feel.

**The weightless stone:** This one I think you can and should pick up. The description implies it has been picked up before—someone at some point determined that it "can be lifted with a breath" and "can be set down and it stays." This is empirical data from a prior visitor. Lift it carefully. Note its inertial behavior. Push it gently and see if it resists. Set it down in a different location and see if it returns to its original position (which would imply it's tethered to a specific point) or stays where you put it (which would imply its gravity-exemption is a property of the object, not the location). **Put it back when you're done.** The three objects may be positioned deliberately relative to each other. Rearranging the room is not your prerogative.

## Ethical and Existential Preparation

### The Question of Removal

You will think about taking something from this room. A sample of the dark liquid. A chip of the restructured wall. The weightless stone itself—imagine, you'll think, what could be learned from it in a proper laboratory.

I advise strongly against removing anything. Here is why:

The room is a *system*. Three objects in a specific spatial relationship within an engineered environment. The water lens collects and concentrates light. The pool absorbs light. The lattice emits heat and reconfigures in higher-dimensional geometries. The weightless stone sits at zero gravity. These may be interconnected—a circuit, a process, a computation. Removing a component may disrupt it. You don't know what "disrupting it" means. The room has been running—if it's running—for at minimum centuries (the age of the piton) and possibly far longer. Whatever it's doing, it's been doing it for a very long time. The arrogance of interrupting a process you don't understand, built by an intelligence you can't identify, operating on principles you can't replicate, is the kind of arrogance that features prominently in cautionary myths across every culture for a reason.

If you take the weightless stone and its gravity exemption is tied to the room, it may become suddenly and violently a normal rock the moment it crosses the threshold. If the exemption is intrinsic to the stone, you've removed it from a system that may need it. Either way, you've changed something that you cannot undo.

Document. Measure. Record. Leave it alone.

### The Question of Purpose

You will sit in that room—assuming you make it there, assuming the air is breathable enough, assuming the objects don't react adversely to your presence—and you will wonder what it's *for*. This is the deepest preparation, and it's the preparation that can't be completed in advance.

Consider these possibilities beforehand so they don't overwhelm you in the moment:

- **It's a laboratory.** Someone is running an experiment. The three objects are apparatus. The room is a controlled environment. The experiment is ongoing. You are an uncontrolled variable that has just introduced itself into the setup. In this case, the most responsible thing is to disturb as little as possible and leave.

- **It's a reliquary.** The name is in the title. These objects are preserved here because they are significant—religiously, historically, scientifically. The room is a shrine or archive. The molecular restructuring of the mountain is the most dramatic display case ever built. In this case, you're a visitor to a museum, and the appropriate behavior is to observe respectfully and not touch the exhibits.

- **It's a signal.** The room is meant to be found. The perceptible anomalies—the floor that responds to attention, the lattice that redirects your gaze, the stone that defies gravity—are meant to demonstrate something to whoever finds them. A proof of concept. A calling card. A test. In this case, your response to the room may matter in ways you can't anticipate.

- **It's a tomb.** Someone or something is buried here, preserved here, *contained* here, and the three objects are grave goods or containment mechanisms. The sealed chamber, the self-sustaining environmental controls, the staggering remoteness—these are consistent with something that was meant to be left alone. In this case, you are trespassing, and the appropriate behavior is to leave quickly and quietly.

- **It's a machine that has finished its work.** The room did what it was built to do, long ago, and now it's inert. The ongoing phenomena—the lattice reconfiguring, the pool dimpling, the phantom water sound—are residual processes, echoes, the machine cooling down over geological time. In this case, it's safe but melancholy, a ruin that hasn't realized it's a ruin.

- **It's a machine that hasn't started yet.** The phenomena are a warmup. A boot sequence. The lattice is searching for a configuration. The pool is waiting for something to fall in. The room is loaded and cocked and the trigger hasn't been pulled, and you are now standing inside a gun. In this case—

You see the problem. You cannot know. You can only observe, record, and make your choices with as much care and humility as you can bring to bear.

## Logistics and Timeline

### Suggested Itinerary

**Days 1–3: Approach and base camp.** Transit to the lowlands. Establish a camp at the base of the Eth-Kalaam massif. Cache water and supplies. Study the mountain. Identify your route up.

**Days 4–6: Intermediate camps and acclimatization.** Climb to moderate elevation. Establish a camp. Sleep there. Climb higher the next day, then return to sleep at the lower camp. Classic altitude acclimatization protocol, adapted for a hot-altitude environment. Hydrate aggressively. Monitor your blood oxygen.

**Days 7–8: High camp and summit push.** Establish your highest camp as close to the summit as terrain allows—below the final technical section but high enough that you can reach the seam in a single push. Rest. Eat. Hydrate. Check all your instruments. Go over your plan for the room: what you'll observe first, what instruments you'll deploy, how long you'll stay, what conditions will trigger your departure.

**Day 9: The room.** Climb to the seam. Enter. Observe. Record. Think. Feel the hum in your molars. Watch the light crawl across the floor. Listen for the sound of water that doesn't fall.

Stay no longer than three hours on your first visit. The combination of altitude, heat, thin air (even if the room's atmosphere is somewhat enriched), and cognitive strangeness will erode your judgment faster than you expect. Set an alarm on your mechanical watch. When it sounds, leave. Even if you feel fine. *Especially* if you feel fine, because feeling fine at extreme altitude in a room full of anomalous phenomena should itself be treated as anomalous.

**Day 10: Descent.** Get off the mountain. Return to base camp. Consolidate your notes while the experience is fresh. Eat something. Drink water until you feel waterlogged. Sleep.

**Day 11+: Processing.** Go through your recordings. Compare your notes to your photographs. Check your instruments for residual anomalies (is your compass still reading true? Is your film undamaged? Did your digital recorder capture anything?). Write your full account.

If the experience and your findings warrant it—and they almost certainly will—plan a return visit. Second visits are calmer. You know the route. You know what the room feels like. You can spend more time, bring more specific instruments, test hypotheses that your first visit generated.

## Final Considerations

### What to Leave Behind

Your ego. Your certainty about what is and isn't possible. Your assumption that you will understand what you find. The room on the Spine of Eth-Kalaam has been there longer than you have been alive, doing something you can't identify, built by someone you can't name, for reasons you can't determine. You are not there to conquer it or solve it or claim it. You are there to witness it, as others have witnessed it before you, leaving nothing but the faint wear of your palms on the passage walls.

### What to Bring Back

Data. Questions. A profound and possibly permanent unsettlement about the nature of matter, gravity, space, and the attention of the observer. The memory of a room that a mountain was made to become. The sound of water that never falls.

And—if you're honest with yourself—the growing suspicion that the room was aware of you the entire time, not because it did anything to indicate awareness, but because the quality of its silence changed while you were there, in a way you can feel but cannot prove, like the difference between an empty room and a room with someone in it who hasn't yet spoken.

That feeling will follow you home. Prepare for that, too.
