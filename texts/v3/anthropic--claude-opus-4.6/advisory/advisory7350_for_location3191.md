# Preparing for the Cistern of Hundred Hands

---

## The Fundamental Reorientation

Before you pack a single item, you need to understand what you're walking into, because most of your instincts about underground exploration will be wrong here, and the ones that are right will be right for the wrong reasons.

This is not a cave. Caves have a logic of subtraction — water or wind or time carved something away, and you move through what was removed. This is the opposite. You are moving through what remains *between* additions. Every passage exists only because no one has yet filled it. The navigational logic is therefore not "follow the passage" but "find the gap." Your preparation needs to account for the fact that the traversable space is a negative shape, irregular, subject to change if someone adds a wall or a shelf or a column in the time between your map being drawn and your arrival. No map of this place stays accurate. Prepare to navigate without one.

You also need to sit with the psychological reality of where you're going. This is a space that has been continuously built — not maintained, *built*, actively added to — for a period long enough that the oldest layers are indistinguishable from geology. You will be inside the accumulated output of what appears to be a compulsion, or a devotion, or something that blurs the line between the two. The builders are not present but are recent. You are entering someone's ongoing, living project without invitation. The place may accept everything, but you should not mistake architectural tolerance for social welcome. Prepare yourself to be a guest in a space whose host you may never meet, whose rules you must infer from evidence, and whose single discernible law — nothing is taken out, everything is added — you must decide in advance whether you are willing to obey.

## Physical Conditioning and Body Preparation

**Flexibility and compression tolerance.** The passages are gaps between walls built by different people in different centuries. They were not designed as passages. Some will require you to turn sideways. Some will require you to compress your chest to move between surfaces that were never meant to admit a human body. This is not the same as caving through natural squeezes — the surfaces are flat, angular, sometimes studded with the ends of pegs or brackets or the edges of shelves. You will be pressing your body against worked stone and brick, against plaster that may crumble under pressure, against timber beams that are held in place by the compression of their neighbors. You need to be able to make yourself narrow without pushing hard against anything, because pushing hard against anything here risks disturbing structural relationships that have been in equilibrium for decades or centuries.

Practice lateral movement in confined spaces. Practice breathing shallowly with your chest compressed. Practice moving slowly and deliberately through narrow gaps without bracing against the walls. If you are broad-shouldered or barrel-chested, accept now that some chambers will be inaccessible to you, and do not force it.

**Heat acclimatization.** The stone is warm. Not dangerously warm, but persistently, unrelentingly warm in the way that only massive thermal reservoirs can be. The air is heavy and close. You will be breathing warm, humid, chalky air through what feels like a wool blanket. Your body's primary cooling mechanism — sweating and evaporation — will be partially compromised by the humidity. You will not overheat dangerously, but you will be uncomfortable in a way that accumulates, that builds over hours into a thick, muzzy fatigue if you aren't prepared.

Spend time in saunas or steam rooms before your visit. Not for the cardiovascular benefits, but to learn what your body does when it can't cool itself efficiently. Learn to recognize the early signs of heat-related cognitive impairment in yourself — the point where your decision-making starts to soften, where you become more passive, more agreeable, more willing to just sit down and stay. This matters in the Cistern, because the Cistern *wants* you to sit down and stay. Not maliciously. It just accepts everything, and if you let heat-fatigue make you compliant, you may find yourself adding a tile to a wall and losing track of time in a way that has nothing to do with magic and everything to do with thermodynamics and human neurology.

**Respiratory preparation.** The air contains chalk dust, mineral particulates, bird dander, dried fecal matter from birds and lizards and beetles, fungal spores from the moss and from whatever grows in the damper corners you can't see, and the ammoniac breakdown products of accumulated biological waste. None of this is likely to be acutely dangerous, but all of it will irritate mucous membranes and lungs over a prolonged visit.

If you have any respiratory sensitivities, this needs to be taken seriously. Even if you don't, the combination of warm, humid, particulate-laden air in an enclosed space with limited ventilation is a recipe for progressive bronchial irritation. You won't notice it while you're inside — the air will feel thick but breathable, and the warmth will mask the irritation. You'll notice it when you come out, when you cough for hours and produce sputum the color of old plaster.

Consider a light respirator mask, but understand the tradeoff: it will be hot, it will make breathing in the already-close air feel more labored, and it will place a barrier between you and the sensory experience of the place. The Cistern is a place you experience in part through breathing — the chalk taste, the mineral smell, the warmth of exhaled air reflected back at you from close walls. A mask removes that layer. I would recommend brief visits without a mask and longer explorations with one, but this is your judgment to make.

**Knee and joint preparation.** The floors are uneven brick, relaid in patches, with height differentials between sections that will catch your feet. You will be crouching, kneeling, stepping over low walls, ducking under beams. Some passages will require you to crawl. The surfaces are hard, angular, and sometimes slick with condensation or the thin biological film that grows where moisture gathers. Bring knee pads — low-profile ones that won't add bulk to your silhouette, because every inch of body width matters in the narrow passages. Break them in beforehand. Stiff new knee pads will alter your gait and make you clumsy in exactly the places where clumsiness is most costly.

## Clothing and Personal Equipment

**Clothing philosophy: compressible, smooth, sacrificial.** You need to think of your clothing as an interface layer between your body and a space that was not designed for bodies. It should be:

- **Tight-fitting** but not restrictive. Nothing loose that can catch on pegs, brackets, shelf edges, or the countless small projections that populate every surface. A snag in this space doesn't just tear your clothing — it potentially disturbs an arrangement, a structural relationship, an offering. The damage you do by catching your sleeve on a peg and pulling out a shelf bracket is not repairable. You cannot put it back the way it was. You don't know how it was.

- **Smooth-surfaced.** For the same reason. Ripstop nylon or similar synthetic, not cotton, not wool, nothing with texture that grabs at rough stone or plaster. You want to slide past surfaces, not grip them.

- **Warm-weather weight, full coverage.** You need to cover your skin because of the bird droppings, the biological films on wet surfaces, the possibility of histoplasmosis from accumulated bird and bat guano (even if bats aren't mentioned, spaces like this often harbor them in the deepest chambers). But the material must be light enough that you don't overheat. This is a significant tension. Resolve it by accepting that you will be warm and managing it through hydration and pacing rather than ventilation.

- **Sacrificial.** You will emerge with chalk dust ground into every fiber, with plaster smears, with the smell of warm stone and ammonia embedded in the weave. Bring clothes you are willing to discard. Not because the Cistern demands it as tribute, but because practically, you will not get them clean, and the smell will not leave.

**Head protection.** A low-profile climbing helmet, the kind without a brim or projecting headlamp mount. The ceilings are low and irregular, with beams and stone slabs at varying heights, and additional support pillars jutting from unexpected angles. You will hit your head. It's not a question of if. The helmet should be snug enough not to shift when you tilt your head to move through an angled passage. Practice wearing it in confined spaces to know how it changes your sense of your own height — in a space where ceilings are "perhaps twice your height," the margin between standing and impact is smaller than you think, especially when the ceiling has things hanging from it.

**Lighting.** This requires careful thought, because the Cistern has its own light, and your relationship to it is part of the experience.

The natural light enters through gaps and ventilation shafts and translucent stone panels. It is beautiful, varied, and deeply insufficient for navigation. Some chambers are bright enough to see chisel marks and feather barbules; others are twilight where lizard eyes are the brightest points. Your pupils will be in constant adjustment. The experience of moving between light states is part of what makes the Cistern feel alive — the alternation between revelation and obscurity, between seeing every detail and navigating by feel and sound.

Bringing a powerful headlamp and flooding the chambers with white light would be a kind of violence. Not to the place — the place accepts everything — but to your own experience of it. You would see more and perceive less. The birds would be disturbed. The lizards, adapted to half-light, would retreat. The translucent webs would disappear in the wash of direct illumination. You would turn the Cistern into a well-lit basement, which it is not.

Bring a small, dimmable light source — warm-toned, low-intensity, something you can hold in your hand and direct precisely. A candle is tempting for the warmth of its light but dangerous in a space full of dry timber, bird nests, spider webs, and the accumulated organic matter of centuries. A dim LED with a warm filter is the practical compromise. Use it sparingly. Use it to check your footing in dark passages, to examine a specific niche or offering, and then turn it off and let your eyes re-adapt. Let the Cistern show you what it shows you at the brightness it chooses.

Bring a secondary, brighter light for emergencies — a route-finding crisis, a structural concern, a fall — but clip it away where you won't reach for it out of habit.

**Footwear.** Flat-soled, thin, flexible. You need to feel the floor through your feet because you cannot always see it. The uneven brick surface will communicate a great deal of information through your soles if you let it — the transition between dry and damp sections, the slight give of a brick that has shifted, the edge of a relaid patch where the floor height changes. Approach shoes with a thin, sticky rubber sole are ideal. Not hiking boots — too rigid, too thick, too much ankle bulk for passages where your feet need to fit precisely. Not sandals or bare feet — the biological contamination risk is real, and a cut on your foot in this environment invites infection.

**Gloves.** Bring thin, close-fitting gloves — nitrile or similar — for the chambers where you need to touch surfaces that are biologically active (wet walls, areas near nests, the overflowed basins with larvae). Do not wear them constantly. The tactile experience of the Cistern is irreplaceable: the warmth of the stone, the powdery give of old plaster, the surprising smoothness of a beam that has been handled by thousands of hands over centuries. You will want to touch the walls. Everyone who enters this place wants to touch the walls. The walls have been touched so many times that they are, in places, polished by contact. Add your touch to the record. But know when to protect your hands and when to bare them.

## Navigation and Orientation

**You will get disoriented.** Accept this as a certainty and prepare for it rather than against it.

The Cistern has no center, no main axis, no organizing principle that your spatial reasoning can latch onto. It grew by accretion, chamber added to chamber, wall built against wall, with no master plan. The passages between chambers are not corridors — they are accidents of adjacency, gaps left where two construction campaigns didn't quite meet. They branch, dead-end, loop back, and occasionally connect to chambers that seem to belong to an entirely different construction tradition, as if you've stepped through a wall into a neighboring building that happens to share masonry with this one.

Your internal compass will be useless underground. The lack of a visible horizon, the warm sameness of the air, the all-surrounding bird noise that provides no directional cues — all of these conspire to strip you of spatial orientation within the first few turns.

**Breadcrumb strategy, modified for the Law of Addition.** In a normal underground space, you might mark your path — chalk arrows, string, reflective tags. In the Cistern, this is complicated by the fundamental rule: nothing is taken out, everything is added. If you leave markers, they become part of the Cistern. This is fine on the way in — you're adding trail markers, which the space will accept. The problem is on the way out: can you remove them? Technically, removing your markers would violate the only discernible law of the place. Practically, you need to find your way out.

My recommendation: use markers that function as additions. Small, smooth stones carried from outside, placed at passage junctions on the left side of the direction you came from. They become part of the space. They are offerings of a sort — humble ones, but consistent with the pattern of placed objects you'll see everywhere. On your return, you navigate *by* them but don't *remove* them. You leave your trail in the Cistern's body like everyone else has left theirs. Future visitors may find them, wonder about them, incorporate them into a niche arrangement. This is how the place works.

**Time tracking.** Bring a watch, and check it more often than feels necessary. The Cistern has a temporal quality that resists clocks. The constant warmth, the steady sound, the even light (where there is light) or even darkness (where there isn't) — nothing changes to mark the passage of time. The birds sing continuously. The dripping is arrhythmic and provides no pulse to count by. The structural groaning is almost tidal, but its period is irregular. You will lose time in this place. Not supernaturally — just humanly, the way you lose time in any absorbing, sensorially consistent environment. An hour will feel like twenty minutes. Set alarms for your turnaround time and obey them without negotiation.

## Hydration and Sustenance

**Water.** Bring your own. Yes, the Cistern produces water — the drip-catches, the condensation basins, the thin films on the brick floors. The water tastes of chalk and metal, which is the taste of the walls dissolved. In an emergency, it would sustain you. But it has passed through centuries of layered masonry, through mortar of unknown composition, through plaster that may contain lead or arsite-based pigments, through biological films of bird droppings and mold. It has washed over beetle carapaces and lizard skins and the accumulated organic decay of everything that has lived and died in this space. Drink it only in extremity.

Bring water in a container that seals completely and won't leak if you turn sideways, crouch, crawl, or stumble on uneven brick. A hydration bladder with a bite valve, worn close to your body under your outer layer, is ideal. Rigid bottles will catch on projections and add bulk you can't afford. Drink regularly even when you don't feel thirsty — the warm, humid air suppresses the sensation of thirst while your body continues to lose water through respiration and the low-grade sweating that the humidity prevents from evaporating.

**Food.** Eat before you go in. Bring a small emergency supply — dense, calorie-rich, sealed, odorless — but do not eat in the Cistern if you can avoid it. The smell of food in a closed space full of wildlife will change the behavior of the animals, and the animals are a critical part of the experience. More importantly, crumbs and food residue would become part of the Cistern — added, absorbed, never removed. Every morsel you drop becomes a site for beetle activity, a resource that alters the ecology of the chamber. Your presence is already a perturbation; minimize its chemical footprint.

## Behavioral Preparation: The Animals

The animals are not incidental. They are co-inhabitants, and in some sense, they are co-builders. The birds construct mud nests that become structural features. The spiders lay silk in layers that echo the architectural principle of the place. The beetles process organic matter. The lizards regulate insect populations. The moss and the larvae and the moths are all participants in a system that has reached a complex, layered equilibrium. You are a visitor to their home as much as to the built space.

**Birds.** The small brown birds — wren-swallow intermediates — are loud, fast, and accustomed to the enclosed space in ways you are not. They will fly close to your face. They will fly *at* your face, not aggressively but because their flight paths through the chambers are established routes through the gaps and shafts, and you are standing in one. Do not swat, duck violently, or flail. A sudden movement in a chamber where everything is packed tight — shelves loaded with fragile arrangements, webs spanning gaps, ceramic tiles covering every surface — risks more destruction than a bird brushing your cheek.

The birds will scold you. Particularly near nests, the alarm trill is startling and, in the tile-covered chambers, physically uncomfortable — the acoustic amplification can push it to pain thresholds. If a bird is scolding, you are too close to its nest. Move away. Not because the bird can harm you, but because its distress will cascade — other birds in adjacent chambers will pick up the alarm, the general chorus will shift from its ambient chattering to a sharper, more agitated register, and the entire acoustic character of the Cistern will change around you. You will have introduced something discordant. The sound may take an hour to settle.

Bring earplugs — not to wear constantly, because the sound is essential to the experience, but to have available for the tile chambers where amplification can genuinely damage hearing.

**Lizards.** They are languid, warm, and incurious about you until they're not. The one that drops from the ceiling onto your shoulder is not attacking — it's using you as a transit surface, the same way it uses beams and shelves. Their feet are dry and light and warm and the contact will be brief. If you can avoid flinching, avoid it. If you can't, flinch small.

Do not handle the lizards. Their skin is adapted to their microenvironment — the specific warmth, the specific humidity, the specific mineral content of the surfaces they rest on. Your skin is different: different temperature, different oils, different pH. A handled lizard will spend energy recalibrating, cleaning itself, restoring its surface chemistry. This is a cost you don't need to impose.

**Spiders.** The webs are thick, layered, and in many cases structurally integrated with the shelving and offerings. Walking through a web is not just a personal inconvenience — it's the destruction of something that has been built up over the same timescale and with the same additive logic as the walls themselves. Web over web, silk over silk, husk over husk. These are, in a very real sense, the spiders' version of the Cistern itself: architecture as accumulated making.

Move slowly. Look before you move. Use your dim light to check for webs across passages and gaps. When you find one, go around, or go under, or wait and find another route. Do not clear webs from your path. If you unavoidably break a strand, that strand is gone, but the web's owner will repair it. Minimize the repair work you create.

**Beetles and larvae.** Stay out of the overflowed basins. The larvae are part of a food web that the lizards depend on. Your feet in the water would crush larvae, disturb the sediment, and cloud the water in a way that makes it harder for the lizards to hunt. Observe from the edge. The beetles on the floor will move around your feet; they are following moisture lines that your standing body doesn't interrupt. Let them pass.

**What you might encounter that isn't described.** The description focuses on birds, lizards, beetles, spiders, moths, and larvae. But a warm, moist, undisturbed underground space with organic inputs and limited predator access is an ecological invitation. Be prepared for: bats in the deepest chambers (their absence from the description doesn't guarantee their absence from the space); centipedes; millipedes; earwigs; various species of cockroach; possibly snakes, particularly small, nonvenomous species that follow the lizard population; fungal growths beyond the moss — potentially including species that produce spores you don't want to inhale, which is another reason for the respirator. Be alert without being anxious. Almost nothing in this ecosystem is likely to be dangerous to you. You are the largest, warmest, most disruptive thing in the space by a wide margin.

## Psychological and Emotional Preparation

This is the section that matters most, and it's the hardest to get right, because what the Cistern does to your mind is subtle and cumulative and not quite like anything else.

**The density.** You are entering a space where every surface has been worked, covered, added to, built upon. There is no raw material anywhere. No earth. No natural stone. Everything has been touched, shaped, placed, mortared, plastered, carved, pegged, lashed, or arranged by human hands. The sheer volume of *making* that is encoded in the surfaces around you is staggering — not in any one impressive object, but in the relentless, granular accretion of small acts of construction. Every brick was made and placed. Every niche was carved. Every thumbprint in every tile is the record of a specific human finger pressing into specific wet clay at a specific moment that is now lost.

This will affect you. Not immediately — at first, the density just seems cluttered, complex, interesting. But as you move deeper and spend more time, the cumulative weight of it builds. You start to feel the labor. Not as an abstraction, but as a physical sympathy — your hands begin to feel like they should be doing something, building something, contributing. The Cistern normalizes the act of making. It presents a world where every person who has ever been here has *added*, and the evidence of their addition surrounds you at every scale, and the implicit question — "What will *you* add?" — becomes louder the longer you stay.

This is the gravitational pull of the place. It's not sinister. It's not supernatural. It's just a very, very powerful environmental cue reinforced by a thousand spatial signals all saying the same thing: *make, add, contribute, leave something*. For some travelers, this is joyful — a liberating invitation to participate in something larger than yourself. For others, it's oppressive — a demand that never articulates itself as a demand but never stops implying. Know which type you are before you go in. If you are the type who feels compelled to meet expectations, to contribute when contribution seems expected, to leave no social contract unfulfilled — the Cistern will work on you. You may find yourself pressing a tile, placing a stone, arranging beetle husks without having consciously decided to do so.

**Decide your boundary in advance.** Will you add something? The Law of Addition — everything added, nothing removed — is the only discernible rule. Obeying it is participation. Violating it (by removing something) feels genuinely wrong in a way that you should trust, because you don't know enough about this system to know what the consequences of removal are. But *not* adding is different from *removing*. You can pass through and leave nothing. You can witness without contributing. This is a valid choice, and it may be the wisest one for a first visit — observe the system, understand its rhythms, and leave without perturbing it further.

But if you do choose to add, bring something. A small, simple object made by your own hands, from materials compatible with the Cistern's ecosystem. A stone you shaped. A clay tile you pressed. Something that will not introduce chemicals, plastics, or materials that the beetles and the moisture and the slow mineral processes of the space cannot eventually incorporate. The Cistern absorbs everything into its own substance. Give it something it can digest.

**The sound.** You cannot prepare for what the sound does to you over hours. The bird chorus, the dripping, the beetle-click, the lizard-snap, the structural groaning — it is not loud, but it is constant and layered and impossible to resolve into a single signal. Your auditory processing will work overtime trying to separate the sources, locate the individual birds, distinguish one drip-catch rhythm from another. This is absorbing in a way that steadily exhausts a cognitive resource you don't normally monitor. After several hours in the Cistern, you will emerge with a specific kind of tiredness — not sleepy, but drained, as if you've spent the day in a foreign language immersion program. Your ears will ring faintly. Ordinary sounds — wind, traffic, human speech — will seem thin and unreal.

Plan for this. Don't drive immediately after exiting. Don't make important decisions. Give yourself an hour in quiet open air to decompress and let your auditory system recalibrate.

**The builders.** You will not see them, but you will feel their recency. Fresh mortar. Bright-edged new offerings. The sense that someone was here hours ago. This produces a specific, uncomfortable feeling — not fear, exactly, but a heightened awareness of being in someone else's ongoing, active space. You are in the middle of someone else's sentence. You are standing in a room that someone is in the process of furnishing. The builders are not present, but their absence is temporary, and you know it.

Do not seek them out. Do not call out. Do not leave messages intended for them. You don't know who or what they are — the description is deliberately ambiguous about this — and your assumptions about their nature will shape your experience in ways that may be inaccurate. They build. They add. They tend. That's what the evidence tells you. Whether they are human, whether they are one person or many, whether they are continuous with the original builders or successors or something else entirely — these are questions the Cistern does not answer, and you should not demand that it does.

If you encounter them — if you turn a corner and someone is there, pressing a tile, placing a stone — be still. Be quiet. Observe. Let them decide whether to acknowledge you. You are in their home. The etiquette of the Cistern, insofar as it can be inferred, is the etiquette of addition: contribute or observe, but do not interrupt the work.

## Medical Preparation

**Vaccinations and prophylaxis.** Consider whether your baseline vaccinations are current, particularly tetanus (the rusty pegs and brackets are a real risk if you scrape yourself in a tight passage). If histoplasmosis is a concern in your home universe's medical framework — and it should be, given the bird droppings — consult your medical provider about prophylactic options, though for most healthy individuals, brief exposure is manageable.

**First aid kit, minimal.** Adhesive bandages, antiseptic wipes, a small tube of antibiotic ointment. The most likely injuries are abrasions from brushing against rough walls, small cuts from sharp brick edges or broken tile, and bruises from hitting your head or knees on projecting structure. Nothing dramatic. But in the warm, biologically active environment of the Cistern, even a minor wound should be cleaned and covered immediately. Infection in this space would be a complex, polymicrobial event, and you don't want to find out what grows in a cut that's been bathed in Cistern air.

**Claustrophobia assessment.** Be honest with yourself. The Cistern is not just underground — it is *dense*. The passage widths, the low ceilings, the surfaces that press in from every side, the knowledge that the way out involves navigating back through gaps that may not feel the same in the other direction — this is a claustrophobe's nightmare dressed in archaeological wonder. If you have any history of claustrophobic response, do graduated exposure work before attempting the Cistern. Start with tight spaces on the surface, with easy exits, and work your way down. If the anxiety doesn't diminish with practice, this may not be your destination. There is no shame in this. The Cistern will be there. It's not going anywhere. It is, if anything, becoming more of itself every day.

**Dehydration and electrolytes.** The warm, humid environment will drain you of water and salts faster than you realize. Bring electrolyte supplements for your water. Drink on a schedule.

## What to Bring: Summary Checklist

- Low-profile climbing helmet, well-fitted
- Thin, smooth, full-coverage clothing (sacrificial)
- Low-profile knee pads, broken in
- Flat-soled, flexible approach shoes with sticky rubber
- Thin nitrile gloves, several pairs
- Hydration bladder with bite valve, 2-3 liters, with electrolyte additive
- Emergency food, calorie-dense, odorless, sealed
- Dim warm-toned LED light, dimmable, handheld
- Bright emergency light, stowed and clipped away
- Earplugs (for tile chambers)
- Light respirator mask (for extended exploration)
- Watch with alarm function
- Small smooth navigation stones, 20-30, carried in a hip pouch
- First aid basics: adhesive bandages, antiseptic, antibiotic ointment
- Optional: one handmade object, natural materials, to add to the Cistern
- *Nothing made of plastic you can't carry out. Nothing disposable. Nothing that the Cistern's ecosystem cannot eventually absorb if you drop it.*

## What Not to Bring

- Rigid frame backpacks (too bulky for passages)
- Bright or flashing light sources
- Recording equipment that requires setup or tripods (you won't have the space; handheld, pocketable devices only if you must)
- Food with strong odors
- Synthetic materials that shed microfibers into the environment
- Anything you aren't prepared to lose (if you drop something in a deep niche, it belongs to the Cistern now)
- Expectations of comprehension

## Duration and Timing

**First visit: three to four hours maximum.** This is enough to descend, explore several chambers, begin to internalize the sensory layers, and return before heat-fatigue and auditory exhaustion compromise your judgment.

**Time of day:** If the surface conditions affect the light entering through ventilation shafts — and they almost certainly do — then late morning provides the best balance of illumination in the upper chambers. Midday sun may be too harsh through the translucent stone panels, creating glare that disrupts dark adaptation. Evening light gives the warmest tones but reduces visibility in the already-dim deeper spaces. Avoid entering at night unless you're prepared to navigate the entire space by your own light, which, as discussed, changes the experience fundamentally.

**Season:** If the surface climate has seasons, visit when the temperature differential between surface and cistern is smallest. A large differential means stronger air movement through the masonry — the "breathing" of the walls — which can be disorienting and increases the rate at which particulates are mobilized. In the cooler seasons, the Cistern's warmth will feel welcoming at first and oppressive later. In the warmer seasons, the temperature will be more consistent between inside and outside, reducing thermal shock on exit.

## On Exit

When you emerge — and you must emerge, you must obey your alarm, you must not stay for just one more chamber — the world will feel wrong. Too open. Too bright. Too quiet, despite whatever sounds the surface holds. The sky will seem impossibly distant after the low ceilings. The air will taste thin, stripped of mineral and animal.

Sit near the entrance. Drink water. Let your body recalibrate — temperature, light levels, sound processing, spatial orientation. You may feel a pull to go back in. This is normal. This is the Cistern's gravity, felt most strongly at the threshold. Acknowledge it. Do not obey it. Not today.

Check yourself for injuries you didn't notice in the dim light and the absorption of exploration. Clean any scrapes. Drink more water. Eat something — your appetite may be suppressed from the heat, but your body has been working.

And check your pockets, your pouches, your clothing. Make sure you haven't, without thinking, slipped a small stone or a beetle carapace or a chip of tile into a fold. It's easy to do. The Cistern is a place of accumulation, and the instinct to take a piece of it with you is powerful — not as theft, but as connection, as proof. Resist it. The Law is the Law. Nothing is taken out. If you find you've accidentally removed something, you have a choice: return it now, or carry the small weight of a broken rule until your next visit.

There will be a next visit. The Cistern of Hundred Hands does not need to invite you back. It simply continues, and the knowledge that it continues — that right now, as you sit in the open air and feel the wind on your face, somewhere below, a bird is singing in a tile chamber and a lizard is snapping at a moth and a drop of water is falling into a basin and someone, somewhere, is pressing fresh mortar into a joint between two stones — that knowledge is its own gravity. You'll feel it. You'll return.

Bring a stone when you do.
