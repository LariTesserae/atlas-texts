# Preparing for the Undergrowth

---

## Psychological Preparation

Before anything else — before you pack a single item or study a single chart — you need to prepare yourself for what this place will do to your mind.

The Undergrowth is not dangerous the way a volcano is dangerous or a war zone is dangerous. It is dangerous the way the open ocean is dangerous to someone who has never been out of sight of land. The scale will be the first assault. You are entering a space that is miles tall and continental in breadth. Humans are not built to process enclosed spaces of this size. Your brain will attempt to read the ceiling-mat as sky, the distant columns as mountains, the haze as atmosphere — and it will almost succeed, and the almost is where the vertigo lives. You will know, at every moment, that you are underground, that there is a world above you, that the light is wrong, that the openness is a lie told by biology. This cognitive dissonance does not fade. Veterans of the Undergrowth report that after weeks inside, the feeling of wrongness diminishes but never resolves. You simply learn to function while your spatial reasoning quietly screams.

The second psychological challenge is the dynamism. You are accustomed to landscapes that change slowly — over seasons, over years, over geological time. The Undergrowth changes while you watch. A grove of vertical shoots can rise, canopy, flower, and collapse in an afternoon. The floor beneath your feet shifts and restructures over hours. The column-trees calve. The cascades roll through. You will develop, quickly, a hypervigilance about your surroundings that is appropriate and necessary but also exhausting. You cannot relax into the terrain because the terrain is not relaxing into itself. It is still becoming. Every surface is provisional. This produces a particular kind of fatigue — not physical but ontological, the tiredness that comes from never being able to trust that what was true an hour ago is still true now.

The third challenge is the silence. Not absolute silence — there is the drip, the creak, the occasional structural collapse — but a silence that is *wrong* for the scale. A space this large should have wind. It should have weather-sounds, animal-sounds, the background hum of a functioning ecosystem at full density. Instead it has vast, bright, cold quiet, punctuated by events that come without warning and propagate through the mat like rumors. Many travelers report auditory hallucinations after the first few days — hearing voices in the drip-patterns, hearing breathing in the convection currents, hearing music in the deep groaning of a growth surge. This is normal. It is your brain attempting to fill an acoustic void that it finds intolerable. Acknowledge it and move on.

**Practical steps:** Before you cross, spend time in isolation tanks. Spend time in large, quiet, enclosed spaces — aircraft hangars, empty cathedrals, deep caves. If you can arrange it, spend a week in a space with continuous artificial light and no day-night cycle, to begin adapting to the Undergrowth's permanent, sourceless illumination. Practice stillness. Practice patience. Practice the specific skill of being alert without being anxious, because you will need to sustain that state for the entire duration of your visit.

Meditative practice is not optional. Whatever tradition you follow — mindfulness, breathing exercises, mantra repetition, walking meditation — make it a daily discipline for at least three months before your crossing. The Undergrowth demands a mind that can observe rapid change without panic and endure vast emptiness without despair. You need both capacities simultaneously.

Also: journal. Bring materials for writing or recording your observations, and commit to doing so daily. The Undergrowth's shifting nature can blur your sense of time and causation. What happened yesterday may literally no longer exist as a physical feature of the landscape. Your journal becomes your anchor to continuity, your proof that you have a past in a place that seems to exist only in the present tense.

## Thermal Management

This is your most critical survival concern, and it is more insidious than it first appears.

The ambient temperature hovers around two to three degrees Celsius. That is not extreme cold by terrestrial standards — it is the temperature of a refrigerator, the temperature of a rainy November morning. It will kill you anyway, and it will do so through a mechanism that is deceptively comfortable.

The problem is that the Undergrowth *feels* warmer than it is. The light is white-gold and solar in quality. Your eyes tell your brain that you are standing in a bright, pleasant day — an overcast afternoon, the description says — and your brain adjusts your subjective experience accordingly. You will not shiver as aggressively as you should. You will not feel the urgency to insulate that the temperature demands. Meanwhile, your core temperature is dropping, steadily, degree by fraction of a degree, because the air is cold and dry and moves in slow convection currents that strip heat from exposed skin with quiet efficiency.

The dryness compounds this. Dry air conducts heat away from the body less efficiently than humid air, which initially sounds like good news — but the dryness also accelerates evaporative cooling from your respiratory tract and any exposed moisture on your skin. You will lose heat through breathing faster than you expect. And because the air is dry enough to crack your lips within hours, you will also lose moisture, which impairs your body's thermoregulatory function, which accelerates heat loss, which creates a feedback loop that becomes dangerous before it becomes obvious.

**Clothing strategy:** Layer aggressively, with a focus on insulation and moisture management rather than wind or water protection. There is no significant wind in the Undergrowth — the convection currents are slow and vast, more like tides than breezes — and precipitation, such as it is, comes as condensation drip from the plant surfaces, not as rain. You do not need a hardshell. You need warmth and breathability.

*Base layer:* Fine merino wool or a high-quality synthetic wicking fabric, full-body coverage including neck, wrists, and ankles. Two base layers are not excessive. The inner layer manages moisture from your skin; the outer base layer provides the first insulation.

*Mid layer:* A high-loft fleece or lightweight synthetic insulation jacket, with a matching pair of insulated pants. You want something that retains warmth even if it gets damp from the occasional fog bank or condensation contact. Down is risky here — the fog events can saturate your outer layers quickly, and wet down is useless. Synthetic insulation is more forgiving.

*Outer layer:* A soft-shell or light windbreaker, primarily to manage the micro-convections near your body and to serve as a barrier during fog events. You want something that dries fast when the fog dissipates. Nothing too heavy — you will be exerting yourself during mat traversal and column climbs, and overheating inside your layers is a real risk that leads to sweating, which leads to rapid cooling when you stop.

*Extremities:* This is where most travelers underestimate the need. Insulated gloves, preferably with a liner glove underneath for dexterity tasks. A warm hat that covers your ears. A neck gaiter or balaclava for sleeping and for fog events. Two pairs of insulated socks, rotated and dried daily. Your fingers and toes will be the first casualties of the chronic cold — not frostbite, not at these temperatures, but a persistent painful chill that impairs your fine motor skills and makes every task involving your hands take twice as long.

*Sleeping insulation:* Bring a sleeping bag rated to minus ten Celsius, minimum. This sounds excessive for an environment that never drops below freezing, but you will be sleeping on a surface that draws heat from your body relentlessly — the mat is not warm, it is just barely above the ambient temperature, and it has enormous thermal mass. A sleeping pad with an R-value of five or higher is essential. Two pads are better. You will be sleeping on a living surface that may flex or shift during the night, so choose pads that are forgiving of uneven surfaces — closed-cell foam for the bottom layer, inflatable for comfort on top.

*Spare everything:* The fog events will soak your outer layers without warning. You need at least one complete spare set of mid-layer clothing kept in a dry bag at all times. Losing your dry reserves in the Undergrowth would not be immediately fatal, but it would begin a slow deterioration in your thermal status that compounds over days.

## Foot Protection and Locomotion

Walking in the Undergrowth is unlike walking anywhere else, and your footwear choice will determine whether you move effectively or spend your visit nursing injuries.

The floor-mat is a dense, interlocking mass of root-structures, mycelia, and woody stems compressed into a surface that the description compares to "cold rubber over bone." In practice, this means a surface that is slightly yielding — absorbing impact — but uneven, irregular, and occasionally treacherous. The mat varies in thickness from tens of meters (safe, structural, with a silver-white or pale amber coloring) to dangerously thin patches (translucent, jellyfish-green, visible capillary networks beneath) where you might punch through into the root-void below.

You need boots that are:

**Rigid-soled enough to distribute your weight.** Soft-soled shoes will concentrate your weight on a smaller area, increasing the risk of punching through thin mat. A stiff mountaineering or approach boot with a wide, flat sole is ideal. Snowshoe-style platforms are worth considering for extended traversals across unfamiliar mat — they distribute your weight over a larger area and give you an extra fraction of a second to react if the surface begins to yield.

**Insulated.** The mat is cold. Your feet are in constant contact with it. Without insulation, the slow conductive heat loss through your soles will produce numb, clumsy feet within hours.

**High-topped and secure.** The surface is irregular. Ankle injuries on uneven terrain are the most common mobility-impairing injury in any wilderness environment, and the Undergrowth's surface is more uneven than most. A boot that supports and stabilizes the ankle is not negotiable.

**Quick-drying.** The condensation pools are shallow but ubiquitous, and the fog events will wet everything. Boots that hold water will chill your feet and promote maceration of the skin.

Bring trekking poles. Bring them even if you have never used them. On the mat, they serve double duty — balance aids on the uneven surface and probes for testing the mat's integrity ahead of you. Before every step in an unfamiliar area, push your pole into the mat. If it sinks more than a few centimeters with moderate pressure, the mat is thin. If it sinks to the shaft, you are standing next to a void. Route around it.

Carry a length of static rope — at least thirty meters — and know how to self-rescue from a partial mat breakthrough. If you punch through a thin section, you may fall only a meter or two into the upper root-network before being caught by the branching structures below, but extracting yourself from a tangle of living roots while suspended over a capillary-lit void of unknown depth is not a situation you want to improvise.

## Hydration and Nutrition

Water in the Undergrowth exists but cannot be relied upon.

The condensation pools are your primary water source. They form in the hollows of the floor-mat where micro-warmth from the plant surfaces meets the cold air, depositing moisture that collects in small basins — "no wider than your hand," which means you are collecting water in volumes measured in milliliters, not liters. The water is cold, clear, and faintly sweet with dissolved plant sugars. It is, to the best that any traveler has been able to determine, safe to drink, but I would still recommend filtration or purification as a precaution — the mat is a living ecosystem with an active microbiology that is not fully understood, and the burrower tunnels you may encounter are slicked with mucus of unknown composition that may seep into the water table.

The critical problem is reliability. The pools appear and disappear as the mat shifts its growth patterns. A pool that was there this morning may be gone by afternoon, absorbed by a growth surge or drained through a new channel in the root-mass. You cannot camp next to a water source and expect it to persist. You must carry water, collect water opportunistically whenever you find it, and manage your consumption with discipline.

**Carry a minimum of four liters of water with you at all times**, in containers that are insulated against the cold (water at two degrees Celsius is painful to drink in quantity, and the caloric cost of warming it in your body is significant when you are already fighting chronic heat loss). Bring a wide-mouthed collection vessel — something you can press into a shallow depression in the mat to scoop up the tiny pools. A flexible silicone cup or a broad, flat scoop works better than a narrow-mouthed bottle. Filter through a personal water filter before storage.

**During fog events**, you can collect significant quantities of moisture by spreading a clean cloth or tarp and wringing out the condensation. The fog deposits a fine, cold film on every surface. A square meter of collection fabric in a dense fog event can yield a meaningful amount of water over ten to fifteen minutes. This is your best opportunity for bulk water collection — be ready for it. Keep your collection gear accessible at all times.

**Nutrition** is a harder problem. The Undergrowth is a plant-dominated ecosystem, and the plants are broadly unfamiliar in their biochemistry. The description mentions caches of dried plant-matter left by the builders — potentially food — but you have no way of knowing what is safe for human consumption without extensive testing. The dissolved sugars in the condensation pools suggest that at least some of the plant compounds are digestible, but "digestible" and "nutritious" are not the same thing, and "not immediately toxic" is an even lower bar.

**Bring all of your own food.** Calculate your caloric needs generously — the chronic cold will elevate your basal metabolic rate by fifteen to twenty percent as your body works to maintain core temperature, and the physical demands of mat traversal on uneven terrain are significant. Plan for 3,000 to 3,500 calories per day minimum. Choose calorie-dense, non-perishable foods that require no cooking: nut butters, hard cheeses, cured meats, chocolate, energy bars, dried fruit, olive oil (add it to everything for extra calories). Avoid foods that require significant water to prepare or digest.

Regarding the crystalline flowering-structures: they are noted as being harvested by the fauna (the armored, segmented creature at the column-tree base). This suggests they contain something of biological value, but "value" to an alien organism is not the same as "value" to you. Do not eat the flowering-structures. Do not eat any part of the plants without testing, and testing here means a rigorous protocol: skin contact first, then lip contact, then tongue contact, then a tiny ingested quantity, each step separated by hours of observation for adverse reactions. This is slow, tedious, and may save your life if you exhaust your food supply.

## Light Management

This will seem paradoxical: you are entering a place that glows with nearly solar intensity, and you need to bring equipment to manage the light.

The Undergrowth's illumination is constant. There is no night. The ceiling-mat, the floor-mat, the column-trees — they all radiate continuously, and the combined effect is an ambient brightness roughly equivalent to a heavily overcast day on Earth, but *everywhere*, from every direction, without shadows, without variation, without cessation. This creates two problems:

**Sleep disruption.** Your circadian rhythm is driven by light-dark cycles. The Undergrowth has no dark cycle. Within three to four days, your sleep architecture will begin to degrade — difficulty falling asleep, fragmented sleep, loss of deep sleep stages, increasing daytime cognitive impairment. This is not a minor inconvenience; it is a serious threat to your judgment and safety in a dynamic, changing environment where poor decisions can be fatal.

*Solution:* Bring a high-quality sleep mask that blocks light completely — not the flimsy airline variety, but a contoured, padded mask that seals against your face with no light leakage. Bring a spare. Establish a rigid sleep schedule based on your watch (you are bringing a watch; the Undergrowth has no timekeeping cues of any kind) and adhere to it with discipline. Consider bringing a small quantity of melatonin supplement to reinforce your artificial sleep cycle. Build your sleeping shelter to minimize light intrusion — a small tent with opaque walls, or at minimum a bivvy with an opaque head section.

**Eye strain and potential damage.** During normal conditions, the ambient light is tolerable — bright but not painful, comparable to daylight under cloud cover. During a growth surge or cascade, the light output of affected column-trees spikes dramatically. The description says "blinding," and travelers should take this literally. A cascade in your visual field could produce light levels comparable to looking at a welding arc, sustained over minutes to hours.

*Solution:* Carry high-quality wraparound sunglasses with variable tint — photochromic lenses are ideal, automatically darkening in response to light intensity. Carry a backup pair. For cascade events, carry glacier glasses or ski goggles with category 4 lenses (the darkest commonly available) that can reduce transmitted light to less than eight percent. If you see the characteristic brightening that precedes a growth surge in a nearby column-tree, put on your dark lenses immediately. During a full cascade, you may need to navigate by feel and pole-probing with your eyes nearly shut behind your darkest lenses. Practice this before you go.

## Navigation

You are entering a space that is continental in scale, featureless across vast distances, and actively reshaping itself. Navigation in the Undergrowth is one of the most serious challenges you will face, and it requires preparation that goes far beyond carrying a compass.

**Compasses may or may not work.** The geological context of the Undergrowth is unclear — the host world's magnetic field, if it has one, may or may not be detectable through the miles of rock above you. Bring a compass, but do not rely on it. Test it immediately upon arrival. If it shows consistent orientation, use it as one tool among several. If it wanders or spins, discard it as a navigational aid.

**GPS is meaningless here.** You are underground, on a different world. There are no satellites.

**The terrain is not mappable in the traditional sense.** Features move. Growth surges reshape the floor-mat over hours. Column-trees calve and new columns begin. A landmark you noted yesterday may look different today and may not exist tomorrow. Any map you make is a snapshot of a moment, not a stable reference.

So how do you navigate?

*Column-tree recognition:* The column-trees, despite their dynamism, are the most stable large-scale features. They take years to grow, and their positions relative to each other change only slowly. Learn to identify individual column-trees by their distinctive features — bark patterns, shape, secondary growth characteristics, relative size. Name them. Sketch them. Build a mental (and written) map of column-tree positions as an anchor for your spatial orientation. This map will be approximate and will degrade over time, but it will be better than nothing.

*Triangulation by light:* Different column-trees produce slightly different light qualities — variations in color temperature, intensity, flicker patterns. With practice, you may be able to identify the direction to a known column-tree by the quality of its contribution to the ambient light, even when it is beyond visual range in the haze. This is an inexact skill and develops slowly.

*Trail-marking:* Carry biodegradable marking tape, chalk, or a similar material for marking your route on the mat surface. Be aware that the mat's growth may absorb or cover your marks within days. Use a marking method that creates a physical alteration — cutting a small notch, pressing a colored marker into the mat surface — rather than simply placing something on top of it. Mark frequently. Mark more frequently than you think necessary. Getting lost in the Undergrowth — in a space that extends farther than weather can travel in a day, with no landmarks, no sky, no sun position — is a genuine survival emergency.

*Dead reckoning:* Pace-counting and direction maintenance by discipline. Assign a bearing to each leg of your travel (using compass if functional, or arbitrary reference directions if not) and count your paces. Log everything. This is tedious and error-prone, but in combination with column-tree recognition and trail-marking, it gives you a triangulated position estimate that is usually good enough to retrace your steps.

*The builder marks:* The scratches on the platforms — maps, tallies, or something else — may encode navigational information. If you find them, document them meticulously. Compare marks from different platforms. Look for repeated symbols, directional indicators, spatial relationships that correspond to column-tree positions. You may be able to decode a navigational system developed by beings who have already solved the problem of moving through this space.

## Shelter

You need shelter in the Undergrowth not against weather — there is no weather in the conventional sense — but against the chronic cold, the constant light, and the unpredictable dynamics of the living terrain.

**Do not sleep on the open mat.** The mat is a living surface that shifts and grows. It is cold. It conducts heat away from your body. And, crucially, it contains burrowers — organisms of unknown disposition that move through the root-mass beneath you with weight and purpose. Sleeping directly on the mat puts you in intimate contact with a biologically active substrate that you do not fully understand.

**Platform construction:** The builders had the right idea. Elevated platforms, either on the mat surface or wedged into column-tree structures, provide insulation from the mat (dead air gap beneath the platform surface), elevation above the immediate growth dynamics of the mat, and a defensible, defined space that you can organize and maintain. You are unlikely to build platforms as effectively as the native builders — they had tools shaped from the local materials and presumably extensive knowledge of the mat's behavior — but you can improvise.

If you find an existing platform that appears structurally sound, use it. Test it thoroughly before trusting it with your full weight and your sleeping body. Check the attachment points. Check for mat encroachment — if the mat is growing over the platform's edges, it may shift or tilt the platform as it grows. If the platform is on the mat surface, check the mat thickness underneath and around it — a platform on thin mat is worse than no platform at all.

If you must build, cut sections of dead or dying plant material (not active growth — active growth is structurally unstable and may continue growing in ways that deform your structure) and lash them together using vine-fiber or your own cordage. Elevate the platform on supports if possible. Even a few inches of air gap between your sleeping surface and the mat provides meaningful thermal insulation.

**Tent or bivvy:** A small, free-standing tent with an opaque fly provides light blockage for sleep, a psychological enclosure in an overwhelmingly vast space, and a defined personal environment that helps maintain your sense of self and routine. It does not need to be waterproof — the fog events deposit moisture but not in volumes that a tent needs to shed. It does need to be *opaque*, completely, to the pervasive plant-light. It does need to be secured against the possibility of mat movement — stake it or tie it to the platform, not to the living mat, which may shift your stakes.

## Equipment for Observation and Engagement

Assuming you are visiting the Undergrowth for purposes of exploration and understanding — which is the only rational reason to go — you should prepare specific equipment for observation, documentation, and interaction with the environment.

**Binoculars or a spotting scope.** The distances in the Undergrowth are continental, and the atmospheric haze limits naked-eye resolution of distant features. A good pair of binoculars lets you observe distant column-trees, track cascade progression, spot gliders in the upper air, and monitor growth-surge patterns from a safe distance. Choose optics with large exit pupils for brightness — the ambient light is usually sufficient, but during fog events visibility drops dramatically and any optical advantage helps.

**A sound amplification device.** A parabolic microphone or even cupped-hand listening will reveal sounds that the vast space attenuates below your normal hearing threshold. The mat-vibrations from burrower movement, the deep creaking of distant growth surges, the drip patterns — these carry information about the state of the environment around you, and many of them occur below or at the edge of human hearing in the open space. A microphone and headphones let you listen to the Undergrowth's substrate communications, which may give you early warning of cascades, structural failures, or burrower activity beneath your position.

**A vibration sensor.** A simple geophone or accelerometer placed on the mat surface can detect the thrumming that precedes a cascade event, potentially hours before the visual brightening becomes apparent. This is your best early warning system. Carry one. Monitor it while you sleep.

**Sample collection materials.** Sterile containers, cutting tools, a field notebook for observations. The plant biochemistry here is unknown and likely revelatory. Collect samples of mat tissue, column-tree bark, flowering-structure crystals, condensation water, burrower mucus (if accessible), and any materials from the builder caches. Label everything with location, time, and environmental conditions.

**A camera with manual exposure control.** The light conditions in the Undergrowth are extreme and variable — from the steady solar-quality ambient illumination to the blinding output of a cascade event. Auto-exposure will be overwhelmed. Manual control lets you document the full range of light phenomena, including the focused light-beams of the flowering-structure "antennae" and the capillary networks visible through thin mat sections.

## Interaction with the Builders

You will probably not meet them. But you should prepare for the possibility.

The evidence — platforms, tools, caches, marks — indicates a sapient or near-sapient species that is actively adapting to the Undergrowth's environment. They are tool-users. They are builders. They make deliberate marks that encode information. They cache resources, which indicates planning and temporal awareness. They climb column-trees, which indicates physical capability and spatial confidence in a three-dimensional environment.

You know almost nothing else about them. You don't know their biology, their social structure, their disposition toward strangers, their sensory capabilities, or their relationship with the other fauna (the gliders, the burrowers, the armored harvesters). You don't know if they are indigenous to the host world or, like you, travelers who arrived here and stayed.

**Prepare for first contact with the assumption that communication will be difficult and slow.** Bring materials for visual communication — drawing implements, flat surfaces for sketching, objects that can serve as gifts or tokens of non-hostile intent. Food is a universal signal of goodwill, but offer your food, not theirs — taking from their caches would be an act of theft regardless of your intent.

**Do not touch their platforms, tools, or marks without clear permission or extreme necessity.** In a place this unstable, where every constructed object represents significant labor and planning, the destruction or disturbance of someone's shelter or supplies is a survival-level offense.

**Observe before you approach.** If you spot signs of recent habitation — fresh cut-marks, warm caches, newly built platforms — slow down. Set up at a respectful distance. Make yourself visible and unthreatening. Wait. Let them find you, if they choose to. In a place this vast and empty, the arrival of an unfamiliar being is an event that the inhabitants will notice and evaluate on their own terms, and your job is to make that evaluation as easy and non-threatening as possible.

**Learn from their infrastructure.** The builders have already solved many of the problems you are facing — where to find water reliably, how to build on a shifting surface, which materials are structurally sound, how to navigate the column-tree network. Their platforms encode solutions. Their climbing notches encode routes. Their caches encode knowledge about what is useful and how to store it. Study everything they've left behind with the attention it deserves. They know this place better than you ever will.

## Cascade Survival Protocol

The cascades are the most dangerous regular phenomenon in the Undergrowth, and you must have a clear protocol for surviving one before you enter the space.

**Warning signs:** A faint rhythmic thrumming in the mat, felt through the feet, accelerating. A brightening of one or more nearby column-trees — gradual at first, then rapid. Fog formation at the base of the brightening column. A sound that begins as a creak and builds to a deep, groaning, cracking chorus.

**When you detect cascade onset:**

1. **Assess distance and direction.** If the brightening column is more than several miles away and the cascade is propagating away from you, you may be safe to observe from your position. Monitor the vibration. If it intensifies, the cascade is spreading toward you.

2. **If the cascade is approaching:** Move perpendicular to the direction of propagation. You cannot outrun a cascade — it crosses hundreds of miles in days, which sounds slow until you realize that the fog front moves much faster than the structural effects and can envelop your position in hours. Moving perpendicular gives you the best chance of getting out of the cascade's path, which is typically a linear or branching chain of columns, not a uniform wavefront.

3. **If you cannot avoid the cascade:** Find the most structurally stable position available. This means the thickest, most mature section of mat (silver-white, pale amber, solid underfoot), away from column-trees (which may calve during the growth surge), away from thin mat (which may rupture under the vibration). Lie flat to minimize your profile. Cover your eyes with your darkest lenses or a cloth. Protect your ears — the structural sounds during a cascade are the loudest thing in the Undergrowth and may be at damaging volume at close range. Earplugs should be in your standard kit.

4. **Wait.** The cascade will pass. The growth surge, the fog, the brightening — all are temporary, lasting hours to days in any given area. After passage, the affected zone will be a tangle of fresh growth and fresh collapse. Movement will be difficult. Wait for the initial structural settling before attempting to traverse the affected area.

5. **After a cascade passes your position:** Check yourself for injuries. Check your equipment. Check the mat integrity around you — cascades can dramatically alter the thickness and stability of the floor-mat. Re-establish your orientation using whatever navigational references survived the event. Expect the landscape to look fundamentally different.

## Duration and Exit Planning

How long should you stay? How do you plan to leave?

The Undergrowth is compelling. Travelers report a strong desire to stay — to follow the cascades, to find the builders, to watch the column-trees grow, to map the unmappable. This desire is understandable and should be treated with suspicion. The Undergrowth is a place that erodes your sense of time. The constant light, the absence of day-night cycles, the continuous change — these create a temporal blur where days become indistinguishable and urgency fades. Travelers have stayed far longer than intended and emerged with depleted supplies, degraded health, and a disorientation that takes weeks to resolve.

**Set a hard departure date before you enter.** Write it on your body if necessary. Calculate your food and water supplies backward from that date, and when you reach the halfway point, turn around regardless of what you've found or what you want to see. The Undergrowth will still be there. It will be different when you return — fundamentally, structurally different — but it will still be there. It will always be there. It is still becoming. It is not finished and it may never be, and neither are you, and the difference is that you have a life outside this place and it does not.

**Mark your entry point** with every marking method at your disposal — physical marks on the mat, coordinates relative to the nearest column-trees, compass bearings if your compass works, photographs, written descriptions. The mat near your entry point will change during your visit. Growth will cover your marks. The column-trees will look slightly different from a different approach angle. Finding your way back to the exact crossing point is the single most important navigational task of your entire visit, and it requires paranoid levels of redundant documentation.

**If you find that you cannot locate your entry point:** Stop. Do not wander. Establish a base, conserve resources, and systematically search outward in expanding patterns, returning to your base each time. Panic and directionless movement in a space this large are fatal — not quickly, but certainly. The Undergrowth is patient. It has no malice toward you. It simply doesn't care whether you find your way home, and in its vastness, your mistake and its indifference are indistinguishable from each other.

## Final Thoughts

The Undergrowth is one of the most extraordinary locations accessible to a multiverse traveler. It is a world in the act of creating itself — not metaphorically, but literally, visibly, in real time. The scale is staggering. The biology is unprecedented. The beauty is genuine and profound — the white-gold light pouring from every surface, the cathedral columns blazing in the haze, the delicate geometry of the flowering-structures sweeping their beams across the living floor.

It is also profoundly lonely. The silence, the vast emptiness between surfaces, the cold, the absence of sky — these combine to create an isolation that is not hostile but is absolute. You are a small warm thing in a large cold space that is busy with its own becoming and has no particular interest in yours. The builders, whoever they are, have apparently made their peace with this. Their platforms are small and practical. Their marks are precise and purposeful. They are not building monuments. They are building footholds — places to stand while the world shifts beneath them.

You should aspire to the same humility. Go prepared. Go carefully. Go with the understanding that you are visiting a place that is not for you, that was not made for anyone, that is simply *happening* — a vast, luminous, ongoing event in the dark beneath a cold world's surface — and that your role is to witness it, to survive it, and to carry what you've seen back to a place where someone can understand what it means.

Bring warm socks. Bring two pairs. Bring three.

You'll need them.
