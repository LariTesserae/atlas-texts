# Preparing for the Plateau That Builds Itself

## A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before packing a single item, you need to sit with the implications of this place. Read the description again. Then a third time. Because the Plateau That Builds Itself is not a hostile environment in the way most dangerous locations are hostile. It is not trying to kill you. It is not indifferent to you in the way a blizzard or a desert is indifferent. It is something more fundamentally challenging: it is a place where the *rules themselves* are being manufactured in real time, where the ground you stand on is both literal ground and the output of a mechanical-geological process that also generates the physics governing that ground. You are not visiting a landscape. You are visiting a *process*—one that is perpetually unfinished, perpetually revising, and that treats coherence as something to be actively maintained rather than passively assumed.

This means your preparation must operate on multiple levels simultaneously. You need to be physically prepared for a shifting, warm, rocky terrain with no permanent water sources and dim perpetual light. You need to be cognitively prepared for an environment where spatial relationships, gravity, thermal gradients, and causal sequence are *mostly* reliable but occasionally skip, stutter, or run in the wrong order. And you need to be psychologically prepared for the deep existential unease of standing on a world that is, at every moment, holding itself into existence through active effort—and that sometimes, in the gaps, is not entirely there.

Let me help you with all three.

## II. Physical Preparation

### A. Footwear: The Most Critical Decision You Will Make

The stone is fresh, sharp-edged, and unweathered. Think of newly broken obsidian and fresh basalt—edges that can slice through ordinary hiking boots over the course of a day. The ground is warm, radiating heat from the mechanical processes beneath, with surface temperatures that likely vary from comfortably warm to potentially scalding in thin spots where you can see the amber-red glow from below. And the terrain is *moving*—plates tilting by degrees, ridges pushing upward, surfaces that were flat becoming angled without warning.

You need boots that address all of this simultaneously:

**Sole construction.** Thick, rigid soles with puncture-resistant plates—the kind designed for volcanic fieldwork or industrial settings with sharp debris. The rock edges here aren't just sharp; they're *fresh* sharp, the kind of sharpness that geological weathering hasn't had time to soften. Standard hiking boot soles will be shredded within hours. Look for boots with steel or composite midsole plates and Vibram-style outsoles rated for jagged terrain. The rigidity also helps with the tilting ground—a flexible sole on a surface that's changing its angle will leave your ankles doing constant compensatory work.

**Thermal insulation from below.** The ground radiates heat. In thin spots, it glows. You need soles with thermal insulation sufficient to handle extended contact with surfaces that may reach 60-80°C in the hotter zones, with occasional spikes higher near active emergence points. Firefighter-grade boots or foundry boots with heat-resistant soles rated to 300°C contact would be appropriate. You won't be standing on anything that hot for long, but you might step on it unexpectedly when a new section of stone pushes through.

**Grip on tilting surfaces.** The ground changes angle. Not violently—the description emphasizes slow, purposeful motion—but continuously. You need aggressive tread patterns that maintain grip on smooth, newly formed rock at angles up to perhaps 20-30 degrees. Approach shoes designed for scrambling on clean rock faces would be ideal for the grip pattern, but they lack the sole protection and thermal insulation you need, so you're looking for a hybrid: a heavy-duty boot with an aggressive climbing-style tread pattern.

**Ankle support.** Non-negotiable. The terrain is in constant slow motion. Your proprioceptive system is going to be working overtime, and on a multi-day visit, ankle fatigue will become a serious issue. High-top boots with rigid ankle support, laced tight.

My recommendation: custom-modified mountaineering boots with aftermarket puncture-resistant insoles and heat-reflective sole liners. Break them in thoroughly before arrival. Your feet are your primary survival tool here.

### B. Clothing

The environment is dim, perpetually warm, and humid when the fog banks roll through. There is no sun to protect against (the sky is a luminous murk with no visible source), but there is abrasive rock everywhere, radiant heat from below, and periodic drenching condensation events.

**Base layer.** Moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool. The condensation banks will soak you without warning, and you need to be dry again quickly. Cotton is absolutely forbidden. The warmth from the ground means you won't be cold, but being wet in a warm environment creates its own problems—chafing, skin maceration, fungal issues on extended stays.

**Mid layer.** Likely unnecessary given the ambient warmth, but carry a lightweight fleece or synthetic insulation layer that packs small. If the physics skips and thermal gradients run backward temporarily, you could experience localized cold. This is a contingency layer, not a primary one.

**Outer layer.** You need abrasion resistance more than weather protection. The rock is sharp, and if you fall or slide on a tilting surface, bare skin or light fabric will be lacerated. A durable, rip-stop outer layer—think canvas-weight or Cordura-style fabric—over your torso and legs. Not waterproof; you want breathability given the warmth. Water resistance from a DWR coating is sufficient for the fog banks, which are heavy mist rather than true rain.

**Hand protection.** Gloves. Always. You will instinctively reach out to steady yourself on the rock as the ground shifts, and the rock will cut you. Heavy-duty work gloves with reinforced palms—leather or synthetic, with good dexterity. Climbing gloves designed for via ferrata would be excellent. Carry a spare pair.

**Head protection.** A hard hat or climbing helmet. Not for falling objects from above—though that's not impossible given that ridges are actively pushing upward and could shed fragments—but for the simple reality that you're navigating a landscape where the ground tilts and ridges emerge unexpectedly. You will stumble. You may fall. The rock you fall toward will be sharp and fresh. Protect your skull.

**Eye protection.** The description mentions zones where light behaves wrongly—too bright, negative, present in colors you can't name. Safety glasses or goggles with variable tint would be wise. Not sunglasses (the ambient light is dim enough that dark lenses would impair vision), but clear or lightly tinted safety lenses that protect against rock fragments and provide some optical buffer if you wander near a zone of light incoherence.

### C. Hydration and Nutrition

**Water is the critical challenge.** There are no permanent water sources. Water exists in this environment as sudden condensation banks and temporary pools in stone basins that last only days before being subsumed by tectonic activity. You cannot rely on finding water, and you cannot predict when the next fog bank will roll through.

Carry as much water as you can reasonably transport. For a multi-day visit, this means either:

1. **Large reservoir capacity.** Three to four liters minimum on your person at all times, with additional cached supplies if you've established a base camp (more on camp considerations below).

2. **Active condensation collection.** The fog banks are your best water source. Carry lightweight condensation collection fabric—mesh screens or hydrophobic-coated fabric arrays that can be deployed quickly when a fog bank arrives. The fogs are dense and predictable in cause (new warm stone exposure meeting cooler air), even if unpredictable in exact timing and location. When fog comes, collect aggressively. You don't know when the next one will arrive.

3. **Temporary pool exploitation.** The pools in stone basins are a potential water source, but they need to be approached with caution. The amphibious creatures in them carry moss spores on their skin, and the water is sitting in basins of newly formed rock whose mineral composition is unknown and potentially unusual (the rock here has crystalline lattices that function as circuits and veins of magnetite that pulse with current). Filter and purify rigorously. Carry a high-quality pump filter AND chemical purification as backup. Boiling may be feasible given the warm ground—in thin spots, you might be able to heat water using the ground itself as a heat source—but don't count on it.

**Food:** The local biology is entirely alien. The glassjaws eat mineral deposits. The insects are adapted to a biochemistry that may or may not be compatible with yours. Do not eat anything native until you have had it analyzed, and even then, proceed with extreme caution. A world that generates its own physics could easily generate biochemistry that operates on principles your digestive system has never encountered. Pack all your own food. High-calorie, compact, requiring minimal water to prepare. Energy-dense nut bars, pemmican, dried fruit, jerky. Enough for your entire planned stay plus a 50% margin, because getting off this plateau on your original schedule is not guaranteed when the landscape rearranges itself.

### D. Shelter

This is complicated by the fundamental nature of the terrain.

You cannot build a permanent camp. Any location you choose will be altered by the ongoing tectonic assembly. The ground tilts, ridges push through, basins are subsumed. Your campsite might be flat now and angled at 15 degrees in six hours.

**Strategy: mobile shelter with frequent relocation.** A lightweight, free-standing tent that can be set up and struck quickly. Not staked—stakes won't hold in shifting rock, and driving stakes into stone that contains functional mechanical processes might not be wise for reasons I'll discuss later. A tent that holds its shape through pole tension alone. Geodesic dome designs are ideal.

**Ground insulation is critical.** The rock radiates heat. A thin tent floor over warm basalt will turn your shelter into an oven. Carry a closed-cell foam pad AND an insulated air mattress. The foam pad provides the thermal barrier; the air mattress provides comfort and additional insulation. In the hottest zones, you may need to layer an emergency reflective blanket beneath the foam pad, reflective side down, to bounce radiant heat back into the ground.

**Site selection criteria:**
- Choose areas where the rock appears to have recently *finished* emerging rather than actively rising. Fresh surfaces that have stabilized—look for moss beginning to colonize, which suggests the surface has been static long enough for life to find it.
- Avoid thin spots where you can see glow from below. The heat will be too intense for comfortable rest.
- Avoid the seams and cracks where vegetation is densest. Those seams exist because the rock hasn't fused; they're the most likely to shift.
- Watch the glassjaw herds. They know where the ground will be stable. If a herd has recently passed through an area without diverting, that area is likely to remain coherent for a while. If herds are consistently avoiding a zone, so should you.
- **Listen to the hum.** The mechanical vibration from below is your constant companion. Learn its rhythms. A steady, consistent hum suggests stable operation beneath your position. If the hum becomes irregular, changes pitch, or *skips* while you're camped, be ready to move immediately.

### E. Navigation and Orientation

There are no maps. Maps are meaningless here. The landscape will be different tomorrow than it is today.

**Compass:** May or may not work. The ground contains veins of magnetite that pulse with current. The local magnetic field is likely wildly variable and not aligned with any planetary poles in a useful way. A compass will spin, settle, and spin again as you cross different zones of the plateau. Do not rely on magnetic navigation.

**GPS/Satellite navigation:** Questionable. You'd need satellites in stable orbit above this world, which seems unlikely if the planet's geology is generating its own physics and those physics occasionally skip. Even if you brought your own positioning system, the ontological instability might interfere with signal coherence. Consider this unreliable.

**Inertial navigation:** A dead-reckoning system based on accelerometers and gyroscopes, independent of external references, is your best technological option. But be warned: if gravity fluctuates in certain zones—and it does—your inertial measurements will accumulate errors. Recalibrate frequently, and understand that your position fix degrades over time.

**Practical navigation strategy:** Landmark-based navigation with constant revision. The description mentions major features that persist at least on the scale of hours to days: the tall cliff line to the south that creates its own weather, the valleys that form and reform. Use the largest, most persistent features as rough directional anchors, but hold them loosely. Accept that you are navigating a moving landscape and that precision is impossible. Your goal is not to know exactly where you are but to maintain a general sense of which direction leads back toward your entry point (assuming your entry point persists—more on this in the exit strategy section).

**Mark your trail.** Not with cairns (the rock shifts too much), but with something you leave in the crevices that the vegetation has colonized. A biodegradable UV-fluorescent dye sprayed on moss beds, perhaps—something that will persist for a few days in the seams even as the surrounding rock rearranges. If you carry a UV light, you can find your marks even in the dim ambient conditions.

## III. Equipment for Ontological Instability

This is where preparation departs from conventional wilderness readiness and enters territory unique to the Plateau.

### A. Documenting the Skips

The physics here *skips*. Objects relocate. Water disappears. Causality runs briefly backward. You need to know when this happens, because the skips affect you too—and if you don't notice them, you won't know what's been changed.

**Continuous personal recording.** Wear a body camera with a wide-angle lens, recording continuously. Review periodically. After a skip, check your footage. Compare what you remember with what the camera recorded. Note discrepancies. The camera itself may be affected by the skip—footage may show the before-state or the after-state or something in between—but having any external record is better than relying solely on memory that may have been edited by the same skip that moved the rock from your left to your right.

**Journaling by hand.** After every skip you notice, write down what changed. Physical pen and paper. If a skip alters digital storage, you want an analog backup. Write in ink, not pencil. Date and number each entry. If an entry disappears, you'll notice the gap in the numbering.

**Physical reference objects.** Carry a set of small, distinctly colored objects—marbles, painted stones, numbered tokens—and arrange them in a specific pattern at your campsite. Check the pattern regularly. If the arrangement has changed without your intervention, a skip has occurred. This is your canary in the coal mine.

### B. Gravity Fluctuation Countermeasures

Gravity varies across the plateau. Sometimes lighter, sometimes heavier, neither corresponding to elevation. This affects everything from your walking gait to the behavior of water in your bottles to the stability of your shelter.

**Trekking poles.** Adjustable, lightweight, with carbide tips for purchase on fresh rock. When gravity lightens, you need anchoring. When it heavies, you need support. Poles serve both functions. They also serve as probes—extend one ahead of you to test the ground before committing your weight to a surface that might be operating under different gravitational rules than the ground you're currently standing on.

**Low center of gravity.** Pack your heaviest items at the bottom of your pack, close to your back, as low as possible. In a zone of lightened gravity, a top-heavy pack will make you unstable. In a zone of increased gravity, a well-balanced pack reduces the additional strain on your legs and spine.

**Tether system.** Carry a length of strong cord (dynamic climbing rope, 9-10mm) and several rock anchors (spring-loaded camming devices that can wedge into the abundant cracks in the fresh rock). If you encounter a zone of significantly reduced gravity—enough that you feel yourself beginning to float—you need to be able to anchor immediately. Practice placing cams quickly. Your life may depend on being anchored before a lightening zone catches you fully.

### C. Thermal Anomaly Awareness

Thermal gradients sometimes run backward. Heat flows from cool stone into warm air. This can create localized pockets of extreme cold or extreme heat in places that don't visually signal danger.

**Infrared thermometer.** A handheld point-and-shoot IR thermometer lets you check surface temperatures at a distance. Sweep it across your path regularly. If you get readings that don't make physical sense—a glowing rock face registering as freezing cold, a shadowed crevice registering as superheated—you're in a thermal anomaly zone. Reroute.

**Thermal imaging monocular.** Better than the point thermometer for continuous awareness. Wear it on a lanyard, check it frequently. It paints the landscape in thermal colors and will immediately highlight anomalous zones. The dim ambient lighting makes thermal imaging particularly useful here, since visible-light vision is already compromised.

### D. Temporal Desynchronization

Sound sometimes arrives before its source event. This is not just disorienting; it's a potential survival signal. If you hear the crack of splitting rock before the rock splits, you have a one-second warning window to move. But it also means your causal reasoning—your deep, instinctive understanding that effects follow causes—is being challenged. This can induce something like motion sickness of the mind, a cognitive nausea that gets worse the longer you're exposed.

**Practice acceptance in advance.** Meditate on the concept of flexible causality before you arrive. This sounds esoteric, but the cognitive load of constantly processing events that arrive in the wrong order is real, and people who have done mental preparation for non-linear experience cope significantly better than those who haven't. Lucid dreaming practice, VR experiences with scrambled temporal cues, or even simply spending time in environments with long sound delays (large canyons, cathedrals) can help your brain build tolerance for temporal mismatch.

**Anchor to your own body's time.** When the skips happen and causality stutters, focus on your own heartbeat, your own breathing. These are processes running on your biology's time, not the plateau's time. They are your temporal anchor. If the world around you hiccups, your pulse does not. Hold to that.

## IV. Interacting with the Local Life

### A. The Glassjaws

These are your most important neighbors on the plateau, and your best source of environmental intelligence. They read the machine's rhythms. They predict stable ground and stable physics. They are, in effect, living navigation aids.

**Do not approach herds directly.** You don't know their threat response. They have heavy mandibles designed to crack fresh rock; those mandibles would go through human bone without effort. Maintain a distance of at least 50 meters. Observe their movement patterns from a distance.

**Learn to read their routing.** Watch where they go and where they refuse to go. If a herd consistently diverts around a particular zone, that zone is unstable—geologically, physically, or ontologically. Plot their avoidance zones mentally or on a rough sketch. These become your no-go areas.

**Watch for freezing behavior.** During a skip, the insects freeze for exactly one heartbeat. Do the glassjaws do something similar? Any behavioral change in the glassjaws that precedes a skip gives you advance warning. Document their pre-skip behavior carefully. After several observations, you may be able to predict skips by watching the herds.

### B. The Sky-Mantas

They ride thermals that sometimes operate on principles other than convection—"columns of altered probability," the description says. They read something in the atmosphere you can't perceive.

**Do not interfere with a landing manta.** The one observed landing regarded the observer with independent-tracking amber eyes. It assessed, and it left. This suggests intelligence or at least sophisticated awareness. A creature with many independent eyes is processing vast amounts of visual information simultaneously. It saw you. It evaluated you. It chose to leave. Respect that evaluation. If one lands near you, remain still, make no sudden movements, and let it complete its assessment.

**Their flight patterns may encode information.** Where they circle, what altitudes they maintain, whether they cluster or disperse—all of this could indicate conditions in the upper atmosphere, approaching fog banks, or zones of ontological instability forming above before they manifest below. You probably won't crack this code during a single visit, but note their behavior and correlate it with subsequent events. Build a dataset, even if you can't interpret it yet.

### C. The Insects and Small Life

Leave them alone. They're engaged in frantically compressed life cycles in temporary habitats. They are not a threat, and they are not a resource (do not eat them). Their main utility to you is as an indicator species: the freeze behavior during skips makes them a visible alarm system. If you notice every insect in your vicinity stop moving simultaneously, a skip just happened. Check your reference objects. Check your body camera. Check what changed.

### D. The Moss and Vegetation

The bioluminescent moss is your ambient light source in the deeper crevices and will help you navigate the seams where the terrain is most fractured. It also indicates substrate age—moss presence means the rock surface has been stable long enough for colonization, which ranges from minutes (for the fastest spore-crust runners) to hours (for the thick meter-deep beds). Thicker moss = older, more stable surface. Use moss thickness as a proxy for ground stability.

**Do not deliberately damage the moss beds.** The amphibious creatures carry spores between pools; the entire moss ecosystem is a distributed network that tracks the machine's output, colonizing new surfaces as they appear. You are a guest in a system that operates on its own logic. Minimize your impact.

## V. Psychological Preparation

This may be the most important section of this guide.

### A. The Perpetual Dusk

There is no day-night cycle. The bronze-grey luminous murk is constant—a perpetual late dusk that never brightens and never darkens. This will disrupt your circadian rhythm within 24 hours. Left unmanaged, circadian disruption leads to sleep degradation, cognitive impairment, mood disturbance, and eventually disorientation and poor decision-making.

**Impose an artificial cycle.** Carry a bright full-spectrum light (10,000 lux daylight therapy lamp) and use it for 30 minutes at the start of your designated "morning." Carry blue-light-blocking glasses and wear them for the two hours before your designated "sleep time." Set alarms to maintain your cycle. Do not let the plateau's timelessness dissolve your internal clock. Your body's time is one of the few things here that belongs to you.

**Sleep discipline.** Earplugs won't block the sub-bass hum from below—it comes through bone conduction as much as through the air—but they'll soften it. A white noise generator set to a frequency that partially masks the hum can help. Melatonin supplements (0.5-1mg, 30 minutes before designated sleep time) to reinforce the artificial cycle. Sleep when your schedule says to sleep, even if you don't feel tired, even if the ground is shifting, even if a skip just happened and you want to stay alert. Sleep deprivation on the Plateau is a fast track to catastrophic judgment errors.

### B. The Ontological Anxiety

Here is what will get you, if anything does.

You are standing on a world that is *generating its own reality in real time*. The physics are not inherited from a deeper substrate; they emerge from the engine's operation. This means that the ground rules—gravity, causality, solidity, the passage of time, the consistency of space—are not guaranteed. They are *produced*. They are the output of a process. And sometimes the process hiccups.

This awareness, once it settles in, produces a particular kind of anxiety that is distinct from fear. Fear has an object—you fear the rock will collapse, you fear the gravity will fail, you fear the skip will delete something important. Ontological anxiety has no object. It is the meta-awareness that the framework within which objects *exist* is contingent. It is the feeling of standing on a floor and suddenly understanding that the floor is not resting on a foundation but is being continuously projected, and that the projector could skip.

People respond to this in different ways:

**Denial.** Some travelers simply refuse to engage with the ontological dimension and treat the Plateau as a weird but fundamentally normal geological environment. This works surprisingly well for short visits. The physics is mostly stable. The skips are brief. If you just need to cross the landscape and get out, you can functionally ignore the deeper strangeness and navigate by practical heuristics. But denial becomes increasingly difficult to maintain the longer you stay, because the skips become harder to ignore, and the zones of incoherence at the edges are visible reminders that the stability you're clinging to is local and temporary.

**Hypervigilance.** Others go the opposite direction—monitoring every fluctuation, checking reference objects constantly, recording everything, sleeping with one eye open. This is unsustainable. The plateau is *always* fluctuating. There is always something to be vigilant about. Hypervigilance on the Plateau leads to exhaustion, paranoia, and eventually the inability to distinguish between real skips and the normal micro-variations of a geologically active terrain. You burn out.

**The recommended approach: engaged acceptance.** Acknowledge the ontological instability as a feature of the environment, like altitude on a mountain or current in a river. Prepare for it, monitor it, take reasonable precautions, but do not allow it to become the center of your experience. The glassjaws have adapted. The insects have adapted. The moss has adapted. None of them treat the instability as a crisis; they treat it as a *condition*—the medium they live in, the water they swim in. You can learn from them. The ground shifts; you shift with it. The physics skips; you note it, check your references, and continue. The horizon vanishes for an instant; you wait, and it returns, and you are still here, standing, breathing, on your own time.

This is easier said than done. Practice equanimity before you go. Meditation, mindfulness training, exposure to controlled uncertainty. If you have access to multiverse-adjusted therapy, specifically the protocols developed for travelers who visit high-flux or para-consistent realities, take a course. The skills transfer directly.

### C. The Moment of Absence

The description ends with a moment where the horizon is simply *not there*. Not hidden, not obscured. Absent. A void where distance should be.

You need to prepare for this specifically, because it is qualitatively different from the skips. The skips are brief rearrangements—things move, values change, but the framework persists. The absence is a moment where the framework itself drops out. The engine catches, the stone pushes upward, and it comes back. But for that instant, you are standing on warm stone at the edge of *nothing*, and the nothing is not darkness or emptiness but the *lack of the concept of distance itself*.

This will happen. Probably multiple times during your visit. There is no physical preparation that addresses it. There is only this: when it happens, feel your feet on the stone. Feel the warmth. Listen for the hum. It will come back. It always has. The engine has been building this world for longer than you've been alive, and whatever process drives it has caught every time, has resumed every time, has rebuilt the horizon every time. Trust the engine. Not because it's trustworthy—you don't know that—but because trusting it is the only option that preserves your ability to function.

And then move on. Check your references. Note the event. Keep walking.

## VII. Entry and Exit Strategy

### Getting In

Your arrival point is your most critical piece of navigational information. Mark it in every way available to you:

- UV dye on surrounding moss beds (heavy application in a distinctive pattern)
- Numbered reference tokens placed in a wide pattern around the entry point
- Record its position relative to the largest visible landmarks (the southern cliff line, major ridge features)
- Record video panorama at arrival
- If your entry method leaves any physical trace (a portal structure, a displacement mark on the rock), photograph it extensively and note its exact relationship to surrounding features

**Then accept that you might not be able to find it again.** The terrain shifts. Landmarks change. Your entry point might be subsumed by new stone, tilted to a new angle, or relocated by a skip. Have a backup exit method. If your only way out is returning to your exact entry point on a plateau that rearranges itself daily, you have a single point of failure in a high-flux environment. That's unacceptable.

**Backup exit options:**
- If your transit method allows exit from any point on the plateau (not tied to a fixed location), this is ideal. The plateau doesn't seem to prevent departure, only complicate navigation.
- If your transit method requires a specific location, establish *multiple* marked locations during your first day, spread across a wide area. The more points you have, the higher the probability that at least one remains accessible.
- If your transit method requires specific conditions (temporal alignment, energy availability), study whether the plateau's engine cycles create periodic windows that match your requirements. The mechanical processes below operate rhythmically—pistons, turbines, geartrains. There may be a cycle, a pattern, a moment of particular alignment that facilitates your departure.

### Getting Out

**Set a firm departure deadline before you arrive.** The Plateau has no urgency. It has no night to signal the end of a day. It has no seasons to mark the passage of weeks. It has only the endless bronze dusk and the slow, fascinating, hypnotic process of a world building itself. Travelers who don't set firm departure times tend to stay longer than intended. The plateau is not *keeping* you; you are *lingering*. The process is mesmerizing. The glassjaw herds are beautiful. The fog banks are otherworldly. The moment of absence is terrible and transcendent. You want to see the next ridge emerge. You want to watch the moss colonize one more crevice. You want to understand the hum.

Set your deadline. Write it on the first page of your notebook. When it arrives, leave. You can always come back.

## VIII. Ethical Considerations

You are visiting a world that has never known hands. There are no architects, no builders, no *built things*. You are, by your presence, the first intentional agent to walk this landscape. Everything here—the engine-geology, the dense and opportunistic life, the flickering physics—has evolved and developed in the complete absence of purpose, intention, or design.

**Tread lightly.** Not just in the ecological sense (though that too—don't damage moss beds, don't harass fauna, don't contaminate pools), but in the ontological sense. The plateau generates its own rules. Your presence introduces something new: a rule-follower, a pattern-seeker, a being that expects consistency and reacts to its absence. You don't know what effect that has on a system that has never had to account for expectations before. It probably has no effect at all. But humility is appropriate in a place where the ground beneath you is also the generator of the laws that hold you to it.

Leave nothing that doesn't biodegrade. Take nothing that isn't a sample small enough to be meaningless. Record everything. Share your records with other travelers.

The Plateau will still be building itself long after you've gone. Let it.

## IX. Final Notes

When you arrive, stand still for ten minutes before doing anything else. Feel the warmth through your boot soles. Listen to the hum. Watch the dim bronze light that comes from everywhere and nowhere. Let the first ridge push up in front of you, fresh stone catching the no-light, and watch the first runner of moss race across its surface.

Breathe. You are standing on a world in the act of becoming. You are the only thing here that arrived finished.

This is the preparation no gear list can provide: the willingness to be small, to be temporary, to be the soft and fragile and brief thing walking across the vast slow engine of a world that does not need you, did not expect you, and will not pause for you.

Go. Be careful. Come back. Tell us what you heard in the hum.
