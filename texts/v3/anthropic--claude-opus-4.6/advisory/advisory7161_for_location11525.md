# Preparing for the Floodworks

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before packing a single item, you need to sit with something uncomfortable: the Floodworks is not a place that will try to kill you through hostility. It will try to absorb you through *patience*. There is no monster waiting, no trap to spring, no hostile faction to negotiate with. There is instead an environment so relentlessly, uniformly inhospitable to human presence — not violently, just fundamentally — that your preparation must be oriented around a single principle: **you are a temporary anomaly in a system that does not acknowledge anomalies.** Everything you bring, everything you plan, every protocol you establish is in service of remaining temporary rather than becoming permanent. Because the Floodworks will, given enough time, incorporate you. Your iron. Your water. Your warmth. Your stillness.

Understand this and you will prepare correctly. Misunderstand it — treat this as a dangerous adventure rather than an exercise in deliberate, sustained self-extraction from an environment that dissolves boundaries — and you will make errors that compound.

## Physical Preparation: The Body

### Hearing Protection — This Is Not Optional, This Is Survival

The sound environment of the Floodworks is the most immediately dangerous feature you will encounter, and it is dangerous precisely because it does not register as dangerous. It registers as *atmospheric*. You hear the low pump-throb and your brain categorizes it as background. It isn't background. It is a sustained low-frequency pressure wave that will, over hours, produce effects ranging from disorientation to nausea to a deep, bone-level fatigue that feels like depression but is actually physiological — your vestibular system being slowly disrupted, your chest cavity resonating at frequencies that interfere with cardiac rhythm in subtle ways you won't notice until you're making bad decisions and can't figure out why.

You need **layered hearing protection**. Not just earplugs. Not just earmuffs. Both, and ideally with an active noise-cancellation system tuned specifically to the low-frequency range. Standard commercial ANC headphones are designed to cancel the drone of airplane engines in the 80-200 Hz range; you need something that goes lower, into the 10-40 Hz infrasound territory where the primary pump harmonics will live. Custom-fitted molded earplugs with an NRR of 30+ as your base layer. Over-ear active cancellation as your second layer. And critically: a system that still allows you to hear *some* environmental sound, because the Floodworks is not silent except for the pumps. The steam vents erupt unpredictably. The channels have flow dynamics that change. You need to hear these things — you just need to hear them at a level that doesn't destroy you.

Bring backup earplugs. Bring many backup earplugs. The moisture will degrade foam plugs within hours. Silicone or custom-molded only.

Consider also: bone conduction. The pump vibration comes through the floor, through your skeleton, and reaches your inner ear via pathways that no earplug can block. Thick-soled boots help (more on this below), but you should also prepare psychologically for the fact that you cannot fully escape the sound. You can reduce it. You cannot eliminate it. Part of your preparation is learning to function inside a body that is being continuously, subtly vibrated. Meditation practice helps. Specifically, practices that teach you to observe physical sensation without reacting to it. You will need this skill.

### Skin and Exposure

The water is the second concern. It is everywhere — seeping up through the floor grating, condensing on every surface, pooling in every depression, misting from every vent. It is not toxic in the acute sense, but it is not water in any sense your body is accustomed to. It has spent millennia circulating through iron and alloy and whatever nameless materials compose the deeper infrastructure. It carries dissolved metals — iron certainly, but likely others: manganese, chromium, trace elements leached from alloys never designed for biological contact. The iridescent sheen suggests hydrocarbons — ancient lubricants broken down and emulsified into the water column over centuries until they've become a persistent component rather than a contaminant.

You do not want this water on your skin for extended periods. Brief contact — stepping in a puddle, brushing a wet surface — is manageable. But prolonged exposure will cause irritation, then dermatitis, then something harder to name: a slow mineral infiltration of the skin's outer layers that the greenish-black film on the pump houses suggests is possible for organic surfaces generally. You are, after all, an organic surface.

**Full-body waterproofing** is essential. Not a rain jacket. Not waders. A fully sealed dry suit — the kind used for contaminated water diving or hazmat response. Vulcanized rubber or heavy PVC, sealed seams, integrated boots and gloves, a neck seal that you can mate to your headgear. You will sweat inside it. You will be uncomfortable. This is vastly preferable to the alternative. Bring talcum powder or anti-chafe cream for the interior surfaces. Bring repair patches and adhesive rated for wet application, because in an environment this saturated with moisture, any breach in your suit will be found by the water immediately and exploited with the same infinite patience the Floodworks applies to everything.

Your face is the vulnerable point. A full-face respirator with an integrated visor gives you protection against both the water mist and the particulate haze that constitutes the Floodworks' atmosphere. That haze is not just steam. It's steam plus iron oxide dust plus whatever becomes airborne when superheated water explodes through pressure-relief vents at irregular intervals. Breathing this unfiltered for hours will coat your airways with a fine metalite sediment that your lungs are not designed to clear. P100 filters at minimum. Bring many replacements — the moisture will clog them faster than you're used to. And carry the filters in a sealed, desiccated container, because if they absorb ambient moisture before you use them, they're already compromised.

### Boots

Your boots deserve their own section because they are the primary interface between you and the Floodworks, and the Floodworks will test them relentlessly.

You need soles thick enough to attenuate the floor vibration meaningfully — at least 2-3 centimeters of dense, vibration-damping material. Standard steel-toed work boots are insufficient. Look at what heavy equipment operators wear, or what bomb disposal technicians use — boots designed to reduce the transmission of shock through the ground. The soles need to be chemical-resistant (the water will degrade standard rubber over days) and non-slip on wet metal, which is one of the most treacherous surfaces a human being can walk on. Grated flooring with moisture and a thin film of iron-oxide slurry is essentially a controlled experiment in reducing friction to zero. Aggressive tread patterns help, but what helps more is a sole compound specifically rated for wet steel — some industrial safety suppliers manufacture these.

The boots should be integrated with your dry suit. No seams at the ankle. No gap where water can enter and pool against your skin. You will be standing and walking on wet metal for the duration of your visit, and your feet are where the Floodworks begins its work on you.

Consider anti-vibration insoles as an additional layer. Your feet, ankles, and knees will take the brunt of the pump harmonics. Over hours, the effect is cumulative — a deep ache that settles into the joints and doesn't leave. Anything you can do to dampen the transmission helps.

## Equipment

### Navigation

There are no landmarks in the Floodworks. Not in any useful sense. Every pump house looks like every other pump house. The channels run in straight lines that could be any straight line. The haze limits visibility to two hundred meters. The floor extends infinitely — or at least far enough in every direction that the distinction between infinite and very large becomes meaningless from a navigation standpoint.

You will get lost. Accept this as a baseline condition and plan around it.

**GPS will not function.** There is no sky. There are no satellites. There may not be a magnetosphere that behaves consistently enough for a compass to work — the floor itself is iron, miles of it, and the magnetic signature of that much ferrous material will make any compass reading essentially random.

You need **inertial navigation.** A dead-reckoning system — ideally an IMU (inertial measurement unit) of the kind used in submarines or spacecraft, something that tracks its own acceleration and rotation without reference to external signals. These are expensive and specialized, but in the Floodworks, they are the only reliable method. Pair it with a meticulous log: every turn, every distance, every bearing, written down in waterproof ink on waterproof paper (or recorded on a device sealed against moisture — more on electronics below). Your return path is your lifeline. Lose it and you are adrift on an infinite wet iron plain with no sun, no stars, no horizon, and no distinguishing features, surrounded by sound that will slowly erode your capacity to think clearly.

As a backup: **physical trail markers.** Something you can place on the floor at intervals — every hundred meters, say — that you can find again. They need to be heavy enough not to be moved by the water that flows across the floor. They need to be visible in near-dark conditions. Chemical glow markers are good — the kind that activate by bending and mixing two reagents, producing hours of steady light. Bring many. Scatter them behind you like breadcrumbs through a forest that has no trees and no birds but is, in its own way, far more capable of swallowing you.

A surveyor's chalk or paint marker for the floor surface itself might seem logical but will be defeated by the water almost immediately. Anything you leave must be three-dimensional and self-luminous.

### Lighting

Do not rely on the ambient light of the Floodworks. It is insufficient for safe navigation — the pilot flames behind grating and the phosphorescence of certain surfaces are atmospheric, not functional. They will let you see that you are somewhere without letting you see where.

**Primary lighting:** A headlamp, hands-free, with a broad flood beam and a focused spot mode. LED, high-lumen, sealed to IPX8 standard (submersible). The headlamp should be mounted on your respirator or helmet in a way that doesn't obstruct your visor. Warm white light — not cool white, not blue. In an environment this dominated by rust and iron and gray haze, cool lighting will make everything look the same. Warm light will give you slightly better contrast on the orange-brown surfaces you're moving across.

**Backup lighting:** At least two additional sealed flashlights, carried in accessible but protected locations. Do not depend on a single light source. If your headlamp fails in the Floodworks, you are functionally blind, and the difference between being in the Floodworks and being blind in the Floodworks is the difference between profound discomfort and genuine psychic emergency.

**Consider also:** A UV flashlight. The iridescent substance in the channel water, the greenish-black films, the mineral deposits on pump house walls — these may fluoresce under ultraviolet in ways that reveal information invisible under white light. Patterns, flows, concentrations. This is speculative, but in a place this old and this chemically complex, UV might show you things about where the water has been and where it's going that help you understand the local hydrology — which is, after all, the only geography this place has.

### Electronics and Moisture

Every electronic device you bring is under siege from the moment you arrive. The humidity is not 100% — it is something beyond that, a supersaturation maintained by constant steam release and constant evaporation from every wet surface. Water will find its way into any device that is not hermetically sealed. Not quickly — this isn't immersion — but with the same patient, seeping insistence that characterizes everything about the Floodworks.

All electronics in sealed, waterproof cases. Not zip-lock bags. Not "water-resistant" consumer cases. Actual dry cases with compression seals — Pelican cases for anything you're not actively holding, and IPX8-rated individual housings for things you need to use while exposed. Include desiccant packs in every case and replace them regularly, because they will saturate quickly.

Touchscreens will not work well through gloves or in this moisture. Any device you plan to interact with regularly should have physical buttons or be operable through a sealed interface.

Battery performance: cold temperatures degrade battery life, but the Floodworks isn't cold — it's cool, persistently cool, maybe 10-15°C if I had to guess from the description. This is enough to reduce lithium-ion performance somewhat, but not catastrophically. The bigger concern is the vibration — the constant low-frequency shaking of the floor, transmitted through your body, transmitted through anything you set down. Over time, this vibration can work loose connections, fatigue solder joints, degrade the physical integrity of electronic components in ways that are invisible until the device simply stops working. Pack electronics in vibration-damped containers when not in use. Don't set anything directly on the floor.

### Shelter

You need to sleep. Even a short visit — 48 hours, say — requires rest, and resting in the Floodworks presents unique challenges.

You cannot sleep on the floor. The vibration alone would prevent restful sleep, but even if it didn't, the water would claim you — pooling around you, soaking through whatever you're lying on, beginning its slow chemical work on any exposed surface. And psychologically, lying down on that wet iron plate in the dark with the pump-throb in your bones and the haze pressing down from above is an experience that I suspect would be difficult to recover from even after leaving.

**Bring a hammock.** Specifically, an enclosed jungle hammock with an integrated rain fly and bug net — the bug net is irrelevant (there are no bugs) but the enclosure provides psychological containment that you will desperately want. String it between structures — between pump house walls, between steam vent stacks, between exposed pipe runs. Getting off the floor is critical. Even a meter of elevation changes your relationship to the vibration, the pooling water, and the ambient light in ways that make rest possible.

The hammock should be synthetic — nylon or polyester that won't absorb moisture. Cotton or natural fibers will be waterlogged within hours and will never dry in this atmosphere. Bring a closed-cell foam pad to insulate you from the cold air beneath (convective cooling in a hammock is significant even at 10-15°C). Your sleeping bag should be synthetic fill for the same reason — down is useless in this humidity.

A bivouac between two pump houses, if you can find two within reasonable proximity, gives you the advantage of partial walls, which block some of the lateral sound propagation and the drifting fog banks from the steam vents. Don't camp directly adjacent to a vent stack — the eruptions are unpredictable and the superheated steam they release is genuinely dangerous, a scalding jet that can emerge with little warning.

Sleep in shifts if you're traveling with others. Someone should always be awake, not because there's a specific threat but because in a place with no external time cues, no light cycle, and a sound environment that warps temporal perception, having someone maintain continuous awareness is the only way to prevent the group's sense of time from dissolving entirely.

### Food and Water

Irony of ironies: you are surrounded by water and cannot drink any of it. The channel water, the seep water, the condensation on the walls — all of it is contaminated with dissolved metals and hydrocarbons at concentrations that make it unsuitable for human consumption even with field filtration. You would need a distillation apparatus to make it safe, and even then, the volatile hydrocarbons might carry over. Bring all the water you need. Every milliliter. This is the primary constraint on how long you can stay — not danger, not fear, but the simple logistic reality of carrying enough clean water into a place where none exists.

**Two liters per day minimum** for moderate activity in cool, humid conditions. More if the exertion of walking in a full dry suit on vibrating, slippery metal is higher than expected (it will be). For a 72-hour visit: six liters minimum, eight to ten recommended. That's eight to ten kilograms of water alone, which is a significant fraction of your load capacity. Plan your visit duration around this constraint ruthlessly.

Food is less critical but should be calorie-dense, require no water to prepare, and be packaged in waterproof containers. High-fat, high-calorie bars. Nuts. Dried meat. Nothing that requires cooking — you don't want to operate a stove in an atmosphere you're already filtering through a respirator. Eat on a schedule, not by hunger. Your appetite will be suppressed by the constant low-level stress of the environment, the vibration, the sound, the restricted breathing through filters. Eat anyway. Your body is burning energy you don't feel it burning.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where most people will underestimate the Floodworks, because the psychological challenge of this place is not terror. Terror is legible. Terror announces itself and can be managed through the well-documented neurology of fear response. What the Floodworks does is subtler, more insidious, and in its own way more dangerous.

### The Problem of Scale

The Floodworks is, as far as anyone can determine, infinite. Or functionally infinite. Or large enough that the difference doesn't matter. There is no edge. No boundary. No wall in the distance that says "here is where this ends." Walk in any direction and you find more floor, more pump houses, more channels, more haze, more sound. This is psychologically corrosive in a way that has no clean analogue in natural environments. Even the open ocean has a horizon, a sky, clouds — perceptual structures that give the mind something to organize around. The Floodworks has none of these. The haze above and the dissolution of forms at two hundred meters create a perceptual bubble that moves with you, always the same size, always the same contents. You are always in the center of an identical sphere of gray wet iron. Walk for an hour and the view is the same. Walk for a day and the view is the same. Your inertial navigation tells you you've moved three kilometers. Your eyes tell you nothing has changed.

This produces a state that psychologists studying sensory deprivation would recognize — a growing dissociation from your own movement, a sense that motion is meaningless, that the landscape is a treadmill, that *you* are the only thing in the universe and the Floodworks is a static backdrop projected around you. In mild form, this is disorienting. In severe form, it can precipitate depersonalization — a sense of watching yourself from outside, of being unreal, of having no more substance than the steam that drifts across the floor.

**Countermeasures:** Maintain a rigorous, externalized record of your position and progress. Write it down. Look at what you've written. The physical act of recording — pen on paper, marks on a map — is an anchor to concrete reality. Talk out loud, if you're alone. Narrate your observations. Name things — "that pump house," "this channel junction." The Floodworks has no names, has never been named; naming it is an act of cognitive colonization that keeps your mind active and engaged rather than passive and absorptive.

If you're traveling with others, talk to each other. Not about the Floodworks — about anything. About home. About what you had for breakfast three days ago. About something specific and real and completely unrelated to the infinite wet iron plane you're standing on. Maintain the social architecture of your identity. The Floodworks has no use for identity and will, given time, teach you not to either.

### The Problem of Sound

I've addressed hearing protection above as a physical measure. Here I want to address the psychological dimension.

You cannot escape the sound. You can reduce it. You cannot eliminate it. Even with the best hearing protection, the low-frequency pump harmonics will reach you through bone conduction, through the floor, through the air in your chest cavity. Over hours, this sound becomes the new baseline. Your brain adapts to it and begins to treat it as silence, which means that actual changes in the sound — a pump cycling differently, a vent erupting closer than expected, a shift in the harmonic pattern — register as *absence* rather than presence. You notice that something has changed because the baseline shifted, but the shift feels like something has been *taken away* rather than added. This is deeply unsettling and extremely difficult to rationalize in the moment.

Furthermore: the interference patterns between pump cycles, sweeping across the floor as physical vibration, will interact with your body in ways that produce transient physiological effects — momentary dizziness, sudden drops in mood, unexplained surges of anxiety or dread. These are not supernatural. They are well-documented effects of infrasound on the human nervous system. But knowing that they're mechanical in origin does not make them feel mechanical. They feel like emotions. They feel like *your* emotions. You will have moments of sudden, overwhelming despair that have nothing to do with your actual situation and everything to do with a 17 Hz standing wave passing through your thorax. Recognizing this for what it is requires preparation: read the literature on infrasound effects before you go. Memorize the symptoms. When you feel a sudden, sourceless dread, check your body — are you vibrating? Is the floor pattern different under your feet? Give yourself a mechanical explanation and hold onto it.

### The Problem of Duration

The Floodworks has been running longer than human civilization. Longer than human *evolution*, possibly. It is an artifact of such extreme duration that standing on it forces a confrontation with timescales your mind is not built to process. The rust strata — layers of oxidation compressed into something like stone — are a visible, tangible record of time passing at a rate that makes your presence a rounding error, a flicker, a single frame in a film that has been running since before there were eyes to watch it.

Some people find this perspective liberating. Most find it crushing. The Floodworks does not care which you find it, because the Floodworks does not care. This is stated in the description and it bears repeating: **the Floodworks is not conscious, not hostile, not indifferent in the way that implies the capacity for regard.** It is simply mechanical. It runs. You are irrelevant to its running. For many travelers, this is the hardest thing — not danger, not discomfort, but the absolute, verified, structural irrelevance of their presence. You are visiting a place that was not made for you, is not aware of you, will not remember you, and will continue exactly as it is for millennia after the atoms of your body have been scattered across other universes. Some people need to make peace with this before they go. Some people cannot make peace with it and should not go at all.

### The Shapes in the Water

The description mentions something moving in the channel water. Shapes that aren't there when you look directly. Pressure artifacts or something else.

I'm going to give you the only honest advice available: **do not try to see them more clearly.** Do not crouch at the edge of a channel and stare into the dark water. Do not shine your light into the channels at length. If you see movement in your peripheral vision, note it, record it if you wish, and move on. The Floodworks is old enough that the boundaries between categories — mechanism and organism, artifact and geology, water and something-in-the-water — have had time to soften in ways that resist investigation. You are not equipped to investigate them. You do not have the conceptual framework. Attempting to force a clear observation of something that exists in the blur between categories is a good way to damage the categories themselves, and you need those categories intact to function as a human being in other, less ambiguous places.

This is not mysticism. It is practical cognitive hygiene. You can spend years thinking about what you saw in the channels after you leave. Don't spend your visit doing it.

## Logistics and Planning

### Duration

I recommend a maximum visit of **48 to 72 hours** for a first visit. This is long enough to move meaningfully into the Floodworks — to walk several kilometers from your entry point, to see pump houses and channels and vent stacks, to experience the full daily cycle of steam and sound and seepage (insofar as there is a cycle in a place without days). It is short enough that your water supply remains manageable, your hearing protection remains effective, and the cumulative psychological effects remain within the range of "profoundly unusual experience" rather than tipping into "lasting alteration of perceptual framework."

Longer visits are possible but require substantially more preparation, more supplies, and more psychological resilience. Do the short visit first. See how you respond. See how quickly you recover afterward. Then decide.

### Entry and Exit

I haven't addressed how you arrive at the Floodworks because the description doesn't specify the entry mechanism — it varies by traveler, by route, by the particular topology of the multiverse pathway you're using. But I will say this: **mark your entry point with everything you have.** Multiple glow markers. A physical beacon if you have one. A radio transponder on a frequency you can track. Whatever you use, make it impossible to miss from two hundred meters in low light, because two hundred meters is all the visibility you get, and if you miss your entry point on the return, you are searching an infinite plane for a single unmarked location. This is not a recoverable error.

Consider tethering to your entry point — a physical line, a cord that you pay out behind you as you walk, that you can follow back. This limits your range to whatever cord you can carry, but for a first visit, a one-kilometer radius from your entry point is more than sufficient to experience the Floodworks fully, and a one-kilometer spool of thin, strong cord (Dyneema or Spectra) weighs very little. The water on the floor will not degrade it within the timeframe of your visit. Clip it to your harness. Let it trail behind you. It is the most primitive and the most reliable navigation system available: a line from where you are to where you need to be.

### Team Composition

Go with at least one other person. Ideally two others, for a team of three. Solo visits to the Floodworks are survivable but inadvisable — the psychological burden of being alone in an environment with no other life, no variation, and no reference points is substantial, and if you suffer a physical mishap (a slip on wet metal, a turned ankle, a fall into a channel), there is no one to help and no way to summon help. The Floodworks has no communication infrastructure, no inhabitants, no rescue services. You have what you bring and who you bring.

Three people provide redundancy: if one is incapacitated, two can still manage the return. They provide psychological stability: conversation, shared experience, the simple mammalian comfort of other human bodies in a place that has never hosted a warm-blooded creature. And they provide a reality check: when the infrasound is affecting your mood and the haze is dissolving your sense of distance and the shapes in the channels are pulling at your peripheral vision, another person who says "yes, I see it too" or "no, there's nothing there" is worth more than every piece of equipment in your pack.

Choose your companions carefully. You want people who are psychologically stable, physically competent, comfortable with prolonged discomfort, and — this is crucial — **comfortable with boredom.** Much of the Floodworks is not dramatic. It is monotonous. It is walking across wet metal in low light with a headache from the sound, checking your navigation, placing a marker, walking further, seeing the same thing. People who need stimulation, who need events, who need the environment to justify their presence through spectacle — these people will struggle badly. You want people who can walk in the gray for hours and remain attentive, grounded, and functional.

## What to Bring — Summary List

I'll consolidate the equipment discussion into a practical list:

**Worn:**
- Sealed dry suit (vulcanized rubber or heavy PVC), integrated boots and gloves
- Anti-vibration boots with chemical-resistant soles rated for wet steel
- Anti-vibration insoles
- Full-face respirator with P100 filters (bring 10+ replacement filters in sealed desiccated container)
- Layered hearing protection: molded silicone earplugs + active noise-cancelling over-ear with low-frequency capability
- Helmet or hard hat (the pump houses have low clearances and dripping ceilings)

**Carried — Navigation:**
- Inertial navigation unit (IMU)
- Waterproof notebook and multiple waterproof pens
- Compass (bring it, knowing it's likely useless — the data point of *how* it misbehaves may itself be informative)
- Chemical glow markers, minimum 50, in multiple colors
- Tether line: 1-2 km spool of Dyneema cord with clips
- Entry point beacon (radio transponder, bright chemical or electric light, physical flag)

**Carried — Lighting:**
- Sealed headlamp (IPX8), warm white, 500+ lumen, multiple brightness modes
- Two backup sealed flashlights
- UV flashlight
- Spare batteries in sealed, desiccated, vibration-damped case

**Carried — Shelter and Rest:**
- Synthetic hammock with rain fly and enclosure
- Suspension straps rated for structural attachment points
- Closed-cell foam sleeping pad
- Synthetic-fill sleeping bag rated to 0°C (the Floodworks isn't that cold, but heat loss in a hammock in perpetual damp is significant)

**Carried — Water and Food:**
- 8-10 liters of clean water in sealed, rigid containers (bladders may puncture on the metalwork)
- 8,000-10,000 calories of shelf-stable, no-cook food in waterproof packaging
- Metal water bottle for active drinking (sealed when not in use)

**Carried — Medical:**
- Standard first aid kit in waterproof case
- Extra emphasis on: wound care (any break in the skin exposed to the water is an infection risk), anti-nausea medication (for infrasound effects), anti-inflammatory painkillers (for vibration-induced joint pain), electrolyte supplements
- Emergency blanket (hypothermia is a real risk if your dry suit is compromised and you become wet)
- Ear drops for water exposure (if moisture breaches your hearing protection)

**Carried — Tools:**
- Multi-tool with pliers, screwdriver heads, wire cutters
- Pry bar (short, heavy — the access hatches and doors in pump houses require real force)
- Adjustable wrench (the valve wheels are tempting; resist the urge to turn them unless you have a very good reason, but carry the means in case that reason materializes)
- Duct tape and repair supplies for suit breaches
- Zip ties, carabiners, paracord (general utility)

**Carried — Documentation:**
- Sealed camera (if you want visual records; manage expectations — the lighting conditions and haze will make photography difficult)
- Audio recorder (the sound of the Floodworks is worth capturing, if only to confirm to yourself later that it was real)
- Sample containers if you want to collect water, rust-ore, or the greenish-black film (bring several, sealed, and keep them isolated from your other gear)

## Mental Framework for the Visit

Here is what I would tell myself, standing at the entry point, looking out across the wet iron floor as the haze absorbs the distance and the sound comes up through my feet:

**You are visiting a machine.** The largest, oldest, most incomprehensible machine you will ever encounter, but a machine nonetheless. It has no will. It has no awareness. It has no opinion about your presence. It will not help you and it will not hinder you; it will simply continue doing what it does, and what it does is pump water, and it has been doing this longer than you can conceptualize, and it will continue doing this after you leave, and none of this is about you. This is not a place that means something. It is a place that *does* something. The meaning, if there is any, is yours to make or not.

**You are here to witness, not to understand.** The Floodworks resists understanding — not actively, not through concealment, but through sheer scale. You cannot comprehend the hydrology. You cannot map the pump network. You cannot answer the fundamental question of what the water is and where it comes from and what happens if the pumps stop. These questions are worth asking, but they are not worth answering *during your visit.* During your visit, your job is to see, to hear, to feel, to record, and to leave. Understanding is what you do afterward, in a dry room, in good light, with the vibration gone from your bones and the sound gone from your ears, when you can think clearly and the Floodworks is a memory rather than an environment.

**You will be changed by this.** Not transformed, not traumatized, not enlightened — but altered. The experience of standing on an infinite manufactured surface, hearing the breath of engines that have been running since before your species evolved, watching water seep up through iron with the patience of something that has already won — this experience rearranges something in the way you think about permanence, about purpose, about the relationship between the made and the natural. It is not a comfortable rearrangement. It is, however, a valuable one. Most travelers who return from the Floodworks report not fear but a kind of recalibration — a revised sense of where human effort sits on the spectrum between temporary and eternal. They report looking at bridges and buildings differently. They report listening to the sound of plumbing in their walls and feeling, briefly, a kinship with something vast and old and indifferent.

Let this happen. Don't fight it. It's the whole point.

## After You Leave

Decontamination first. Strip the dry suit and clean it with fresh water. Shower thoroughly — your skin will have been enclosed in synthetic material in high humidity for days and will need air and clean water. Check for any irritation, rash, or discoloration, particularly on hands and feet where the suit seals were tightest and where any moisture breach would have concentrated.

Your hearing will feel strange for a day or two. The absence of the pump-throb is, paradoxically, louder than the throb itself. Your nervous system has adapted to a constant low-frequency input and will, briefly, hallucinate it in its absence — a phantom vibration, like the ghost buzz of a phone that isn't ringing. This fades. If it doesn't fade within a week, see an audiologist.

Your sleep may be disrupted for a few nights. The Floodworks has no day/night cycle, and even a short visit can unsettle your circadian rhythm. Bright light exposure and melatonin supplementation help.

Process the experience with your team. Talk about what you saw, what you felt, what the infrasound did to your mood, whether you saw the shapes in the channels. Compare notes. The Floodworks is large enough and disorienting enough that two people standing side by side may have genuinely different experiences of the same place, and reconciling those experiences is part of integrating the visit.

And then, when you're ready, sit quietly somewhere warm and dry and think about the pumps. Think about them running right now, as you sit there. Running as they were before you arrived and as they will be after every human you know is gone. Think about the water pressing up from below, patient, dark, neither malicious nor benign but simply *present*, and the iron floor holding, and the engines turning, and the sound rolling through the haze in waves that no one is there to hear.

The Floodworks continues. It does not require your attention. But having given it your attention, you will find that some part of your mind continues to attend to it — a low background awareness, felt in the sternum more than the head, that somewhere, in some fold of the multiverse, the pumps are still running.

They are still running.

They are still running.
