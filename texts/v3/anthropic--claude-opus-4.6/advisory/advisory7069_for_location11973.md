# Preparing for the Cisterns of Kav Morat

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Sit with it for longer than feels necessary. The Cisterns of Kav Morat are not dangerous in the way that most hazardous destinations are dangerous. There is nothing here that will attack you, chase you, poison you suddenly, or collapse beneath you. The danger — and there is danger — is environmental, cumulative, and psychological. It is the danger of a place that is totally saturated, infinitely patient, and profoundly indifferent to your presence. You are not unwelcome. You are simply irrelevant to the plateau's purpose, whatever that purpose is, and the plateau will not adjust itself to accommodate your biological needs.

You are visiting a landscape-scale artifact that has no ecology, no shelter in the conventional sense, no food sources, no dry ground, and no visible sun. The air is thin and saturated with moisture. The light is constant, even, and shadowless. There are no landmarks in the navigational sense — the cisterns repeat with variations but without hierarchy, and the horizon is luminous and featureless. You will be walking on perpetually wet tile at altitude, surrounded by thousands of open basins of mineral-laden water, in cool air that never moves, under a sky that never changes.

This is a place where you can die comfortably and slowly of exposure, dehydration (paradoxically), disorientation, or simple despair, all while surrounded by more water than you have ever seen in your life. Prepare accordingly.

## Physical Preparation

### Footwear

This is the single most critical equipment decision you will make. The tile surfaces of Kav Morat are perpetually wet. Not merely damp — wet, with a thin film of mineral water that is constantly being replenished by seepage through the grout and condensation from the saturated air. The tile is ceramic, smooth, cream-colored, and slightly concave. The walkways between cisterns are narrow, often barely wide enough for two people, and they are bordered on both sides by open water basins filled to within a hand's breadth of the edge. A fall here doesn't mean tumbling down a slope. It means stepping sideways into a cistern that may be six feet deep, full of mineral water with an unusual viscosity, while wearing whatever you brought with you.

You need footwear with exceptional wet-surface grip. Not merely waterproof — waterproof is irrelevant, because your feet will be wet regardless — but *grippy on wet smooth ceramic*. Think along the lines of what competitive sailors or technical divers wear: soft rubber soles with aggressive siping (the thin slits cut into the rubber that channel water away from the contact patch), ideally with a non-marking compound designed for polished wet surfaces. Approach shoes, hiking boots, trail runners — none of these are appropriate. Their tread patterns are designed for dirt, gravel, and rock. On wet tile, they will hydroplane.

The best option is probably a purpose-acquired pair of deck shoes or wet-wading boots with a razor-siped sole. Test them before you go. Find a tile surface — a bathroom floor, a commercial kitchen — wet it thoroughly, and walk on it. Turn. Stop suddenly. Step sideways. If you feel any slip at all, find different shoes.

The soles should also be thin enough to maintain proprioceptive contact with the surface. The description mentions feeling a faint vibration through the soles of your feet — the subsonic movement of water through the channel system. This may serve as a navigational aid or an early warning system for changes in the plateau's behavior. You want to be able to feel the ground. Thick, cushioned soles will isolate you from information you may need.

The uppers should be minimal and fast-draining. Your feet will be wet the entire time. Neoprene booties (2-3mm) are a reasonable option if you can find ones with appropriate soles, as they will maintain warmth even when saturated. Avoid anything with a leather upper or absorbent lining — it will become waterlogged, heavy, and will never dry in that atmosphere. Avoid anything with metal eyelets or hardware — the mineral content of the water is uncharacterized and may be corrosive to certain metals.

Bring a second pair. If one pair fails — a sole delaminates, a seam splits — you cannot afford to be barefoot on wet tile for the duration of your visit. Going barefoot might seem appealing given the conditions, but bare skin on wet mineral tile has almost no grip at all, and a fall into a cistern while barefoot means you have nothing protecting your feet from the chiseled channel edges and brick surfaces underwater.

### Clothing

The environment is cool, thin-aired, perpetually damp, and shadowless. There is no wind. The light is bright and diffuse. These conditions dictate a very specific clothing strategy.

**Base layer:** Synthetic or merino wool, form-fitting, moisture-wicking only in the sense that it manages the moisture already on your skin — because nothing will dry here. The air is at or near full saturation. Evaporative cooling is not going to happen. Wicking fabrics work by spreading moisture across a larger surface area to promote evaporation, but when the air cannot accept more water vapor, the fabric simply stays wet and spread-out. Merino wool is preferable because it retains insulating properties when wet and resists odor over multi-day wear. Synthetic (polyester, nylon) is lighter and more durable but will feel clammy faster.

**Mid layer:** A thin fleece or wool layer, primarily for insulation. The air is cool — the description says "cool and thin," consistent with a high-altitude environment, perhaps equivalent to 2,500-3,500 meters elevation based on the noted respiratory effect. At that altitude with saturated air and no sun, ambient temperature is likely in the range of 8-15°C (46-59°F). Without wind, this is manageable but not comfortable in just a base layer, especially when everything is damp. A 100-weight fleece that can tolerate being wet indefinitely is ideal. Avoid down. Avoid cotton. Avoid anything that loses its insulating properties when wet.

**Outer layer:** This is where conventional wisdom breaks down. A waterproof shell seems logical — you're in a wet environment — but consider: the moisture is coming from everywhere. From below (seepage through tile), from the air (near-100% humidity, condensation on every surface), from contact with structures (every wall, every surface, every bench-like form is damp). A waterproof shell will keep external moisture off your torso, but moisture will still enter at the neck, cuffs, and hem, and your own perspiration will be trapped inside because the humidity differential between your microclimate and the ambient air is near zero. Waterproof-breathable membranes work by exploiting a vapor pressure gradient — they push moisture from the humid interior to the drier exterior. When the exterior is as humid as or more humid than the interior, the membrane effectively becomes a plastic bag.

A better approach may be to accept wetness and focus on insulation. A wool or synthetic soft shell that sheds liquid water but does not attempt to be waterproof — something that allows airflow even in still conditions and maintains warmth when damp. The goal is not to stay dry. The goal is to stay warm while wet. This is a sea-kayaking mentality, not a hiking mentality.

**Legs:** Similar principles. Synthetic or wool base layer, with a durable outer layer that tolerates perpetual dampness. Quick-dry hiking pants are designed for occasional wetting followed by drying — they won't dry here. Wear something you're comfortable being wet in for the full duration. Board shorts over a merino base layer is honestly not a terrible option if the temperature is toward the warmer end of the expected range.

**Head and hands:** A wool or fleece beanie to retain heat. The diffuse bright light may cause eye strain — the description notes everything gleaming, the light bouncing between water and tile endlessly — so bring quality sunglasses with a photochromic or low-light lens. Polarized lenses would be ideal for cutting the glare off the water surfaces and potentially allowing you to see deeper into the cisterns, which may be useful for understanding the system. Thin synthetic gloves may be warranted if the temperature is cool enough, but you'll want to be able to feel surfaces, so fingerless or convertible-tip gloves are preferable.

### Hydration and the Paradox of Water

You are surrounded by more water than you could drink in a lifetime, and you should assume you cannot drink any of it.

The water in the cisterns carries a mineral turbidity — described as resembling thinned cream, with a faint viscosity and likely high dissolved calcium or similar mineral content. The description speculates "something calcium, somethingite" and notes it may be leached from the plateau substrate. Without chemical analysis, you must assume this water is not safe for consumption. High dissolved mineral loads can cause gastrointestinal distress, kidney stress, and depending on the specific minerals involved, potentially worse. The water may also contain dissolved compounds from the chalite waterproofing layer (described as chalky, dense, faintly blue) that could be toxic.

Furthermore, the water is described as lifeless — no algae, no biofilm, nothing biological. This is presented as possibly being due to the mineral content. Water that is hostile to all microbial life is water you should regard with extreme suspicion. It may simply be too alkaline, too mineral-rich, or it may contain something actively biocidal.

Bring all the water you will need for the duration of your visit, and then bring more. At altitude with saturated air, your hydration needs are complicated. You won't lose much moisture through perspiration (evaporation is negligible), but altitude increases respiratory water loss, and you'll be breathing deliberately due to the thin air. Estimate 2-3 liters per day minimum, more if you're exerting yourself navigating the complex. Carry it in sealed, durable containers — the environment will corrode or degrade anything that isn't inert.

**Do not** bring a filtration system expecting to use the cistern water. Standard backpacking filters remove particulates and biological contaminants. They do not remove dissolved minerals. Even a reverse-osmosis system would struggle with the volume of dissolved solids implied by the description. If you are staying long enough that carrying sufficient water is physically impossible, you have one option: collect the condensation from the air or from the collection surfaces before it enters the channel system and mixes with the mineral-laden cistern water. The condensation should be relatively pure — it's distilled by the evaporation-condensation cycle. Bring a clean collection vessel and find a condensation dish that you can intercept before its drip enters a channel. The yield will be slow — you're collecting droplets — but the water should be drinkable. Test it first if you have any means to do so.

### Food

There is nothing to eat here. Nothing grows. Nothing lives. Bring calorie-dense, moisture-resistant food for the full duration of your visit plus a safety margin. The saturated atmosphere will affect packaging — paper and cardboard will become soggy within hours. Use sealed, waterproof containers. Nuts, dried meat, hard cheese, dense energy bars with waterproof wrappers. Avoid anything that requires preparation or cooking — there is no fuel source mentioned, and you should not introduce combustion byproducts into an environment this still and enclosed. The air doesn't move. Smoke, fumes, or heat from a stove would linger around you indefinitely.

### Altitude Considerations

The "faint insufficiency" in the lungs and the need to breathe "more deliberately" suggest meaningful altitude. This is not extreme altitude sickness territory based on the description — you're conscious, functional, and ambulatory — but it's enough to notice. If you're coming from a low-elevation origin, acclimate before arrival if your transit method allows it. If not, move slowly for the first several hours, stay hydrated, and monitor yourself for headache, nausea, or unusual fatigue. The exertion level of navigating the cistern complex is low — you're walking on flat surfaces — but the constant alertness required for wet-tile footing and narrow walkways between open water will elevate your heart rate and breathing more than the physical effort alone would suggest.

## Navigation and Orientation

This is going to be a problem. Possibly the most significant practical problem you face.

The Cisterns of Kav Morat have no landmarks. The cisterns are variations on a theme — different sizes, different shapes, different internal structures — but they repeat without hierarchy. There is no central structure, no tallest tower, no distinctive feature that you can orient toward. The horizon is luminous and featureless. The sky is an unbroken white with no visible sun. There are no shadows — therefore no shadow-based direction finding, no time-of-day estimation, no east-west axis. The light is constant. The walkways branch and intersect without obvious pattern. The channels between cisterns follow a logic based on subtle grade variations that you cannot perceive visually.

You can get lost here in the most absolute sense possible: not just unable to find your way back, but unable to determine that you are lost, because every direction looks approximately the same. The environment offers no feedback on your position. You could walk in an enormous circle and never know it.

**Preparation for this:**

1. **Mark your entry point.** Whatever method you use to arrive, mark the location conspicuously and durably. Don't use anything that the moisture will degrade — no paper, no chalk (it will dissolve into the wet tile and become invisible). Use something physical and waterproof. A bright-colored waterproof gear bag left at the entry point. A stack of stones — except there are no loose stones here. Bring your own markers. Brightly colored waterproof tape adhered to a nearby wall or cistern edge. Something you can see from a distance against the uniform pale background.

2. **Trail marking.** As you explore, leave markers at every intersection or decision point. Bring a large supply of something small, bright, waterproof, and heavy enough not to be displaced (there's no wind, so displacement isn't a major risk, but you don't want markers falling into cisterns). Small waterproof adhesive dots applied to tile edges. A spool of brightly colored marine cord that you can lay along the walkways. The cord approach is probably best — it gives you a continuous trail to follow back — but you'll need a lot of it. The complex is vast.

3. **Compass and GPS.** Bring them but don't rely on them. You don't know if this plateau exists in a universe with the same magnetic field as yours, or if satellite systems (yours or anyone else's) have coverage here. A compass may work, may spin, may point in a direction that has no meaningful correspondence to your transit origin. GPS almost certainly won't function. What they can do, if they work at all, is give you a consistent reference direction. Even if compass north here doesn't mean anything objectively, it gives you a fixed bearing that you can use to maintain consistent travel — "I walked compass-north for two hours, so I need to walk compass-south for two hours to return."

4. **Dead reckoning.** Count your paces. Note turns. Keep a written log on waterproof paper (dive slates, Rite-in-the-Rain notebooks) of your movements. "200 paces bearing 340, turned left at a deep rectangular cistern with stepped edges, 150 paces bearing 270..." This is tedious. It is also the most reliable way to return to your entry point if all other methods fail.

5. **The channel system as a map.** This may be the most important navigational insight available to you. The water in the channels flows according to gravity, following the subtle grade of the plateau. If you can determine the general direction of water flow — even though the grade is nearly imperceptible — you have a fixed reference axis. Water flows downhill. The downhill direction is consistent (unless the plateau has a complex surface topology, which is possible but which the description doesn't suggest). Bring a small spirit level or a container of water you can set on the tile to detect grade. Better yet, carry a few small, lightweight objects that you can place in the channels to observe their drift direction. The current is barely perceptible, so you'll need something sensitive — a tiny piece of something buoyant and visible. But be mindful of introducing foreign material into the system. Use something inert. A small bead of sealed, waterproof material. Watch it, note the direction, retrieve it.

6. **Elevation.** You cannot see the edges of the plateau. But a plateau has edges. If you walk consistently in one direction, you should eventually reach a margin. Whether that margin is safe to approach, what lies beyond it, and whether the construction simply continues over the edge and down the slope — these are unknowns. But the edge, if you find it, would be a landmark, and following it would give you a navigable perimeter. This is a long-game strategy for extended visits, not a practical approach for a brief survey.

## Psychological Preparation

This is where I want to spend real time, because the Cisterns of Kav Morat present a psychological environment unlike most destinations a multiverse traveler will encounter, and the risks are subtle and cumulative.

### The Absence of Life

There is nothing alive here. Let that settle. No plants, no animals, no insects, no microbial film, no algae. Nothing. You will be the only living thing on the plateau for as far as you can see in every direction. For most people, this produces an initial sense of calm — the quiet, the cleanliness, the order — followed by a gradually intensifying unease that can escalate into something much more difficult.

Humans are calibrated for environments that contain life. Even in a desert, there are insects, birds, lizards, tough scrub plants. Even in arctic environments, there are microbes in the snow, the possibility of a passing bird. The complete absence of life is something most humans have never experienced, and it produces a specific kind of loneliness that is qualitatively different from mere solitude. It is the loneliness of being a category error — a biological entity in a mineral world. You don't belong here. Not in the sense that you're unwelcome, but in the sense that the concept of "belonging" doesn't apply. You are not part of the system. The cisterns were not built for you. The water is not for you. The entire plateau is executing a purpose that does not include you, and it will continue executing that purpose whether you are standing on it or not.

This can produce, over time, a dissociative response — a feeling that you, too, are becoming inanimate. That the moisture on your skin is not condensation but a kind of mineral encrustation. That your stillness, when you pause, is becoming permanent. That the plateau is absorbing you into its infrastructure the way it absorbed the bedrock beneath it.

**Counter this actively.** Talk to yourself. Sing. Bring a recording of familiar music and play it — the dead acoustics of the plateau will swallow the sound quickly, but hearing a human voice, even a recorded one, will anchor you. Bring a journal and write in it — the act of forming words about your experience keeps you in the role of observer rather than component. Bring something alive if you can — a sealed terrarium, a living plant in a waterproof container, even a small culture of yogurt or sourdough starter. Something that is metabolizing, growing, doing what living things do. Keep it visible. Touch it. It is proof that biology still functions here, that you are still in a universe where things grow.

### The Sameness

The environment is relentlessly, magnificently, oppressively uniform. Pale tile, milky water, white sky, diffuse light. No shadows, no color variation to speak of, no contrast. This will affect your perception over time. Without visual variety, the brain begins to lose its calibration for distance, size, and time. You may find that cisterns that seemed far away are suddenly close, or that you've been walking for what feels like minutes but has been hours. The shadowless light provides no time cues. There is no sunset coming. There is no noon to mark the halfway point. The light just *is*.

**Bring a reliable watch.** Not a phone — the moisture will kill it. A waterproof watch with a simple analog display. Check it regularly. Set alarms for meals, for hydration, for navigation logging, for turnaround time. Give your time here structure, because the environment will not provide any. Without external structure, the plateau's own rhythm — that slow, subsonic hum, that tidal breathing — will become your rhythm, and its timescale is not human. It has the patience of geology. You do not.

### The Pressure

The description mentions "a slow pressure against the underside of your thoughts." Take this literally. There is something about this place — the subsonic vibration, the mineral water, the saturated air, the sheer density of purposeful construction — that exerts a kind of cognitive pressure. It is not painful. It is not even unpleasant, exactly. It is more like the feeling of being in a room where someone is about to speak — an anticipatory tension, a sense that something is imminent, something is gathering. The plateau is waiting for something, and if you stay long enough, you will start waiting too, and you may not know what you're waiting for, and you may not want to leave until it arrives.

This is the most dangerous thing about Kav Morat. Not the wet tile, not the altitude, not the mineral water. The *waiting*. The plateau is patient in a way that is contagious. Travelers have presumably (in the logic of the multiverse) stayed longer than they intended. Sat on one of the bench-like forms, dipped their hand in a cistern, watched the slow drift of water through a channel, and forgotten that they had somewhere to be. Forgotten that they were visitors. Forgotten that the plateau's purpose is not their purpose.

**Set a hard departure time before you arrive.** Write it on your arm in waterproof marker. Set multiple alarms. Tell someone — if there is anyone to tell — when you expect to return. And when the alarm goes off, leave. Even if you feel like you're on the verge of understanding something. Even if the hum seems to be resolving into meaning. Even if the light seems to be shifting. Leave. You can come back. The plateau will still be here. It has the patience to wait for your return, and you should have the discipline to insist on the departure.

## Behavioral Guidelines

### Moving Through the Space

Walk slowly. Always. The walkways are narrow, wet, and bordered by open water. There are channel grooves crossing your path every few paces — easy to catch a toe on if you're not watching. The low curbs along walkway edges are ankle height, enough to trip on but not enough to prevent a fall into a cistern. Keep your eyes on your footing. Resist the temptation to look around while walking — stop, look, then walk again.

When stepping over channels, step *over*, not *in*. The channels are sharply carved with fresh edges that could cut skin, and disrupting the water flow might have consequences you can't predict. The system is interconnected. A disruption in one channel could propagate. Probably not dramatically, but you're a guest in someone else's infrastructure. Don't interfere with it.

Do not enter the cisterns. Do not swim. Do not wade. You don't know how deep they are (some are at least six feet), you don't know what's at the bottom, the water has unusual viscosity that could impede swimming, and getting out would require hauling yourself up onto a wet tile edge with no handholds. If you fall in — and this is a real risk — stay calm, move to the nearest edge, and use the stepped features present in some cisterns to climb out. If there are no steps, find a channel outlet or sluice gate that you can grip. The brick pedestals that rise from some cistern centers could also serve as emergency handholds. Get out quickly. The water is warmer than the air, which might feel pleasant, which is exactly why you should get out quickly. Comfort in this environment is a trap.

### Interacting with the Infrastructure

Touch things if you want. Examine the grout thumbprints, feel the trowel marks, inspect the tile work. This is one of the great pleasures of Kav Morat — the haptic richness of hand-built construction at inhuman scale. But interact respectfully. Don't pry at tiles. Don't chip mortar. Don't attempt to raise or lower the sluice gates — you don't understand the hydraulic system, and you don't know what changing the water distribution would do. The gates are all currently open. Leave them open.

The niches in the freestanding walls — the empty brick-sized recesses — will tempt you to place something in them. A marker, a small object, a token. Resist this temptation. You don't know what they're for. Filling a niche that was left deliberately empty could have meaning in a system you don't understand. Similarly, if you find anything in a niche that wasn't visible from your initial approach — an object, a deposit, a residue — do not remove it. Document it. Photograph it if your equipment allows. Leave it.

### The Subsonic Hum

You will feel it. Everyone does, presumably. It registers below hearing, as a vibration in the feet or a pressure in the chest or — as described — a sensation against the underside of your thoughts. It has a rhythm. The rhythm is slow. Do not attempt to synchronize with it. Do not sit still for extended periods trying to "tune in" to it. Do not meditate on it. I say this not because it is inherently harmful but because it appears to be the primary mechanism by which the plateau erodes your sense of urgency and replaces it with its own timescale. Engaging with the hum deliberately is like staring into deep water — it invites descent.

If you find the hum becoming more noticeable, more compelling, more *meaningful* — this is your cue to move, to speak, to eat something, to check your watch, to consult your navigation log. Activity and purposeful cognition are your defenses against the plateau's patient gravity.

## Philosophical Preparation

I include this section because Kav Morat is not merely a physical destination. It raises questions that will occupy you during your visit and after, and thinking about them in advance — developing at least a preliminary framework — will help you process the experience rather than being submerged by it.

**Who built this, and why?** You will not answer this question during your visit. The builders are absent. They left only the evidence of their hands — the thumbprints, the trowel marks, the kiln-batch variations. The construction tells you they were numerous, patient, and working with a consistent shared understanding of purpose. It does not tell you what the purpose is. The cisterns hold water in reserve. Water that is lifeless, mineral, purposeful. Reserved for what? You will want to know. The plateau will not tell you. Accept this before you go. You are visiting the instrument, not the performance. The symphony has not yet begun. You may never hear it.

**What does it mean to build something this large by hand?** The scale is staggering and the method is primitive — hand tools, hand-formed brick, finger-applied mortar. This is not a contradiction. This is a statement about time and priority and the nature of devotion to a purpose. Whatever the builders' purpose is, they valued it enough to entomb a plateau in tile, by hand, one thumbprint at a time. Sitting with this — the sheer human-hours of labor, the generations of hands — is part of the experience. Let it humble you. Let it confuse you. Both reactions are appropriate.

**What is the plateau waiting for?** You will feel the anticipation. It is architectural. It is hydraulic. It is built into the system the way tension is built into a drawn bow. The cisterns are full. The system is primed. Something has not yet happened. You will want to be present when it happens. This wanting is the most seductive and most dangerous thing Kav Morat will do to you. Recognize it as belonging to the place, not to you. You did not come here to wait. You came here to visit. Hold the distinction.

## A Final Note

When you leave Kav Morat — and you must leave; set your alarm, honor it — you will carry the moisture with you for a while. Not just on your skin and in your clothes, but in the pace of your breathing, in the deliberateness of your steps. You will find yourself walking slowly on dry ground, listening for a hum that isn't there. You will look at ordinary tile floors with new attention, noticing the grout, imagining the hands. You will be thirsty in a way that water doesn't quite fix, because the thirst isn't for water — it's for the patience you left behind, the sense of being in the presence of something vast and purposeful and almost ready.

This is normal. It passes. But it leaves a residue, like the mineral film on the cistern walls, and you may find yourself wanting to go back.

You can go back. But prepare again first. Check your equipment. Set your alarms. Write your departure time on your arm. The plateau will still be there, still waiting, still full, still humming its slow subsonic hum beneath a white sky that has no shadow and no sun.

And one day — perhaps — whatever it's waiting for will come. And you'll want to have been ready for that too, though no one can tell you what that preparation would look like, because no one has seen it yet.

The cisterns are full. The channels are flowing. Everything is new.

Go carefully. Come back.
