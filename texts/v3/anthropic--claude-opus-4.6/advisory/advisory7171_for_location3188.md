# Preparing for the Tiered Works of Grellam Shelf

## A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Preliminary Psychological Preparation

Before you pack a single item, before you study a single map (and the maps will fail you, but we'll get to that), you need to prepare the organ that will be under the most sustained assault in the Works: your mind.

### Understanding the Nature of the Challenge

The Works are not dangerous in the way a jungle full of predators is dangerous, or a frozen waste, or a war zone. The Works are dangerous in the way that a very long, very quiet hallway is dangerous to someone prone to intrusive thoughts. The threat is ontological. You will spend your time in a place where the fundamental rules of spatial reality are not so much broken as politely ignored, and the human mind was not built to handle that with grace.

**Start with meditation practice.** Not the relaxation kind — the awareness kind. Vipassana, if you have access to a teacher. Zazen, if you prefer structure. What you need is the ability to observe your own perceptual experience without immediately narrativizing it. When a bloom occurs — when space acquires that additional axis and you can suddenly see the interior cross-section of a wall and the layered innards of a cable simultaneously — you need to be able to witness that experience without your brain scrambling to build a story about what is happening. The story is where the panic lives. The raw experience, people report, is actually quite gentle. It's the interpretation that breaks you.

**Spend time with impossible objects and optical illusions before departure.** Penrose triangles. Escher prints. Necker cubes. Not to desensitize yourself — you cannot desensitize yourself to a bloom any more than you can desensitize yourself to suddenly having a new sense organ — but to build a kind of cognitive flexibility, an acceptance that perception and reality are not always in agreement and that this disagreement is survivable.

**Practice spatial reorientation exercises.** Spend time in environments that challenge your sense of direction. Caves. Multi-level parking structures. The older parts of cities where the street grid breaks down. Practice being lost and not panicking. Practice arriving somewhere unexpected and continuing to function. In the Works, you will walk a straight corridor and end up where you started. You need to have already made peace with the concept that your legs did their job correctly and space itself was the unreliable actor.

**Consider working with a therapist who specializes in existential anxiety before departure, if you have access to one.** The Works provoke a very specific form of distress in some travelers: the realization that the world you are standing in was entirely made, that there is no natural substrate beneath it, that "down" may not terminate in anything at all. This is not the kind of fear that adrenaline addresses. It's the kind that sits in the back of your skull at three in the morning. Having language for it, having practiced sitting with it, will serve you enormously.

### The Slowness Problem

Most multiverse travelers are accustomed to locations that present acute challenges — environments that will kill you in hours if you're unprepared, cultures that demand immediate social navigation, physical dangers that require quick reflexes. The Works will not kill you in hours. The Works will, if anything goes wrong, kill you over weeks, through disorientation, through the gradual erosion of your confidence in physical reality, through the quiet despair that comes from being unable to find your way back to a community and wandering deeper into the structure instead of shallower.

Prepare yourself for a slow experience. Bring activities for long periods of stillness. A journal. A sketchbook. A practice of some kind — knitting, carving, anything that keeps your hands busy and your mind anchored in the tactile world. The brightness never changes. The temperature barely varies. The silence presses in. Without an anchor to the sensory present, time becomes slippery, and people have reported losing hours — sitting down to rest on a walkway, watching the rafter birds wheel through the golden air, and standing up to find that what felt like twenty minutes was the better part of a day.

## II. Physical Preparation and Fitness

### Cardiovascular and Joint Conditioning

The Works are a vertical environment. You will be climbing stairs. Enormous quantities of stairs. The elevators exist, yes, and we will discuss them at length, but any traveler with an ounce of sense will use the stairs for routine vertical movement, because the stairs go where they appear to go (mostly) and the elevators go where the Works want them to go (always).

The staircases are industrial. Metal grating. Steep risers. Spiral configurations that can run for dozens of stories without a landing. Your knees, your ankles, your hips, and your cardiovascular system need to be ready for sustained vertical travel under load (because you will be carrying supplies).

**Begin a stair-climbing regimen at least three months before departure.** If you have access to a building with twenty or more stories, climb it daily, wearing a loaded pack. Build up to the point where you can ascend and descend thirty stories without stopping and without significant distress. The Works will ask more of you than this — some tier-to-tier transitions involve climbs equivalent to forty or fifty stories — but thirty will get you through most of the habitable middle tiers.

**Pay particular attention to joint health.** The alloy surfaces of the Works are hard and unforgiving. There is no soil, no give, no cushioning anywhere. Every step you take is on metal or ceramic or compacted polymer. Your joints will take a beating. Strengthen the musculature around your knees and ankles. Consider glucosamine supplementation if your joints are prone to inflammation. Stretch religiously.

**Cold tolerance.** The Works are cool — not dangerously cold, but persistently, endlessly cool in the way that large metal structures in a thermally stable environment are cool. The kind of cool that you don't notice for the first hour and that has settled into your bones by the sixth. Your body will be spending energy on thermoregulation constantly. Factor this into your caloric planning.

### Vision

Your eyes will be the most overworked part of your body after your legs. The perpetual bright, golden-white illumination of the light-channels means you will never get a break from photon bombardment. There is no night. There is no dimness except in the deep tiers, and you should not be going to the deep tiers.

**Have your eyes examined before departure.** Ensure your prescription is current if you wear corrective lenses. Bring a backup pair. Bring a third pair if you can manage it. There are no opticians in the Works.

**Train yourself to sleep in bright conditions.** The Works do not get dark. Even the inhabited communities, which have rigged up curtains and partitions and improvised shutters, are never truly dark — the light bleeds through, reflects off alloy surfaces, finds its way into every corner. You need to be able to fall asleep in conditions equivalent to a brightly lit room. Practice this before departure. Sleep with the lights on. Use a sleep mask if you must, but learn to sleep without one as well, because you may lose it.

## III. Equipment and Gear

### The Non-Negotiables

**Footwear.** This is the single most important piece of equipment you will bring. You need boots — proper boots, with thick soles, ankle support, and aggressive tread. The alloy floor-plates of the Works are not uniformly flat. They have grooves, seams, rivets, drainage channels, patches of threadwire, and occasional areas where condensation has made the surface slick with a thin film of oily moisture. You will be walking on metal grating, climbing metal stairs, traversing catwalks with open mesh floors through which you can see the tier below. You need footwear that grips, that protects, that supports.

Bring two pairs. Alternate them daily. The condensation will work its way into your boots no matter what you do, and having a drier pair to switch to while the other airs out (insofar as anything can "air out" in the Works' sluggish, barely-moving atmosphere) will prevent the skin problems that plague long-term residents.

The soles should be insulating. The alloy floor-plates conduct heat away from your feet with quiet efficiency, and cold feet are not merely uncomfortable — they are the beginning of a cascade of thermoregulatory problems that will sap your energy and compromise your judgment.

**Eye protection.** Bring high-quality tinted goggles or glasses with adjustable light filtering. Not sunglasses — the light in the Works is not sunlight, and standard UV filtering is not the issue. You need something that can reduce overall brightness by 40-60% while preserving color accuracy. The golden-white light of the light-channels is information-rich; you want to dim it, not distort it. Many residents of the Works have adapted physiologically — their pupils have learned to contract more than baseline human — but you will not have this adaptation, and the first few days of unrelieved brightness will give you headaches that feel like someone is pressing their thumbs into the backs of your eyeballs.

Also bring a pair of fully clear, sealed goggles — the kind a welder might use as a base layer. The condensation occasionally produces drips from overhead surfaces, and the oily residue in the moisture is an irritant. Getting it in your eyes is painful and temporarily blinding.

**A sleep mask.** I know I said to train yourself to sleep without one. Do that. But bring one anyway. It's a small item and its value on the nights when you're exhausted and disoriented and the light is relentless cannot be overstated.

**Gloves.** Thin, dexterous gloves for general wear — the alloy surfaces will leach heat from your hands every time you grip a railing or lean against a wall, and over time this becomes maddening. Also a pair of heavier gloves for any situation where you need to move threadwire. The wire is not sharp exactly, but it has a fibrous, abrasive quality, and handling it with bare skin over time produces small cuts that heal slowly in the Works' damp, cool environment.

**Layered clothing.** The temperature is stable but cool — somewhere around 12-15°C (54-59°F) in the middle tiers, slightly warmer in the lower tiers, slightly cooler in the upper ones. You want a moisture-wicking base layer, an insulating mid-layer, and a shell that can handle the persistent dampness without becoming waterlogged. Nothing cotton. Cotton holds moisture and the Works are all moisture. Synthetic or merino wool base layers. A fleece or light down mid-layer. A light, breathable waterproof shell.

Bring more socks than you think you need. Then bring more. The condensation gets into everything, and dry socks are a currency of comfort in the Works.

### Navigation Equipment

**A compass will be of limited use.** The Works are saturated with metallic mass and ambient electromagnetic fields, and a magnetic compass will point in whatever direction the nearest pipe bundle suggests, which is not north. However, bring one anyway. In areas free of spatial anomalies, a compass can at least tell you whether you're maintaining a consistent heading, even if you don't know what that heading is. When the compass starts spinning — or worse, when it locks firmly onto a bearing that you know is wrong because you've been walking in a straight line and it's tracking through ninety degrees — you have information. You know you're in or near a fold or seam. That information has value.

**A pedometer or step counter.** In the absence of reliable spatial navigation, the most useful information is often the simplest: how far have you walked? How many stairs have you climbed or descended? In a fold, your step count will tell you that you've walked farther than the apparent length of the corridor. In a seam, the discrepancy between stairs climbed and tiers traversed will alert you to the anomaly. These are not precise instruments of navigation; they are anomaly detectors. Treat them as such.

**Chalk or paint markers.** The oldest and most reliable navigation technique in the Works. Mark your path. Mark every intersection, every staircase entrance, every elevator door. Use a consistent notation system decided before you arrive — an arrow for direction of travel, a number for sequence, a symbol for anomalies encountered. The residents will recognize what you're doing and leave your marks alone; path-marking is a universally respected practice.

Be aware that in fold zones, you may encounter your own marks ahead of you. This is expected. It is also deeply unnerving. Prepare yourself for it.

**String or cord.** In areas where spatial anomalies are dense, some travelers unspool a thin cord behind them as they walk, anchored at their starting point. This is effective for short explorations — a few hundred meters at most. Beyond that, the cord becomes a tripping hazard in the narrow corridors, and other travelers or fauna may disturb it. But for investigating a suspected fold — walking forward and seeing if your cord crosses your path from behind — it is invaluable.

**A detailed notebook.** Not for journaling (though you should journal — see the section on psychological maintenance). For mapmaking. You will be making your own maps. Every traveler in the Works makes their own maps, because no two people's experience of the Works' spatial layout is entirely consistent. Maps are personal documents in the Works. They are records of one person's path through a space that is not obligated to present the same geometry to the next person.

Record everything. Tier numbers, where visible. Distinctive features — an unusually large junction box, a threadwire mat of a particular color, a drainage channel with a memorable curve. The position of stairs relative to the platform edge. The direction of drainage flow (which is one of the few consistently reliable directional indicators — fluid flows downhill, even in the Works, even in the folds). The location and behavior of elevator shafts.

**Do not rely on electronic mapping devices.** The electromagnetic environment of the Works plays havoc with sensitive electronics. Devices that depend on GPS are useless — there is no sky, no satellites, no external reference frame. Devices that use inertial navigation will accumulate errors rapidly in fold zones. Simple mechanical and analog tools — compass, pedometer, chalk, notebook — are more reliable than anything with a processor.

### Lighting

You might think you don't need supplemental lighting. The Works are, after all, relentlessly bright. But there are exceptions, and the exceptions are dangerous.

The light-channels occasionally have gaps — stretches of a few meters or a few dozen meters where the luminous substance has been exhausted or displaced. These gaps create pockets of relative darkness that your eyes, fully adapted to the overwhelming brightness, will experience as near-total blindness. The transition from full illumination to a light-channel gap is instantaneous and disorienting.

Carry a headlamp with a red-light mode. Red light will preserve your adaptation to the brightness better than white. Use it only in the gap zones. Bring spare batteries — more than you think you need — because the ambient electromagnetic fields can accelerate battery drain in some devices.

For deep-tier exploration (which, again, you should not be doing, but if you must): carry a primary headlamp, a secondary handheld light, and chemical light sticks as a backup. The deep tiers are not fully dark, but the light that reaches them is attenuated and strange, and electronic lights have been reported to flicker and fail in ways that seem inconsistent with simple battery depletion.

### Sustenance

**Water.** The condensation that collects on surfaces throughout the Works is technically water, but it carries dissolved metals, trace polymers, and an oily organic residue that is the metabolic byproduct of cable mite biofilm. It is not immediately toxic to humans, and the residents drink it after filtering — but their gut flora has adapted over generations to handle the contaminants. Your gut flora has not.

Bring a high-quality water filtration system. A pump filter with activated carbon is the minimum. A filter with both carbon and ceramic elements is better. A distillation setup — even a small, portable solar still (though "solar" is the wrong word; "luminal still" might be more appropriate, using the light-channel radiance as a heat source) — is best for extended stays.

You should plan to carry at least two liters of clean water at all times, plus your filtration equipment. Dehydration is a real risk — the cool, dry-ish air of the Works promotes insensible water loss that you won't notice because you're not sweating, and the brightness discourages sleep, which means you'll be active for longer periods and burning through water faster than you expect.

**Food.** There is no agriculture in the Works. The residents subsist on a combination of traded goods (some communities have established trade routes to other locations in the multiverse, though these connections are intermittent and unreliable), cultivated cable-mite preparations (the mites are edible; they taste like metallic shrimp, apparently, and are a reliable protein source once you get past the texture), gutter-eel jerky, and a kind of flatbread made from a starchy paste cultivated from certain threadwire-adjacent biofilms.

As a traveler, you should bring your own food. High-calorie, compact, non-perishable. Energy bars, nuts, dried fruit, jerky, dehydrated meals if you can heat water (and you can — the thermal gradient in the Works means there are always surfaces warm enough to heat a pot on, particularly near the floor-plates of the lower tiers). Plan for roughly 3,000-3,500 calories per day, accounting for the stair-climbing, the constant low-grade thermoregulation, and the metabolic cost of sustained alertness in a spatially unstable environment.

If your stay extends beyond what you can carry, you will need to begin eating local food. Do this gradually. Start with the gutter-eel preparations, which are the most digestible for off-worlder stomachs. Move to cable-mite preparations cautiously. Avoid the biofilm-paste flatbread until your system has had at least two weeks to adjust, as it contains compounds that cause severe cramping in people not accustomed to it.

### Medical Supplies

**Standard first-aid kit** — bandages, antiseptic, pain relievers, anti-inflammatories, antihistamines.

**Antifungal cream and powder.** The persistent dampness and cool temperatures of the Works create ideal conditions for fungal skin infections, particularly in the feet and groin. Apply preventatively. Daily. Without fail.

**Anti-nausea medication.** Blooms cause nausea in roughly one-third of people who experience them. The nausea is usually transient — it passes within an hour — but it can be severe, and vomiting while disoriented in a spatially anomalous zone is a good way to compound your problems.

**Electrolyte supplements.** To compensate for the insensible water loss.

**Eye drops.** Artificial tears, preservative-free. The brightness and the air quality will dry your eyes relentlessly.

**A sedative.** Something mild. Something you've tested before and know how you react to. For the nights when the brightness and the silence and the impossibility of the geometry around you conspire to make sleep unachievable without chemical assistance. Use sparingly. But have it.

**Ear plugs.** Not for noise — the Works are quiet. For the sound the Works make. The breathing. The deep, sub-audible vibration that transmits through the floor-plates and up through your skeleton. Most people find it soothing after a while. Some people find it maddening. Earplugs won't block it — it's transmitted through bone conduction — but they'll reduce the audible component enough to take the edge off.

## IV. Understanding the Social Environment

### The Communities

The people of the Works live in clusters of 50-500 individuals, concentrated in the middle tiers — roughly Tier 80 through Tier 200, by the local numbering system, though the numbering system is approximate and contested. They have repurposed industrial spaces into living quarters: large chambers that might once have been storage bays or processing rooms, fitted out with partitions made from salvaged paneling, heated by redirected thermal conduits, furnished with items that are part scavenged, part traded, part handmade from threadwire and scrap.

These communities are neither hostile nor especially welcoming. They are cautious. The Works produce occasional travelers — people who emerge from the elevators or the staircases from tiers that no one has visited, speaking languages that no one in the community recognizes, carrying tools made from alloys that don't match the local metallurgy. The residents have learned to treat strangers with a kind of careful, practical neutrality: you are welcome to stay, to trade, to share information about the tiers you've visited. You are expected to contribute to communal work — primarily maintenance of the water filtration systems, monitoring of nearby spatial anomalies, and cultivation of cable-mite colonies. You are not expected to stay forever, and your departure, when it comes, will be accepted without ceremony.

**Bring trade goods.** Knowledge is the most valuable currency in the Works. If you have information about the layout of tiers that a community has not explored — particularly information about the location and behavior of seams, folds, and blooms — you can trade it for food, shelter, and guidance. Physical goods are also valued: tools (especially precision tools like calipers, small wrenches, and wire cutters), clean fabric, uncontaminated water, medical supplies, and anything that can be used to mark paths (chalk, paint, durable markers).

Do not bring electronic devices as trade goods unless they are extremely simple and robust. The electromagnetic environment will degrade them, and offering a tool that will fail within weeks is a fast way to destroy trust.

**Learn the vocabulary.** The three-hundred-plus terms for spatial anomalies are not academic curiosities — they are survival tools. When a resident tells you that the corridor ahead has a *stitch* (a micro-fold that displaces you three to five meters laterally without any perceptible transition), you need to understand what that means and how to navigate it (walk slowly, maintain contact with one wall, and be prepared to find yourself on the other side of the corridor from where you expected to be). When they warn you about a *deep breath* (a slow, periodic expansion and contraction of the space within a room, causing the walls to appear to move apart and together over a cycle of several minutes), you need to know that this is disorienting but not dangerous, as distinguished from a *gulp* (a sudden, non-periodic spatial contraction that can trap objects — or people — in compressed architectural features).

You will not learn all three hundred terms before you arrive. But learn the critical ones — fold, seam, bloom, stitch, gulp, deep breath, slipstair (a staircase that connects to a different tier each time it is used), and deadlight (a section where the light-channels have failed completely and the resulting darkness is so absolute that it seems to have physical weight). These are the anomalies most likely to affect your travel through the middle tiers.

### The Cartographers

There is a loose, multi-community guild of individuals who devote their lives to mapping the Works. They are brilliant, dedicated, and almost universally eccentric. They drink. They argue. They maintain archives of hand-drawn maps that fill entire rooms, stacked in layers that themselves sometimes seem to exhibit spatial anomalies (though this may be apophenia — when you spend your life studying impossible geometry, you start seeing it everywhere).

The cartographers are the closest thing the Works have to a scholarly class, and they will be interested in you — specifically, in where you came from, how you arrived, and whether you can provide any external reference frame for the Works' location within the multiverse. They have theories. Many theories. Some believe the Works are an artifact — built by someone, for some purpose, at some definable point in the past. Some believe the Works are a natural structure, a geological formation in a universe where the geology produces ironwork instead of limestone. Some believe the Works are a computational process — that the pipes carry data, the valves control flow, the light-channels are output displays, and the entire structure is executing a calculation so large and so slow that its purpose will not be apparent for eons.

Engage with the cartographers if you can. They are the best source of navigational information, and their maps — while not reliable in an absolute sense — represent the accumulated spatial knowledge of generations. A cartographer's map of the middle tiers will show you where the stable folds are, where the known seams connect, which staircases are reliable and which are slipstairs, and where the deadlights begin. This information can save your life.

In exchange, the cartographers will want your map. Your personal record of your path through the Works. Give it to them freely. It costs you nothing and adds to the collective understanding of a place that resists understanding.

## V. Navigating Spatial Anomalies

### General Principles

**Move slowly.** The anomalies of the Works are generally gradual and gentle, and they are much easier to detect and navigate at walking pace than at a run. If you feel that something is wrong — if the corridor seems longer than it should be, if the tier number on a staircase landing doesn't match your count, if the light seems to be coming from a direction that doesn't correspond to the light-channel above you — stop. Sit down. Take stock. Consult your notes. Mark your position. Then proceed slowly, observing.

**Trust your instruments over your senses.** Your step counter says you've walked 400 meters but the corridor appears to be only 100 meters long? Trust the step counter. Your chalk marks indicate you've been here before but you have no memory of this junction? Trust the chalk. Your senses are being processed by a brain that evolved in a spatially consistent environment, and that brain will smooth over, rationalize, and narrativize spatial anomalies in ways that are comforting but inaccurate. The instruments are stupid. Stupid is what you want.

**Maintain physical contact with surfaces when navigating known anomalies.** In a fold or stitch, keeping one hand on the wall gives you a continuous tactile reference that can help your brain stay oriented even when your visual and proprioceptive systems are providing contradictory information.

**If you experience a bloom, sit down immediately.** Do not try to walk during a bloom. The additional spatial dimension that your visual system is suddenly processing will override your balance and your proprioception. People who try to walk during blooms fall. Falling on the Works' metal surfaces is always painful and sometimes dangerous. Sit down, close your eyes if you need to, breathe, and wait for it to pass.

**Do not attempt to map or measure a bloom while it is occurring.** I know this is tempting. I know you will want to. Resist. The information your senses provide during a bloom is real — people have reported seeing the interior structures of walls and pipes and these descriptions have been confirmed by subsequent physical investigation — but the process of trying to record that information while your spatial cognition is in crisis is a recipe for a panic attack. Write down what you remember afterward. That's good enough.

### The Elevators

We need to talk about the elevators.

They exist. They are functional. The cables hum. The doors open and close. The panels have buttons — hundreds of them, most unlabeled, some warm to the touch, some that seem to depress slightly under your finger even before you push them. The elevators will take you places.

The problem is that you cannot reliably predict where.

An elevator that has been regularly used by residents to travel between Tiers 120 and 135 will generally continue to do so. Generally. The residents use the elevators for routine vertical transit within known, stable zones, and for these short, well-traveled routes, the elevators are reasonably predictable. But the moment you push a button that is outside the elevator's habitual range — a button that is unlabeled, or labeled in a script you don't recognize, or warm — you are committing yourself to a destination that may not correspond to any tier anyone has visited.

People have entered elevators on Tier 150, pressed an unfamiliar button, and exited on tiers where the architecture is made of materials no one in the middle-tier communities has ever seen. Tiers where the light-channels produce light of a different color. Tiers where the gravity is perceptibly different. Tiers where the rafter birds are the size of large dogs and build nests from what appears to be woven copper cable as thick as your wrist.

Some of these people found their way back. Some did not.

**Rules for elevator use:**

1. **Only use elevators that residents are actively using for known routes.** Ask which elevators are safe. They will tell you.
2. **Never press an unlabeled button.**
3. **Never press a warm button.**
4. **If an elevator opens and you did not call it and no one is inside, do not enter.** This is the single most important rule. The empty elevator is the Works' most famous temptation and its most reliable method of removing people from the known tiers. The cables hum. The interior is clean and well-lit. The panel glows. Everything about it suggests an invitation. Decline.
5. **If you must explore by elevator, do so with a partner. Agree on a time limit. Carry enough supplies for a multi-day return journey by stairs. Mark the elevator's location before you enter it.** Be aware that the elevator may not be in the same location when you need it again.

### The Deep Tiers

Everything below roughly Tier 50 (by the middle-tier numbering system) is considered the deep tiers. The light is dimmer. The temperature is warmer. The threadwire is thicker and more extensive. The condensation is heavier. The cable mites are larger and behave differently — less like individual organisms and more like a coordinated substrate, covering entire walls in a shimmering, slowly-moving layer.

The deep tiers are where the something-elses live. The large, slow shapes that no one has documented but everyone has heard. The grinding. The patient shifting of weight.

**Do not go to the deep tiers.**

I will not belabor this point, because you are an adult and a multiverse traveler and you understand risk. But I will say this: the deep tiers are where experienced residents — people who have lived their entire lives in the Works, who navigate folds and seams and blooms as casually as you navigate hallways — refuse to go. The rafter birds, which fear nothing in the middle tiers, will not descend below a certain point. The gutter eels, which live their entire lives in drainage channels and seem to have no spatial awareness beyond the next few meters of channel, will reverse direction rather than flow into a downward-sloping channel that leads to the deep tiers.

The silence is different down there. It is not the aggressive, library-like silence of the middle tiers. It is a listening silence. The silence of something that has noticed you.

If you go, go prepared for a multi-week expedition. Go with companions. Go with enough supplies to ascend under your own power if the stairs don't cooperate. Go with the understanding that the spatial anomalies are more frequent and more severe below Tier 50 — that folds are tighter, seams are less stable, blooms are more intense and longer-lasting, and there are anomaly types down there that the three-hundred-term vocabulary of the middle tiers has no names for, because no one who experienced them came back articulate enough to name them.

And if you hear the grinding — the slow, heavy, patient shifting of something very large across a metal surface, transmitted through the floor-plate and into your feet — go up. Do not investigate. Go up.

## VI. Ongoing Psychological Maintenance

### The Brightness

It does not stop. It will never stop. After three days, the absence of darkness will begin to feel like a form of pressure, as though the photons are accumulating on your skin. After a week, you will start to crave darkness with a physical intensity that surprises you — not sleep, necessarily, but *darkness*, the simple absence of light, the visual equivalent of silence. Your sleep mask will become one of your most treasured possessions.

Use it. Use it every time you sleep. Remove it slowly when you wake, giving your eyes time to adjust rather than going from total darkness to full brightness in an instant.

And find the deadlights. Not the dangerous ones — not the deep-tier deadlights where the darkness has physical weight and things move in it. The small ones. The little pockets of failed light-channel on the middle tiers, a few meters across, where the luminous substance has run out and the alloy surfaces are lit only by reflected glow from the surrounding brightness. The residents know where they are. Some communities have built rest areas near them — simple benches facing into the relative darkness, where people go to give their eyes a break. Spend time there. The relief is enormous.

### Journaling

Write every day. Write about what you saw, where you went, how you felt. Write about the anomalies you encountered, the people you spoke to, the sounds you heard. This is not just for your records. This is for your sanity.

The Works have a quality of unreality that accumulates over time. After days of walking through corridors that fold back on themselves, climbing stairs that skip tiers, watching your compass spin and your chalk marks appear ahead of you, you will begin to doubt your own experience. Did you really see that? Were you really on that tier? Is this really the same corridor you walked yesterday, or has a seam shifted and replaced it with a structurally identical corridor from a different part of the Works?

Your journal is your anchor. It is your record of what actually happened, written by you in the moment, before your memory had a chance to rationalize or distort. Read it regularly. Let it remind you of what is real.

### The Breathing

You will notice the Works breathing. The slow, sub-audible vibration in the floor-plates. The rhythmic quality of the condensation — heavier for a period of days, then lighter, then heavier again. The almost-perceptible expansion and contraction of the air in the inter-tier gaps.

People react to this differently. Some find it comforting — a heartbeat, a sign of life, a reassurance that the Works are stable and functional. Some find it terrifying — a reminder that the structure around them is not inert, that it is doing something, that they are inside something that is, in some definition of the word, alive.

If you are in the latter category, you will need coping strategies. The earplugs help with the audible component, but the vibration comes through the floor and the walls and the railings and cannot be blocked by anything short of full isolation from all surfaces — which, in the Works, is impossible. You could try to stay on the upper tiers, where the vibration is weakest, but this limits your access to communities and resources.

The most effective coping strategy, reported by multiple long-term residents, is acceptance. Not intellectual acceptance — you can intellectually accept that the floor is vibrating without that acceptance reaching the part of your brain that manages anxiety. Somatic acceptance. The kind of acceptance that lives in your muscles and your gut. Breathe with it. Match your inhale to the Works' inhale, your exhale to its exhale. Some residents describe this as a kind of meditation. Others describe it as surrendering. The distinction may not matter.

### Departure

The Works do not trap people. This is important. The Works do not have locked doors, or inescapable tiers, or spatial anomalies specifically designed to prevent departure. People leave the Works regularly, through the same multiverse transit points they used to enter — assuming they can find them again.

This is the critical caveat. The transit points are locations in the Works where the boundary between the Works and the rest of the multiverse is thin enough to cross. They are spatial anomalies — but anomalies of a different character from the folds and seams, anomalies that connect not to other parts of the Works but to other places entirely. They are uncommon. They are not well-marked. And, like all spatial features of the Works, they are not guaranteed to be stable.

**Before you arrive, identify your transit point as precisely as possible.** Mark it the moment you arrive. Mark the surrounding area extensively. Note its position relative to every landmark you can identify — tier number, staircase proximity, distinctive architectural features, threadwire patterns, drainage flow direction. Take photographs if your device is functional. Draw it. Describe it in words. Create enough redundancy in your records that you could find this location from multiple directions, even if some of your route markers have been disturbed.

Visit your transit point regularly during your stay, to confirm that it is still there and still functional. If it shifts — if it moves to a different location, or if the tier it's on becomes involved in a seam — you need to know immediately, not after days of searching.

**Have an exit plan. Always have an exit plan.**

The Works are extraordinary. They are one of the most remarkable locations in the accessible multiverse — a structure of impossible scale and impossible geometry, built by no one for no purpose, inhabited by a unique and tenacious ecosystem, humming with a slow patience that makes human time seem frantic and trivial. They reward the visitor who approaches them with respect, with patience, and with the right equipment.

But they are not home. They were not built for you. They do not care whether you stay or go, whether you thrive or falter, whether you find your way back to the transit point or wander deeper into the structure, tier after tier, staircase after staircase, corridor after corridor, the light never changing, the temperature never changing, the silence pressing in, the floor breathing beneath your feet, the elevator at the end of the hallway opening its doors and waiting, always waiting, patient as the threadwire, patient as geology, patient as whatever moves in the deep tiers and grinds against the metal in the dark.

Take the stairs.
