# Preparing to Visit the Plateaus of Gathered Stone

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description and resist your first impulse, which will be to plan this like a hiking trip. It is a hiking trip in the way that walking through Venice is a hiking trip — technically true but missing the point. You are entering a landscape-scale labyrinth with no map, no trails, no infrastructure, no inhabitants, and no center. The place is hundreds of miles across. You cannot see it all. You cannot see most of it. You will experience the fraction of it that your legs and your time and your navigation skills permit, and that fraction will be a thin thread drawn through an incomprehensibly vast structure. Make peace with that before you go.

The psychological preparation matters as much as the physical preparation, perhaps more. This place will do something unusual to your mind. It looks like a city. It sounds, in wind, like something designed. Every corner you turn will present you with forms that look intentional — doorways, corridors, plazas, ledges that look like shelves, recesses that look like alcoves. Your pattern-recognition systems will fire constantly, insisting that someone made this, that there is meaning in the arrangement, that the next passage leads somewhere on purpose. It doesn't. The stone broke along bedding planes. The water followed gravity. The result is architecture without an architect, and the cognitive dissonance of that — the persistent, low-grade sensation that you are in a place that *should* have meaning but doesn't — is the defining experience of being here. Some people find this profoundly peaceful. Some people find it deeply unsettling. Most people oscillate between the two. Know which way you tend to lean, and prepare accordingly.

## Physical Fitness and Movement Capacity

You need to be a competent walker on wet, uneven stone. This is not negotiable. The passage floors are perpetually damp — not flooded, not running with water, just filmed with moisture that makes every surface uncertain underfoot. You will be walking on limestone and slate that has been scalloped and grooved by millennia of seep-water. Some surfaces are smooth. Some are textured. Some are covered with a biological film — a thin layer of algae or microbial mat — that is essentially invisible until your foot slides on it. You need ankle stability, proprioceptive awareness, and the kind of patient, deliberate footfall that becomes automatic only after long practice on rocky terrain.

Your knees will take sustained low-grade abuse. The passages undulate. The floors are not level — they follow the tilt of the bedding planes, which means you are constantly adjusting to subtle grades, stepping over raised lips of stone where one stratum meets the next, navigating around rubble from wall collapses. If you have knee issues, address them beforehand. Trekking poles will help enormously, but they need rubber tips that grip wet stone, not carbide points that skitter on it.

You don't need to be extraordinarily fit in a cardiovascular sense. The plateau is relatively flat — the variation is in the labyrinth's internal topography, not in gross elevation change. But you need endurance. You will be walking for many hours a day if you want to cover any meaningful distance, and the constant micro-adjustments to wet, uneven terrain will fatigue your stabilizer muscles far faster than walking on a trail would. Train by walking on wet rock. Walk streambeds. Walk coastal rock shelves at low tide. Walk the worn stone floors of old cathedrals after rain. Get your feet educated.

If you plan to climb any of the remnant mesas — the watchtower-like columns that rise above the labyrinth — you need basic scrambling skills and a head for exposure. The description suggests these are climbable without technical gear, but the stone will be wet, the holds will be slick, and a fall from thirty or forty feet onto broken limestone is not survivable in a place with no rescue infrastructure. Be honest with yourself about whether the view is worth the risk. It probably is. But be honest.

## Footwear

This is the single most important gear decision you will make. Get it wrong and the trip becomes a misery of slipping, blistering, and anxiety.

You need shoes or boots that grip wet limestone. This means a sole compound optimized for wet rock — something like Vibram Megagrip or Stealth rubber, the kind used on approach shoes for climbing. Standard hiking boot soles, even good ones, are designed for dirt trails and will betray you here. The mineral film on the stone, the algae in the damp sections, the smooth water-worn surfaces in the drainage gutters — these demand a sole that maintains friction when wet.

The shoe needs to drain or at least not hold water. Your feet will get wet. There is no preventing this. Water is everywhere — seeping from walls, filming the floors, collecting in puddles you will step in because they are unavoidable in narrow passages. A waterproof boot will keep water out for a while and then, once it gets in over the top or through a seam, will hold it against your foot indefinitely. A non-waterproof shoe with good drainage and a quick-drying upper is a better choice. Your feet will be wet, but they will be consistently wet rather than trapped in a slowly warming puddle.

Bring at least two pairs. Rotate them. Dry the off-duty pair as much as conditions allow — which won't be much, given the ambient humidity, but any drying is better than none. Bring many pairs of socks. Merino wool, thin to medium weight. Change socks at least twice a day. Your feet are your transportation, your safety system, and your primary interface with the environment. Treat them with the seriousness they deserve.

Consider gaiters — not for waterproofing, but to keep small stones and grit out of your shoes. The passage floors are littered with tiny fragments of limestone and slate from ongoing erosion, and these work their way into shoes with ruthless efficiency, creating hot spots and blisters.

## Navigation

This is the central practical challenge of the visit, and you need to think about it carefully.

There is no map of the labyrinth. Even if one existed, it would be out of date — the place is actively rearranging itself through rockfalls and erosion. The passages are confusingly similar. The walls are the same stone, the same color, the same texture, varying only in the details of their lichen colonies and mineral staining. The overcast sky provides no sun position for orientation. The light is even and directionless. The labyrinth's internal geometry is fractal — self-similar at multiple scales — which means that local landmarks repeat in confusing variations throughout the maze.

You will get disoriented. Accept this. Plan for it.

**GPS and digital navigation:** If the multiverse instance of this place has GPS coverage (not guaranteed — check the specifics of the variant you're visiting), a GPS device with breadcrumb tracking is invaluable. Not for following a route, since there is no route, but for recording where you've been so you can return to your camp or entry point. Mark your camp as a waypoint. Mark every intersection. Mark the mesa tops you climb, the plazas you rest in, the edges of the plateau if you reach them. Download the GPS tracks each evening and study them. They will reveal the structure of the labyrinth around your position — the drainage directions, the passage orientations, the clustering of intersections — in a way that walking through it cannot.

If there is no GPS coverage, you fall back on traditional methods, and they are hard here.

**Compass:** Essential. The overcast will deny you celestial navigation most of the time. A compass gives you a consistent reference direction. Pick a bearing when you leave camp each morning and hold to it loosely, allowing for the labyrinth's refusal to offer straight paths. Note the bearing back to camp. When in doubt, reverse your bearing and work your way back. A compass alone won't prevent you from getting lost in the maze's internal complexity, but it will prevent you from becoming *catastrophically* lost — walking for days in the wrong gross direction.

**Marking:** This is ethically and practically complex. The instinct is to mark your path — leave chalk marks on walls, stack small cairns at intersections, tie flagging tape to projections. The place has no cultural prohibition against this (no one is here to object), but the moisture will dissolve chalk within days, rain will topple cairns, and flagging tape will become one more piece of debris in a landscape that has no debris. If you mark, use methods that will degrade naturally. Arrange small stones in patterns at intersections — triangles pointing the way back. The next rain may scatter them, so don't rely on them for return navigation, but they help in the short term.

**Dead reckoning:** Count your paces. Note your turns. Keep a written log. "Left at the orange lichen colony. Right at the plaza with the three rubble boulders. Straight through the leaning-wall section for 400 paces." This is tedious. It is also the most reliable short-range navigation method in a labyrinth. Do it.

**Getting lost:** Carry a whistle. If you are traveling with others and become separated, a whistle carries further than a voice in the labyrinth's acoustics, though the echo patterns will make directionality difficult. Agree on a signal — three blasts means "I'm lost, come find me." Establish a time-based protocol: if you're not back at camp by a certain hour, your companion begins searching along your last known bearing.

If you are alone and become truly lost — disoriented, unable to find your camp, uncertain of the direction to the plateau edge — stop. Sit down. Drink water. Eat something. Think. The plateau has edges in every direction. If you pick a compass bearing and walk it consistently, you will eventually reach an edge. It might take days. You might have to climb mesas to verify your gross position. But the plateau is finite. You cannot be lost in it forever, only for longer than you'd like.

## Water and Food

**Water:** The irony of this place is that you are surrounded by water and may struggle to drink it safely. The seep-water is heavily mineralized — it has been dissolving limestone on its journey through the stone, and it carries dissolved calcium carbonate, possibly along with whatever else is in the local geology. In small quantities, this is harmless. In the quantities you'd need to drink to stay hydrated while walking all day on wet stone (which is more dehydrating than you'd think, because the cool air and physical effort combine to drive water loss without obvious sweating), the mineral load could cause gastrointestinal distress.

Bring a filtration system that handles dissolved minerals — a basic particulate filter won't suffice. A purifier with activated carbon will reduce mineral taste and some dissolved contaminants. Alternatively, bring the means to collect rainwater, which falls every few days and is essentially distilled. Set out collection vessels (a tarp, a wide-mouthed container, even a waterproof jacket arranged as a funnel) when rain approaches. The rain is steady when it comes, and you can collect significant quantities.

The puddles are tempting. They look clean. They are shallow and clear. But they sit in mineral depressions and concentrate whatever the seep-water carries, plus they harbor insect larvae (the gnats mentioned in the description). Filter and treat puddle water before drinking.

Carry enough water for at least two days beyond your planned needs. If you get lost, water is your primary survival constraint. The labyrinth will not let you die of exposure in this season (it's cool but not cold), and you can survive without food for weeks, but dehydration in a maze with uncertain water sources is a genuine danger.

**Food:** Bring everything you need. There is nothing to forage here. The vegetation is moss, lichen, ferns, and wiry grass — none of it calorically useful, some of it potentially toxic (many lichens contain acids that are not kind to human digestion). The insects are too sparse to harvest. The birds are too small and too few to hunt, and the raptors are inaccessible on the mesa tops. The hypothetical nocturnal mammals are invisible. This is not a landscape that feeds visitors.

Pack calorie-dense, moisture-resistant food. The ambient humidity will get into everything. Sealed packaging is essential. Anything in paper or cardboard will be damp within hours and degraded within days. Hard cheeses, nuts, dried meats, dense grain bars, chocolate sealed in foil — these travel well in wet conditions. Cooking is possible but requires fuel you carry in, since there is no wood (nothing here grows tall enough to burn meaningfully). A small gas stove for hot drinks and rehydrating meals is worthwhile for morale as much as nutrition. A hot drink in a cool, damp, gray labyrinth is a disproportionate comfort.

## Shelter and Sleep

You will not find shelter in the traditional sense. The labyrinth is roofless. Every passage, every chamber, every plaza is open to the sky. When it rains, you get rained on. The overhangs mentioned in the description — places where wall collapses have created partial canopies, or where the leaning walls in the tilted districts nearly meet overhead — offer some protection, but they are unreliable and hard to find on demand.

**Tent or tarp:** A freestanding tent is difficult to pitch here. The floor is stone. You cannot drive stakes into limestone. Bring a tent that can be anchored with rocks placed on its stake-out points, or use guy lines tied to rock projections and rubble boulders. A tarp shelter, strung between walls in a narrow passage, is actually more practical here than a tent — the walls provide the structure that the ground denies. Bring plenty of cord and practice rigging a tarp in confined spaces before you go.

**Sleeping surface:** You are sleeping on stone. Bring a sleeping pad with serious insulation and cushioning — closed-cell foam at minimum, an inflatable pad ideally, both if you can carry the weight. The stone is cool and will draw heat from your body all night through conduction. A thin pad on limestone at 50°F will leave you chilled and aching by morning. Insulate generously.

**Sleeping bag:** A synthetic bag, not down. Down loses its insulating capacity when wet, and everything here gets damp eventually. The humidity alone will introduce moisture into your insulation over the course of days. A synthetic bag rated to around 35-40°F should be adequate for the described conditions — cool but not freezing. Bring a waterproof stuff sack or dry bag to store it during the day. A damp sleeping bag in a damp labyrinth is a compounding problem.

**Camp selection:** Choose your campsite carefully. Look for passages that are wide enough to be comfortable but narrow enough to provide some wind protection. Avoid passages that show signs of active drainage — the gutters in the floor, the mineral staining, the smooth water-worn channels. When rain comes, these passages will carry water. You don't want to wake up lying in a stream. Look for slightly elevated areas within the passages — raised sections of bedding plane, rubble platforms, any surface that sits a few inches above the surrounding floor. Check the walls above your chosen site for loose stone. Look for fresh fracture surfaces (pale, unweathered stone with sharp edges) that indicate recent rockfall activity. Look for cracks above you that are widening. The description makes clear that wall collapses are ongoing. The probability of one occurring directly above your camp on any given night is very low, but the consequences are severe. Choose a site where the walls are solid and the ceiling (such as it is) shows no signs of imminent failure.

## Clothing

Layer. The temperature is stable but cool, and your exertion level will vary enormously — walking hard through the maze, you'll generate heat; sitting still at an intersection contemplating the lichen, you'll chill quickly. A moisture-wicking base layer, an insulating mid-layer (fleece or light synthetic puffy), and a waterproof shell. The shell will be on your body most of the time. Rain comes every few days, but the ambient seep and drip from the walls will spot your clothes constantly. A good waterproof-breathable shell keeps the external moisture out while letting your exertion moisture escape. In theory. In practice, the humidity differential between inside and outside the jacket is minimal, so breathability is somewhat academic. You will be damp. Accept this. Manage it rather than prevent it.

Bring more clothing than you think you need. Drying anything here is nearly impossible — the humidity is too high, the sun too rare and too diffuse. Wet clothing stays wet. Rotate through your layers, hanging the off-duty ones inside your pack or under your tarp where they're at least protected from direct rain, even if they don't truly dry.

A hat with a brim is essential — not for sun, which is rarely direct, but for rain. Water dripping into your eyes while you're navigating wet stone is a safety hazard, not just an annoyance. Gloves are worth bringing — thin, quick-drying gloves for scrambling on wet stone, which you'll do constantly when stepping over rubble, bracing against walls, climbing mesas.

## Safety and Emergency Preparedness

**Rockfall:** This is the primary acute hazard. Walls calve. Slabs fall. The description makes this clear. You cannot predict where or when. You can minimize your exposure by not lingering at the base of high walls that show signs of undercutting (a visible gap or softer eroded layer at the base, with harder stone overhanging above). Move through the deep, narrow passages with purpose rather than dawdling. When you hear a rockfall sound — the dull crack and cascading rattle — stop and listen. Try to determine the direction. If it's close, move away from the nearest high walls. If it's distant (and most will be distant, given the labyrinth's size), simply note it and continue.

Wear a helmet. This will feel absurd. You are walking through what looks like a city, not climbing a cliff. But loose stone is everywhere — on ledges above you, on the walls beside you, perched on remnant overhangs — and a falling piece of limestone the size of a fist, dropping from thirty feet, can fracture a skull. A lightweight climbing helmet adds negligible weight and may save your life. Wear it in the deep, narrow passages. Take it off on the open plazas if you like.

**Slips and falls:** The second major hazard. Wet stone. Uneven surfaces. Biological film. You *will* slip at some point during any trip of more than a day. When you do, you want to fall onto relatively flat stone, not into a crevice or off a mesa. Move carefully. Test your footing. Don't rush. Don't walk in the passages after dark — the even, shadowless daylight that makes the textures so visible is also what keeps you safe, and without it, the wet stone becomes genuinely treacherous.

**Hypothermia:** The conditions are classic for mild hypothermia — cool, damp, windy, with limited ability to dry wet clothing. You won't freeze to death here in this season, but prolonged chill saps your energy, impairs your judgment, and makes navigation errors more likely. Stay fueled. Stay as dry as possible. Change into dry layers when you stop moving. Get into your sleeping bag before you start shivering, not after.

**Injury management:** You are alone (or in a small group) in a labyrinth with no rescue services. A broken ankle here is a genuine emergency. Carry a comprehensive first aid kit with emphasis on wound cleaning (the mineral-laden water and biological film on the stone mean that any cut or scrape is exposed to potential infection), blister management (your feet will develop blisters despite your best efforts), and splinting materials. Know how to improvise an evacuation if someone in your party can't walk. A rigid splint, a trekking pole crutch, a plan for navigating the labyrinth one passage at a time with an injured person — think these through before you need them.

**Communication:** If the multiverse variant supports satellite communication devices, bring one. A satellite messenger or personal locator beacon gives you a link to the outside in emergencies. If it doesn't support satellite communication, bring a signal mirror (useful if any search party is above you — on the mesas, in an aircraft) and a whistle. Accept that in many multiverse variants, if something goes seriously wrong deep in the labyrinth, you are on your own.

## Duration and Pacing

How long should you stay? This depends on what you want from the experience.

**A few days** gives you a taste. You enter from the plateau edge, walk into the labyrinth for a day, camp, explore the surrounding district, and walk back out. You'll see one small fraction of one edge district. You'll experience the damp, the acoustics, the lichen, the even light. You'll hear a rockfall in the distance. You might see pipits. You'll turn corners and feel the persistent, unsettling conviction that someone designed the passage you're standing in. It will be enough to understand the place conceptually. It won't be enough to feel it in your bones.

**A week to ten days** lets you reach the interior. You can walk deep enough into the labyrinth that the edge is out of sight, out of reach, out of mind. The character of the place changes as you move inward — the passages may be older, less affected by edge-weather, more deeply colonized by lichen. You'll begin to develop a navigational intuition, a feel for the passage orientations, the drainage patterns, the way the districts connect. You'll start to recognize types of intersections, types of wall-collapse patterns. The labyrinth will begin to seem, if not legible, at least partially coherent. This is the minimum duration I would recommend for anyone who wants to genuinely experience the Plateaus rather than merely visit them.

**A month** is what the description implies is possible, and it would transform the experience from a trip into something closer to a residency. You would develop a deep familiarity with a significant section of the labyrinth. You would witness multiple rain cycles, hear the distributed rushing of drainage through the maze, watch the seep-water respond to pressure changes. You would find the intersections that function as nodes and begin to understand the labyrinth's large-scale topology in your district. You would see the raptors often enough to learn their patrol patterns. You might find the nocturnal mammals' scat and piece together their territories. You would, in some small way, become part of the ecology — a large mammal moving through the passages, your footsteps joining the water's footsteps, your path through the maze joining the trickles' paths.

A month requires serious logistical planning. You either carry a month's food (impractical without caching) or arrange resupply from the plateau edge (which requires that someone knows where you are, which requires reliable navigation, which is the thing this place makes hardest). Cache food at the edge before entering and plan return trips to resupply. Or accept that a month in the interior means lighter rations toward the end and the disciplined management of dwindling supplies. This is not a trip for people who need abundance.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section that matters most, and it's the one that gear lists and navigation strategies can't address.

**Solitude.** If you go alone, you will be alone in a way that few terrestrial environments can produce. The labyrinth is visually enclosing — the walls rise on all sides, limiting your sight lines to the length of the current passage. The overcast sky is featureless. The sounds are subtle — seeping water, distant rockfalls, wind-drone, the occasional pipit call. There are no human artifacts, no trails, no signs, no litter, no evidence that anyone has ever been here or ever will be. The solitude is not the solitude of a wilderness, which always contains the implied presence of a civilization you've temporarily left. This is the solitude of a place that has no relationship to civilization at all. It resembles a city. It is not a city. You are the only citizen.

Some people need this. It's a profound experience — to walk through architecture without an architect, to see intention where there is none, to feel the pull of pattern in a place where pattern is the result of physics, not purpose. It can produce a deep, meditative stillness. It can also produce a creeping, subtle dread — the sense that you are the only thing in this entire vast structure that has a mind, and that the structure doesn't care, and that the structure's indifference is total.

If you are prone to anxiety in enclosed spaces, the narrow passages will challenge you. If you are prone to existential vertigo — the sensation of your own smallness and irrelevance — the mesa-top views of the labyrinth extending to the horizon will challenge you. If you need human contact for psychological stability, do not go alone, and even with companions, know that the labyrinth's acoustics and sight lines will separate you frequently. You will turn a corner and your companion will be gone, and you will be alone with the stone, and you will have to be okay with that.

**The illusion of meaning.** The single most psychologically distinctive feature of this place is its resemblance to something built. You will walk through doorways that are not doorways. You will enter plazas that are not plazas. You will follow corridors that feel like they lead somewhere — and they do lead somewhere, but that somewhere is just more labyrinth, arranged in the same patterns, by the same physics, and the feeling that you're getting closer to the center, the purpose, the explanation, will never be fulfilled because there is no center, no purpose, no explanation.

This can be deeply frustrating for goal-oriented minds. Prepare yourself to walk without arriving. To explore without discovering. To look without finding. The labyrinth does not reward ambition. It rewards attention. The person who stops at an intersection and spends an hour watching lichen will have a richer experience than the person who walks fifty miles trying to find the heart of the maze.

**The sound.** When the wind picks up and the labyrinth begins its layered drone — the sum of air moving through ten thousand corridors — you will feel something that is difficult to prepare for intellectually but that you should know is coming. It sounds designed. It sounds like a chord being played. It sounds like the place is *doing something* — communicating, expressing, performing. The knowledge that it's just wind through rock will not prevent the feeling. Let the feeling happen. Sit with it. It is one of the most remarkable acoustic experiences available in any multiverse, and it arises from nothing more than erosion and air pressure. That's the point. That's the whole point of this place.

## What to Bring for Engagement (Not Just Survival)

The practical gear keeps you alive. This gear keeps you present.

**A journal.** Paper, not digital. Something with waterproof pages (Rite in the Rain or similar). A pencil, not a pen (pencil works when wet). Write down what you see. Sketch the passages. Note the lichen colors, the sounds, the quality of the light at different times of day. Map your route. The act of recording forces attention, and attention is the currency of this place.

**A camera**, if you want, but understand its limitations. The even, shadowless light is technically easy to photograph in (no harsh contrasts, no blown highlights) but emotionally difficult to photograph well. The labyrinth's power is in its scale and its repetition, and photographs tend to flatten both. A single passage looks like a slot canyon. A plaza looks like a rocky clearing. The photograph cannot show you what makes this place unique — the fact that it goes on for hundreds of miles, that it *all* looks like this, that the pattern repeats and repeats and repeats until the repetition itself becomes the subject. Try to photograph it anyway. The attempt teaches you something about the limits of representation.

**Binoculars.** For the mesa-top views. For watching the raptors. For studying the lichen colonies on high walls that you can't reach. For looking at the labyrinth from above and trying to discern its large-scale structure, which will be tantalizingly almost-visible from a height — patterns in the passage orientations, clusters of intersections, the drainage networks traced in darker mineral stain.

**A hand lens or magnifying loupe.** Ten-power magnification. For the lichen. Up close, the lichen colonies are extraordinary — intricate three-dimensional structures, reproductive bodies, the chemical warfare zones at the boundaries between species. They are the most alive thing here, the most active, the most engaged in the business of existing in this place. Give them your attention. They've earned it over centuries.

**An audio recorder.** For the wind. For the distant rockfalls. For the seep-water's quiet emergence from the cracks. For the pipit calls. For the rain's distributed rushing through the maze. These sounds do not translate well to memory. Record them. Listen to them later. They will bring the place back more powerfully than any photograph.

## Ethical Considerations

You are visiting a place with no human history, no cultural significance, no inhabitants to offend. The ethical weight is therefore entirely ecological and existential.

**Leave no trace** to the extent possible. Your footprints on wet stone will fade. Your camps will leave no mark if you choose your sites carefully. Carry out all waste — food packaging, human waste (pack it out; there is no soil to bury it in, only rock), broken gear, everything. The labyrinth has no decomposition infrastructure for human artifacts. A food wrapper dropped in a passage will sit there, essentially unchanged, for as long as the passage exists. Which may be centuries.

**Do not disturb the lichen.** Seriously. A colony three feet across is hundreds of years old. Scraping it with your pack as you squeeze through a narrow passage destroys decades of growth in an instant. Move carefully. Turn sideways. Protect the walls from your gear. The lichen are the closest thing this place has to citizens, and their slow, chemical, century-long lives deserve your respect.

**Do not move the stone.** Don't rearrange rubble for convenience. Don't knock down walls to create shortcuts. Don't stack cairns that will persist beyond your visit. The labyrinth is in the process of building itself through erosion, and every stone is where physics put it. Leave them.

## Departure

When you leave the labyrinth — walking back toward the edge, the passages gradually becoming more familiar if you've been careful with your navigation — you will feel the warm wind coming up from the lowlands below and smell green things growing in real soil, and part of you will want to keep going, to walk back in, to follow one more passage, to turn one more corner.

This is normal. The place has a pull. It is the pull of pattern, of emergence, of the unsettling beauty of a world that looks designed but isn't. It will stay with you. The memory of the even light, the wet stone, the distant rockfall sound, the wind-chord in the passages — these will surface at unexpected moments for years afterward, usually when you encounter something in an ordinary city that reminds you how thin the line is between built and emerged, between intended and accidental, between architecture and erosion.

You went to a city that no one built. You walked its streets. You listened to its music. You left it as you found it — damp, patient, vast, and busy with the slow work of becoming whatever it will become next.

Prepare well. Walk carefully. Pay attention. The stone has been waiting a long time, and it has a great deal to show you, and it will show you none of it on purpose.
