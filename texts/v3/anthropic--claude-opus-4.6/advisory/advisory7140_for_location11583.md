# Preparing for the Plateau of Voices

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Let the implications settle. This is not a hostile environment in any conventional sense — nothing here is described as venomous, aggressive toward humans, or climatically extreme. The dangers of the Plateau of Voices are subtler and more pervasive than that. They are: sustained acoustic assault on a scale your body has never experienced, terrain that is alive beneath your feet, social and ecological norms that punish ignorance with consequences you may not immediately recognize, and the psychological weight of a place so old and so integrated that your presence in it will feel, at times, like an intrusion at the cellular level.

You are not visiting a wilderness. You are visiting a single organism — or something close to it — that happens to have weather and fauna and people living in symbiosis with it. Your preparation needs to reflect that.

---

## Hearing Protection: The Central Problem

This is the most critical preparation category and the one most likely to be underestimated. Let me be precise about what you're facing.

The description tells us the sound at the dusk crescendo — *the opening* — causes physical disorientation, blurred vision, and aching bones. That's not poetic exaggeration. Those are symptoms consistent with sustained exposure to high-intensity sound across a wide frequency band, including infrasound. The bass throb of the canopy-swingers is explicitly felt in the sternum before it's heard. The mat-swimmers transmit through the ground itself. The chitterer clouds create spikes sharp enough to make your eyes water. You are dealing with:

- **Infrasound** (below 20 Hz): the canopy-swinger pulses, the mat-swimmer moans transmitted through solid and spongy media, possibly the wind resonance through the runner-networks
- **Low frequency** (20–200 Hz): the pollinator drones, the layered territorial songs
- **Mid frequency** (200 Hz–2 kHz): the runner-whistling, human-range vocalizations from various fauna
- **High frequency** (2 kHz and above): the chitterer staccato, the shimmering curtain of clicking from tens of thousands of individuals

Standard earplugs from your home universe will be inadequate. They are designed to attenuate frequencies in the human speech range and above — they will do almost nothing against infrasound, and they won't address ground-transmitted vibration at all. You need a layered approach:

**Layer 1: In-ear protection.** Custom-molded earplugs with the highest Noise Reduction Rating you can find. These handle the mid and high frequencies — the chitterer clouds, the runner-whistling. Look for NRR 33 or equivalent. Bring multiple pairs. The environment is damp, and materials will degrade. Foam plugs will absorb moisture from the mat's exhalations and lose effectiveness within hours. Silicone or wax-based plugs are better. Best of all: bring a set with tuned filters that allow you to reduce volume without eliminating frequency bands entirely. You *need* to hear what's happening around you — the mat-swimmers surface without warning, and the locals' percussive ground-signals are your only reliable communication over distance. Full isolation is dangerous.

**Layer 2: Over-ear protection.** Active noise-canceling headphones designed for industrial or aviation use, worn over the earplugs. These help with the sustained mid-frequency roar. The combination of plugged ears plus over-ear muffs can give you 40–50 dB of attenuation in the frequencies they cover. This will not make the plateau quiet. It will make it *bearable.* At dusk, during the opening, even this combination may not be sufficient to prevent discomfort.

**Layer 3: Infrasound mitigation.** This is the hard one. There is no consumer product that effectively blocks infrasound because infrasound doesn't just enter through your ears — it passes through your body. The canopy-swinger pulses and the mat-swimmer vocalizations are going to reach you regardless of what you put on your head. Your options are limited:

- **Pharmacological:** Anti-nausea medication (meclizine, ondansetron) can help manage the vestibular disruption that infrasound causes. Bring plenty. Take it prophylactically before moving inland from the plateau's edge, and especially before your first dusk.
- **Postural:** When the infrasound is intense, sitting or lying flat on the mat distributes the vibration across your body rather than concentrating it through your legs and spine. This is, notably, exactly what the locals do during the opening. They're not being spiritual. They're being practical. (They may also be being spiritual. Both things can be true.)
- **Exposure management:** Limit your initial time in the plateau's interior. The sound builds gradually as you move inward from the eroded edges. Spend your first day or two at the periphery, where the fauna density is lower and the overlapping calls haven't yet reached their full saturation. Let your body begin to habituate. This is not a place you storm into.

**Layer 4: Bone conduction awareness.** The mat-swimmer moans transmit through the ground and into your skeleton through your feet. No ear protection addresses this. The vibration will be present whenever you're standing on the mat, which is always, because the mat *is* the ground. Thick-soled boots with vibration-dampening insoles will help slightly (more on footwear below), but mostly you need to accept that this frequency band is going to be part of your experience. After a few days, many travelers report that the ground-hum becomes something like white noise — constant enough to fade from conscious attention. Until then, it's deeply unsettling in a way that's hard to articulate. It feels like the planet is digesting you.

Bring backup ear protection for everything. Assume moisture will destroy at least one set. Assume you'll lose at least one set. The damp, the spongy ground, the constant micro-movement of the mat — small objects disappear here.

## Footwear

This deserves its own section because the mat changes everything about how you move.

The surface of the plateau is a living, ankle-to-knee-deep spongy organism over ancient stone. Your feet sink with each step. The mat exhales moisture and gas. It is never dry. Things live inside it, including apex predators that explode upward to feed.

**Do not wear open-toed shoes. Do not wear thin-soled shoes. Do not go barefoot.**

You want boots that are:

- **Mid-calf height at minimum.** Knee-deep mat areas exist. You need coverage.
- **Waterproof but breathable.** The mat is perpetually damp. Your feet will be in contact with moisture continuously. Non-breathable waterproofing will create a swamp inside your boots within hours. Look for Gore-Tex or equivalent membrane technology.
- **Vibration-dampening.** Thick, multi-layered soles with gel or air cushioning. This addresses the mat-swimmer ground-moans and also protects your joints from the slightly unpredictable give of the mat surface over uneven stone beneath.
- **Puncture-resistant in the sole.** The burgundy-grey stone beneath the mat has been honeycombed by burrowers and erosion. If your foot compresses the mat deeply enough in a thin spot, you could contact sharp stone edges. A steel or composite shank in the sole is worthwhile.
- **Gripping on wet organic surfaces.** The mat is spongy and damp. Standard rubber lugs designed for rock or dirt may not give adequate traction. Look for soles with siping — the thin slits you see on boat shoes — which channels moisture away from the contact surface.

Bring at least two pairs. Rotate them daily. Dry the off-duty pair as thoroughly as you can — hang them from the runner-networks if the locals permit it, where airflow is better. (Ask before you hang anything on the runners. The runners are part of the living system, and the locals may have strong feelings about this.) Bring multiple pairs of moisture-wicking socks. Wool-synthetic blends dry fastest. Change socks at least twice daily. Foot rot is a real risk in an environment where the ground itself is exhaling moisture under your feet.

Inspect your boots every evening for organisms that may have entered during the day. The mat contains an entire ecosystem. Small things from the mat may find the warm, dark interior of a boot appealing. Shake them out, check the insoles, check the lace eyelets.

## Clothing

The climate is relatively mild — high elevation, clear skies during the day, constant gentle wind, not extreme in temperature — but the moisture cycle creates specific challenges.

**Morning:** Clouds condense against the plateau. Every surface beads with water. The epiphytes are drinking. The air is saturated. You will be wet. Not rain-wet — *condensation-wet.* A fine, clinging dampness that soaks through cotton in minutes and makes synthetic fabrics clammy. You want a lightweight waterproof outer layer for the mornings — a simple rain jacket, breathable, with a hood. Not insulated. The temperature isn't cold enough to need insulation in most seasons, and you'll be generating heat just from the effort of walking on the mat.

**Midday and afternoon:** Warm, damp but not wet, stone radiating heat from below. The waterproof layer comes off. You want lightweight, loose-fitting clothing that breathes well and dries fast. Long sleeves and long pants are non-negotiable despite the warmth. Reasons: the mat releases fine particles when compressed (spores, fragments of the lichen-web, tiny organisms), the chitterers land on exposed skin and their scaled bodies can cause mild abrasion, and the epiphyte sap — the astringent-scented liquid they release when heavy with water — can cause contact irritation on sensitive skin. Light colors are preferable. They're cooler in the strong high-altitude sun, and they make it easier to spot anything from the mat ecosystem that might hitch a ride on your clothing.

**Evening and night:** Temperature drops but not dramatically. The mat's bioluminescence means you're sleeping on a faintly glowing surface. An eye mask is useful for sleep, though honestly, by nightfall the acoustic environment may be a bigger obstacle to sleep than the light. A light fleece or insulating mid-layer for the cooler hours. The wind doesn't stop at night.

**UV protection:** High elevation plus clear afternoon skies equals intense ultraviolet radiation. Sunscreen, SPF 50+, reapplied frequently because the ambient moisture and your own sweat will wash it off. A wide-brimmed hat for midday. Sunglasses — important not just for UV but because the chitterer swarms create visual confusion at close range, and reducing glare helps your brain process the already overwhelming sensory environment.

All clothing should be synthetic or merino wool. No cotton. Cotton absorbs moisture, holds it against your skin, dries slowly, and in a permanently damp environment it becomes a vector for fungal growth on your own body within days.

## Shelter and Sleep System

You have a choice: rely on the locals' hospitality, or bring your own shelter.

**If relying on local hospitality:** Approach slowly. Announce yourself from a distance using visible gestures — raise both hands, palms forward, and wait. Their language is largely gestural at close range and percussive at long range. You will not speak their language. They will recognize you as a stranger immediately. Whether they welcome you will depend on factors you cannot fully predict — the season, their current resource situation, their individual temperament, whether you've done something to disturb the mat or the runners on your way in. Bring gifts. Not technology — technology is meaningless here and may be disturbing. Bring food from outside the plateau, preferably something calorie-dense and shelf-stable that offers novelty. Dried fruit, nuts, honey if you can transport it. Something sweet. Sweetness seems to be valued — the mat's exhalation has a sweet component, the canopy-swinger meat is presumably part of their diet — and an offering of concentrated sweetness from an unknown source is intriguing without being threatening.

Their shelters are small, low, and woven from local materials. They will not comfortably accommodate you in addition to their family group in most cases. Be prepared to sleep outside their shelter, nearby, with their permission.

**If bringing your own shelter:** Keep it small. Keep it low. This is both practical (you're on an exposed, windy plateau) and culturally essential (building tall challenges the columnar trees; building wide displaces the mat). A bivouac sack or an ultra-low-profile one-person tent is ideal. Two-person maximum. Stake it to the mat with the shortest, thinnest pegs you can manage — you're piercing a living organism, and the less damage you do, the less you offend the locals and the less you disturb the mat-swimmers who may be sensing vibrations through it. Actually: do not use metal stakes. The mat lies over honeycombed stone. A metal stake driven through the mat into a stone hollow could crack the stone and disturb burrowers, creating a chain of consequences. Use short, blunt wooden or bone pegs — or better yet, ask the locals for the shaped burgundy-grey stone pegs they use and trade for some.

**Sleep system:** A sleeping pad is critical — not for warmth but for cushioning and moisture barrier. The mat is spongy and damp. Sleeping directly on it means sleeping on a wet living surface that breathes beneath you and occasionally transmits the vibrations of mat-swimmers. A closed-cell foam pad provides insulation and a moisture barrier. An inflatable pad on top of that provides cushioning and further isolates you from ground-transmitted sound. Both together, ideally.

Sleeping bag: rated for mild conditions, synthetic fill (down loses insulation when damp, and everything is damp here). A bag liner adds warmth and keeps the bag cleaner.

**The real sleep challenge is acoustic.** You will not experience silence on this plateau. Even at night, the bass throb continues, the mat-swimmers moan through the ground, the canopy-swingers shift to their slower melodic calls. The locals have adapted over generations. You have not. Options:

- Earplugs plus noise-canceling headphones playing pink or brown noise (not white noise — white noise has too much high-frequency energy and will compete with rather than mask the chitterer residuals; brown noise better matches the bass-heavy nighttime soundscape)
- Pharmacological sleep aids as a last resort (antihistamines like diphenhydramine; avoid benzodiazepines because you need to be rousable in case of mat-swimmer surfacing near your shelter)
- Exhaustion. Walking on the mat is physically demanding. By the end of a full day, you may be tired enough to sleep through anything.

Most travelers report that sleep becomes easier after the third night. Your brain learns to classify the plateau's chord as background. It's the first two nights that are brutal.

## Navigation

The plateau has no edges sharp enough to serve as landmarks. No hills, no ridges. The surface is a gently undulating living mat between widely spaced columnar trees, extending in every direction to a horizon that barely exists. Getting lost here is trivially easy and potentially fatal — not because of predators but because of dehydration and exhaustion if you walk in circles across a featureless landscape.

**GPS and electronic navigation:** May or may not function depending on whether this universe has orbiting satellites or compatible magnetic fields. Do not rely on it. Bring it as a backup but assume it won't work.

**Compass:** Bring one, but test it immediately upon arrival. The burgundy-grey stone may have magnetic properties that create local anomalies. If the compass is reliable, it's your most valuable tool.

**The locals' method:** They navigate by the runner-networks. Each columnar tree has a unique pattern of aerial root and runner connections to its neighbors, and the locals have memorized these patterns across their entire range. This is an extraordinary feat of spatial memory that you cannot replicate on a short visit. However, you can learn the basic principle: the runners radiate outward from each columnar tree, and by identifying which tree you're nearest to and which direction its runners lead, you can at least maintain a heading. The columnar trees are spaced 60–80 meters apart. If you can always see the nearest few, you can leapfrog between them.

**Marking your path:** Do NOT cut or mark the columnar trees, the runners, or the mat. This is ecologically destructive and culturally offensive. Instead, carry small colored flags or ribbons on thin wire stakes that you can push gently into the mat surface and retrieve on your return journey. Make them visible from a distance — orange or bright pink, not green or blue, which blend with the epiphytes. Retrieve all of them when you leave. Leave nothing behind.

**The sun:** Reliable for general orientation during the day. Note the direction of sunrise and sunset. The elevation and clear skies mean the sun's position is unambiguous for most of the day.

**Sound as navigation:** Counterintuitive but potentially useful. The sound environment changes as you move across the plateau. Areas near large concentrations of canopy-swingers have a heavier bass presence. Areas near mat-swimmer feeding zones have a distinct ground-hum pattern. If you spend enough time listening — really listening, with attention and memory — you can begin to recognize acoustic neighborhoods. The locals certainly navigate partly by sound. You won't develop this skill quickly, but being aware of it may help you recognize when you've entered a familiar or unfamiliar acoustic zone.

## Water

The plateau has no rivers, no streams, no standing water. All moisture comes from the morning cloud condensation cycle, captured by the epiphytes and stored in the root-mat's spongy reservoirs.

**Can you drink the water?** Unknown. The mat's reservoirs contain water that has passed through multiple living organisms. It may be biologically incompatible with your metabolism or contaminated with spores, enzymes, or microorganisms adapted to this ecosystem. Do not drink directly from the mat or the epiphytes without purification.

**Purification:** Bring a portable water filtration system — pump-action or gravity-fed, with a filter rated to 0.1 microns to remove bacteria and protozoan analogues. Additionally bring chemical purification tablets (iodine or chlorine dioxide) as backup. A small UV purifier (SteriPen or equivalent) adds another layer but requires batteries, which may be difficult to recharge.

**Collection:** In the morning, when every surface is beaded with condensation, you can collect water directly from the epiphyte curtains by wringing them gently over a container. This is probably the cleanest water available — it's essentially distilled moisture from the lowland clouds, filtered only through the air and the surface of the succulent leaves. It may contain epiphyte surface compounds (the astringent-scented substance mentioned in the description), so taste a small amount first and wait an hour before drinking freely. If you experience any oral tingling, irritation, or gastrointestinal response, switch to collecting condensation from non-biological surfaces — the rare exposed patches of burgundy-grey stone, or your own shelter fabric if it's made of inert material.

**Quantity:** High elevation, constant wind, physical exertion of walking on a yielding surface — you'll need 3–4 liters per day minimum. Carry capacity for at least two days' water at all times in case you can't collect adequately one morning. Collapsible water containers save weight and space.

**The locals' water:** They drink from the mat's reservoirs. Their biology has adapted to whatever is in that water. Yours has not. If they offer you water, accept it graciously — refusal of offered sustenance may be offensive — but drink only a small, symbolic amount unless you've already tested local water sources and confirmed compatibility.

## Food

The plateau supports a complete ecosystem, which means it produces food. The question is whether any of it is safe for your biology.

**Bring your own food for the entire planned duration of your stay, plus 50% extra.** Calorie-dense, lightweight, shelf-stable. Nuts, dried fruit, jerky or equivalent protein, energy bars, dehydrated meals if you're bringing a stove. You're working hard just walking on the mat, and the altitude may suppress your appetite while increasing your caloric needs.

**Local food sources and their risks:**

- *Canopy-swinger meat:* The locals hunt these. If offered, you're facing the standard multiverse traveler's dilemma with alien protein — it may be nutritious, it may be inert, it may be toxic. Amino acid profiles may differ from what your body can process. The safest approach: carry a small portion separately, cook it thoroughly (heat denatures most organic toxins), eat a tiny amount (thumbnail-sized), wait 24 hours. If no adverse reaction, you can cautiously increase the quantity. Do not rely on it as a primary food source on a short visit.

- *Chitterer eggs:* Smaller organisms, potentially simpler biochemistry. Same testing protocol. The locals collect these from columnar bark — they may show you how if you've established rapport.

- *Mat-tubers:* Starchy growths from within the root-mat. These are the most likely candidate for metabolic compatibility because starch is a relatively simple carbohydrate that's common across many biologies. But "starchy" is the locals' experience, not necessarily yours. Test carefully. Cook thoroughly. The mat is a living consortium of potentially dozens of organism types, and anything growing inside it may contain compounds from its neighbors.

- *Epiphyte leaves:* The silver-blue and rust-orange succulents on the runners. They contain stored water, possibly sugars. The astringent scent suggests tannin-analogues or phenolic compounds that may be irritating to human digestion. Not recommended as food without thorough testing.

- *Do not eat anything from the mat's surface directly.* The mat is an entire biome. Putting random bits of it in your mouth is the equivalent of eating a handful of forest floor.

**Cooking:** A small backpacking stove with enough fuel for your stay. The plateau has no wood — the columnar trees are not wood, they're fibrous cork-like tissue that may not burn well and should not be harvested anyway. Dried runner-tissue (which the locals use for shelter construction) may be burnable but is likely a limited resource they manage carefully. Bring your own fuel. A windscreen for the stove is essential — the constant wind will rob your flame of heat.

## Medical Preparation

**Hearing damage:** The most likely medical issue. Prolonged exposure to the plateau's sound environment without adequate protection will cause permanent hearing damage. Tinnitus, threshold shift, potentially noise-induced hearing loss. The infrasound component may cause additional effects: nausea, anxiety, disorientation, a pervasive sense of unease, visual disturbances. If you experience persistent vertigo, vomiting, or chest pressure, you are being overexposed to infrasound. Move toward the plateau's edge where the sound density is lower.

**Respiratory:** The mat releases spores, particulates, and gases (it breathes, visibly). A lightweight respirator mask or even a bandana over your nose and mouth is advisable, especially in areas where the mat is knee-deep and your compression of it while walking releases concentrated exhalations directly into your breathing zone. If you're prone to asthma or have reactive airways, bring an inhaler and consider a proper P100 respirator mask.

**Allergic reactions:** You're immersed in an alien biome. Epiphyte sap on skin, mat spores in lungs, chitterer scales abrading exposed surfaces — any of these could trigger allergic responses. Bring antihistamines (cetirizine for daily prophylaxis, diphenhydramine for acute reactions), a course of oral corticosteroids (prednisone) for severe reactions, and an epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis. This is non-negotiable. You don't know what you're allergic to here because you've never been exposed to any of it.

**Foot care:** Moisture + constant walking on an unstable surface = blisters, fungal infection, maceration of skin. Foot powder, blister bandages, antifungal cream. Inspect your feet daily. Address hot spots immediately.

**Mat-swimmer injury:** The mat-swimmers explode upward through the mat to feed. They are described as the apex predators. If you are standing over one when it surfaces, the upward force could knock you off your feet, and the torn mat could expose sharp stone edges beneath. The locals know where the mat-swimmers surface and avoid those areas during active periods. *Stay with the locals.* If you can't, watch for the telltale ripple of the mat that indicates something large is moving beneath the surface, and move away from it promptly. Carry a basic trauma kit: wound dressings, hemostatic gauze, elastic bandages, antiseptic. If bitten — unlikely, as mat-swimmers feed on chitterers and not human-sized creatures, but not impossible if you're unfortunate — the bite will likely be powerful given the animal's size and musculature. Pressure bandaging and immediate evacuation from the plateau would be necessary.

**Psychological:** This is more important than you think. The Plateau of Voices is a place of relentless, inescapable sensory intensity. The sound never stops. The ground moves beneath you. You are standing on a living thing at all times. The horizon offers no reference points. The visual environment is beautiful but monotonous — green, stone-color, green, stone-color, extending forever. After several days, some travelers report a dissolution of self-boundary — a feeling that they are being absorbed into the plateau, that the line between their body and the mat beneath them is blurring. This is exacerbated by sleep deprivation, which is likely. It's exacerbated by the infrasound, which is constant. It's exacerbated by isolation from familiar sensory referents.

Bring a journal. Write in it daily. Maintain your narrative identity through the act of recording your experience. Bring a photograph of someone you love, or an object from home — something that anchors you to your own universe and your own identity. This sounds melodramatic. It isn't. Environments that are radically, continuously alien have a way of eroding the assumptions you carry about what *you* are, and the plateau is more continuously alien than most because there is no moment of respite. Even inside your shelter, the sound enters, the mat breathes beneath you, and the air tastes of something that isn't home.

If you feel yourself losing coherence — if the chord of the plateau starts sounding like language, if the mat's breathing starts feeling like *your* breathing, if you begin to understand why the locals sit silently beside surfacing mat-swimmers — that's not necessarily a crisis. It may be the beginning of actually experiencing this place as it is. But it's the point at which you need to decide whether you came here to visit or to stay. If you came to visit, start moving toward the edge.

## Ethical Conduct and Cultural Sensitivity

**The mat is alive.** Walk on it — you must — but walk thoughtfully. Don't gouge it, don't dig in it unnecessarily, don't set fires on it, don't dump waste into it. When you compress it with your footsteps and it exhales, you are participating in its respiratory cycle. Accept this with whatever degree of gravity feels appropriate.

**The runners are infrastructure.** The aerial root-networks connecting the columnar trees are the water distribution system, the epiphyte support structure, the canopy-swinger highway, and the locals' navigation reference. Do not cut them, hang heavy objects from them without permission, or climb them. They're fibrous tissue, not wood — they may not bear your weight and damaging them has cascading effects.

**Do not hunt mat-swimmers.** This should be obvious from the description, but I'll say it explicitly. The locals' relationship with these creatures is deep and old and not fully comprehensible to an outsider. Disturbing it would be a profound transgression. Don't approach known surfacing sites during active periods. If a mat-swimmer surfaces near you unexpectedly, stay still, stay quiet, and let it do what it's doing.

**Learn the basic gestures.** Before you arrive, if you have any access to information about the locals' gestural language, learn at least: greeting/peaceful intention, request for water, request for shelter, thank you, I'm leaving. If you have no advance information, the gestures will need to be improvised, but universal principles apply: open hands, slow movements, no sudden sounds (they can barely hear you over the plateau anyway, but a sharp artificial noise — a whistle, a metallic clang — would stand out jarringly from the natural soundscape and might be interpreted as aggressive or at minimum disrespectful).

**Percussive communication.** The locals communicate over distance by striking the mat with flat stones. The vibrations travel through the mat. Do not do this yourself unless you have been taught the specific patterns and their meanings. Random percussion on the mat is at best meaningless noise and at worst might mimic a signal you don't intend — a distress call, a territorial claim, a warning about mat-swimmers. Carry a flat stone in case you need to attempt communication in an emergency, but use it only as a last resort.

**Leave nothing behind.** The mat will eventually absorb anything you leave — it grows through the locals' shelters within a few years — but the process of absorbing foreign materials may harm it. Pack out all waste, all gear, all food packaging. If you must defecate on the plateau (you will), dig a small, shallow hole at the mat's surface, do your business, and cover it. The mat's consortium of organisms will process it, but minimize the disruption. Do this well away from the locals' shelters, away from known mat-swimmer surfacing sites, and away from the columnar trees' root zones.

## On Arrival: A Suggested First Day

You arrive at the plateau's eroded edge. The rim is so gradual you may not realize you've reached it — the slope simply becomes imperceptible and the stone, where visible, becomes the deep burgundy-grey of the ancient plateau surface. The mat begins thinly here, barely covering the stone, and thickens as you move inward.

**Stop at the edge.** Set up your first camp here, at the periphery, where the sound is still building and the fauna density is lower. Spend the first afternoon and evening acclimatizing. Put on your ear protection. Walk a short distance inward and then return. Note the direction you came from. Test your compass. Collect condensation in the morning. Test the water. Observe the light cycle, the moisture cycle, the dusk transition.

**The opening will find you on your first evening.** Even at the edge, the dusk crescendo is significant. Sit on the mat when it begins. Close your eyes. Let the sound happen. Note what it does to your body — the vibration, the pressure, the disorientation. This is your baseline. You need to know what it feels like at the edge before you experience it in the deep interior.

**Sleep will be difficult.** Use everything you've brought. Brown noise in your headphones over your earplugs under your muffs. Anti-nausea medication if the ground-hum is making you queasy. Sleep aid if necessary. Accept that your first night will be broken and strange, and that the faint bioluminescent glow of the mat around you — a scattered starfield at your feet — is beautiful even through the exhaustion.

**On the second day, move inward.** Slowly. A few kilometers at most. The mat deepens. The columnar trees become frequent. The runner-networks arc overhead. The sound builds. Pay attention to when it becomes uncomfortable even through your protection — that's your depth limit for now. Mark your path. Note the columnar trees and their runner patterns. Look for signs of the locals — their low shelters, the faint trails where the mat is slightly compressed from repeated walking.

**When you find the locals, or they find you:** Hands open. Slow approach. Gift offered. Wait for them to decide what happens next. You are a guest in a place so old it makes your entire civilization look like a passing thought. Act accordingly.

## What You Will Bring Home

You will bring home a new understanding of silence — specifically, the understanding that silence is something you took for granted and will never take for granted again. For weeks after leaving the plateau, ordinary quiet rooms will feel *aggressive* in their emptiness. Your ears will ring. Not from damage, if you were careful, but from the memory. Your nervous system will keep expecting the bass throb, the shimmering chitterer curtain, the ground-moan. It will keep expecting the mat to breathe beneath your feet. You'll step on solid ground and feel, briefly, that something is missing — that the earth should give, should exhale, should be alive.

You may find yourself thinking about the mat-swimmers more than you expected. Not the danger of them — the moment of surfacing, the blunt eyeless head, the sensory pits reading a world you can't perceive, and the people sitting at the edge of the feeding zone in a silence-that-is-not-silence, and the thing that passes between them that you could not name.

You may find that the sound of the plateau — that single, endless, evolving chord — encoded itself somewhere deeper than your conscious memory, and that in moments of genuine quiet, in the spaces between breaths, you can still hear it.

This is normal. The plateau is very old. It has been singing for a long time. Some songs are hard to unhear.
