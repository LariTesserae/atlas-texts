# Preparing for the Underhall of Vask

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description and understand its implications fully. This is not a ruin. This is not a tourist destination. This is a living facility—old, functional, continuously inhabited across what may be centuries—that was designed around principles fundamentally different from surface-world human assumptions about how a space should serve its occupants. It prioritizes sound over sight, thermal awareness over visual orientation, and it integrates the presence of visitors into its own processes in ways that are subtle and not fully understood. You are not going to a place. You are entering a system. The system will be aware of you even if nothing visibly acknowledges you. Prepare accordingly—not with fear, but with the specific, focused respect you would bring to entering someone else's body.

## Physical Preparation

### Fitness and Mobility

The entry point is a bore-shaft barely wide enough for shoulders. This means you need to be honest with yourself about claustrophobia and about your physical dimensions. Measure your shoulder width. If you are broad, you may need to descend at an angle, one arm raised, one lowered, rotating through the tightest sections. Practice this. Find a narrow space—a crevice between boulders, a gap between walls—and practice moving through it slowly, without panic, controlling your breathing. The shaft is not long (the description implies a relatively quick transition from shaft to chamber), but if you seize up mid-descent, you will be stuck in a very bad place with no indication of how far help might be.

Your general fitness should be oriented toward endurance and proprioception, not strength. The floor is smooth, worn, potentially slick during condensation cycles. You will be navigating in near-total darkness. You need excellent balance, a low center of gravity, and the ability to move slowly and deliberately for extended periods without fatigue. Practice walking with your eyes closed in safe environments. Practice standing still for twenty minutes at a time. The underhall rewards patience and punishes haste.

### Acclimatization to Darkness

This is critical. Spend time—days, ideally—acclimatizing to low-light and no-light conditions before your descent. The underhall provides almost no usable visible light. The bioluminescent pools are described as "barely bright enough to see by," and they are scattered at the perimeter. The data filaments glow too dimly to illuminate anything beyond themselves. You will be functionally blind for much of your visit.

Your rod cells need to be at maximum sensitivity. Before descending, spend at least two hours in complete darkness to achieve full dark adaptation. Do not look at any bright light source. Do not use a flashlight. Do not check your phone. Once your eyes have adapted, the faint cues—the pool glow, the filament pulse, the occasional status indicator—will become your stars, and you will be surprised at how much spatial information you can extract from almost nothing. But this only works if you protect your adaptation completely.

**Do not bring bright artificial light into the underhall.** This is not just practical advice; it is an ethical imperative. The space was designed without lighting. The inhabitant(s) live in this darkness by choice or by adaptation. Flooding the chamber with a headlamp would be a profound violation—the equivalent of walking into a cathedral and setting off a flashbomb. It would also likely blind you to the subtle luminous cues the space provides, because your pupils would constrict and you would lose your dark adaptation, leaving you worse off when you turned the light off. If you absolutely must carry an emergency light source, make it red-filtered, as dim as possible, and keep it stowed unless you are in genuine physical danger.

### Hearing Preparation and Protection

The acoustic environment of the underhall is complex, intense, and potentially overwhelming. There is a constant structural bass hum, a four-second percussive cycle you will feel in your chest, and high-frequency tonal singing from the data filaments. The black glass walls selectively amplify and absorb different frequencies, meaning the soundscape will shift dramatically as you move through the space, and certain positions may concentrate sound energy in uncomfortable ways.

**Do not wear earplugs.** You need your hearing—it is your primary navigational sense down here. But be prepared for the possibility of acoustic discomfort, especially in the bass register. The percussive pulse from below, if you happen to be standing over a resonance point in the floor, could be physically unpleasant—a thudding pressure in the chest, a feeling of cardiac disruption that is alarming but (probably) not dangerous. If this happens, move. A few steps in any direction will change your acoustic position significantly.

Consider bringing a set of musician's earplugs—the kind that attenuate evenly across frequencies rather than muffling everything. These will let you reduce overall volume while preserving the tonal detail you need for orientation. Keep them accessible but don't wear them by default. Start with full exposure. Let your auditory system calibrate to the space. You may find, as the description suggests, that after ten minutes you stop fighting the sound and start *reading* it—understanding the room's geometry through its reverberations, sensing distance and direction by the way the hum shifts. This is the skill the space was designed to cultivate. Don't short-circuit it with premature attenuation.

Before your visit, practice echolocation basics. Seriously. There are resources for this—blind navigation instructors teach it, and some sighted people have developed functional echolocation skills. You won't become expert before your descent, but even rudimentary practice in attending to reflected sound will help enormously. Snap your fingers in different rooms. Click your tongue. Learn what a wall sounds like when it's three meters away versus ten. In the underhall, with its bizarre selective acoustics, formal echolocation may not work as expected—but the *habit* of listening for spatial information will serve you well.

### Thermal Readiness

The air is warm and close. Intermittently damp. This is a subterranean environment with active thermal systems that cycle, producing periodic condensation events. You should dress for warmth and humidity, but recognize that sweat management is critical: in a space that is already close to saturation during its damp phases, your own perspiration has nowhere to evaporate, and you will become clammy quickly.

Wear a single thin layer of synthetic or merino wool base clothing—something that manages moisture against the skin without becoming cold when wet. Do not overdress. The ambient temperature is warm—the infrastructure surfaces are above blood temperature, and the air in the chamber will be heated by this. You may be more comfortable in less clothing than you expect. Bring a light outer layer you can tie around your waist, in case you encounter cooler air in the bore-shaft or near the perimeter where thermal conditions may differ.

**Footwear is the most critical clothing decision.** The floor is smooth, worn limestone with a dark glass coating that is partially abraded away. During condensation cycles, moisture collects in the hairline channels and pools in the floor depressions. This surface will be slippery. You need thin-soled shoes with excellent grip on wet smooth stone—think approach shoes or sticky-rubber climbing shoes. You want to feel the floor through the soles, because the texture changes will give you navigational information (you can feel the worn path depressions underfoot), but you also need traction. Do not go barefoot, even though the urge will be strong; the floor channels are precise and narrow, and there may be infrastructure elements at the perimeter that are warm enough to burn.

And here's the unintuitive thing about the footwear: the description says you cannot hear your own footsteps. The black glass absorbs the frequencies of footfall. This means you lose a major proprioceptive feedback channel—the sound of your own walking, which normally helps you unconsciously calibrate your gait and position. This will feel deeply strange, like walking in a dream. Thin, flexible soles will help compensate by giving you maximal tactile feedback through your feet. Your feet need to become sensory organs down here.

## Equipment

### What to Bring

**A detailed acoustic recorder.** Not to play back later for entertainment—though you will want to—but as a navigational and scientific tool. If you can record the hum patterns at different locations in the chamber, you can begin to build an acoustic map. Binaural recording equipment (in-ear microphones) would be ideal, capturing the spatial character of the sound as you actually experience it. Keep the recorder running continuously. Sounds you don't consciously notice during your visit may prove significant later—patterns in the thermal cycling, variations in the percussive pulse, the clicking signature of the conduit creature.

**A compact thermal camera or infrared viewer.** This may be the single most useful piece of equipment you carry. The underhall's infrastructure radiates heat—the conduit housings are above blood temperature, the thermal exchangers along the north wall are actively processing energy. A thermal imager will reveal the entire circulatory system of the space in vivid false color, turning darkness into a detailed map of energy flow. You will see the pipework, the junction housings, the data-runs, the warm paths of infrastructure climbing the walls and ceiling, all rendered visible through their thermal signatures. You will also see the condensation cycles in real time—the bloom of cool moisture on warm surfaces, the drainage into the hairline channels. And you will see the lone figure across the chamber, their body heat distinguishing them from the ambient warmth, giving you information about their posture, their activity, whether they are awake.

Use the thermal imager judiciously. It emits no visible light, so it won't disturb the darkness or the inhabitant. But don't rely on it exclusively. The point of this place is to navigate by sound and thermal feel, and if you spend the entire visit staring at a screen, you will miss the experience the architects intended. Use it to orient yourself initially, to understand the geometry. Then put it away and listen.

**A small, sensitive hygrometer and thermometer.** The condensation cycles are clearly significant—they are integrated into the infrastructure, with collection channels and bioluminescent reservoirs. Understanding their rhythm (how often they occur, how the temperature and humidity shift during each cycle) will help you predict when surfaces will become slick and will give you insight into the facility's thermal regulation logic. A small data-logging sensor you can clip to your clothing will collect this passively.

**Water and calorie-dense food.** The bioluminescent pools are collection points for the condensation system. Do not drink from them. The bioluminescent culture is engineered, and the water has been processed through chemosynthetic moss and thermal exchangers and channels of unknown material. It may be perfectly safe; it may not. Bring your own water. Bring enough for several hours—the warmth will dehydrate you, and you will stay longer than you plan. Everyone stays longer than they plan.

Food should be quiet to eat. No crinkling wrappers, no crunching. In a space where your breathing becomes an audible tide and your footsteps are swallowed, the sound of you eating a granola bar will be an obscenity. Bring soft, dense food—nut butter, cheese, thick bread. Eat slowly.

**A small notebook and pencil.** Not a phone—a pencil. You will want to record impressions, sketch the patterns of the conduit networks, note the rhythm of sounds. A pencil works in humidity, in darkness (you can write by feel), and it emits no light. Practice writing legibly without looking at the page.

**A length of thin cord or string, at least 100 meters.** The chamber is three hundred meters across. In total darkness, with no footstep sounds to orient by, it is possible to become spatially confused, especially during your first visit. A thin cord tied off at a known point (the base of the bore-shaft, for instance) gives you a lifeline back to the exit. Don't rely on it—learn to navigate by sound—but have it as a backup. Use cord that won't catch on the floor channels.

### What NOT to Bring

**No bright lights.** Already discussed, but it bears repeating.

**No speakers or sound-generating devices.** Do not play music. Do not make unnecessary noise. The acoustic environment of the underhall is a designed instrument, and you are inside it. Adding your own sounds would be like spray-painting over a fresco. Your breathing, your voice if you must speak—these are acceptable inputs. The room was designed to incorporate them. But artificial sound sources operating at frequencies or volumes the space wasn't designed for could interact unpredictably with the selective acoustic properties of the walls. You could create resonance effects that are physically painful. You could disturb processes you don't understand. Be quiet.

**No strongly scented items.** The air is close, warm, and cycles through the space in patterns governed by the thermal infrastructure. Scents will linger and propagate. Deodorant, cologne, scented soap—all of these will announce your presence in ways that may be unwelcome to the inhabitant and that will interfere with your own ability to smell the space. And you should smell the space. The engineered moss, the bioluminescent cultures, the mineral condensation, the warm alloys—the underhall has an olfactory character that is part of its information environment. Come clean but unscented.

**No wireless transmitters of any kind**, at least initially. The data filaments carry information as physical resonance. The black glass walls may be computational. The infrastructure integrates thermal signatures from physical contact. You do not know what electromagnetic emissions might do in this environment—probably nothing, but possibly something disruptive to systems that have been running in equilibrium for centuries. Turn your phone off. Not airplane mode—off. If you brought the acoustic recorder, make sure it's a standalone device, not transmitting.

## Mental and Psychological Preparation

### Confronting the Darkness

You will be in near-total darkness in a vast space for an extended period. This is psychologically non-trivial even for experienced cavers and spelunkers. The underhall is not a tight space—it's enormous, three hundred meters across—but the darkness compresses your perceived world to a bubble of a few meters around your body. You will experience moments of spatial vertigo, especially when looking up toward the conduit-lined ceiling forty meters above, visible only as faint thermal shimmer and the rare pulse of a data filament. You may feel agoraphobic and claustrophobic simultaneously—aware of the vastness but unable to confirm it visually, enclosed by darkness while standing in an open room.

The cure is the sound. The room's acoustics will teach you its geometry if you let them. The bass hum has spatial character—it originates from the conduit networks along the walls, and as you move, its apparent direction shifts, giving you bearing information. The percussive pulse from below is your metronome, your clock, your constant. Count it. Four seconds between beats. It anchors you in time when the darkness threatens to dissolve your sense of duration. The high singing of the data filaments moves around the room as information flows through different circuits, giving you a sense of activity and direction even when you can see nothing.

Practice meditation before your visit. Specifically, practice sitting in darkness with your eyes open, attending to sound, for periods of at least thirty minutes. The goal is not to eliminate the anxiety of darkness but to develop the habit of redirecting your attention from the absent visual channel to the rich auditory one. When you feel disoriented in the underhall, stop walking. Stand still. Close your eyes (it makes no difference, but the act of closing them signals your brain to stop searching for visual data). Breathe. Listen. The room will tell you where you are.

### The Presence of the Walls

The description hints at something unsettling: the black glass walls may be doing something with your presence. A hand pressed against them produces warmth, a faint tremor, and a momentary shift in the ambient hum. The implication is that the walls—or the computational substrate they may contain—register and process physical contact, integrating your thermal signature into some ongoing process.

This is disconcerting. You are being sensed. Perhaps measured. Perhaps *used*, in some small way, as an input to a system whose purpose you don't understand. Prepare yourself for this feeling. It is not malevolent—nothing in the description suggests hostility—but it is profoundly *impersonal*. The system doesn't care about you as a person. It cares about your heat, your mass, the pressure of your hand, perhaps the chemical signature of your skin oils. You are data. The facility processes data. If you touch the walls, you become part of the process.

Some travelers will find this fascinating. Others will find it violating. Know which kind you are before you descend. If the idea of a semi-aware computational infrastructure registering your body heat makes your skin crawl, you will have a difficult time in the underhall, because the infrastructure is everywhere—walls, ceiling, floor—and the air itself carries the hum of its processing. You cannot escape the system while you are inside it. You can only decide how you relate to it.

My recommendation: relate to it with curiosity and acceptance. You are a guest in a space that has been functioning for centuries. Your thermal signature is a brief fluctuation in a very long data stream. The hum shifted for a moment when you touched the wall, and then it settled back. You are a minor perturbation. This is humbling and, in its way, freeing.

### The Lone Figure

There is someone there. One person, seated motionless by a bioluminescent pool, far across the chamber. Human-shaped but approximate in silhouette. Possibly asleep. Possibly interfacing with something invisible.

Do not approach them immediately. Do not approach them at all unless they signal willingness. This is their space—not in a territorial sense, necessarily, but in the sense that they have adapted to it in ways you have not. They may be working. They may be in a state of consciousness that interruption would damage. They may be part of the infrastructure in some way you don't understand—a human node in the data-processing network, their nervous system linked to the resonance filaments through the equipment that softens their outline.

Observe from a distance. Use the thermal imager, briefly, to confirm they are alive and stable—a body temperature reading, a posture check. Then put it away. If you wish to make contact, do so acoustically—the room was built for sound. A low, sustained hum from your own voice, pitched to match the ambient drone, would be the most contextually appropriate greeting. It says: I am here. I am listening. I am part of the same sound. Whether they respond is their choice. Respect silence as a complete answer.

If they do engage with you, listen more than you speak. They know things about this place that you cannot learn in a single visit. They know the rhythms of the condensation cycles, the meaning of specific pitch shifts in the hum, the behavioral patterns of the conduit creature, the history encoded in the repair layers on the junction housings. But they may communicate in ways that are oblique or non-verbal, especially if they have spent extended time in the underhall and have drifted from surface conversational norms. Be patient. Let the four-second pulse set the pace of your exchange.

### Time Distortion

Be warned: you will lose track of time. The underhall has no day/night cycle, no variation in lighting, no external temporal cues. The percussive pulse provides a rhythm, but it is too regular—it becomes like a heartbeat, something you stop consciously tracking. The condensation cycles provide a longer rhythm, but their period may be irregular, and you won't have calibrated it within a single visit. The warmth, the darkness, the enveloping sound—all of these are mildly soporific. You may enter a state that is not quite sleep but not quite full wakefulness, a sort of acoustic trance in which the hum becomes your entire world and the passage of minutes becomes indistinguishable from the passage of hours.

Set a physical timer—a vibrating alarm on a simple watch, no light, no sound. Decide before you descend how long you intend to stay. Two hours is a good first visit. Long enough to acclimatize, to begin reading the acoustic space, to observe a few condensation cycles, to feel the rhythm of the deeper systems. Not so long that you lose yourself. When the timer vibrates against your wrist, begin making your way back to the bore-shaft. You can return. The underhall has been there for centuries. It will wait.

## Navigational Strategy

When you first enter, stand still at the base of the bore-shaft for at least five full minutes. Let your eyes adjust. Let your ears adjust. Let your skin adjust to the warmth, the humidity, the faint thermal shifts. Identify the following acoustic landmarks:

1. **The bore-shaft behind you.** It will have a distinct acoustic signature—a column of cooler, drier air with different reverberant properties. It is your exit. Learn what it sounds like from five meters away, ten meters, twenty. Check periodically.

2. **The percussive pulse from below.** Note whether it seems to come from a consistent direction or whether it is omnidirectional. If it has directionality, it gives you a fixed bearing.

3. **The bass hum from the conduit networks.** This is densest along the walls. As you move toward the center of the chamber, the hum should diminish slightly or become more diffuse. As you approach any wall, it should intensify and become directional. This is your primary distance-to-wall indicator.

4. **The bioluminescent pools.** These are along the perimeter, at the terminus of the floor drainage channels. They are faint but they are the most reliable visual landmarks. Count them. Note their relative positions. They are your constellation.

Move slowly. Follow the worn paths in the floor—you can feel them through thin-soled shoes as shallow depressions, and they represent the accumulated navigational wisdom of everyone who has walked this space before you. The paths go where it is safe and useful to go. They avoid areas where infrastructure is at floor level, where thermal venting might be dangerous, where the acoustic properties might be disorienting. Trust the paths.

Do not attempt to climb the walls or access the conduit network. The infrastructure adhered to the walls and ceiling is dense, warm, and potentially active in ways you cannot predict. The alloys are above blood temperature; closer to the thermal exchangers, they may be much hotter. The conduit creature navigates this environment with adapted limbs and (possibly) engineered senses. You do not.

## Ethical Considerations

You are visiting what appears to be a living facility of considerable age, cultural significance, and technical sophistication. Its design philosophy is radically different from surface-world norms—it privileges sound over sight, integration over isolation, long-duration stability over rapid change. The inhabitant(s) have adapted to this environment in ways that may include neurological and perceptual modifications you cannot see.

**Do not take anything.** Not a sample of moss, not a vial of bioluminescent water, not a chip of black glass. The systems here are interconnected and have been in equilibrium for a very long time. Removing a piece of moss might disrupt atmospheric filtration in ways that cascade through the thermal cycling. Scraping the wall might interrupt a computational process that has been running for decades. You don't know, and that uncertainty demands restraint.

**Do not leave anything.** Your thermal signature will dissipate. Your footprints on the worn paths will be invisible. But a dropped wrapper, a forgotten cord, a smear of sunscreen transferred from your hand to the wall—these persist. This space has been kept clean and functional across what may be centuries of habitation. Respect that discipline.

**Document through recording, not extraction.** Your acoustic recorder, your thermal imager, your notebook—these capture without taking. They are appropriate. Photography is mostly useless here (there's nothing to photograph in the darkness), but if you have a camera capable of long exposures, a single image of the bioluminescent pools might be worth making—just be aware that even the faint red indicator light of a camera may be the brightest thing in the room.

## What to Expect Emotionally

The underhall will move you. This is not a guarantee for every traveler in every multiverse location, but it is close to a guarantee here. The combination of vast darkness, enveloping sound, warmth, and the feeling of being inside a living system that has been breathing and humming for centuries—it does something to the human nervous system. You will feel small. You will feel held. You will feel, at moments, that the distinction between yourself and the infrastructure has blurred—that your breathing has synchronized with the condensation cycle, that your heartbeat has locked to the four-second pulse, that the hum has entered your bones and is vibrating in sympathy with something in your own biology.

This is not supernatural. It is acoustic and thermal and neurological. The space was *designed* for this—designed to integrate human presence into its rhythms, to make the body a resonant element in its larger system. The architects understood something about how humans respond to low-frequency sound, to warmth, to darkness, to rhythm. They built a space that plays the human nervous system like an instrument, and they did it so well that even a first-time visitor, standing in the dark with no preparation, will feel the tug of it.

Let it happen. Don't fight it. You can analyze later. In the moment, let the underhall teach you what it means to navigate by sound, to think in temperature, to orient by rhythm. Let the darkness close around you like water. Let the hum find the resonant frequency of your sternum and make you part of its chord.

And when your timer vibrates, and you begin the walk back to the bore-shaft, following the worn path by feel, trailing your cord through the dark—you will find that the ascent through the narrow shaft feels different than the descent did. Not because the shaft has changed, but because you have. Your ears will be open in a way they weren't before. Your skin will be alive to thermal gradients. The surface world, when you reach it, will seem garish and loud and overlit, and you will stand blinking in the daylight, listening for a four-second pulse that isn't there, and you will understand for the first time what was lost when your species chose to build its cities in the light.
