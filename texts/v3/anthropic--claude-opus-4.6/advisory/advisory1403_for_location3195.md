# Preparing for the Ridgeline at the End of Wearing

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are visiting a place where the concept of *arrival* has already been interrogated by geology and found wanting. The plateau is not a destination in the way a city is a destination, or even in the way a mountain summit is a destination. It is a remainder. You are traveling to what is left when everything that could be subtracted has been. This means your preparation must account not just for physical survival — though that is non-trivial — but for a psychological and perceptual encounter with a landscape that has achieved a state of completion you have never witnessed, set against a sky that has achieved a state of permanent incompletion you have also never witnessed. The tension between these two facts is the actual environment. The rock and the air are just how it expresses itself.

You need to prepare for both.

---

## Physical Survival: The Atmosphere

The air is thin. Not Everest-thin, not kill-you-in-hours thin, but thin enough that you will feel it in everything you do. Think high Andean altiplano — 4,000 to 5,000 meters equivalent, perhaps, though the comparison is imperfect because there is no sea level and the atmospheric composition may have drifted over the eons in ways that don't map neatly to any terrestrial analogue. Expect roughly 55-60% of the partial pressure of oxygen you're used to at sea level. This means:

**Acclimatization is mandatory.** If your multiverse transit deposits you directly onto the surface, you will need supplemental oxygen for the first several days. Bring a closed-circuit rebreather system — not a simple bottled oxygen rig, because you don't know how long you'll be there, and the logistical reality of the plateau (no resupply, no shelter, no infrastructure of any kind) means you need a system that can recycle. A modern closed-circuit rebreather with chemical CO2 scrubbers and a modest oxygen supply can extend your comfortable operating time significantly. Bring at least three complete sets of scrubber cartridges and two independent oxygen bottles. The systems should be rated for dusty environments — more on that shortly.

Carry acetazolamide (Diamox) or an equivalent altitude medication. Begin taking it 48 hours before transit if your method allows. It will help your kidneys compensate for respiratory alkalosis and reduce the risk of altitude sickness, which at this elevation can include cerebral and pulmonary edema — conditions that will kill you on a plateau with no evacuation possible.

**The air is dry.** Profoundly dry. There is no moisture cycle. The oceans are a geological memory. Relative humidity will be in the single digits or lower. You will lose water through respiration at a rate that will surprise you, especially combined with the altitude-driven increase in breathing rate. Your mucous membranes will crack. Your eyes will sting. Your skin will split at the knuckles and around the nostrils within days if unprotected.

Carry petroleum-based balms for skin. Carry preservative-free artificial tears in quantity — not the small vials, the large bottles, and multiple of them. A hydration bladder with an insulated drinking tube that you can sip from continuously is essential; do not rely on remembering to drink from a bottle. At this level of dryness, by the time you feel thirsty, you are already clinically dehydrated. Calculate your water needs at a minimum of five liters per day, more if you are moving. How you carry or produce this much water is one of the defining logistical challenges of the visit — I will address it below.

**The mineral dust.** This is perhaps the most insidious physical hazard. The description specifies dust "fine as talc," which means particles well below 10 microns, likely below 2.5 microns, possibly submicron. This is respirable dust. It will penetrate deep into your lungs. Over a short visit — days to weeks — this is an irritant. Over a longer stay, it is a pathway to silicosis or a silicosis-like fibrotic condition, depending on the mineral composition of the gneissic residue. Gneiss is typically rich in quartz, feldspar, and mica. Quartz dust is the classic cause of silicosis. Assume the worst.

Wear a respirator rated for fine particulate. An N100 or P100 half-mask respirator at minimum. Better: a full-face respirator that also protects your eyes from the dust. Best: integrate your respiratory protection with your rebreather system so you are breathing filtered, oxygen-enriched air at all times. The dust, remember, is not just on the ground — it is organized into continent-spanning atmospheric structures above you, and when the descending cells press those structures low, you will be immersed in a moving curtain of ancient mineral powder. Those events last only seconds, but seconds of inhaling submicron quartz dust are seconds you will regret years later.

Bring sealed goggles as a backup even if you have a full-face mask. Bring multiple sets of filters. The dust is so fine that filter loading will be slower than in a coarser-dust environment, but the exposure is continuous, and you cannot wash or replace filters once they are saturated.

## Physical Survival: Temperature

Fifteen degrees Celsius as a baseline, dropping below freezing when high-altitude cold cells descend. This is manageable but demands layering, because the transitions can be rapid — the description implies timescales of minutes for atmospheric reconfiguration, and a descending cold cell that drops surface temperature from 15°C to -5°C in a quarter hour is not just plausible but expected.

Carry a layering system built around:

- A moisture-wicking base layer (though "moisture" will be minimal given the dryness — the base layer's primary job here is comfort and insulation)
- A midweight insulating layer (synthetic fill or high-loft fleece; avoid down, which performs poorly if it somehow gets damp and is harder to manage in dusty environments)
- A wind-resistant outer shell — not waterproof, because there is no water. What you need is particulate resistance and wind resistance. A tightly woven softshell fabric is ideal. Sealed seams are helpful not for rain but for dust infiltration.
- Insulated gloves and a balaclava or neck gaiter for the cold descents
- A warm hat and sun hat — separately. The white, even light from a star that has been burning steadily for four billion years will deliver UV radiation that, in the thin, dry, dustless interludes, is essentially unfiltered. Sunscreen rated SPF 50+ applied to all exposed skin, reapplied frequently, is essential.

A sleeping bag rated to -15°C or -20°C. You will be sleeping on exposed gneiss with no possibility of shelter unless you bring it. The rock will conduct heat away from your body efficiently. A closed-cell foam pad is mandatory — an inflatable pad provides better insulation but is vulnerable to puncture on the mineral surface. Bring both: closed-cell foam as a base layer, inflatable pad on top.

## Physical Survival: Water and Food

This is the hardest problem.

There is no water on the plateau. None. The description makes this clear: the oceans are gone, there is no moisture cycle, the atmosphere cannot produce clouds. The lichen extracts what it needs from mineral substrate, not from atmospheric moisture. You cannot rely on condensation, precipitation, or any natural water source.

You have three options:

**Option one: Carry everything.** At five liters per day and a weight of five kilograms per day of water alone, a two-week visit requires 70 kilograms of water. This is not feasible for a person on foot, even with a cart or sledge, which will not move easily over gneissic terrain (though the surface is described as scoured and smooth, so a wheeled cart might work on the flatter expanses).

**Option two: Atmospheric water extraction.** There are devices — thermoelectric condensers, desiccant-based atmospheric water generators — that can extract moisture from air. But these are designed for atmospheres with at least some humidity. At the humidity levels implied by this world, extraction rates will be vanishingly low. You might get a few hundred milliliters per day from a device designed to produce liters. This is supplementary at best.

**Option three: Mineral extraction.** Some minerals contain bound water — hydrated silicates, phyllosilicates, clays. If the gneissic substrate contains any hydrated minerals, it is theoretically possible to heat-extract water from crushed rock. This requires energy (solar is available — the light is consistent and the sky is clear between dust events), equipment to crush and heat rock, and a condenser to capture the released vapor. This is a frontier survival technique at best. Research the mineralogy of high-grade gneiss before your visit and bring a portable XRF spectrometer to identify hydrated phases in the field. If the lichen is surviving on mineral-bound water released through slow chemistry, the water is *there* — it is simply locked in crystal structures and released at rates measured in molecular transactions per century. You need to speed that process up with heat.

My honest recommendation: carry as much water as you can, supplement with atmospheric extraction, bring mineral extraction capability as an emergency backup, and plan your visit duration around your water supply. A week is achievable with serious load-carrying. Two weeks pushes the limits. Beyond that, you need a base camp with a solar-powered water extraction system and time to establish whether it can produce enough to sustain you.

Food is simpler by comparison. Bring calorie-dense, low-water-content provisions: freeze-dried meals (but remember you need water to reconstitute them, which compounds the water problem), nut butters, hard cheeses vacuum-sealed, energy bars, dried meats. There is nothing edible on the plateau. The lichen is almost certainly not nutritious in any meaningful way — it grows a millimeter per century and metabolizes rock; its biochemistry will be alien to your digestion even if it isn't toxic. The crawling things are too rare, too small, and too inscrutable to consider as food, and eating them would be both ethically uncertain and analytically wasteful.

## Shelter

There is no natural shelter. The plateau is featureless. The scalloped basins are centimeters deep. The structures — the engineered pylons and frames and pedestals — are small and should not be used as shelter, for reasons I will discuss under "Conduct."

Bring a freestanding tent rated for high winds, even though the surface wind is described as minimal. Here is why: the atmospheric cells are descending and ascending unpredictably. A descending cell that brings cold can also bring a transient wind event at the surface — perhaps not the ferocious speeds of the jet streams at altitude, but enough to collapse an improperly secured shelter. More importantly, the tent is your primary defense against dust intrusion during low-curtain events and against radiative heat loss at night. The even white light suggests the star is overhead for a significant portion of the day, but nights — when they come — will be radiatively brutal. With no moisture, no cloud cover, and thin air, the surface will lose heat to space rapidly. Expect nighttime temperatures well below what the daytime baseline would suggest.

Stake the tent with rock anchors — drill-in anchors or heavy deadman anchors, because tent stakes will not penetrate gneiss. Alternatively, weight the tent with bags filled with crushed rock. Bring a small geological hammer and chisel for this purpose. The rock is ancient and hard, but gneiss has foliation planes that can be exploited to break off manageable pieces.

## Navigation

There are no landmarks. The plateau extends beyond the horizon in every direction. The foliation bands in the rock provide *local* texture but not navigational reference — they swoop and arc in patterns that record ancient tectonic pressures, not directions useful to a traveler.

GPS will not function — there are no satellites. Magnetic compass may or may not work depending on whether the planet retains a functional magnetic field; a world this geologically quiescent may have a weakened or dead dynamo. Bring one anyway, but do not rely on it.

Your primary navigation tools should be:

- **Solar navigation.** The star's position gives you a reliable directional reference during the day. Determine the planet's rotational axis relative to the star, establish cardinal directions, and navigate by shadow and angle. A solar compass or sextant is more useful here than any electronic device.
- **Inertial navigation.** A high-quality inertial measurement unit (IMU) will track your position relative to your starting point through dead reckoning. Bring one with a long battery life. Supplement with a pedometer and careful pace-counting.
- **The structures.** If you can locate and catalog the positions of nearby structures, they serve as navigational references. But "nearby" on this plateau means potentially hundreds of kilometers away. You may be able to see some of them from great distances — the matte black ones will be visible as points of absolute darkness against the pale rock — but distances will be extremely difficult to judge in the featureless terrain and the refractive distortion of the shear layers.

Mark your base camp. Use something unambiguously visible: a bright orange marker flag on a tall pole, or a flashing beacon if you have the power budget. In terrain this featureless, failing to find your base camp is a death sentence.

## Equipment for Observation

You will want to observe. This place demands observation. It will, in fact, offer little else — there is nothing to *do* on the Ridgeline except witness it, and the witnessing is the point. Prepare accordingly.

**Optical instruments.** Binoculars of the highest quality you can obtain, preferably image-stabilized, because the atmospheric refraction will make distant observation tricky and you will want to resolve detail in the dust structures. A spotting scope with a tripod for studying the structures and the crawling organisms. A macro lens or loupe for examining the lichen, the rock foliation, and the surface textures of the structures. If you can obtain one: a field-portable microscope, optical or electron, for examining the nested complexity of the structures' surfaces. The description suggests that the structures reveal increasing detail at increasing magnification; you will want to see how far you can follow that recursion. The answer — that it continues past your instrument's resolution — will be important for your understanding of the place.

**Recording instruments.** Cameras, obviously, with lenses from ultra-wide to macro. But also: a high-quality audio recorder with sensitive microphones. The silence of this place is not silence — it is a specific acoustic environment defined by the absence of the sounds you expect and the presence of sounds you don't. The low chest-felt exhalation of a descending dust curtain, the subsonic thrum of atmospheric shear (which you may feel but not hear), the faint mineral ticking of the rock as it thermally cycles between day and night — these are worth capturing. Bring a contact microphone to press against the rock surface. Bring a long-baseline infrasound detector if you can manage it.

**Atmospheric instruments.** An anemometer (you will be surprised by how little wind registers at the surface, and that data is useful). A barometric altimeter (there is no sea level, but tracking relative pressure changes will tell you about the descent and ascent of atmospheric cells). A LIDAR or laser rangefinder pointed upward to measure the altitude and structure of the dust formations. A simple particle counter to measure dust concentration at ground level, which will spike during low-curtain events and give you warning to seal your respirator.

**Geological instruments.** A rock hammer and hand lens are the minimum. A portable XRF spectrometer tells you the composition of the substrate. A magnetometer tells you about the rock's magnetic history. A hardness testing kit. A small collection of sample bags — though take samples sparingly and with thought; this surface has been stable for a time period that dwarfs your ability to feel its weight, and removing even a fragment is a kind of transgression against patience.

## Conduct Around the Structures

This requires its own section because the structures require their own mode of approach.

They are engineered beyond your comprehension. This is stated plainly in the description and should be taken at face value. The information density in the outermost four millimeters of a single pylon exceeds current material science. The metal that has grain that moves. The frozen-smoke translucence. These are artifacts of a capability so far beyond yours that the gap is not one of degree but of kind — like an ant encountering a semiconductor fabrication facility.

**Do not touch them.**

Not because they are dangerous — though they might be. Not because touching them might damage them — it almost certainly won't; whatever made them made them to endure in an environment that has eroded mountains to nothing. But because you do not know what touching them means. The structures interact with the atmospheric turbulence in ways that are observable but not understood. They may be sensors. They may be actuators. They may be something for which you have no category. Placing your hand on one might do nothing. It might register. It might alter a measurement that has been running for longer than your species has existed. You don't know. Err on the side of not inserting yourself into a process you cannot comprehend.

Observe them from a distance of at least two meters initially. Approach closer only after extended observation — hours, not minutes — to establish baseline behavior. Watch the dust flow around them. Watch the light on their surfaces. Watch for changes in the moving grain of the metallic ones. Document everything. If a structure seems to react to your presence — if the dust deviates differently, if the surface grain accelerates, if the quality of the air near it changes — retreat slowly and observe the return to baseline.

**Do not attempt to take a sample from a structure.** I know the temptation. A material whose outermost four millimeters contain more information than you can decode — of course you want to take a piece home. But you don't know whether a fragment can be removed without consequence, you don't know whether the structure's function depends on its wholeness, and you don't know whether the act of cutting into matter that has been "told to be something else" will release the something else or simply destroy the telling. Leave them intact. Your instruments — your cameras, spectrometers, microscopes — are your interface. Use them.

The crawling things do not approach the structures. That is noted in the description. Take it as data. Perhaps the structures are irrelevant to the crawling things. Perhaps the crawling things know something you don't.

## Conduct Regarding the Living Things

The lichen has been growing for longer than most species exist. A colony that spans a scalloped basin — hundreds of meters across — represents an unbroken continuity of life that may predate the emergence of complex organisms on your home world. **Walk around it.** Not across it. The description says the crawling things cross lichen without deviation, but the crawling things weigh grams and have distributed pressure across many limbs. You weigh seventy kilograms and concentrate it on two boot soles. Even lichen that has survived for geological ages is vulnerable to compression, and the fractal edges — the millimeter-per-century growth front — are the most delicate and the most important, because they represent the lichen's current relationship with time. Step on the center and you damage a record. Step on the edge and you set back a process that measures its progress in centuries. Walk around.

If you encounter one of the crawling things: stop. Sit down, slowly. Do not approach. Observe. You may have the only extended observation of this organism that has ever occurred, or that will occur for decades. Note its speed, its heading, its morphology. Does it navigate by reference to the foliation lines? To the position of the star? To the structures? To something else entirely? How many limbs? What is the texture of its integument? Does it leave a trace on the rock? Watch it until it passes beyond your vision. Record everything. Do not collect it. There might be three of them in a space the size of a European country. Taking one would be an act of incomprehensible proportional violence.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section most travelers will skip in their eagerness to get to the packing list. That is a mistake. The Ridgeline at the End of Wearing is not physically the most dangerous place in the multiverse — it is survivable with appropriate gear and careful planning. But it is psychologically among the most demanding, and the demands are subtle, which makes them more dangerous, not less.

**The scale.** The plateau extends to the horizon and beyond in every direction without feature, without interruption, without even the suggestion that somewhere, eventually, it stops. You have likely experienced open landscapes before — deserts, oceans, ice sheets. This is different. Those landscapes carry the implication of edges: the desert ends at mountains, the ocean at shores, the ice at coastline. This does not end. It is the *result* of ending. Everything that could provide variety has been worn away, and what remains is the irreducible substrate of a world that once had everything your world has — oceans, mountains, forests, possibly cities, possibly civilizations — and has outlived all of it. You are standing on a world-sized gravestone, except it has outlived the memory of what it marks.

This produces, in most visitors, a feeling that is not quite vertigo and not quite grief and not quite awe but contains elements of all three. The Germans might call it something compound and precise. I don't have the word. It manifests as a heaviness behind the sternum, a difficulty completing thoughts, a tendency to stand still and stare at the middle distance for longer than you intend. It is not dangerous in itself, but it can erode decision-making and motivation, which *is* dangerous when you need to manage water supplies and navigation and dust exposure with precision.

**The silence.** Real silence — not the silence of a quiet room, which is full of the building's breathing, but the silence of a world-spanning mineral surface under a thin, dry atmosphere with no life large enough to make sound — is physiologically disorienting. Your auditory system, receiving no input, will begin to generate its own: tinnitus, phantom sounds, the roaring of your own blood, crackles and pops that seem to come from the rock or the air but come from your inner ear adjusting to the absence of calibration. After several days, some travelers report auditory hallucinations — voices, music, rhythmic sounds that seem to emanate from the structures or from beneath the rock. These are almost certainly endogenous. Almost certainly. The structures' function is unknown, and the possibility that they produce subsonic or infrasonic emissions that your brainstem interprets as quasi-auditory experience cannot be excluded.

Bring music. Bring familiar recordings. Bring podcasts, audiobooks, the sound of a friend's voice. Not to play constantly — the silence is part of the experience, and you should sit with it, learn from it — but to break it when you need to break it, when the phantom sounds start to feel too organized, too purposeful, when you start to suspect the plateau is trying to tell you something. It isn't. Probably.

**The timescales.** This is the hardest thing. You are standing on a surface that has been stable for a period of time you cannot feel. You can know the number — billions of years — but you cannot *feel* it, in the same way you can know the distance to a star but cannot feel the emptiness between. And yet the place insists that you try. The foliation lines in the gneiss, sweoping in arcs that record pressures from when mountains existed — those lines are an invitation to contemplate a duration that your nervous system is not built to process. The lichen that grows a millimeter per century — that is an invitation to stand at the edge of a colony and try to feel the weight of the time represented by its radius. The structures whose grain moves over hours — even that, the shortest timescale on offer, is an invitation to sit and watch and feel the hours pass and understand that you are watching the fastest thing on this plateau and it is achingly slow.

Some travelers find this peaceful. They sink into the deep time, let it wash over them, let the urgency of their ordinary lives dissolve against the plateau's patience. Others find it annihilating — a confrontation with their own brevity that they cannot metabolize. Know which kind of traveler you are before you go, if you can. If you don't know, bring a journal. Write in it. The act of writing — of making marks that represent thoughts that represent your specific, temporary, human experience of a place that will outlast your species — is a way of insisting on your own timescale in the face of the plateau's. It is a small act of temporal defiance. It helps.

## Duration of Visit

I recommend five to seven days for a first visit. Less than five days does not give you time to acclimatize to the altitude, adjust to the silence, observe a full diurnal cycle in detail, and locate and study at least one structure. More than seven days begins to strain your water supply and your psychology in ways that require a level of preparation and experience that a first-time visitor is unlikely to have.

If you plan a longer expedition — and some travelers will want to, because the plateau rewards patience the way almost no other landscape does — you need a base camp with solar-powered water extraction, redundant shelter, and a communication schedule with someone off-world. The plateau does not forgive logistical failures. There is no one to rescue you. The crawling things will not help. The structures will not help. The lichen will grow over your remains at a millimeter per century, and in a few hundred thousand years, the dust will bury you, and the wind will unbury you, and bury you again, and in the fullness of time the gneiss will incorporate you into its patient, banded truth, and you will become part of what cannot be broken down further.

This is not a threat. It is simply a description of what happens here to things that stop.

## Final Notes

Bring something from home. Something small, something with no survival function, something that matters to you — a photograph, a letter, a stone from a river, a child's drawing. Not to leave behind (leave nothing behind) but to hold. On the second or third evening, when the dust curtains are catching the lateral light and painting the sky in pale amber and rose, when the silence has settled into your bones and the gneiss is cooling under your feet and the unimaginable age of the surface is pressing up through your boot soles like a vibration too low to feel but too real to ignore — you will want to hold something that proves you come from a world where things are still *happening*. Where rivers still run and mountains still stand and rain still falls and erosion is a process, not a completed project. Where things begin.

Hold it. Remember that you are from a place of beginning, visiting a place of ending, and that both of these are real, and that you are standing at the boundary between them, and that this is the rarest thing — rarer than the lichen, rarer than the crawling things, rarer than the structures with their impossible matter — because *you are the only thing on this plateau that knows what time feels like from the inside*.

The plateau does not know. The plateau is what time looks like from the outside, when it is finished.

Go. See it. Come home.
