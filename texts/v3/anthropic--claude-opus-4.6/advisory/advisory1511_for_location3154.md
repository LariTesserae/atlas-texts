# Preparing for the Hydroponic Reliquary

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description and understand the fundamental tension of this place: it is a tropical lowland ecosystem forcibly installed at extreme altitude inside a mountain. Every system in the room is fighting thermodynamics, fighting the mountain's desire to be cold and dry and lifeless. You are entering an environment that exists through sheer engineering stubbornness, and your body will feel the contradiction. The air will tell your lungs you're in a greenhouse in Borneo. Your inner ears and your blood oxygen will tell you you're on a high peak. These two messages will not reconcile. Prepare for that dissonance specifically, because it is the defining physical experience of this place and it cannot be fully mitigated—only managed.

---

## Altitude Acclimatization

This is the most critical and most easily underestimated preparation. The description says your ears want to pop. The air feels thinner than the humidity suggests it should. The engineering compensates for temperature and moisture but *cannot* compensate for atmospheric pressure. You are at significant elevation—likely somewhere between 3,500 and 5,500 meters, given the description of cold, dry, scouring wind outside and the suggestion that exposed water would freeze rapidly.

**Begin acclimatization days or weeks in advance.** If the multiverse travel deposits you at the Reliquary's altitude without gradual ascent, you need pharmaceutical preparation:

- **Acetazolamide (Diamox):** Begin taking 125-250mg twice daily at least 24 hours before arrival, ideally 48. This is a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor that accelerates your kidneys' bicarbonate excretion, forcing a compensatory metabolic acidosis that mimics the respiratory alkalosis your body normally takes days to produce at altitude. Side effects include tingling in the fingers and toes, altered taste (carbonated beverages taste flat and metallic), and increased urination. All manageable. The increased urination will be relevant—you'll need to hydrate aggressively, and the room's humidity will make you *think* you're not losing water when you are.

- **Dexamethasone** as a backup for acute mountain sickness symptoms. Carry 4mg tablets. This is a glucocorticoid that reduces cerebral and pulmonary edema. It doesn't aid acclimatization—it masks symptoms—but if you develop a splitting headache and nausea inside the Reliquary, you need it to function while you figure out your next move.

- **A portable pulse oximeter.** Clip-on finger type. Check your SpO2 regularly. The warm, humid air will make you feel less altitude-sick than you actually are—the thermal comfort is deceptive. If you drop below 85% saturation, you need supplemental oxygen or you need to descend, regardless of how you feel. The humidity doesn't add oxygen. This is the trap.

- **Supplemental oxygen.** Carry a small cylinder with a demand-valve regulator and nasal cannula. Not a full mountaineering rig—you're indoors, the environment is warm, you don't need the bulk. But having 15-20 minutes of supplemental O2 available could be the difference between a headache and a cerebral edema crisis if you're sensitive to altitude. A lightweight aluminum D-cylinder will suffice.

**The humidity will confuse your thirst response.** When the air is this saturated, your body's evaporative cooling barely functions, and you don't feel the dry-mouth signal that usually prompts drinking at altitude. But altitude still dehydrates you—increased respiration rate, increased urination from acclimatization drugs, the basic physics of lower partial pressure of water vapor at altitude (which the room overrides inside but which your body was already responding to before you entered). Drink proactively. Bring electrolyte powder. Aim for at least a liter per hour of light activity, more if you're sweating.

## Thermal and Humidity Management

The room is hot and wet. Extremely wet. Breathing feels like drinking something thin. This is specific and physiologically meaningful.

At 100% or near-100% relative humidity with warm temperatures (the reservoir radiates perceptible heat from a meter away, the walls are warm to the touch—we're talking at minimum 30-35°C, possibly higher near the reservoir), your body cannot cool itself through evaporation. Sweat will not evaporate. It will bead on your skin and mix with the condensation already forming on every surface. Your core temperature will rise.

This creates a compound problem with altitude: your heart is already working harder to circulate less-oxygenated blood, and now it must also work harder to shunt blood to your skin for radiative cooling (the only mechanism that functions when evaporative cooling fails). Cardiac output demands are elevated on two axes simultaneously. If you have any cardiovascular concerns whatsoever, consult a physician before this trip. This is not a joke. The combination of hypobaric hypoxia and heat stress is synergistically dangerous in ways that neither condition alone predicts.

**Clothing:**

- Wear as little as dignity and safety allow. Lightweight, loose-fitting synthetic fabric—no cotton. Cotton absorbs water from the air and becomes a sodden, clinging layer that traps heat against your skin. A synthetic moisture-wicking shirt (even though it can't wick to evaporation in this humidity, it at least won't absorb and hold water the way cotton does) and synthetic shorts or lightweight pants.

- **Footwear is critical.** The floor is textured steel grating that is perpetually wet. You need shoes with aggressive drainage and non-slip soles designed for wet metal. Think water shoes with steel-grate-compatible tread, or lightweight approach shoes with Vibram-type soles rated for wet surfaces. Do not go barefoot—the grating is textured (presumably for grip), which means it has edges and ridges that will abrade wet skin. And beneath the grating are drainage channels and reservoirs of warm nutrient water. Falling through a compromised section of grating into warm, nutrient-rich water would be unpleasant and potentially dangerous. Test every step. The moss is colonizing surfaces it shouldn't be—including, potentially, the grating itself. Moss on wet steel is approximately as slippery as ice.

- **Gloves.** Bring thin nitrile or latex gloves. Not for warmth—for handling. Every surface is wet. Every surface is either steel (slippery when wet), concrete (abrasive when wet), volcanic glass (potentially sharp), or covered in biological material. The moss growing over access panels and valve handles means that if you need to interact with any of the room's infrastructure, you'll be gripping through a layer of living material growing on wet metal. Gloves give you friction and protect your skin from prolonged exposure to the nutrient solution, which—given its opacity and mineral content—may be mildly irritating with extended contact.

- A **cooling towel** (the kind you soak and snap to activate evaporative cooling) will be nearly useless here due to the humidity. Instead, bring a small insulated container with ice water and a cloth. Applying genuinely cold water to your wrists, neck, and forehead forces convective cooling even when evaporative cooling fails. This is a crude but effective thermoregulation tool.

## Respiratory Protection and Air Quality

You are breathing the room's circulatory system. The description specifies atomized nutrient solution being sprayed from ceiling nozzles. This means you are inhaling an aerosol of dissolved mineral salts and whatever the plants' metabolic outputs include.

**Bring a respirator.** Not a cloth mask—a proper half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and an organic vapor cartridge. The particulate filter catches the nutrient aerosol droplets. The organic vapor cartridge addresses any volatile organic compounds the plants may be off-gassing.

Consider: this is a closed system with no apparent ventilation to the outside. The plants photosynthesize under continuous light, producing oxygen—good. But they also respire, consuming oxygen and producing CO2, especially the enormous biomass of roots, moss, and non-photosynthetic tissue. The algae in the reservoir photosynthesize as well, but also respire. In a sealed system this dense, CO2 levels may be elevated beyond comfortable ranges, and certain metabolic byproducts—ethylene, various terpenes, volatile aldehydes—may accumulate to concentrations you wouldn't encounter in a ventilated space.

**Bring a portable multi-gas detector.** At minimum, it should read O2 percentage and CO2 ppm. Acceptable O2 is 19.5-23.5%. Below 19.5%, you are in an oxygen-deficient atmosphere and must use supplemental oxygen or exit. CO2 above 5,000 ppm (0.5%) will cause headaches, dizziness, and impaired cognition—symptoms that overlap with altitude sickness, making diagnosis difficult. Above 40,000 ppm (4%), CO2 becomes acutely dangerous. In a sealed room packed with biomass and no evident air exchange, these readings are not theoretical concerns—they are the first thing you should check upon entry.

The sterility of the system—no insects, no decomposers—suggests some kind of UV sterilization, ozone treatment, or chemical biocide in the water loop. If ozone, residual atmospheric ozone could be irritating at the concentrations needed to maintain sterility. This is another reason for the respirator and multi-gas detector. Ozone at 0.1 ppm causes throat irritation; at 0.3 ppm, it causes coughing and chest pain. These are well below concentrations you'd smell (ozone's odor threshold is around 0.01-0.02 ppm, so you *might* detect a faint sharp, clean smell—but in the overwhelming green humidity of this room, you might miss it).

## Equipment Protection

Everything you bring into this room will get wet. Not splashed—*saturated*. The air itself is depositing water on every surface. Your electronics, your papers, your optics, your metal tools—all of them will be coated in a warm film of condensation within minutes of entering.

- **All electronics in waterproof cases.** IP67 minimum. Your phone, your gas detector, your pulse oximeter—seal them. The warm nutrient-laden moisture is more corrosive than clean water. Dissolved mineral salts will accelerate galvanic corrosion on exposed contacts.

- **Anti-fog treatment for any optics.** If you wear glasses, treat them. They will fog instantly upon entry. The temperature differential between you (cooler, having come from the cold mountain exterior) and the room (hot, wet) means you are the coldest surface in the space for your first 15-20 minutes inside. Condensation will form on *you* before it forms on the walls. Your lenses, your camera lenses, your gas detector's sensor windows—all of it. Anti-fog wipes, Cat Crap paste, or hydrophilic coatings. Apply before entry.

- **Do not bring paper.** It will become pulp. If you need to take notes, use a waterproof notepad (like Rite in the Rain) with a pressurized pen (like a Fisher Space Pen) or a mechanical pencil with waterproof lead. Better yet, use a sealed voice recorder.

- **Your bag itself** should be waterproof. A dry bag or a pack with a waterproof liner. Everything inside will still accumulate condensation—the humidity penetrates—but at least you won't be pouring out pooled water when you open it.

## Navigation and Movement

The room is approximately fifteen meters across at its widest, but navigable space is drastically reduced by the hydroponic arrays, the central reservoir, hanging root systems, drooping fern fronds, and root-choked drainage channels. You will be turning sideways, ducking, stepping over obstacles, all on wet steel grating, in flat shadowless light that eliminates depth perception.

**The light is the subtle danger here.** Industrial-bright, flat, shadowless noon—this sounds comfortable but is actually disorienting. Shadows are how your brain calculates distance, edge, and surface angle. Without them, you lose spatial judgment. The grating beneath your feet, the root tangles you're stepping over, the gap between your shoe and the next solid surface—all of these will be harder to judge than they should be. Move slowly. Move deliberately. Test your footing before transferring weight.

- Bring a **small, bright flashlight with a tight beam.** Not for illumination—the room is well-lit. For *creating shadows.* When you need to judge the depth of a drainage channel or whether a section of grating is structurally sound beneath its moss covering, angle a tight beam across it. The shadows it casts will give you the depth information the ambient light denies you.

- **Mark your path.** The room isn't large, but it is dense and visually repetitive—green upon green, steel upon steel, all at the same apparent distance. Disorientation is possible. Bring small, brightly colored waterproof markers (silicone cable ties work well) and mark your route as you move through the space. The moss will eventually grow over them, but not in the timeframe of a single visit.

- **Do not touch the central reservoir water.** It is warm, opaque with dissolved nutrients, and the only aquatic environment in a room that is otherwise sterile. Whatever biological activity is occurring in that water—algae at minimum, possibly bacterial mats—is the room's biochemical engine. Disturbing it could introduce contaminants (from your skin, your clothing, your breath) that the system may not be designed to handle. Also: you don't know what's dissolved in there. Concentrated hydroponic nutrient solutions can be mildly caustic. The warmth of the water (radiating perceptible heat from a meter away suggests 40°C+) adds risk. Don't fall in. Don't lean over it. Don't reach in.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the preparation most people skip and the one that matters most for this particular location.

The Hydroponic Reliquary is not dangerous in the way a combat zone or a collapsing structure is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way a sensory deprivation tank is dangerous—it removes the stimuli your brain uses to maintain its model of time, space, and self, and replaces them with a narrow, repetitive, overwhelming input stream.

**What will happen to you psychologically in this room:**

1. **Time dissolution.** There is no day/night cycle. The light never changes. The soundscape never changes—pumps hum, condensation drips, silence absorbs. Your brain's circadian anchoring will begin to slip within 2-3 hours. After 6-8 hours, you will have difficulty estimating how long you've been inside. After 24 hours, your sense of time will be seriously degraded. **Bring a watch. Set alarms. Decide your departure time before you enter and do not renegotiate it while inside.**

2. **Scale confusion.** The flat light eliminates depth cues. The repetitive green visual field eliminates landmark differentiation. The room is fifteen meters wide but feels both claustrophobic (because it is packed, because you are always touching or brushing against something) and vast (because the light makes distances uncertain). This is genuinely disorienting and can produce a mild derealization effect—the feeling that the room is not quite real, or that you are not quite in it. This is normal. It is the product of specific visual conditions, not a property of the place. Acknowledge it and keep moving.

3. **The plants' indifference.** The description emphasizes this repeatedly: the room does not need you. Does not notice you. You are a brief perturbation in humidity. This is an unusual psychological experience. Most environments respond to human presence—animals flee or approach, other people acknowledge you, weather acts on you. Here, nothing responds. The plants grow on their timescale, the systems cycle, and you are irrelevant. For some people, this is deeply peaceful. For others, it triggers a specific existential discomfort—a feeling of being *unnecessary* that can shade into something darker during prolonged exposure. Know which type you are before you enter. If prolonged anonymity to your environment causes you distress, limit your visit duration.

4. **The sound.** The padded silence is the most psychologically potent feature of the room. Sound doesn't echo—it's absorbed by the biomass. Your footsteps vanish the instant they're made. Your voice, if you speak, will sound flat and close and dead, as though you're speaking into a pillow. This acoustic deadness, combined with the sub-audible pump vibration (which you feel in your chest and sinuses more than hear), creates a peculiar sensory state: you are aware that you are in a *working* space—something is happening, energy is being spent—but it produces no sound your ears can properly register. Your brain will try to compensate by becoming hypervigilant to the sounds that do exist: the drip of condensation, the tick of your shoes on the grating. These will become disproportionately significant. After several hours, you may find yourself waiting for the next drip with an intensity that surprises you. This is normal auditory compensation in a low-stimulus environment. It is not pathological, but it is absorbing, and it can make it hard to focus on tasks.

**Mitigation strategies:**

- Bring music or a podcast on a sealed audio player with bone-conduction headphones (which leave your ears open for environmental awareness). Having a controllable audio source gives your brain something to anchor its temporal processing to. Songs have known durations. Podcasts have narrative arcs. These are temporal landmarks in an otherwise landmarkless soundscape.

- Talk to yourself. Seriously. Narrate what you're doing, what you're seeing. Your own voice, even flattened by the acoustic deadness, is a known quantity. It confirms your presence in a room that is otherwise uninterested in confirming it.

- Bring a companion if possible. Another human being in this space changes it fundamentally. You become each other's temporal anchors, environmental references, sanity checks. The room's indifference is much easier to bear when shared.

## What to Bring: The Complete Kit

Organized by priority:

**Tier 1: Survival and Safety**
- Supplemental oxygen (small aluminum D-cylinder with demand valve and nasal cannula)
- Portable multi-gas detector (O2, CO2, ozone minimum)
- Pulse oximeter
- Acetazolamide (started 24-48 hours prior)
- Dexamethasone (4mg tablets, emergency use)
- 2-3 liters of water with electrolyte supplements
- High-calorie, easily digestible food (the altitude suppresses appetite; the heat suppresses it further; eat on schedule, not by hunger)
- Half-face respirator with P100/OV cartridges
- Waterproof first aid kit with blister care, anti-fungal cream (the moisture will find every fold and crease of your skin), and anti-nausea medication

**Tier 2: Mobility and Orientation**
- Wet-rated non-slip footwear
- Tight-beam flashlight for shadow creation
- Waterproof route markers
- Watch with alarm function
- Thin nitrile gloves (several pairs—they'll degrade in the moisture)

**Tier 3: Documentation and Communication**
- Waterproof camera in IP68 housing, with anti-fog treated lens
- Waterproof notepad and pressurized pen
- Sealed voice recorder
- All electronics in individual waterproof cases with desiccant packets (which will saturate quickly, so bring extras)
- Bone-conduction headphones with sealed audio player

**Tier 4: Comfort and Psychology**
- Minimal lightweight synthetic clothing
- Cooling supplies (insulated container with ice, cloth for cold-water application)
- Anti-fog lens treatment
- Music/podcast/audio content loaded on player
- A small personal comfort object—a familiar tactile thing you can hold. A smooth stone, a metal coin, a carved piece of wood. Something dry and known. In a room where everything is wet and growing and strange, having one thing in your hand that is none of those things is worth more than you'd expect.

## The Approach

You have to get to the room first, and the room is inside a mountain, high up. The description says above the clouds. The approach, whatever form it takes, involves ascending to significant altitude in conditions described as cold, dry, and windy. This means you need mountaineering approach gear—insulating layers, wind protection, eye protection against UV at altitude—that you will then need to *shed* almost entirely before or immediately after entering the Reliquary. The thermal transition is extreme: from sub-zero wind-scoured mountain exterior to 30°C+ saturated tropical interior, possibly within the span of walking through a door or airlock.

**This transition is physiologically shocking.** Your body will be in cold-adapted mode—vasoconstricted, metabolically ramped up for heat production—and will suddenly be hit with warm, wet air that demands the opposite response. Expect a period of 15-30 minutes of acute discomfort: profuse sweating, flushing, rapid heart rate, possible dizziness or nausea, and immediate total fogging of all cold surfaces you're carrying (including your glasses, optics, and anything metal). Have your transition clothing accessible. Have your anti-fog treatments already applied. Have your respirator ready to don.

Consider staging gear outside the entrance. Leave your mountaineering layers, your cold-weather boots, your wind shell in a protected cache outside the door. Enter the Reliquary in your light kit. This reduces the thermal mass you're carrying into the warm space and reduces the amount of cold-surface condensation you'll generate.

## Duration

Do not stay more than 12 hours on your first visit. Ideally, stay 4-6 hours. The combination of altitude, heat stress, acoustic deprivation, temporal disorientation, and aerosol inhalation is cumulative. You will feel fine at hour 2. You will feel contemplative and slightly strange at hour 4. By hour 8, your judgment about whether you should stay or leave will be compromised by the very conditions that make leaving advisable.

Set your departure alarm before you enter. When it sounds, leave. Do not renegotiate. The room will still be there. The plants will not have noticed your departure. You can always return.

## The Ethical Dimension

One final consideration, less practical but no less important.

This room is an act of extraordinary will. Someone poured concrete and volcanic glass into layers. Someone welded a reservoir and installed pumps and heating systems and hydroponic arrays and sterilization systems inside a mountain peak above the clouds, in a place where nothing grows, and forced a tropical ecosystem into existence through pure infrastructure. The energy required to maintain this—heating the walls, running the pumps, powering the lights perpetually—is immense. The engineering commitment is ongoing and total. If the systems stop, the room freezes and everything inside dies within hours.

You are visiting someone's project. Someone's obsession. Someone's reliquary—a word that means a container for sacred relics, and the relics here are the plants themselves, or perhaps the principle of growth, or perhaps the act of defiance against the mountain's barren hostility.

**Do not damage anything.** Do not remove any plant material. Do not introduce anything biological—no outside soil on your shoes, no food scraps, no spilled water from your own supply (which contains different minerals than the system's solution). The sterility of this system is maintained at enormous cost and for reasons you may not fully understand. The absence of insects and decomposers is not an accident—it is a choice, probably an expensive one, and introducing a single fungal spore or bacterial colony from outside could cascade through the closed loop in unpredictable ways.

You are a guest in something that functions best without you. Move through it with care. Observe with respect. Breathe shallowly if you can. And when you leave, close whatever door separates this wet green impossibility from the cold dry truth of the mountain, and let the pumps hum on in the padded silence without you.

## After Departure

When you exit back into the mountain's cold, your wet clothing will begin to freeze almost immediately. Have dry mountaineering layers accessible *outside* the Reliquary. Change fast. The moisture on your skin and in your lungs will make the cold feel far more severe than it did on the approach—evaporative cooling, which was useless inside, will now work against you with vicious efficiency.

Expect a persistent feeling of the room for hours afterward. The pump vibration will linger in your chest as a phantom sensation. The flat light will make natural shadows seem unusually dramatic. Normal ambient sound—wind, your own footsteps with actual echo, the creak of your pack—will seem almost unbearably loud and rich after the padded silence. You may find yourself reaching for moisture on surfaces that are dry. You may find yourself listening for a drip that doesn't come.

These are normal after-effects of sensory environment transition. They fade within a day.

The memory of the room does not fade as quickly. The green persists. The wet, padded, indifferent green. It stays with you longer than you'd think, not as an image but as a *state*—a remembered way of being in a space that was alive around you in every direction and did not care that you were there. It is a strangely clarifying memory. It reminds you that growth does not require your attention, that systems can sustain themselves in hostile conditions through sheer engineered persistence, that there are rooms inside mountains where the air is thick as broth and the roots reach and the world is green and warm and closed and complete without you.

You will want to go back. Factor that into your preparation.
