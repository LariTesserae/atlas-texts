# Preparing for the Switching Yard at Vael

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything practical, you need to sit with what Vael actually is, because your preparation will fail if it's calibrated to the wrong kind of place.

Vael is not dangerous in the way that makes your pulse quicken. It is not a place of predators or hostile physics or societies that will punish you for transgression. It is something more subtle and, in its way, more demanding: it is a place that is in the process of *becoming*, and it will expect you — not consciously, not with intent, but in the way that wet clay expects the shape of whatever rests on it — to participate in that process simply by being present. You are not visiting a settled world. You are visiting a place where the relationship between human activity and the land beneath it is still being negotiated, in real time, by both parties, and neither party is entirely sure what the other is.

This means your preparation is less about survival gear and more about *disposition*. You need to arrive in the right state of mind, with the right tools, at the right pace. Get any of these wrong and you won't be harmed — you'll simply miss everything that matters, and you'll leave thinking you visited a muddy railyard in a damp climate and wondering what the fuss was about.

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing

The climate is cool, damp, and still. Not cold enough for insulated layers, not warm enough to be comfortable in a single shirt once you stop moving. Think early autumn in the Scottish Highlands or the Pacific Northwest coast — a world of 8-12°C mornings that might reach 15°C by afternoon if the diffuse sun commits to anything, which it probably won't.

**Base layer:** Merino wool, medium weight. Not synthetic. This is partly practical — merino regulates temperature in damp conditions better than anything else — and partly something less quantifiable. Vael is a place where organic materials seem more *appropriate* than synthetic ones. The buildings are wood. The fuel is coal. The machinery is iron and steam. You will feel less conspicuous, less frictionful, in natural fibers. This may sound like superstition. Spend a day there and tell me it's superstition.

**Mid layer:** A wool sweater or a heavy flannel shirt. Something you can open at the collar when the exertion of walking upslope warms you, and button back up when you stand still in the yard and the damp finds you again. The kind of garment that develops character — that pills a little, that holds the ghost of wood smoke in its fibers for days after. Vael will treat this garment well. You'll want to keep it afterward.

**Outer layer:** A waxed cotton jacket or an oilskin coat. Not a modern waterproof shell — those work fine technically, but they *rustle*, they announce you with every movement, and in a place where the ambient sounds are steam hisses and hammer blows and bird chatter and that low rail hum, you don't want to be the loudest thing in the room. Waxed cotton is quiet. It breathes enough for moderate activity. It sheds the kind of damp that Vael produces — not rain so much as a persistent ambient moisture that settles on everything, beading on surfaces, darkening fabric slowly from the outside in. And it smells right. It smells like a thing that belongs near creosote and coal smoke and turpentine-scented conifers.

**Trousers:** Heavy canvas or moleskin. Something that can handle kneeling on wet ballast stones, sitting on wooden platforms, brushing through knee-high willows and fireweed without complaint. Dark colored — not because Vael demands formality, but because everything in the yard will transfer itself to your clothing. Rail grease, coal dust, the dark wet of the ballast, the chlorophyll smear of aggressive vegetation. Dark canvas takes all of this and simply becomes more itself.

**Boots:** This is the most important clothing decision. The ground at Vael is packed earth, ballast stone, wet grass, and raw exposed slope where the railway cuts were made. It is uneven in the way that recently constructed infrastructure always is — not yet worn into smooth paths, full of small surprises where the ground hasn't settled. You need boots with ankle support, a sole that grips wet stone, and enough waterproofing to handle standing in damp grass for extended periods without soaking through. Leather, oil-treated, broken in *before* you arrive. Do not arrive in new boots. The walking at Vael is not arduous — the terrain is gentle, the distances short — but it is *constant*. You will be on your feet all day, shifting between surfaces, and wet feet at 10°C will end your visit in misery by mid-afternoon.

Bring gaiters if you plan to walk the upper slopes above the yard, where the brush is dense and the ground holds water in hidden pockets beneath the vegetation. The willows and dock will soak your trouser legs to the knee within fifty meters if you're unprotected.

**Hat:** A wool cap or a broad soft-brimmed hat. The light at Vael is flat and diffuse, so you're not managing direct sun — you're managing the kind of omnidirectional pale brightness that causes a low-grade headache after hours of exposure if you're sensitive to it. A brim helps. A cap helps differently, by keeping the damp off your scalp and retaining heat through the crown of your head, which is where you'll lose it fastest in the still cool air.

**Gloves:** Bring a pair, light leather or wool. You probably won't wear them all day, but the metalwork in the yard — rail clamps, couplers, the iron runners on the warehouse doors — is cold to the touch in the morning, colder than the air temperature would suggest, as if the metal retains the night's chill longer than it should. If you're going to be handling anything, touching things (and you will want to touch things; Vael invites tactile engagement), gloves protect your hands from both cold metal and the various industrial residues that coat every surface.

### What to Carry

Travel light. Vael is a small place — you can walk the entire yard and the town above it in twenty minutes. You don't need a heavy pack. A satchel or a light rucksack is sufficient.

**Water:** Bring your own for the first day. The water at Vael is almost certainly drinkable — the water tower serves both the locomotives and the settlement, and the source appears to be a spring or stream from the upper slopes, filtered through that dense vegetation — but your gut doesn't know this water yet, and a digestive upset in a place with limited facilities and no pharmacy is a miserable way to experience anything. After the first day, once you've observed how the locals drink and from where, you can transition. Bring a metal canteen, not plastic. Again: partly practical (metal canteen keeps water cooler in mild temperatures), partly about fitting in, partly about something less definable.

**Food:** Bring enough for your first day. Dense, simple food — bread, hard cheese, dried fruit, nuts. Things that don't require preparation and can be eaten with one hand while standing on a platform watching the yard. After you've oriented yourself, you can buy roasted chestnuts from the old woman by the water tower, and there may be other food sources in the settlement — a cookhouse or a communal kitchen associated with the railway workers. But don't count on this. Vael is not a place with restaurants. It's a work camp that is slowly becoming a town, and its hospitality infrastructure, like everything else, is still forming.

**A notebook and pencil.** Not for journaling, though you may want to journal. For *recording observations*. The strangeness of Vael is incremental and cumulative — you will not see the rock move, you will not see the willows lean, you will not hear the rail hum and immediately understand what it means. You will notice things over the course of hours and days that only become significant in aggregate. Write down the position of the fracture planes in the rock cut. Sketch the angle of the nearest willows. Note the time when the condensation lifts, when the rail hum starts and stops, where the jackdaws perch and how the dog reacts. You are building a dataset of one, and your memory is not good enough to hold the resolution you need. Use pencil, not pen — the damp will cause ink to bleed and feather on the page. A soft graphite pencil (2B or 3B) writes reliably on damp paper and produces marks you can read later.

**A watch or reliable timepiece.** Vael operates on its own rhythm, and that rhythm is slow enough that you will lose track of conventional time almost immediately. The diffuse light provides no sharp temporal cues — there's no moment when the sun "moves" in any dramatic way, no golden hour, no stark noon. Morning feels like morning for hours, and then at some point it's afternoon, and you're not sure when it changed. A watch keeps you anchored. You need this anchor not because you have appointments to keep but because the phenomena at Vael are *time-correlated* — the rail hum happens in the morning, the condensation cycle has a pattern — and without a timepiece you can't build the observational record that makes those patterns visible.

**A small folding knife.** Utility tool. Cutting food, trimming a twig to use as a marker, scraping a sample of the pale granite to look at the mica flecks up close. Don't make a display of it; it's a tool, not a weapon, and the people of Vael will understand it as such because everyone there carries one.

**Something to sit on.** A folded square of waxed canvas or a light foam pad. You are going to spend long stretches sitting — on platforms, on ballast, on the slopes above the yard — and the ground is damp and cold. Your body can handle this for thirty minutes before it becomes genuinely uncomfortable and starts sending you signals to move, and those signals will pull you out of the observational state that Vael rewards. A sit pad is a small thing that buys you hours.

## The Approach and Arrival

### Getting There

The specifics of multiverse transit are your own business — you know your method and its requirements. What matters is your arrival point and your first hour.

If you have any control over where you materialize, aim for the eastern approach — the direction from which the railway enters the depression. This lets you walk *in* along the tracks, which is how almost everything arrives at Vael, and it gives you the experience of seeing the fresh rock cuts up close as you approach. These cuts are important. They are the most visible site of the negotiation between human infrastructure and the responsive landscape, and seeing them before you see the yard gives you a baseline. Note the fracture planes. Touch the rock face if you can do so safely. Feel the mica under your fingertips. Register whether the surface feels like stone normally feels — cool, inert, merely geological — or whether there is something else, some quality of surface tension or temperature or texture that doesn't match your expectations. Don't force an interpretation. Just notice.

The walk in along the tracks is perhaps a kilometer from the point where the cuts begin to the yard itself. The drainage ditches on either side will be sharp-edged and probably holding a little standing water. The ballast between the rails will be stable underfoot but uneven — watch your ankles, walk on the ties where possible, step *on* the wood rather than between the ties where ballast has a way of shifting. The creosote smell will build as you walk, intensifying as the yard nears, mixing with the coal smoke and the turpentine of the conifers. Breathe through your nose. Vael is a place you understand partly through smell, and this approach gives you the olfactory introduction in the right order: stone, then creosote, then coal, then the green damp of the vegetation, all of them layering.

### Your First Hour

Resist the urge to explore immediately. When you enter the yard, find a place to sit — the platform outside the dispatching office is ideal if the brindled dog will tolerate your company (it will; approach without direct eye contact, sit down at a respectful distance, let it make the first investigative move) — and simply *be there* for thirty to sixty minutes.

This is not meditation. This is not some mystical requirement. This is practical. The switching yard is an environment with a complex soundscape, a specific rhythm of activity, and a set of ambient phenomena that you need to calibrate to before you can meaningfully observe anything. If you start walking around and looking at things immediately, you'll see surfaces. Sit still and you'll begin to hear the layers — the engine idle underneath the hammer blows, the bird chatter underneath both, and under all of it, if you're lucky and the timing is right, that low sustained hum from the rails that is the most unexplained thing in Vael.

The workers will notice you. In a settlement of perhaps forty structures and a few dozen people, a stranger is an event. But Vael is a railway town, and railway towns understand transit. People come through. The switchman may nod at you. The woman in the heavy gloves may look up from her rail clamp and look back down. The child on the slope will absolutely watch you for as long as you're interesting, and children have a shorter threshold for that than adults. Nobody will interrogate you. If you sit on the platform with an air of patience rather than nervousness, you'll be read as someone waiting for a connection, which is a category Vael understands.

When you're ready — when you've internalized the rhythm of the yard enough that the engine hisses no longer startle you and you've begun to subtract the generator drone automatically — then you can start to move.

## Navigating the Social Landscape

### The Workers

There are maybe a dozen people working the yard on any given day, plus whoever is in the residences, plus the chestnut seller, plus whatever population exists that you don't see because they're inside the machine shop or the dispatching office or up on the ridges doing work you can't observe from below. Total population: maybe forty to sixty people. This is a place where everyone knows everyone, where your anonymity is precisely zero, and where your social currency is determined almost entirely by whether you seem like someone who understands work.

Vael is a working place. Everyone there is doing something — tending a fire, assembling a train, maintaining equipment, cooking, mending, hauling, hammering. There is no leisure class. Even the chestnut seller, who appears to be doing nothing more strenuous than sitting on a crate, is performing an economic function and a social one (she is the town's memory, its oldest resident, its continuity with the pre-railway landscape, and her presence near the water tower is not accidental — she has positioned herself at the intersection of every path through the yard).

You, as a visitor, are not working. This is a deficit. You can partially offset it by being visibly *useful* in small ways — holding a door, stepping clear of a pathway when someone is carrying something, not standing on the active tracks. You can also offset it by being visibly *observant* in a way that reads as professional interest rather than tourism. The notebook helps here. Someone who is watching the yard and writing things down is engaged in a recognizable activity — surveying, assessing, recording. Someone who is watching the yard with empty hands and a vague expression is loitering. Vael is too young and too busy for loiterers.

Don't approach the workers with questions in your first few hours. Let them approach you, or let interactions arise naturally — buying chestnuts, sheltering under the same corrugated roof during a rain shower, standing at the same vantage point watching the same shunting operation. When conversation does begin, let it be about what's in front of you. *That's a heavy load of timber. Where's it headed?* This is the kind of question that a railway person asks, and it opens a conversation that the other person controls and feels comfortable in. Do not start with the rock, the hum, the vegetation, the strangeness. These are not conversational openers at Vael. They are things people know about but discuss obliquely, laterally, if at all.

### The Chestnut Seller

She is the most important person you will talk to in Vael, and you should not seek her out. She will be available when you are ready and not before, and the definition of "ready" is hers, not yours.

Here's what I mean: you can walk up to her cart on your first morning, buy a cone of chestnuts (bring small currency; whatever the local medium of exchange is, have modest denominations, and if you're unsure, bring something universally tradeable — a small quantity of good tobacco, a bar of plain soap, a decent folding knife you're willing to part with as a gift rather than a transaction). She'll sell to you. She'll be polite. But the conversation will be about chestnuts and the weather and nothing more, because she is *assessing* you, and she does this slowly, over multiple interactions, the way the hill assesses the wound of the railway cut.

Come back the next day. And the next. Buy chestnuts each time. Stand nearby and eat them. Don't ask her questions about the strangeness. Don't ask about the hill or the hum. Talk about what's in front of you, or say nothing at all. At some point — it might be the third visit or the seventh — she will say something that is not about chestnuts or weather. She will make an observation about the yard, or the light, or the foxglove, and embedded in that observation will be an opening. Take it gently. Respond in kind — with an observation, not a question. *I noticed the willows lean toward the yard* is an observation. *Why do the willows lean toward the yard?* is a question, and it will close the door you just felt open.

She knows things. She was here before the railway, before the blasting, before the fresh cuts and the raw earth and the smell of creosote. She knew the depression when it was just a depression — a geological palm holding nothing but alder and birch and that dark, turpentine-scented conifer. She has watched the land respond to what was done to it. Her observations — *the hill is learning the shape of the wound* — are not poetry. They are field notes from a long and patient study. But she will share them only with someone who has demonstrated the capacity to receive them at the right speed, which is her speed, which is the speed of Vael.

### The Dog

The brindled dog is not a pet and not a stray. It is a feature of the settlement, like the water tower or the telegraph line. It belongs to the yard in the way that the jackdaws belong to the freight cars — by long association and mutual tolerance.

The dog is also, I believe, an indicator. It reacts to the rail hum. It lifts its head, listens, and puts its head back down. This suggests either that the hum is audible to it (which is informative about the hum's frequency range) or that it perceives the hum through the platform boards as vibration (which is informative about its mechanical nature). Either way, the dog's behavior is a data point, and you should record it — when the dog lifts its head, what time it is, how long it listens, whether its orientation (which direction its head points) correlates with anything.

Do not try to befriend the dog aggressively. Offer no food from your hand. Sit near it, allow it to sniff you at its discretion, and accept whatever level of familiarity it offers, which may range from complete indifference to leaning against your leg while it sleeps. The dog has excellent social instincts — it lives among railway workers, the chestnut seller, transient travelers, and whatever else moves through Vael — and it will calibrate its relationship with you faster than any human will.

## Observing the Phenomena

### The Rock

Go to the eastern cuts in the morning, when the light is flat and the rock faces have that faint sheen. Bring your notebook. Choose a specific section of exposed granite — maybe two meters wide, at eye level — and sketch the fracture planes. Mark the major lines, the angles, the places where mica concentrations are visible. Note the date and time.

Come back the next morning and do it again, on a fresh page.

You probably won't see a difference. The movement is described as weekly-scale, not daily. But the act of close, repeated observation changes your relationship to the rock face. You stop seeing "granite" and start seeing *this* granite — its specific character, its grain, its personality. And that perceptual shift is necessary for what Vael has to teach you, which is that the boundary between inert matter and responsive matter may be less clear than your home universe has led you to believe.

Touch the rock each time you visit. Use the flat of your palm, not your fingertips. Press gently. Feel the temperature, the texture, the degree of moisture. Compare left hand to right hand — sometimes sensitivity differs. Note what you feel. Do not editorialize in your notes. *Surface cool, slightly damp, smooth except at the upper fracture line where a ridge has developed that I don't remember from yesterday* is a good note. *The rock seems aware of me* is not a good note, even if it's what you feel, because it contaminates your future observations with an interpretation you can't yet support.

If you're there long enough — a week or more — you may notice the fracture planes shifting. The engineers have noticed. The retaining walls with their expansion gaps are the engineering response to a phenomenon that is real and measurable, even if its cause is debated. Your sketches, if you've been disciplined, will show the same thing. When they do, sit with it. Don't rush to explanation. Vael doesn't rush, and neither should you.

### The Rail Hum

You may not hear it at all during your visit. It happens every few days, in the morning, when condensation is heaviest on the rail surfaces. You cannot summon it or predict it precisely.

If you're lucky enough to be in the yard when it starts, stop whatever you're doing. Stand still. Listen. Note the time. If you can, place your hand on the nearest rail (carefully — check that no locomotive is moving, check with the switchman if necessary). Feel the vibration. Try to estimate the pitch — is it a single tone or does it have harmonics? Does it change over time? Does it seem to come from a particular direction along the rails, or is it omnidirectional, as if the rail itself is the source?

Watch the dog. Note when it lifts its head and when it puts it back down. Note what the jackdaws do (reportedly nothing, but verify this). Note what the workers do — do they pause? Do they look at each other? Do they continue working without acknowledgment? The social response to the hum is as interesting as the hum itself, because it tells you how normalized the phenomenon has become and how the community processes the unexplained.

Note when the hum stops and how — abruptly or with a gradual fadeout. Note the condensation level on the rails at the time it stops. Note the air temperature if you have a way to measure it.

After the hum has stopped, go to the dispatching office if you've established enough social rapport and ask, casually, whether anything is expected on the line. *Thought I felt something in the rails; any traffic coming?* This mimics the original response to the hum and opens a conversation that the dispatcher may or may not take in an interesting direction.

### The Vegetation

This is the slowest of the three phenomena and the hardest to observe directly, but it is also the most pervasive and possibly the most significant.

Walk the perimeter of the yard, where the maintained ground meets the wild slopes. Notice the edge. It is not a clean line — it's a gradient, a negotiation zone where the packed earth of the lanes gives way to pioneering fireweed and dock, then to denser ryegrass, then to willow and alder, then to the full canopy of the slopes. This gradient is the leading edge of the vegetation's inward movement, and it's worth studying closely.

Pick a few specific plants — a willow sapling, a stand of foxglove, an alder at the edge of the brush — and note their position relative to fixed landmarks (the corner of the warehouse, the base of the water tower, a particular rail). Sketch them. Measure their lean if you can, using your folding knife laid flat against the trunk as a rough plumb line. Note the direction of the lean.

Then look at the root systems where they're visible — in the railway cuts, in drainage ditches, anywhere the earth has been recently disturbed. Are the roots symmetrical, spreading equally in all directions? Or are they asymmetrical, reaching preferentially toward the yard? The description suggests the latter, and if it's true, it's remarkable. Root asymmetry is common in response to water, nutrients, or light gradients, but root asymmetry toward a *railway yard* implies the roots are responding to something the yard produces — heat, vibration, chemical runoff, or something else.

If you're botanically inclined and can do so without causing damage or alarm, excavate a small section of root from a yard-facing plant and a comparable section from the opposite side. Compare the density, the branching pattern, the thickness of the root hairs. This is data. It may or may not tell you anything, but the act of looking, of taking the vegetation's behavior seriously as a phenomenon rather than a quirk, puts you in the right relationship with Vael.

## The Deeper Preparation

Everything above is practical and material. Now I need to talk about the preparation that's harder to pack.

### Adjust Your Tempo

Vael runs slow. The train being assembled today departs tomorrow. The timber was felled in another valley and arrived through a chain of mules and tramways and seasonal flumes. The coal comes weekly, or when it comes. The hill's response to the railway cuts unfolds over weeks and months.

You need to match this tempo, and if you're coming from a faster universe — and most of them are faster — you need to start decelerating before you arrive. In the days before your transit, practice doing things at half speed. Walk slower. Eat slower. Sit for periods of time with nothing to do and resist the urge to fill the emptiness. Train your body to be comfortable with stillness, because at Vael, stillness is not emptiness — it's the state in which the place becomes legible.

If you arrive at Vael's tempo, you will experience the yard as rich, full, endlessly detailed. If you arrive at your normal speed, you will experience it as boring, and you will miss everything.

### Suspend the Explanatory Reflex

You will want to explain what you observe. The rock moves because of thermal expansion. The rail hum is resonance from temperature differentials. The vegetation leans toward the yard because of the heat island effect or mineral runoff from the ballast. These explanations may be correct. They may be partially correct. They may be completely wrong.

The problem is not that they're wrong — the problem is that reaching for them too quickly closes down the observational channel. Once you've decided that the rail hum is resonance, you stop *listening* to the rail hum. You stop noting its characteristics, its timing, its relationship to other phenomena. You've filed it away, and your attention moves on to something else, and Vael becomes smaller.

The engineers at Vael have explanations. The chestnut seller has a different framework. Neither of them has stopped observing, because both of them live there and the phenomena are ongoing, but you don't live there — you have limited time — and every moment you spend constructing theories is a moment you're not collecting the raw observations that the theories should eventually be built from.

Write down what you see, hear, feel, and smell. Write down what the rock does, what the rails do, what the vegetation does. Write down what the people say about these things, in their own words, without paraphrasing or interpreting. Fill your notebook with data, not explanations. You can theorize later, at home, with your notes in front of you and the smell of creosote fading from your jacket. At Vael, just *observe*.

### Prepare for the Feeling

There is a particular emotional quality to being in a place that is becoming itself, and you should know about it before you arrive so it doesn't destabilize you when it hits.

It feels like — the closest analogy I can find — being in the room when a relationship is forming between two people who don't share a language. There's an intensity of attention on both sides. There's a willingness to be changed by the encounter. There's a mutual vulnerability — the land didn't ask for the railway, and the railway didn't fully understand what it was cutting into, and now they're both here, both committed, both slowly adjusting to the other's presence. The willows leaning inward. The retaining walls with their expansion gaps. The roots reaching toward the yard. The rail hum. Neither hostility nor welcome, but something more like *curiosity*, conducted at the speed of geology on one side and the speed of infrastructure on the other, with the humans and the dog and the jackdaws and the foxglove caught in between, living in the gap, making their homes in the space where two vast slow processes are reaching toward each other.

You will feel this. It may manifest as a kind of tenderness — a sudden, unexpected emotional response to the sight of the locomotive engineer eating chestnuts while looking at the rock face, or the chestnut seller sitting on her crate in a posture of infinite patience, or the brindled dog lifting its head to listen for something that has no official explanation. It may manifest as a low-grade unease, a sense that you're witnessing something intimate that you haven't been fully invited into. It may manifest as a deep, quiet joy, the kind that comes from being in a place where nothing is pretending to be finished.

Whatever form it takes, let it. Don't perform it, don't suppress it, don't analyze it in the moment. Feel it and keep observing. The feeling and the observation are not in conflict — at Vael, they're the same thing.

## Practical Logistics

### Duration of Stay

Three days minimum. A week is better. Two weeks is what Vael actually wants from you, but most travelers can't justify that.

Three days gives you: one day to calibrate, one day to observe systematically, one day to follow up on what you noticed. You'll probably catch one instance of the rail hum in three days. You won't see the rock move. You might notice the vegetation lean if you're very attentive with your measurements.

A week gives you: two or three instances of the rail hum, possibly enough repeat observations of the rock face to detect change, enough social rapport with the workers and the chestnut seller to begin having real conversations, and — this is the part that's hard to quantify — enough time for Vael to become *normal* to you, which is when the strangeness stops being exotic and starts being specific, and specificity is where understanding lives.

### Shelter

There may be accommodation available — one of the provisional structures, or a spare room in a worker's residence. Ask at the dispatching office; the dispatcher functions as the de facto administrator of the settlement and will know what's available. Be prepared to pay modestly, in trade goods if currency doesn't translate. Useful trade items: tobacco, soap, matches, sewing needles, small tools, any preserved food that travels well (dried fruit, chocolate, coffee if they have it, tea if they don't).

If no accommodation is available, you can camp. The slopes above the yard are sheltered by the vegetation, the ground drains reasonably well despite the moisture, and the temperatures, while cool, are well within the range of a decent sleeping bag and a waterproof bivvy. Set up above the yard on the northern slope if possible — this gives you a vantage point over the whole depression and lets you observe the morning condensation cycle and the movements in the yard without being in the way. Do not camp in the yard itself; it's a working space and people will trip over you and resent you for it.

### Health and Safety

The physical risks at Vael are the mundane risks of any industrial site: moving vehicles on the rails, coupling operations that can crush hands and feet, hot surfaces on the locomotive, sharp edges on recently cut metal and stone. Stay alert in the yard, stay clear of active operations, and watch where you step. The workers will shout warnings if you're in danger, but they expect a baseline level of situational awareness and will lose patience with someone who repeatedly wanders into harm's way.

The water and air quality are likely excellent — this is a place of minimal industrial pollution in a wet climate with good vegetation cover. The coal smoke is the most significant air quality concern, and it's manageable unless you're severely asthmatic, in which case Vael will be uncomfortable for you and you should consider whether the visit is worth the respiratory cost.

No dangerous wildlife has been mentioned, and the cool damp climate is not one that typically produces venomous anything. The brushy slopes may harbor ticks; check yourself evening and morning if you've been walking through vegetation.

The *unusual* risks at Vael — if you want to call them risks — are the phenomena themselves. The moving rock is not going to collapse on you; it moves on a scale of millimeters per week, and the cuts are engineered with generous margins. The rail hum is not harmful; it's a vibration, possibly felt through the feet if you're standing on the rails, but not at any intensity that would cause physical distress. The vegetation is not aggressive toward humans — it's aggressive toward *unoccupied ground*, which is a different thing. You are not unoccupied ground. The willows will not reach for you in the night.

But. Pay attention to your sleep quality. Some travelers to responsive landscapes report vivid dreams, disrupted sleep patterns, or a sensation of being *observed* that is not threatening but is persistent and can be unsettling. If this happens, it doesn't mean something is wrong. It means you're sensitive to whatever it is that makes Vael what it is — the quality of attention that the landscape brings to the things within it. Acknowledge it, note it in your journal, and don't catastrophize. You're fine. The hill is just — learning your shape, the way it's learning the shape of the wound, the way it's learning the shape of the railway. It's what it does.

## What to Bring Home

Not the chestnuts. They won't keep.

Bring your notebook. Bring the memory of the sounds — I recommend spending one session with your eyes closed, simply listening and naming in your mind each layer of the soundscape, so that you build an auditory memory detailed enough to reconstruct later. Bring the smell of creosote and coal in your jacket, and don't wash the jacket for a while.

If the chestnut seller gives you something — and she may, if you've been patient enough, if you've earned it — bring that. Don't ask what it is or what it means. Put it in your pocket, thank her, and figure it out later. Or don't figure it out. Vael doesn't require you to understand everything. It requires you to *observe* everything, and to carry your observations with you, and to let them settle at whatever pace they settle, which may be slow, which may be very slow, which may be exactly the pace of a hill learning the shape of a wound, or a set of willows leaning toward something warm, or a dog lifting its head to listen for a sound that hasn't been explained yet and may never be.

Go well. Take your time. Watch the foxglove try to peer into the machine shop. That's Vael in a single image — something alive, something curious, something patient, pressing its face against the boundary between the wild and the built, not trying to break through, just trying to *see*.
