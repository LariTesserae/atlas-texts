# Preparing for the Drowned Plateau

---

## Understanding What You Are Entering

Before anything else, sit with the description and understand the fundamental nature of this place, because your preparation will fail if you frame it incorrectly. This is not an underwater dive. You are not descending into an ocean. There is no surface to ascend to, no boat waiting overhead, no decompression stop, no sun-dappled shallows where you transition back to the breathing world. The water here is not a medium you are entering — it is the atmosphere of this location. You must treat it the way you would treat air on a cold, high-altitude plateau in your home world, except that everything about your biology assumes you are an air-breathing terrestrial creature, and nothing about this environment accommodates that assumption.

Your preparation must therefore operate on two levels simultaneously: the practical (how to survive, move, see, and function in a body of water with no surface) and the psychological (how to remain sane and purposeful in a place defined by deep strangeness, deep time, and deep quiet that is not actually quiet at all).

## Respiratory and Life Support

### Primary Breathing Systems

This is the single most critical preparation category. You cannot hold your breath. You cannot surface. There is no surface.

You need a fully closed-circuit rebreather system — not a standard scuba rig, which carries a finite gas supply measured in hours and vents exhaled gas as bubbles. Bubbles are waste, and in an environment with no surface, every molecule of breathable gas you release is gone forever. A closed-circuit system scrubs your exhaled carbon dioxide and recycles your oxygen, extending your gas supply dramatically. But "dramatically" still means finitely. You need to plan your visit duration with ruthless precision.

**Calculate your gas supply conservatively.** A high-quality closed-circuit rebreather with fully charged scrubber canisters and oxygen cylinders might give you 6 to 8 hours of bottom time under normal diving conditions. But "normal diving conditions" assume you are exerting yourself, managing buoyancy actively, dealing with thermal stress — all of which increase your metabolic rate and oxygen consumption. The Drowned Plateau's conditions suggest relatively low physical exertion (slow movement, no fighting currents beyond the gentle thermal shifts), but the cold will increase your metabolism whether you feel it or not. Plan for 4 to 6 hours of usable time, and build in a safety margin that accounts for the possibility that you may become disoriented and need extra time to locate your exit point.

**Bring redundant systems.** A backup bailout gas supply — a small independent cylinder with a separate regulator — is non-negotiable. If your primary rebreather fails (scrubber breakthrough, oxygen sensor malfunction, flooding), you need enough independent gas to reach your exit point. This means your exit point must be established before you begin exploring, and you must never range farther from it than your bailout supply can cover at a calm, measured swimming pace.

**Consider the water chemistry.** The description mentions chemical gradients that the kelp-analogs exploit for energy. This implies the water is not pure H₂O — it is mineral-rich, possibly carrying dissolved gases, sulfur compounds, metallic salts, or other chemicals that could interact with your breathing equipment. Before your first visit, if at all possible, obtain a water sample and analyze it. Certain dissolved gases (hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide at high concentrations, methane) can poison a rebreather's scrubber chemistry or give false readings on oxygen sensors. You may need specialized scrubber media or sensor calibrations. If you cannot analyze the water in advance, treat it as a hostile breathing environment and monitor your rebreather's function obsessively — check your handsets every few minutes, watch for scrubber temperature anomalies, and be alert to the early symptoms of hypoxia (euphoria, tunnel vision, poor judgment) or hypercapnia (headache, confusion, air hunger).

### Water Ingestion and Exposure

Your breathing apparatus keeps water out of your lungs, but the rest of you is immersed. The mineral-saturated water will be in contact with your skin, your eyes (if you use a standard dive mask with an exposed face seal), and potentially your mucous membranes. The calcium carbonate saturation described — enough to form rinds on stone — suggests water that could be mildly irritating or drying to human tissue over extended exposure. A full-face mask or a dry suit with integrated helmet is preferable to a standard wetsuit-and-mask configuration. You want as little direct skin contact with this water as possible until you know what it does to human tissue over hours of exposure.

## Thermal Protection

### The Nature of the Cold

The description is precise: "just above freezing but never quite arriving there." Call it 1-3°C (34-37°F). This is survivable with proper protection but lethal without it. The cold is described as "still" and "mineral" — there is no warm current to seek out, no thermocline to exploit. The temperature is uniform and omnidirectional.

### Suit Selection

A **dry suit** is mandatory. Not a wetsuit, no matter how thick. A wetsuit works by trapping a layer of water against your skin and letting your body heat warm it; in water this cold, your body cannot warm that layer faster than the environment cools it, and you will become hypothermic. A dry suit keeps a layer of air (or, better, argon gas) between you and the water, providing genuine insulation.

Choose a **trilam or crushed neoprene dry suit** with:
- Integrated boots (no exposed feet)
- Dry gloves (your hands will be the first thing the cold takes from you, and you need your hands for navigation, tool use, and equipment management)
- A hood or integrated helmet that covers your head and neck completely
- An argon inflation system separate from your breathing gas (argon is a better insulator than air; a small argon bottle plumbed to your suit's inflator valve is standard in cold-water technical diving)

**Undergarments** matter enormously. Wear a high-loft synthetic undersuit — Thinsulate or similar — rated for the coldest conditions available. Layer if necessary. You will feel bulky. Accept it. The alternative is progressive hypothermia, which begins with shivering, proceeds to impaired judgment (exactly when you need your judgment most), and ends with unconsciousness and death.

**Active heating:** If available in your multiverse's technology base, battery-powered heated undergarments are worth their weight in whatever you use for currency. Heated vests, heated glove liners, heated socks. The batteries add weight but the thermal margin they provide is transformative. In 2°C water, even the best passive insulation will eventually lose the fight against thermodynamics. Active heating buys you time — hours of additional comfort and function.

### Monitoring Core Temperature

Carry a core temperature monitoring device if your technology supports it. A telemetric pill you swallow before the visit, transmitting your core temperature to a wrist display, will tell you when you are cooling faster than you realize. By the time you feel seriously cold in a dry suit, you may already be mildly hypothermic. Set a hard abort threshold — if your core temperature drops below 36°C (96.8°F), you leave. No exceptions. No "just a few more minutes to look at that structure."

## Pressure and Buoyancy

### The Anomalous Pressure

The description contains a critical oddity: despite being within a body of water with no known surface, the pressure is "lighter than that" — lighter than what deep submersion would suggest. This implies the physics of pressure here do not follow the simple rule of one additional atmosphere per 10 meters of water overhead. The water may be less dense than terrestrial seawater. The gravitational field may be different. Or the relationship between depth and pressure may simply not be linear in this multiverse.

**What this means for you:** You cannot calculate your decompression obligation using standard dive tables. You don't know your equivalent depth. You don't know whether the nitrogen (or whatever inert gas is in your breathing mix) is being driven into your tissues at the rate your tables predict, or faster, or slower. This is extremely dangerous. Decompression sickness — the formation of gas bubbles in your blood and tissues when ambient pressure decreases — can be debilitating or fatal, and it is triggered by *changes* in pressure, not by pressure itself.

**Practical approach:** Breathe a mix with as little inert gas as possible. If your rebreather supports it, use a **high-oxygen or oxygen-enriched mix** to reduce nitrogen loading. Be cautious about breathing pure oxygen at unknown pressures — oxygen becomes toxic above approximately 1.6 atmospheres partial pressure, causing seizures that are fatal underwater. You need to measure the ambient pressure at the plateau before selecting your breathing mix. Bring a pressure gauge — even a simple mechanical one — and take a reading as soon as you arrive. If the ambient pressure is close to one atmosphere (which the "lighter" description tentatively suggests), you may be able to breathe a relatively normal mix with little decompression risk. If it is significantly higher, you need a helium-based mix (trimix or heliox) to avoid nitrogen narcosis and oxygen toxicity.

### Buoyancy Management

You are fully submerged with no surface reference. Buoyancy control is how you avoid sinking off the edge of the plateau into the "unseen depths below" and how you avoid floating upward into the "unseen darkness above." Both directions are potentially fatal — the depths may have crushing pressure or chemical conditions hostile to life, and the upper darkness is unknown.

Use your dry suit inflation and a **buoyancy compensator device (BCD)** with redundant inflation. Practice achieving neutral buoyancy before you begin exploring — hover motionless at the plateau's surface level, adjust until you neither rise nor sink, and then adjust again for every change in your equipment load (gas consumption makes your cylinders lighter over time, which makes you more buoyant, which means you will slowly drift upward unless you vent gas from your BCD periodically).

**A depth alarm is essential.** Set it to alert you if you ascend or descend more than 5 meters from your established plateau level. In the darkness between groves, without visual references, you will not feel yourself drifting up or down. The water's gentle thermal currents could push you vertically without your awareness. The alarm catches what your senses miss.

## Navigation

### The Problem

There is no sun, no magnetic field you can count on (you don't know the geophysics of this world), no GPS, no surface landmarks, no stars. The groves are your only visual landmarks, and they are separated by "hundreds of meters of bare stone" in near-total darkness. Getting lost between groves means floating in black water above featureless stone with no directional cues, burning through your gas supply while your core temperature drops.

### Solutions

**Guideline reel.** This is the most reliable navigation tool in zero-visibility environments and has been standard in cave diving for decades. You anchor a thin, high-strength line at your entry point and unreel it as you explore, tying it off at intervals to fixed points — the stone structures between groves are ideal for this. The line is your guaranteed path home. You follow it back when you want to leave, when your gas runs low, when your temperature drops, or when something about this place starts to feel wrong in a way you cannot articulate but should not ignore.

Carry **at least two reels.** One primary, one backup. Carry line arrows — small directional markers you clip to the line — that point toward the exit. In disorientation, you may not remember which direction along the line leads home. The arrows tell you.

**Compass.** Bring one, but do not trust it exclusively. You do not know if this world has a magnetic field, or if the mineral-rich stone of the plateau interferes with compass function. Calibrate it when you arrive — if it behaves consistently, use it as a secondary navigation aid. If the needle wanders or spins, discard it and rely on your guideline.

**Marked waypoints.** At each grove you visit, leave a small, recoverable marker — something that will not be swept away by the gentle currents. A weighted tag, a small LED beacon (if you have waterproof ones), a scratched symbol on a stone. Build your own map as you explore. This serves a secondary purpose: it tells you if you are revisiting places you have already been, which is a sign of disorientation.

**The structures as navigation aids.** The description notes that some cairns are positioned at exact midpoints between groves, "as if marking routes through the dark stretches." Take this seriously. Whatever built them may have faced the same navigation problem you face. Map the cairns. Note their positions relative to the groves. See if a pattern emerges. You may be following a trail that was laid down for exactly this purpose, by something that needed to move through darkness between islands of cold light.

## Lighting

### Your Eyes and the Plateau's Light

The kelp-analog's luminescence is described as dim, localized, and in a color between green, violet, and gray. Your human eyes will partially dark-adapt to this environment, but dark adaptation takes 20-30 minutes and is destroyed instantly by bright white light. You face a choice.

**Option A: Rely primarily on the plateau's own light.** Allow your eyes to dark-adapt fully. Move slowly. Accept that between groves, you will be effectively blind and must navigate by guideline and touch. Within groves, the vegetal light will be enough to see the major features — the fronds, the structures, the stone. Detail work (examining tool marks on stones, studying the mosses, inspecting the central bowl) will be difficult but possible.

This option has a significant advantage: it preserves the character of the place. The Drowned Plateau is a landscape defined by its own dim light. Flooding it with artificial illumination will not make it more comprehensible — it will make it *different*, and the difference will cost you information. The way the light falls, the way shadows pool in the troughs, the way the innermost ring of the great grove's concentric pattern is brighter than the outermost — these are things you can only observe if you are seeing by the plateau's own terms.

**Option B: Carry artificial lights but use them sparingly and with discipline.** A primary light (a focused, dimmable, underwater torch) and two backups (smaller, broader-beam). Keep them off in the groves. Use them only in the dark stretches between groves, and keep them on low power, aimed downward at the stone, to preserve as much of your dark adaptation as possible.

**Option C (recommended): Both.** Carry all three lights. Begin in each grove with them off. Observe by vegetal light. When you need to examine details — tool marks, the bowl's sediment, the structure of the moss-crusts — use a red-filtered light at low power. Red light is less destructive to dark adaptation and will let you see texture and detail without obliterating your sensitivity to the plateau's native illumination. In the dark stretches, use your primary on its lowest setting, aimed at the guideline and the stone immediately ahead of you.

**Document the light.** If you have an underwater camera capable of long-exposure photography, bring it. The color of the kelp-light — "something older, something that sits between green and violet and gray" — deserves to be recorded. A spectrometer, even a small handheld one, would tell you the actual wavelength. This matters: if the light is genuinely outside the normal visible spectrum (perhaps extending into near-ultraviolet), your camera and your eyes may be registering it differently, and the spectrometer will give you the truth.

## Equipment for Documentation and Study

### What to Bring

You are visiting a place with no animal life, vegetation that photosynthesizes without sunlight, structures built by an unknown presence, and a central formation that rhythmically exhales luminous sediment. You should be prepared to document everything you can within your time constraints.

**Underwater writing slate and pencil.** Low-tech, reliable, and essential. Sketch the layout of groves, the positions of structures, the pattern of the concentric rings. Write down observations immediately — in cold water, your memory will be less reliable than you think.

**Cameras.** A wide-angle underwater camera for landscape documentation. A macro lens attachment for close-up work on the structures' tool marks, the moss-crusts, the kelp-analogs' base structures. Video capability for recording the bowl's sediment exhalation during a thermal shift — this is a periodic event and you may only witness it once or twice during your visit.

**Sample containers.** Small, sealable vials for:
- Water samples (from within a grove, from between groves, and from inside the central bowl — three different chemical environments potentially)
- Sediment samples (from the bowl specifically — that "almost powder, calcium-white" material that glows when disturbed)
- Vegetation samples (a small frond fragment, a piece of the moss-crust, a node from the branching base-plants — take minimally, with respect for the slow growth rates described)
- Stone samples (a small chip from a natural surface and a small chip from a worked surface of one of the structures — comparison of weathering and composition may tell you something about the age and origin of the building)

**Handle all samples with clean gloves and uncontaminated containers.** You do not know the biochemistry of this world's vegetation. Cross-contamination with terrestrial biology — your skin cells, bacteria from your equipment — could corrupt your analysis and, more speculatively, could introduce organisms into an environment that has never encountered them.

**Measurement tools:**
- A tape measure or marked line for measuring the structures, the fronds, the distances between groves
- A thermometer (water temperature in different locations — is it truly uniform, or are there microenvironments near the groves?)
- A pressure gauge (as discussed above)
- A current meter if available (measuring the speed and direction of the thermal currents, particularly the shift that triggers the bowl's exhalation)
- A sound recording device — a **hydrophone** is strongly recommended. The plateau's acoustic environment is described in extraordinary detail: harmonic thrums from the stone, crystal pings from the fronds, the constant low roar of water over the edge. These sounds contain information about the structure of the plateau, the behavior of the vegetation, and the hydrodynamics of the environment. Record them. Record them from multiple locations. Record them during and between thermal shifts. You may find patterns that are not apparent to your ear in real-time.

### The Structures: Special Attention

The built structures are the single most significant mystery of this place. Prepare to document them exhaustively.

- Photograph every structure from multiple angles with a scale reference in frame.
- Measure the dimensions of the stones used, the dimensions of the enclosures, the height and course count of the walls.
- Record the orientation of the structures — which direction do they face? Do the rectangular enclosures have consistent alignment? Does the lone northern wall face a particular direction relative to the groves, the plateau's edge, or any other feature?
- Look for any marks that might be symbolic rather than functional — anything that doesn't look like a tool mark or construction artifact. Grooves that form patterns. Stones placed with a specific orientation. Arrangements that repeat.
- **Do not dismantle anything.** Do not remove stones from structures. You are not the first strange thing to visit this plateau, and you should not assume you will be the last. Leave the record intact.

## Psychological Preparation

### The Strangeness Budget

Every traveler has a finite tolerance for sustained strangeness before their mind begins to either normalize the experience (losing sensitivity to important details) or reject it (panic, dissociation, overwhelming desire to flee). The Drowned Plateau will spend your strangeness budget quickly because the strangeness is layered and cumulative:

1. **You are in water with no surface.** This is the base layer. The knowledge that you cannot ascend to air — that there is no air, anywhere, in any direction — will sit in your hindbrain and produce a low-grade survival anxiety that does not go away. It can be managed but not eliminated.

2. **The absence of animal life.** Every underwater environment in human experience is animated — fish, invertebrates, plankton, *something*. The Drowned Plateau's emptiness will feel wrong at a preverbal level. Your pattern-recognition systems will keep searching for movement, for eyes, for the flick of a fin, and they will keep finding nothing. This is more fatiguing than it sounds.

3. **The sound.** The acoustic environment is intense and inescapable. In air, you can create relative silence by staying still. In water, the sound goes through you — through your bones, your chest cavity, your sinuses. The harmonic thrums from the stone will be felt as much as heard. Over hours, this can become either meditative or maddening, depending on your temperament and preparation.

4. **The structures.** These will unsettle you in proportion to how carefully you examine them. Tool marks on stone at the bottom of an animal-less, surface-less body of water, built by something unknown at an unknowable time, for an unknown purpose. The more you look, the more questions you will have, and none of them will be answered during your visit. This is the specific flavor of unease this place offers: not danger, not hostility, but *incompleteness*. The plateau is a sentence interrupted mid-word, and you will feel the pull of the missing meaning.

5. **The bowl's exhalation.** This will be beautiful. It will also feel like witnessing something you were not meant to witness, or something that operates on a scale and a timescale that renders your presence irrelevant. Both feelings are correct. Let them coexist.

### How to Prepare Psychologically

**Meditative practice.** If you have any experience with meditation, mindfulness, or deliberate breath control, use it. The ability to observe your own emotional state without being swept away by it is the single most valuable psychological tool for this environment. When the anxiety of no-surface rises, note it, breathe steadily, and return your attention to your task. When the emptiness begins to feel oppressive, note it, breathe, and look at the kelp-light. When the structures unsettle you, note it, breathe, and document what you see.

**Pre-visit exposure to sensory analogues.** Spend time in dark, enclosed water environments before you go — floatation tanks, dark-water cave dives if you have access, even prolonged sessions in a dark swimming pool. Acclimate your nervous system to the combination of darkness, water, and spatial disorientation. The Drowned Plateau is not a cave (it is open, vast, outdoor in its own way), but the sensory overlap is significant.

**Establish a narrative purpose before you arrive.** Know why you are going. "To see it" is sufficient but vague. "To document the structures and collect samples for analysis" gives you tasks, and tasks give you focus, and focus is the antidote to the kind of existential drift this place invites. The Drowned Plateau will try to make you contemplative. Contemplation is valuable, but it should be chosen, not succumbed to. Let yourself have contemplative moments — standing (floating) in the great grove, watching the bowl exhale its cold light — but return to your tasks afterward.

**Set a hard time limit and honor it.** Decide before you go how long you will stay. Write it on your slate. When the time arrives, leave, even if you feel fine, even if there is more to see, even if the current is about to shift and you want to watch the bowl one more time. The Drowned Plateau "does not want you to leave" — not because it is malicious, but because it is absorbing. Its timescales are geological. It will make hours feel like minutes and minutes feel like a lifetime, depending on where you are in the strangeness cycle. Your timer is your anchor to your own temporal reality.

## Entry and Exit

### The Multiverse Transit Point

You will need to establish clearly — before you go — where your transit point deposits you and how to return to it. If your method of multiverse travel creates a fixed portal, mark its location with the most visible, unambiguous marker you can devise. A bright chemical light stick anchored to the stone. A flashing LED beacon. A flag on a weighted line. Something you can see from a distance, in the dim water, when you are cold and tired and your dark-adapted eyes are uncertain about what is landscape and what is light.

If your transit method does not create a fixed portal — if you carry a device, or if the return is mental/volitional — test it immediately upon arrival. Make sure it works while you are submerged, in cold mineral water, under unfamiliar pressure, surrounded by unfamiliar electromagnetic and chemical conditions. Do not explore until you have confirmed you can leave.

**The edges of the plateau are dangerous.** The description mentions "eroded edges" where water flows over them with a sound like thick wind. These are the places where the plateau drops away into unseen depths. Do not approach the edges unless you are negatively buoyant (weighted to stay on the stone) and tethered to a fixed point. The current flowing over the edge could sweep you into the depths, and you do not know what is down there. The pressure may increase. The water chemistry may change. Your ability to return to the plateau level may be compromised. Treat the edges as cliff edges — which they are.

## Physical Fitness and Conditioning

### Cold Water Acclimatization

Begin cold exposure training weeks or months before your visit. Cold showers, cold water immersion, winter swimming if accessible. The goal is not to eliminate your cold response but to familiarize your body with it so that it does not trigger panic or impair your function when you arrive. You want your body to recognize cold water immersion as a known state, not an emergency.

### Cardiovascular Fitness

Moderate cardiovascular fitness is sufficient — you will not be sprinting. But your heart will be working harder than normal due to the cold (peripheral vasoconstriction increases cardiac workload) and the breathing resistance of a rebreather (slightly harder to inhale and exhale than free breathing in air). A resting heart rate in a healthy range and the ability to sustain moderate exertion for several hours without exhaustion is the baseline.

### Ear and Sinus Health

If the ambient pressure is different from surface atmospheric pressure — which it almost certainly is — you will need to equalize your ears and sinuses during transit. Any congestion, infection, or anatomical difficulty with equalization could make the visit impossible or dangerous (barotrauma to the eardrums, sinus squeeze). Arrive healthy. If you have a cold, postpone.

## Ethical Considerations

### The Structures

You are visiting a place where someone or something built things. You do not know who, what, when, or why. You do not know if they are still present in some form you cannot perceive. You do not know if the structures are functional, sacred, communicative, or none of these. **Treat them as you would treat an archaeological site in your own world: observe, document, do not alter.**

### The Vegetation

The kelp-analogs grow slowly. Their competitive dynamics play out over decades. A single frond damaged by your passage may take years to recover, or may not recover, shifting the competitive balance of its grove in ways you cannot predict. Move carefully within groves. Do not grab fronds for stability. Do not break them. If you take a sample, take the smallest possible fragment from a frond that is already visibly dying or dead.

### The Bowl

The central bowl and its rhythmic exhalation of luminous sediment may be a natural phenomenon. It may also be the only remaining trace of whatever occupied that space "for a very long time." Do not disturb the sediment beyond what the current does naturally. Take a small sample from the periphery, not the center. If the exhalation occurs while you are present, observe from outside the cleared space — do not stand in the bowl.

## What to Expect Emotionally

You will likely pass through several distinct emotional phases during your visit:

**Arrival (first 15-30 minutes):** Sensory overwhelm. The cold, the sound, the darkness, the strangeness of seeing by vegetal light. Anxiety about your breathing system, your buoyancy, your exit point. This is the most dangerous phase psychologically because it is when you are most likely to make rushed decisions. Go slowly. Check your equipment. Establish your bearings. Breathe.

**Exploration (hours 1-3):** Growing fascination. The groves are beautiful. The structures are compelling. The acoustic environment, once your ear adjusts, has a strange music to it. You will begin to feel the timescale of this place — the deep slowness, the epochal patience of plants competing for chemical nutrients in lightless water. This is the phase where you do your best work, where your observations will be sharpest and your documentation most thorough.

**The Middle Passage (hours 3-4):** This is when the strangeness budget runs low. The cold is accumulating in your body despite your suit. The absence of animal life has shifted from notable to oppressive. The sound, which was interesting, is now relentless. You may begin to feel that you are not visiting this place but that it is absorbing you — that you are becoming part of its long, slow, empty ecology, another thing without intention weathering quietly in the dark. This feeling is not metaphysically accurate but it is emotionally powerful and it is the signal to begin wrapping up your work.

**Departure (final hour):** A complex emotion — relief at leaving, combined with a surprising reluctance, combined with the knowledge that you have seen something genuinely unprecedented and will spend a long time understanding what it means. Follow your guideline back. Do not rush. Check your equipment. Breathe.

**After (hours and days following return):** The Drowned Plateau does not stay on the plateau. You will think about it. You will hear its sounds in quiet moments — the thrumming stone, the crystal frond-clicks, the low roar. You will see its light when you close your eyes. You will think about the bowl and what might have rested there. You will think about the lone wall on the northern edge, facing nothing, sheltering nothing, standing in the current like a sentence interrupted mid-word.

This is normal. You visited a place where time moves differently and presence means something different and the absence of life is itself a kind of presence. It will take time to integrate. Let it take time. The plateau is patient. You can be patient too.

## A Final Note

The Drowned Plateau is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is not indifferent in the way a blizzard is indifferent — it is indifferent in the way that stone is indifferent, in the way that a forest is indifferent, in the way that a very old thing is indifferent to a very brief visitor. You will be there for hours. It has been there for epochs. The ratio between those timescales is the essential fact of your visit, and if you hold it in your mind — lightly, without either aggrandizing your own presence or diminishing it — you will see the plateau clearly, and it will give you what it has to give: its strange light, its stranger silence-that-is-not-silence, its unanswered questions written in stacked stone, and the cold, rhythmic breathing of the bowl at the center of the great grove, exhaling its luminous dust into the dark water above a high place that has no sky.

Go prepared. Go carefully. Come back with your samples and your recordings and your sketches and the feeling that will not leave you for a long time.

The current shifts. The bowl exhales. The cold light rises and falls.

You were there. You saw it. That is enough.
