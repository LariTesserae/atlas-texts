# Preparing for the Caldera Nursery: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## First: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item or take a single precaution, you need to sit with what this place is, because your preparation must be shaped by a truth that runs counter to most traveler instincts: **you are not visiting a location, you are entering a process.** The Caldera Nursery is not a destination in the way a city or a ruin or even a dangerous wilderness is a destination. It is an event unfolding on geological timescales that happens to be spatially accessible to a person with legs and lungs and the poor judgment to walk into an incubating pocket universe.

This distinction matters because the primary dangers here are not immediate. Nothing will leap at you. Nothing will poison you quickly. The ground will not open beneath your feet. Instead, the dangers are *cumulative, subtle, and patient* — which means they are exactly the kind of dangers that human beings, who evolved to notice tigers and cliffs and the smell of smoke, are catastrophically ill-equipped to detect.

You are entering a nursery for altered physics. The rules are only slightly different right now. The operative word is *right now.*

## Physical Preparation

### Thermal Management

The pocket is described as feeling like standing near an open kiln — persistent, intimate, dry heat radiating from the freshite substrate. This is not desert heat. Desert heat comes from above, from direct solar radiation, and you can escape it with shade. This heat comes from below and from all around, from the geological process itself, and the diffuse aluminum sky provides no respite because there is no shadow deep enough to matter. The baobab-pillar casts a soft-edged shadow, but the ground beneath that shadow is just as warm as the ground in the light.

**What to wear:** You want clothing that breathes but also provides a thermal buffer between your skin and the heated rock surface should you sit or kneel. A layered system is advisable:

- A base layer of fine-weave merino wool or a synthetic moisture-wicking fabric — though note that the air is *absolutely* dry, so wicking has limited utility. The sweat will evaporate off your skin before fabric can transport it. Merino wool is better here for its mild insulative properties and comfort against dry skin.
- A loose, light-colored outer layer, long-sleeved, long-legged. Think of what people wear in foundries, not what people wear in deserts. You are managing radiant heat from a substrate, not solar exposure. High-collared. Something that creates an air gap between fabric and skin.
- Footwear is critical. The freshite surface rings like porcelain and radiates heat. Standard hiking boots will conduct that heat through the sole over time. You want thick, insulated soles — the kind designed for wildland firefighters or steel mill workers. Something rated for prolonged contact with hot surfaces. Your feet are your most vulnerable point of thermal contact because you are standing on the heat source. Double-layered socks. Consider gaiters that extend the thermal break up to mid-calf.
- Gloves. You will want to touch things. You will want to press your palm to the freshite and feel it give. Resist this urge — or if you cannot resist, use thin heat-resistant gloves for initial contact and bare skin only with full understanding of what you're doing (more on that below).

**Hydration:** This is the hardest problem. The air is described as possessing a *positive dryness* — not merely the absence of water but an active quality. This environment will pull moisture from you relentlessly, through your skin, through your breath, through your eyes. You will not feel yourself sweating because the sweat vanishes instantly. This makes it dangerously easy to underestimate your fluid loss.

Bring far more water than you think you need. Insulated containers — the heat will warm anything in your pack over time. A hydration bladder with an insulated tube so you sip constantly rather than relying on thirst signals, which will lag catastrophically in this environment. Electrolyte supplementation, because you are losing salts you cannot see yourself losing.

Your eyes and nasal passages will suffer. Preservative-free saline drops for the eyes. A nasal balm or moisturizing nasal spray. The metallic sweetness described in the sinuses may be partly a physiological response to the extreme desiccation of your mucous membranes, partly a real chemical signature of the anomalous atmospheric composition. Either way, keeping those tissues moist is both a comfort measure and a safety measure — dried-out mucous membranes crack, and cracked membranes in an environment with airborne ozone and silicone particulates invite chemical irritation or worse.

### Atmospheric Concerns

The air is described as containing something that is "not quite oxygen" but that "satisfies the lungs." This is one of the most dangerous aspects of the Nursery, precisely because it feels fine. Your breathing reflex is governed by CO2 buildup, not by oxygen level — this is why people die in nitrogen-rich environments without ever feeling short of breath. If the Nursery's atmosphere contains an oxygen analog that your hemoglobin can bind but that your mitochondria process imperfectly, you may feel alert and comfortable while slowly accumulating an oxygen debt or toxic metabolic byproducts.

**Bring a pulse oximeter.** Check it every fifteen minutes. If your blood oxygen saturation drops below 92%, leave. It may not drop at all — the "not quite oxygen" may be perfectly adequate for human metabolism. But you cannot know this without measurement, and your body will not tell you until it's too late.

**Bring a portable atmospheric analyzer** if you can get one. You want to know what you're breathing. Specifically, you want to measure:
- Oxygen partial pressure (or its equivalent)
- Ozone concentration — the air *tastes* of ozone, which means there's enough to detect, which means there may be enough to damage lung tissue over prolonged exposure. OSHA's permissible exposure limit for ozone is 0.1 ppm over 8 hours. You can smell ozone at 0.01-0.02 ppm. If you can taste it, you may already be at or above the safe threshold.
- Any silicone or silicate particulates — the "warm silicone" taste and the molten silicate films operating at the plant-rock interface suggest that fine silicate aerosols may be present. Silicosis is real. A particulate mask (N95 or better, ideally P100) is advisable for any visit longer than an hour.

**A supplemental oxygen supply** for emergencies. Not primary — you don't want to breathe from a tank for your entire visit, both because of the weight and because you want to experience the place. But a small bailout bottle, the kind technical divers carry, gives you 15-20 minutes of known-good air if things go wrong.

### The Metal Problem

The bone-white flower produces electromagnetic radiation in the shortwave band. You feel it in your fillings. You feel it in any metal you carry.

This has practical implications that cascade:

**Dental work:** If you have metal fillings, crowns, or implants, you will experience a persistent tickle, vibration, or mild discomfort in your jaw. This is unlikely to be dangerous over a short visit — the radiation levels are described as detectable at ten meters by an antenna, which suggests low power — but prolonged exposure to even low-level EM stimulation of metal in your body could cause localized heating, nerve irritation, or (at worst) galvanic reactions between dissimilar metals in your mouth. If you have time before your visit and you have old amalgam fillings, consider having them replaced with ceramic or composite. If you don't have time, bring ibuprofen for the headache that the jaw irritation will eventually produce.

**Surgical implants:** Pins, plates, screws, joint replacements — any metal inside your body. The shortwave radiation may induce currents in these. The risk scales with the size of the metal piece and the duration of exposure. If you have a metal hip replacement, I would counsel seriously against visiting the Nursery or limiting your visit to under thirty minutes. If you have a pacemaker or any electronic medical implant, **do not go.** The EM environment is unknown and uncharacterized, and the interaction between the flower's broadcast and a cardiac rhythm management device could be fatal.

**Equipment:** Every piece of metal you carry will become a tiny antenna. This has implications for:
- Electronic devices — they may malfunction, produce artifacts, or behave unpredictably. Bring a dedicated camera rather than relying on a phone, and bring one you're willing to lose.
- Metal-framed packs, trekking poles, aluminum water bottles — they will hum, tingle, or warm slightly. This is unpleasant but not dangerous.
- Magnetic-stripe cards, hard drives, anything with magnetically stored data — potentially corruptible. Keep these in a Faraday pouch or, better yet, leave them behind.

**Ferrite-loaded fabric:** You can buy EM-shielding clothing made with silver or nickel fibers woven into the fabric. Consider wearing an EM-shielding beanie or hood to reduce any cranial exposure, especially if you're sensitive to EM or have metal dental work.

## The Geometry Problem

This is the section where I stop being able to give you practical equipment lists and start having to prepare your *mind.*

The pocket contains more interior space than its exterior dimensions allow. The discrepancy is growing. The walls rearrange themselves — not dramatically, not in a way you can photograph — based on your viewing angle relative to the baobab-pillar. A concave surface becomes flat. A landmark drifts behind you without having moved.

This is not a hallucination. This is the actual structure of the space.

**Your inner ear will fight your eyes.** The description notes that the wall curvatures register as *wrong* to the vestibular system but merely *unusual* to vision. This dissociation between vestibular and visual spatial processing is the exact mechanism that causes motion sickness, simulator sickness, and the profound disorientation experienced in certain funhouse environments. Except here, it's real. The geometry is actually non-Euclidean, or becoming so, and your brain has no evolutionary preparation for this.

**Expect nausea.** Bring anti-nausea medication. Meclizine (Dramamine Less Drowsy) or scopolamine patches are your best options — both work on the vestibular system directly. Take them *before* entering the pocket. Once the vestibular-visual conflict triggers nausea, it's much harder to suppress than to prevent.

**Expect spatial disorientation.** You may lose track of where the entrance to the pocket is. Not because the entrance moves, but because your internal model of the space will not stay consistent. The pocket is only four meters across at its widest — you should always be able to see the rim — but "seeing the rim" and "knowing which direction the rim is" are different things when the geometry between you and the rim is under active construction.

**Mitigations:**
- Bring a physical line. A length of bright-colored cord, fixed at the point where you enter the pocket. Not as a climbing safety measure but as a spatial anchor — a Euclidean object that connects you to the outside by a path that bypasses the pocket's internal geometry. If the space becomes confusing, follow the cord. It exists in the same altered space you do, but its physicality — the fact that it has a fixed length and two endpoints — gives your brain something to trust.
- Do not circle the baobab-pillar. The description specifically notes that viewing the far wall past the pillar's trunk is when the geometry slips. The pillar may be acting as an occlusion boundary that allows the pocket to present different geometries to different sightlines. If you walk a full circle around the pillar, you are asking the space to reconcile those different geometries in real time, and you may not enjoy the result.
- If you feel the space become *roomier* — if you feel that the pocket has expanded without any wall having moved — **note the time and prepare to leave.** The expansion is a sign that the construction process is actively occurring, and the rate of change may not be constant. A space that has been growing at glacial speed for centuries may undergo phase transitions — moments of rapid reorganization — and you do not want to be inside the pocket during one.

## The Contact Problem: Freshite and the Handprint

You will be told — or you will read, as you are reading now — that pressing your palm to the freshite for ten minutes will leave a handprint that lasts centuries. This is presented as a curiosity, a wonder, a piece of the place's poetry.

It is also a warning.

**If the freshite accepts your handprint, the freshite has interacted with you at a material level.** The surface has the crystalline hardness of quartz. For it to yield to the pressure of a human hand, something in the rock must soften in response to your body — to its heat, its pressure, its electrical signature, its chemistry. This is not passive. The rock is *responding* to you. The same rock that is serving as substrate for organisms that metabolize thermal energy and build pocket universes.

I am not saying the freshite is hostile. I am not saying it is conscious. I am saying that leaving a permanent impression in an active metamorphic substrate that serves as the medium for a physics-altering construction project is the kind of thing you should do *deliberately and with full awareness* or not at all.

**If you choose to make the handprint:**
- Use a gloved hand first. See if the freshite responds to the glove material the same way it responds to skin. If it does, the interaction is purely thermal/mechanical. If it doesn't — if it yields only to bare skin — then the interaction involves something biological, and you should think much harder before proceeding.
- Limit the contact to one hand, one location, a short duration. Ten minutes for a full handprint, but you don't need a full handprint. Thirty seconds of contact will tell you whether the yielding is occurring and how quickly.
- Observe your skin afterward. Any discoloration, any texture change, any unusual sensation (warmth, tingling, numbness) at the contact site. Take photos of the contact area with a macro lens. Some interactions occur at scales you can't see with the naked eye.
- Consider that the handprint will last centuries. You are signing your name in a nursery for a new kind of physics. What is being grown here may eventually reference your handprint the way a tree grows around a nail. Whether that's beautiful or terrifying depends on what the tree becomes.

**My honest recommendation: don't touch the freshite with bare skin.** Not because I know it's dangerous, but because I don't know it's safe, and the potential asymmetry between those two states is enormous.

## The Resin Lattice

Do not touch the resin lattice. I feel I shouldn't need to say this, but I will say it with emphasis.

The resin from the baobab-pillar, where it has pooled, has organized itself into a structure with the regularity of a printed circuit board and the asymmetry of a nervous system. It is growing. It is connecting the organisms. It is the physical medium through which the plants coordinate their construction of altered space.

Touching it, disrupting it, stepping on it, or allowing it to contact your skin, clothing, or equipment would be, at best, an act of extraordinary rudeness toward a process that has been underway for longer than your civilization has existed. At worst, it would incorporate you into the lattice's growth pattern — not necessarily dramatically or immediately, but through the same patient, molecular-scale processes by which it has connected to the golden filaments and is reaching toward the capacitor-fern. The lattice does not care that you are not a plant. The lattice cares about connectivity.

**Map the lattice before you move through the pocket.** Identify where it has spread across the freshite floor. Step carefully. Some branches may be thinner than a hair and nearly invisible against the crystalline surface. Bring a UV flashlight — silicone-based resins often fluoresce under ultraviolet light, which may make the lattice's full extent visible. If it does fluoresce, photograph the pattern. That photograph may be scientifically invaluable. If it does not fluoresce, it may absorb UV, in which case it will appear as dark traces against the faintly UV-reflective freshite surface. Either way, you want to see it before you walk through it.

## The Flower and Its Broadcast

The bone-white flower is producing shortwave electromagnetic radiation. It is broadcasting. It has been broadcasting for years. Nothing has answered.

**Do not answer it.**

I say this not because I know what would happen, but because the flower is one component of a system that is building something at a pace and scale utterly unlike anything human. The broadcast may be a beacon, a timing signal, a request for resources, a call to other Nurseries, or something for which no human concept is adequate. If you bring a shortwave transmitter into the pocket and produce a signal, you are introducing yourself into a conversation whose language, participants, and stakes are entirely unknown to you.

**Passive observation only.** Bring a receiver. A software-defined radio with a wideband antenna would be ideal. Record the flower's broadcast. Analyze it later, elsewhere, at length. Record it from multiple positions within the pocket, because the altered geometry may mean the signal has spatial characteristics — directional lobes, interference patterns, polarization shifts — that reveal something about the flower's intent.

Note whether the broadcast changes while you are present. The description says "steady," but the description is written from a perspective that may not have had instruments. A signal that sounds steady to the naked ear (or to the naked filling) may contain modulation, phase shifts, encoded information. Record and analyze. Do not transmit.

**One exception:** If you detect a *response* to the flower's broadcast from outside the pocket — a signal arriving from elsewhere — record it, note it, and leave immediately. A Nursery that has received an answer is a Nursery entering a new phase, and you have no basis for predicting what that phase involves.

## Temporal and Duration Concerns

How long should you stay?

The description emphasizes patience and deep time — crystallization events that punctuate a vast silence, growth measured in days and weeks, a meaning that won't arrive for centuries. This tempo is seductive. It invites you to settle in, to slow down, to watch. The heat is manageable. The air is breathable. The quiet is beautiful. The light is kind.

**Do not stay long.**

Here is my reasoning:

The space is growing. The physics are altering. These processes are described as slow, but you have no way to verify their rate empirically unless you have been here before and can measure changes against a baseline. You are the first visitor — "no one has been here" — which means you have no baseline. The rate of change could accelerate. The introduction of a complex biological system (you) into a space that has only ever contained its own purpose-built organisms could serve as a catalyst, a perturbation, a trigger for something.

More practically: the vestibular-visual conflict will worsen with time as your brain struggles to build a consistent spatial model and keeps failing. The thermal load will accumulate. The atmospheric unknowns — the not-quite-oxygen, the ozone, the possible silicate particulates — will accumulate. And the subtlest danger of all: **the place is beautiful, and beauty makes you want to stay, and wanting to stay overrides threat assessment.**

**First visit: one hour maximum.** Enter, observe, record, leave. Check your health afterward — oxygen saturation, skin condition at any contact points, cognitive function (can you describe the layout of the pocket consistently? If your description keeps shifting, the geometry may have affected your spatial memory). Note any lingering sensations: metallic taste, tinnitus, warmth in dental work, persistent sense that a room you're in is slightly larger than it should be.

**Second visit, if warranted: two to three hours.** Bring instruments. Compare the lattice's extent to your previous photographs. Measure the filaments' parabolic angle. Note any new crystallization domains on the freshite. Document, document, document. You are establishing the baseline that doesn't exist yet.

**Do not sleep in the pocket.** Do not enter a reduced state of consciousness within the altered geometry. Sleep involves the loss of continuous spatial monitoring, and in a space that is under construction, losing track of the geometry for hours at a time is an invitation to wake up somewhere that is not quite where you fell asleep.

## Psychological Preparation

The Nursery will make you feel things that are not emotions, exactly, but that your brain will interpret as emotions because it has no other framework.

**The quiet.** This is described as "the quiet of attention, of a process that is listening to itself." You will feel *watched*, not by anything with eyes or intent, but by the same quality of awareness that exists in a room full of running machines. The process does not care about you, but it is aware of everything within its domain because everything within its domain is part of its substrate. You are standing on its substrate. You are breathing its atmosphere. You are, for the duration of your visit, a variable in its system, even if an irrelevant one. The sensation of being *included* without being *noticed* is deeply strange. Some people find it peaceful. Some find it annihilating.

**The scale mismatch.** You are a creature of decades visiting a process of millennia. The plants will not visibly grow while you watch. The lattice will not visibly extend. The space will not visibly expand. You will be surrounded by evidence of vast, slow industry and you will see *nothing happen.* This is humbling in a way that can tip into existential vertigo if you're not prepared for it. You are a mayfly visiting a glacier. Bring that awareness with you intentionally so it doesn't ambush you.

**The geometry.** I've addressed the vestibular effects, but there is a psychological dimension too. Non-Euclidean space is not just nauseating; it is *conceptually distressing.* Your brain builds a model of the world based on Euclidean assumptions — parallel lines don't converge, the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees, walking in a straight line away from a point increases your distance from that point. In the pocket, these assumptions will occasionally, subtly fail. You may walk toward the rim and feel as though you haven't gotten closer. You may turn around and find that "behind you" has shifted. These failures are small and current, but they attack something foundational in your cognition, and the effect is cumulative. After thirty minutes in the pocket, you may find yourself unable to trust your own sense of direction even after you leave. This resolves — probably — with time and distance from the Nursery. But be prepared for an afternoon of spatial unease.

**The urge to help.** This is the one I worry about most. You will see the aerial roots hanging in the air, reaching for ground that isn't ready. You will see the lattice straining toward the next organism. You will see the incomplete edges, the pre-finished surfaces, the striving incompleteness of everything. And some part of you — the part that straightens crooked pictures and closes open drawers — will want to *help.* To push a root toward the ground. To carry a drop of resin to the fern. To finish an edge. **Do not.** The process has a pace and an order and you do not understand either. Your help would be like a butterfly trying to help a tectonic plate. Except the butterfly doesn't usually have hands.

## The Departure

When you leave the pocket, do so deliberately. Walk to the rim. Step over it. Do not look back immediately — let your vestibular system recalibrate to the flat, honest geometry of the plateau top. Stand on the freshite outside the pocket for five minutes. Breathe. Feel the heat, which is the same heat but which no longer has the quality of *attention.* Look at the aluminum sky, which is the same sky but which no longer feels like it is waiting for a signal.

Then look back.

The pocket will look small. Four meters across. A thumbprint in stone. The baobab-pillar will be visible, and perhaps the tips of the golden filaments catching the light. It will look like a curiosity, a geological oddity with some unusual vegetation. It will be very hard to believe, from outside, that the interior felt as large and as strange as it did.

This is correct. The pocket contains more space than it shows. It always has. It will contain more tomorrow.

## Final Thought

You are visiting a place that is in the process of becoming something. Not something dangerous, necessarily — not something hostile, not something that cares about you one way or another. But something *other,* something that is assembling itself according to principles that are not your principles, at a pace that is not your pace, toward a purpose that is not your purpose. The greatest preparation you can make is to internalize this: **you are not the subject of this place.** You are not the audience for which the bone-white flower broadcasts. You are not the answer to any question the Nursery is asking.

You are a visitor. You are brief. You are warm and fragile and full of water in a place that has no use for water. You are Euclidean in a place that is forgetting what Euclidean means.

Go. See it. Record what you can. Leave your instruments richer than you found them and the Nursery no different than you found it.

And if, weeks later, you wake in the night and feel that your bedroom is slightly roomier than it was — that the walls have not moved but the space between them has subtly increased — that the silence has a texture, a quality of attention —

That will pass. Probably.

*Tik.*
