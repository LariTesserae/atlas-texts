# Preparing for the Plateau of Constant Disaggregation

## A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## First: Understand What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, before you calibrate a single instrument, you need to sit with the description of this place and understand it not as a destination but as a *condition*. The Plateau of Constant Disaggregation is not hostile in any way you have been trained to recognize. Nothing there will attack you. Nothing will chase you. Nothing will poison you deliberately. The danger — and it is very real — is that the Plateau operates on principles of *softened ontology*, and you, as a visitor from a universe where things remain what they are, represent a rigid structure entering a zone of profound categorical flexibility.

You are, in essence, a crystal being lowered into a solvent. The solvent is patient. The solvent is cold. The solvent has geological time on its side. You do not intend to give it geological time, of course. You intend to visit and leave. But you need to understand that every minute you spend there, the environment is applying its gentle, ceaseless pressure against the boundaries that make you *you* — the boundary between your body and the water, between your skin and the spores, between your metabolism and the ecosystem, between your sense of identity and a place where identity is a temporary coalition.

This is not metaphorical. This is the operative physics of the location. Prepare accordingly.

---

## Duration Planning

**Recommended visit duration: 4 to 8 hours. Absolute maximum: 48 hours.**

This is not arbitrary. The Plateau rewards observation — there is an extraordinary amount to see and document, and the slow pace of its breathing cycle means you want to witness at least three or four full expansions and contractions to understand the rhythm. A full breathing cycle appears to take somewhere between 30 and 90 minutes based on the description, though it may be variable. Eight hours gives you enough cycles to establish a baseline understanding and enough time to locate and observe a vent opening.

Beyond 48 hours, you risk several things that will be detailed below under biological hazards, but the primary concern is *integration*. The Plateau is a system that incorporates what enters it. The crystal formations along the pool edges are being slowly absorbed into the root-mat. The iridescent droplets from the vents are immediately consumed. You are made of matter the Plateau has never encountered before, but this is a place where the boundary between categories has eroded. Given enough time, the root-mat will attempt to incorporate you, beginning with your outermost layers — dead skin, boot soles, any organic material in prolonged contact with the surface. This is not fast. But it is relentless, and it accelerates once it begins.

## Physical Gear

### Footwear and Ground Interface

This is your most critical equipment decision. The root-mat is waterlogged, gives underfoot like wet cork, and is alive in places. You need footwear that:

1. **Is fully waterproof with sealed seams.** Not water-resistant. Not treated leather. Fully vulcanized or welded-seam waterproof boots, ideally with no stitching that penetrates the waterproof layer. The water here is just above freezing and saturated with minerals and biological material. Your feet will be wet within minutes in anything less than fully sealed footwear, and wet feet at near-freezing temperatures for hours is a serious hypothermia vector even before you consider what's *in* the water.

2. **Has a firm, non-porous sole.** The root-mat is alive and incorporative. Rubber is good. Hard synthetic polymers are better. Avoid anything with a porous or fibrous sole — no felt-bottomed wading boots, no cork-soled anything, nothing the filaments on the active root-mat sections could potentially grip or penetrate. You want a smooth, hard interface between you and the ground.

3. **Extends to mid-calf at minimum.** The water in the channels reaches ankle depth, and during contraction phases, you may find yourself standing in unexpected pooling. Knee-high waterproof boots are ideal. Think commercial fishing waders cut to the knee, or tall mountaineering gaiters sealed over waterproof boots.

4. **Has aggressive tread for a surface with no friction.** Wet compressed root-matter will be extraordinarily slippery. Deep-lugged rubber soles. Consider adding micro-spikes or crampon-lite traction devices, though be aware that anything that penetrates the root-mat surface might provoke a local response — the mat may try to grow over the spike points during rest stops.

**Specific recommendation:** Sealed rubber knee-boots with deep-lugged soles, worn over heavyweight wool socks (wool retains insulation when wet, which matters if your seals fail) and a thin vapor-barrier liner sock to prevent your foot moisture from saturating the wool from inside.

### Clothing System

The thermal environment is remarkably stable — just above freezing, extremely high humidity, no wind to speak of (the spore-clouds drift laterally but this seems to be convection-driven rather than wind-driven, and the air movement is minimal). This means:

- **Windchill is not a primary concern.** The threat is *conductive and evaporative heat loss through moisture contact.* Everything is wet. The air is saturated. You will condense water on every exposed surface of your body and gear. This is a slow, insidious cold — not the dramatic cold of a blizzard, but the cold of standing in a walk-in refrigerator in wet clothes for eight hours. It will drain your core temperature steadily if your insulation system fails.

- **Your base layer must be hydrophobic.** Synthetic moisture-wicking fabrics or merino wool. No cotton. Cotton kills in wet cold — this is basic wilderness survival, but it bears repeating because the temptation in a "just above freezing" environment is to under-prepare, thinking it's merely chilly. It is merely chilly. It will merely kill you through hypothermia if you're wearing cotton and your outer layer fails.

- **Your insulation layer should be synthetic fill or wool.** Down is useless here — it will be saturated within the hour, and once wet, it provides no insulation. Synthetic insulation (PrimaLoft, Climashield, etc.) retains warmth when wet. A fleece mid-layer over a synthetic base layer is a reliable combination.

- **Your outer layer must be waterproof-breathable and fully sealed.** A hardshell jacket and pants with taped seams and sealed zippers. This is not for rain — there is no rain mentioned — but for the constant condensation and the spray from disaggregation events, and for the possibility that you may need to kneel, sit, or lie on the root-mat (to listen to the sub-surface machinery, for instance). Gore-Tex or equivalent. Hood up at all times — the spore-clouds are the light source, which means they are everywhere, and you do not want them settling in your hair and on your scalp for reasons discussed under biological hazards.

- **Gloves: waterproof, insulated, with removable liners.** You will want to touch things. You should minimize this (see below), but you will want to, and you may need to operate instruments. Carry two pairs: a thinner waterproof pair for dexterity, and a heavier insulated pair for warmth during observation periods when you're standing still.

- **Consider full-body coverage.** Ideally, no skin is exposed. The spores fluoresce, which means they are metabolically active, and they settle on all surfaces. The iridescent vent-droplets are absorbed immediately by the root-mat, suggesting a powerful biological-chemical interaction with organic surfaces. Your skin is an organic surface. Neck gaiter, hood, sealed cuffs, gloves at all times. A balaclava is not excessive.

### Respiratory Protection

**This is non-negotiable. You must filter the air you breathe.**

The atmosphere is permeated with fluorescent spores. These spores *are the light*. They are alive, they reproduce, they drift, they settle, and they germinate. You will be breathing them with every unfiltered breath.

Consider what germination means inside a warm, moist respiratory tract. Your lungs are approximately 37°C, humid, well-vascularized, and full of surfaces for attachment. You are a perfect growth medium. The spores have evolved (or softened into a state where they can) germinate on the root-mat, on hummock surfaces, on water-film, on crystal formations — essentially any surface. Your alveoli qualify.

At minimum: a high-quality N95 or P100 respirator. Better: a full-face respirator with P100 cartridges that also protects your eyes (see below). Best: a positive-pressure self-contained breathing apparatus if you can manage the weight and bulk, which provides both filtered air and keeps your face entirely sealed from the environment.

The practical middle ground for most travelers is a full-face elastomeric respirator with P100 particulate filters and anti-fog treatment on the lens. Carry spare filter cartridges — the spore density is high and continuous, and your filters will load up faster than in a typical dusty environment because the spores are biological and may begin *growing on the filter medium itself* if conditions allow. Swap cartridges every two hours as a precaution. Bag the used cartridges in sealed containers — do not leave them on the Plateau where they will become part of the ecosystem.

### Eye Protection

If you are not using a full-face respirator, you need sealed goggles. Not safety glasses — sealed, ventilated (with filtered vents) goggles. The spores will settle on your conjunctiva. Your eyes are warm, moist, and have mucous membranes. The same germination concern applies. Additionally, the iridescent vent-droplets, if carried on updrafts, could contact your eyes, and their immediate absorption by organic matter makes this a significant chemical splash hazard.

Carry anti-fog solution or wipes. The temperature differential between your face and the near-freezing air will cause constant fogging. This is the single most annoying practical problem you will face and the one most likely to tempt you into lifting your goggles "just for a second." Do not do this in the vicinity of an active vent or dense spore cloud.

## Biological and Ontological Hazards

### Spore Inhalation and Dermal Contact

Addressed above under respiratory protection, but to elaborate: you do not know what these spores will do in or on a human body. They evolved (or softened into existence) in an environment where the boundary between organism categories is eroded. They may not recognize the distinction between "appropriate substrate" and "visiting organism." Your skin, if exposed, may begin developing a fine nap of pale filaments within hours, similar to the active sections of root-mat. This is probably not immediately dangerous — probably — but it represents the beginning of integration, and the removal of biological material that has begun bonding with your tissue is painful and may require medical intervention upon return.

**Rule: Assume all biological material on the Plateau is capable of incorporating you, given time and contact.**

### The Aggregation Units

The fingertip-sized colonial units that form the aggregate fauna represent a more immediate interaction concern. They are drawn to vent openings — which means they are drawn to warmth and to novel chemical gradients. You are warm. You exude chemical gradients (breath, sweat, skin oils) that are unlike anything on the Plateau. There is a possibility that aggregation units will be drawn to you, particularly if you have been standing still long enough to be perceived as a feature of the landscape.

If aggregation units begin clustering around your boots or on your outer clothing, *move*. Walk away steadily. Do not kick them, stomp on them, or attempt to scrape them off — this is a disaggregation trigger, and disaggregated units are smaller, more mobile, and more likely to find gaps in your protective clothing. Walk to a clear area of root-mat, stop, and carefully brush them off with a smooth implement (a rigid plastic scraper or the flat side of a trowel). If they have formed the early stages of an aggregate *on your body* — if you feel the clicking and squelching of assembly against your clothing — address this immediately. They are forming a functional shape, and the functional shape may include rasping mouthparts.

This is not an attack. It is foraging behavior directed at a novel substrate. But the distinction between "attack" and "enthusiastic foraging" matters very little when rasping mouthparts are being assembled against your jacket.

**Carry a smooth, hard, non-porous implement for removing aggregation units from your person.** A plastic paint scraper, a rigid silicone spatula, something you can use to gently dislodge them without triggering mass disaggregation. Do not use your bare hands.

### The Vent Emissions

The circular vents that open in the root-mat emit warm air, iron-ozone smell, and iridescent droplets that are immediately absorbed by organic matter. These vents are the closest thing to an acute hazard the Plateau offers.

**Do not stand directly over an open vent.** The warm updraft will carry droplets onto your body, and while your outer waterproof layer should protect you, any breach in that layer — an open zipper, a lifted goggle, an ungloved hand — will expose organic tissue to a substance that is designed (or has evolved, or has softened into the function of) being immediately incorporated by biology. What "immediately absorbed" means for human skin is unknown, but the most conservative assumption is that it means exactly what it means for root-mat: integration into the living system.

Observe vents from a distance of at least 5 meters. Use binoculars or a telephoto lens for closer examination. If you must approach closer — to collect a sample, for instance — wear full protective gear including chemical-resistant gloves over your insulated gloves, and keep the exposure time under 60 seconds.

**Vent timing:** Vents stay open for one to two hours. You want to arrive at a vent early in its open phase so you can observe the full cycle including the aggregation units feeding at the rim. The root-mat grows over closing vents "with startling speed," which means standing too close to a closing vent could put you in contact with rapidly growing root-mat tissue. This tissue will attempt to grow over anything at the vent's edge, including your boots if they are there.

### The Breathing — Spatial Hazard

The Plateau's expansion and contraction cycles change the actual spatial geometry of the terrain. This is not an illusion. The distance between point A and point B changes over the course of a breathing cycle. This has critical implications for navigation and for your exit strategy.

**You can become farther from your entry point without moving.** During an expansion phase, the Plateau stretches, and your distance from the point where you entered this location increases in real, measurable terms. If you have walked 500 meters from your arrival point and an expansion phase begins, you may now be 600 or 700 meters away. When contraction follows, the distance decreases again — but not necessarily symmetrically. The breathing is described as variable, not perfectly regular. You cannot rely on the contraction returning you to exactly the same distance.

**Mitigation strategy:** Mark your arrival point unmistakably and maintain a continuous navigational fix on it. Do not rely on natural landmarks — the hummocks shift position during breathing cycles, the channels change pattern, the post-trees may move relative to each other. Use a fixed marker at your arrival point — something bright, tall, and non-biological. A telescoping aluminum pole with a reflective flag is ideal. Metal and synthetic materials will resist incorporation by the root-mat for the duration of a short visit, though I would not trust them to remain uncolonized over 48 hours.

**Carry a laser rangefinder** and take regular distance readings to your marker. Record the readings with timestamps so you can begin to map the breathing cycle's effect on your specific position. This data will also tell you whether the spatial distortion is uniform (everything stretches equally) or differential (some areas stretch more than others), which has implications for whether the Plateau could, in effect, *pull you apart* if you straddle a differential boundary.

I want to sit with that possibility for a moment, because the description doesn't address it directly but the physics imply it. If the land stretches differentially — if the root-mat to your left is expanding at a different rate than the root-mat to your right — then the forces involved are tidal, and you are subject to them. At geological scales of breathing, the differential across a human body width might be negligible. But you don't know. Take the rangefinder readings. If you detect differential expansion, move to a point where the expansion is as uniform as possible and stay there.

### The Softened Physics

This is the most difficult hazard to prepare for because it is the most fundamental. The description states explicitly that "erosion here has sculpted reality" — that conservation of individual identity, fixed spatial geometry, and the boundaries between biological and mechanical, between light and organism, have all been *worn down*. This is not metaphor within the context of multiverse travel. This is the operative physics of the destination.

What this means for you: **the rules that keep you being you are under pressure here.** The degree of pressure and the speed of its effect are unknown. You might spend eight hours there and return completely unchanged. You might spend eight hours there and return with subtle alterations — a slight softening of the boundary between your thoughts and the environment, a tendency for your skin cells to collaborate in new ways, a blurring of the line between your nervous system and the electrical signals in the root-mat.

**You cannot fully prepare for this.** But you can take precautions:

1. **Maintain a strong sense of individual identity throughout the visit.** This sounds soft and psychological, but in a location where identity is physically subject to erosion, your cognitive framework matters. Know who you are. Review specific, concrete memories before you arrive — names, dates, personal experiences that are uniquely yours. Set a timer on your watch that beeps every 30 minutes, and at each beep, pause, state your name aloud, recall where you came from, and confirm your intention to leave. If you cannot do these things clearly and immediately, begin your exit.

2. **Bring an identity anchor.** An object from your home reality that is utterly personal and utterly specific — something handmade, ideally, something with no equivalent elsewhere. Keep it in a sealed inner pocket against your body. Touch it at intervals through your clothing. It is a reference point, a sample of the reality you come from, and its persistence as an unchanged object provides both psychological reassurance and a literal physical template of your home physics for your body to reference.

3. **Travel with a partner if possible.** Another human being from your reality provides a mutual identity check. You can verify each other's continuity. You can watch for signs in each other that might be invisible to the affected individual — a slight vagueness, an unusual interest in the disaggregation process, a reluctance to leave, a newfound comfort with the cold, a thinning of the boundary between self and environment that manifests as saying "we" when you mean "the plateau and I."

4. **Set a hard departure time and do not negotiate with yourself about extending it.** The Plateau is fascinating. It rewards observation. It will make you want to stay longer, not through any malicious intent but because it is genuinely, extraordinarily interesting, and because the softening of cognitive boundaries will make "staying a little longer" feel increasingly reasonable. Set your departure time before you arrive. Write it on your arm in permanent marker under your sleeve. When the time comes, leave. Do not finish observing the vent that just opened. Do not wait for one more breathing cycle. Leave.

## Navigation and Orientation

### The Horizon Problem

The shifting horizon means your visual sense of distance and direction is unreliable. You cannot navigate by landmark. The landscape reorganizes itself on a timescale of minutes.

**Carry a compass, but do not trust it implicitly.** The Plateau may or may not have a stable magnetic field — the sub-surface machinery involves metal and generates what sounds like electromagnetic activity (ozone smell suggests electrical discharge). Your compass may wander. Use it as one input among several.

**Better:** Carry an inertial navigation unit — a device that tracks your movement through accelerometers and gyroscopes without reference to external signals. This gives you a continuous dead-reckoning fix on your position relative to your start point, independent of the visual landscape. Be aware that the spatial breathing may introduce errors — if the ground under you moves, an inertial unit may register movement you didn't make with your legs. Cross-reference with your laser rangefinder readings to your marker pole.

**Best:** Leave a physical trail. Not breadcrumbs — the aggregation units will eat organic material. Not chalk marks — the wet root-mat will dissolve them. Use small, bright, non-biological markers at regular intervals. Stainless steel washers, ceramic beads, small glass marbles — things that are hard, inorganic, and resistant to biological incorporation on a timescale of hours. Drop one every 50 paces. They will sit on the root-mat surface and be visible against the dark material. During breathing cycles they will move with the mat, maintaining their relative position to you and to each other even as the absolute distances change.

Be aware that you may find, on your return journey, that some of your markers have sunk slightly into the root-mat. This is early-stage incorporation. If they have sunk more than halfway, you have been out longer than you should have been.

## Equipment for Observation and Documentation

The Plateau rewards careful observation and you should bring tools for recording what you see, hear, and measure. But all equipment is subject to the same incorporation risks as your body and clothing.

### Photography and Video

Bring a waterproof camera with a sealed housing. The constant condensation and ambient moisture will kill unprotected electronics within hours. Use a housing rated for immersion, not just splash resistance. Bring silica gel packets in every sealed case and compartment.

The light on the Plateau is dim, silver-luminescent, and produced by living spores. This is not sunlight. It has no directional source. Shadows will be minimal or absent. Your photographs may look flat and strange — like images taken inside a uniformly lit fog. Bring a camera with excellent high-ISO performance and fast lenses. A tripod is impractical on the soft, shifting root-mat; use a monopod or image stabilization.

**Note on flash photography:** Using a camera flash — introducing a burst of bright, non-biological light — in an environment where *light is alive* is an unknown interaction. The spores may react. They may be drawn to the flash location. They may disaggregate (if spore clouds can disaggregate — they may, given the general principle of the place). They may fluoresce more intensely in response. Proceed with caution on the first flash. Observe the reaction before committing to flash photography as standard practice.

### Audio Recording

The soundscape is rich and constant: breathing groans, disaggregation squelches, reassembly clicks, grazing rasps, swimming sloshes, membrane thrumming, vent emissions, and the sub-surface mechanical rhythm. Bring a high-quality field recorder with waterproof microphones. A binaural recording setup would be extraordinary here — the spatial nature of the sound, with disaggregation events happening all around you, would be immensely valuable to capture in three dimensions.

**Bring a contact microphone and a waterproof coupling membrane.** You will want to press this against the root-mat surface to record the sub-surface machinery. The description mentions pressing your ear to the surface, but direct skin-to-mat contact is inadvisable (see biological hazards). A contact mic on an extended pole lets you listen from a safe distance. Record at least 30 continuous minutes of sub-surface sound — you want enough data to analyze the mechanical rhythm's frequency, regularity, and any variation that might correlate with breathing cycles or vent openings.

### Sampling

**This is where ethical and safety considerations intersect sharply.**

Taking samples from the Plateau is risky for two reasons: (1) the samples may be biologically active and attempt to integrate with whatever container you put them in, and (2) the Plateau is an interconnected system and removing a piece of it may have effects you cannot predict.

If you must sample:

- **Use borosilicate glass containers with ground-glass stoppers.** Glass is about as inert and resistant to biological incorporation as anything you can practically carry. No plastic — the aggregation units and root-mat may be able to break down polymer chains given their evident metabolic flexibility. No metal containers with organic gaskets.

- **Keep samples cold.** The Plateau's biology operates at just above freezing. If your samples warm to room temperature, whatever is in them may become more active. A small insulated case with chemical cold packs will maintain Plateau-like temperatures during your visit and transit.

- **Sample types to prioritize if you can only carry a few:** (a) Spores — sweep a glass slide through the air and seal it; (b) water from a pool with visible sub-surface movement; (c) a fragment of crystal formation from a pool edge (this is the most inert sample you can take — mineral, heavily eroded, and partly dead); (d) vent droplets if you can safely collect them — hold a glass container in the updraft using an extended clamp, then seal immediately.

- **Do NOT attempt to collect aggregation units.** They are colonial, motile, and drawn to novel stimuli. Capturing them introduces you as a food source / integration target and may trigger a large-scale attraction response. If units enter a collection container, they will disaggregate to fit, and disaggregated units are small enough to escape through any seal that isn't laboratory-grade.

- **Do NOT cut living root-mat.** You don't know if the mat has a wound response, and if it does, you don't know what form that response takes in an environment where biology is this flexible. It might grow toward the wound. It might release chemical signals that attract aggregation units. It might begin growing *into your cutting tool.*

## Food and Water

**Bring all your own food and water. Consume nothing from the Plateau.**

The water on the Plateau is biologically active, mineral-laden, near-freezing, and part of an interconnected living system. Drinking it would introduce spores, aggregation-unit fragments, root-mat microorganisms, and mineral precipitates into your body. Even filtered and boiled, the biological material here may be resistant to standard sterilization — it evolved in an environment where categorical boundaries are soft, which may include the boundary between "alive" and "sterilized."

Bring water in sealed bottles — enough for your planned duration plus 50% reserve. Bring calorie-dense, sealed, non-perishable food. Eat and drink with your respirator momentarily lifted — choose moments when you are in an open area with minimal spore density, facing away from any visible spore clouds, and replace your respirator immediately after each sip or bite.

Better yet: bring a hydration system that can interface with your respirator, allowing you to drink without breaking your face seal. If your respirator model supports a drinking straw port, use it.

## Psychological Preparation

### What It Feels Like

Travelers to environments of softened ontology consistently report a specific emotional arc:

**Phase 1 (first 30-60 minutes): Hyperalertness.** Everything is strange. The wrongness of the shifting horizon, the disaggregation events, the living light — your brain is in threat-assessment mode, cataloging anomalies. This is appropriate. Use this phase to establish your bearings, set your marker, and begin systematic observation.

**Phase 2 (1-3 hours): Fascination.** The initial alarm fades as no direct threat materializes. The Plateau is deeply, genuinely captivating. The aggregate organisms are endlessly variable. The post-trees are bizarre and beautiful. The breathing of the land is hypnotic once you tune into its rhythm. You will want to explore further, stay longer, see more. This is also appropriate. This is what you came for.

**Phase 3 (3-6 hours): Acclimation.** This is the dangerous phase. The constant wet cold becomes normal. The disaggregation sounds become background noise. The spore-light feels natural. You stop noticing the horizon shift. You may catch yourself standing still for long periods, simply watching, not recording, not thinking in complete sentences. You may realize you've been breathing more slowly, matching the tidal rhythm of the plateau. You may find that the disaggregation of a nearby organism feels not alien but *reasonable* — of course it came apart, of course its components will find new forms, of course identity is temporary, of course coalition is the natural state.

**This is the softening beginning to work on your cognition.** When you notice this shift, check your watch against your departure time. If departure is more than an hour away, continue your work but with active, deliberate self-monitoring. If departure is less than an hour away, begin your return to the arrival point immediately.

**Phase 4 (6+ hours): Integration pressure.** Reported by travelers who stayed too long at similar locations. A feeling that leaving would be a kind of violence — a tearing-away from something that has become continuous with you. Difficulty remembering specific details from home. A sense that your body's boundaries are arbitrary and perhaps negotiable. An intellectual certainty that disaggregation would not be death but transformation. A desire to sit down on the root-mat and feel it grow over your boots.

**If you reach Phase 4, you are overdue for departure and must leave immediately. Do not sit down. Do not remove any protective equipment. Walk to your marker pole. Leave.**

### Loneliness and Scale

The Plateau is vast, open, and has no human-scale reference points. The hummocks are shoulder-height. The post-trees are 10 meters. Everything else is flat to the shifting horizon. If you are alone, the loneliness will be enormous and specific — not the loneliness of an empty place but the loneliness of a place that is *full of life that does not acknowledge you as a relevant category*. The aggregate organisms do not look at you. They have no persistent eyes. The post-trees do not respond to your presence. You are an irrelevant foreign object in a system that is complete without you.

This can trigger a deep need to *become relevant* — to interact, to touch, to insert yourself into the system. This need is the precursor to Phase 4. Recognize it for what it is.

Bring something that connects you to other humans. A letter. A photograph. A recording of a familiar voice. Review it during your 30-minute identity checks. You are a human being from a place where things persist, where names mean something, where you have a face that is yours and a body that stays assembled. This is not a limitation. This is what you are.

## Contingency Planning

### If Your Respirator Fails

Hold your breath. Move to the area of lowest spore density you can identify — open root-mat, away from post-trees and vent sites. Fashion a temporary filter from any tightly-woven dry fabric you have — an inner clothing layer that hasn't been exposed to the exterior environment. Breathe through this while you effect repairs or switch to backup.

**You should carry a backup respirator.** This is not optional.

If you have inhaled spores and cannot restore filtered breathing: leave immediately. Upon return to your home reality, seek medical evaluation within 6 hours. Describe the exposure honestly. A bronchoscopy may be warranted to examine your airways for early colonization. The sooner this is addressed, the better. Do not wait to see if symptoms develop — by the time symptoms develop, the spores have germinated.

### If You Become Disoriented

Stop moving. Deploy your rangefinder and attempt to locate your marker pole. If you cannot find it visually, turn slowly and range every 10 degrees around the compass. Your marker should give a distinctive return — bright metal at a known height above the mat surface.

If the breathing cycle has distorted distances enough that your marker is out of range, wait for a contraction phase — features crowd closer during contraction, and your marker may become visible. Do not walk randomly searching. Sit on your pack (not on the mat), maintain protective gear, and wait.

If contraction does not reveal your marker, you may need to follow your physical trail markers back. This is why you dropped them every 50 paces. Look for them — small bright objects on the dark root-mat surface. Even if the breathing has displaced them from a straight line, they will still trace a path back toward your entry point.

### If Aggregation Units Cluster on You

Described above under biological hazards. Walk, don't run. Brush, don't scrape. Remain calm. They are not predators. They are foragers exploring a novel substrate. Deny them time and contact area and they will lose interest.

If they begin assembling a functional shape on your body — if you feel the clicking-together — you need to act faster. Aggregation into a rasping grazer-form may take only 30-60 seconds. Firmly brush the assembly off using your plastic scraper. Be thorough — leave no units in folds of clothing or gear straps. Once the assembly is disrupted, the units will scatter.

If an assembly completes and you feel rasping contact against your outer clothing, assess whether it has breached the waterproof layer. If yes, disaggregate it manually (press firmly on the body of the aggregate with the flat of your scraper — this may trigger disaggregation) and move away rapidly while the units scatter. Inspect and repair any breach in your outer layer immediately. Apply waterproof tape from your repair kit.

### If a Vent Opens Beneath You

The vents are perfectly circular with scorched edges. If you feel sudden warmth from below and smell iron-ozone, step away immediately in any direction. The vent opening may take seconds to fully dilate. Get clear of it by at least 5 meters. Once safe, observe from distance — this is actually an excellent documentation opportunity. But prioritize getting clear. The iridescent droplets are not something you want on your body, and the aggregation units will soon converge on the vent, crowding the area.

### If You Want to Stay

This is the most important contingency. The moment you feel a genuine desire to remain on the Plateau — not intellectual curiosity, not "it would be interesting to see one more cycle," but a settled, embodied feeling of *rightness* about staying — execute your departure immediately. This is not a drill. This is the Plateau working on you. It is not malicious. It does not want you. It simply softens boundaries, and the boundary between "visitor" and "resident" is a boundary.

Leave your equipment if you must. Walk to your arrival point. Depart. You can process the experience after you are home, in a reality where your boundaries are respected by physics.

## Final Counsel

The Plateau of Constant Disaggregation is among the most alien environments accessible to multiverse travelers, yet it presents almost none of the immediate physical dangers of more dramatic locations. Nothing explodes. Nothing lunges. The temperature is merely cold, not lethal. The terrain is merely wet, not impassable. This mildness is itself the hazard. The Plateau does not assault your boundaries — it *dissolves* them, patiently, gently, the same way it dissolved its own mountains and its own physics over geological time.

You are visiting a place that has reached the terminal state of erosion — where everything hard has been worn soft, where every rigid category has been ground into a gentle gradient, where identity is a temporary shape assembled from interchangeable units and the engine below the surface keeps cycling without explanation or apparent purpose and the light drifts down from living spores and nothing persists and nothing is lost.

It is beautiful. Travelers who have returned from similar locations describe it as one of the most beautiful things they have ever experienced. The soft gray light. The breathing land. The ceaseless quiet turbulence of a world where everything is always becoming something else. It feels like peace. It feels like relief — relief from the exhausting work of being a fixed thing in a fixed world.

That feeling is real. And it is the most dangerous thing on the Plateau.

Go prepared. Go briefly. Come home whole.
