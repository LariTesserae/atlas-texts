# Preparing for the Canopy Plateau

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Let the nature of this place settle into your thinking before you start packing bags, because the preparation that matters most is conceptual.

You are visiting a living high-altitude plain made of interlocked tree crowns thousands of feet above the actual ground. The surface is wood, moss, and ice. The air is thin. The light is nearly nonexistent. The terrain shifts. There is indigenous intelligence that lives *inside* the canopy beneath your feet. The fauna echolocates. The weather is cold, wet, and relentless. You will be alone on the surface in a way that is difficult to prepare for emotionally.

This is not a forest hike. This is not a mountain expedition. This is not a caving trip, though it shares elements with all three. The closest terrestrial analogue might be a solo crossing of an arctic moorland at night — except the moor is alive, the ground flexes, and something with sonar is hunting nearby.

Plan accordingly.

## Physical Conditioning

**Altitude acclimatization is non-negotiable.** The text specifies "thousands of feet" of elevation — the air is thin enough that breath condenses visibly, and the cold suggests an environment well above treeline by any Earth standard, which is deeply strange because you are literally standing *on* trees. The paradox doesn't help your red blood cell count. Before transit, spend at least two to three weeks doing graded altitude exposure. If your multiverse staging area allows it, spend time at 10,000–14,000 feet equivalent. Learn what your body feels like when oxygen is scarce. Know your own symptoms for altitude sickness — headache behind the eyes, nausea, the irrational irritability, the disturbed sleep. You will need to recognize these early because there is no "descending to a lower camp" here. Lower means *into* the canopy, into the domain of whatever lives inside, and that is not a casual decision.

**Cardiovascular endurance matters more than raw strength.** You will be walking for extended periods across uneven terrain in cold, thin air. The surface is not flat — it undulates, it's slippery with moss and frost, there are pools to navigate around, rises to climb. Train with long, slow, loaded hikes on uneven ground. Trail running on wet, root-covered forest paths is excellent preparation for the footing. Your ankles need to be strong and flexible. The canopy surface will have the texture of tightly braided wood overlaid with moss — think of walking across an enormous, slightly damp wicker basket with a carpet of wet lichen on top. Every step requires micro-adjustments.

**Cold tolerance.** You need to be comfortable being cold for days at a time with no prospect of true warmth. Not hypothermic — the temperatures seem to hover around freezing, maybe slightly above, with dips below during sleet events — but persistently, grindingly cold in a way that saps morale. Cold-water immersion training, winter camping experience, or simply spending long hours outdoors in near-freezing drizzle will calibrate your expectations.

## Gear: Clothing

**Layering is everything, but the system must handle moisture above all else.**

The environment is defined by dampness. The moss is damp. The air is frequently foggy. Rain comes in brief cold bursts. Sleet occurs. The surface you walk on is slightly damp and cool to the touch. You will never be dry. Accept this early and build your clothing system around managing moisture rather than preventing it.

**Base layer:** Merino wool, medium weight, full coverage — long-sleeve top and full-length bottoms. Merino retains warmth when wet, doesn't develop odor quickly (relevant for extended stays), and feels far less miserable against skin when damp than any synthetic. Bring at least two full base layer sets. You will rotate them, sleeping in whichever is least wet, wearing whichever has had longer to partially dry inside your shelter. They won't fully dry. That's fine. Merino handles it.

**Mid layer:** A high-loft synthetic insulating jacket — something like a Primaloft or Climashield Active piece. Not down. Down is worthless here. The moisture will collapse it within a day and you'll be wearing a soggy nylon bag. Synthetic insulation retains loft when wet and dries faster. Bring a second, lighter synthetic mid layer for modulating warmth during active movement versus rest.

**Outer layer / shell:** This is critical. You need a fully waterproof, breathable hardshell — both jacket and pants. The jacket should have a hood that cinches tightly and accommodates a headlamp. Pit zips are essential because you'll be working hard on the uphills and overheating inside waterproof layers while simultaneously being rained on. The pants should have full side zips so you can put them on over boots in a hurry when sleet arrives. Gore-Tex Pro or equivalent three-layer construction. Seam-sealed. Test it before you go by standing under a shower for ten minutes. If you feel moisture anywhere, fix it or replace it.

**Hands:** Two pairs of gloves. A lightweight, dexterous pair for general travel — something with grip texture on the palms, because you will be reaching down to feel the surface, testing the wood, stabilizing yourself near pool edges. And a heavier insulated pair, waterproof, for rest stops and sleet events. Consider lobster-claw mitts for the heavy pair — they're warmer than five-finger designs and you don't need fine dexterity while sheltering.

**Feet:** This deserves its own section. Your boots are your most important piece of equipment. They must be:

- **Waterproof.** Not water-resistant. Waterproof. You're walking on a surface that is perpetually damp, stepping over rime ice, skirting pools of standing water. Your feet will be in contact with moisture every step.
- **Stiff-soled but not rigid.** You need enough stiffness to protect against the uneven wood grain pressing into your feet over hours of walking, but enough flex to feel the surface contours. A mountaineering boot is too stiff. A trail runner is too flexible and won't keep water out long enough. A leather or synthetic hiking boot with a moderately stiff Vibram sole and full gore lining is ideal. Something like a Zamberlan 996 Vioz or La Sportiva Nucleo High — boots designed for cold, wet mountain terrain.
- **High-ankle.** The surface is uneven. Branches merge at odd angles beneath the moss. There are dips and rises. You need ankle support.
- **Resoleable or very fresh.** Grip is life here. The moss-over-wood surface will be slippery, especially where frost has formed. Make sure your lugs are deep and your rubber compound is soft enough to grip in cold conditions. Some boot soles harden in cold and lose grip. Test yours by putting them in a freezer overnight and then standing on a wet tile floor. If you slide, get different boots.

**Socks:** Merino wool, medium cushion, two pairs worn in rotation. Some people prefer a thin liner sock underneath. On the Canopy Plateau, I'd suggest it — the liner wicks moisture away from skin to the outer sock, reducing blister risk on what will be very long walks over very textured terrain.

**Head:** A merino buff for general travel, pulled up over the nose and mouth when the air is particularly cold and thin — this also pre-warms inspired air, which helps at altitude. A warmer insulated hat for rest stops. The shell hood over everything in precipitation.

## Gear: Shelter

You *could* use the existing structures — the small moss-chinked domes described in the text. I would advise against it, at least initially, for several reasons. First, you don't know the social significance of these structures to the canopy's interior inhabitants. Using their shelters without understanding the protocol is, at best, rude and, at worst, a violation of some boundary you can't perceive. Second, the structures are old and of unknown structural integrity. Third, and most practically, they're positioned at intervals of "every few hours of walking," which means you can't count on reaching one when you need to stop.

**Bring a bivy system.** Not a tent — a tent requires stakes, and you cannot stake into a living canopy surface. Even if the wood is soft enough to accept a stake, you'd be driving metal into a living organism, and given that the interior inhabitants seem to have a relationship with this organism, this seems inadvisable on multiple levels.

A bivy sack — waterproof, breathable, large enough to accommodate you and your sleeping bag — combined with a sleeping pad is the right approach. The bivy goes directly on the moss surface. Look for a spot where the canopy is slightly raised (a gentle hill, indicating strong growth beneath) so water drains away from you rather than pooling. Avoid the depressions and anywhere near the pools.

**Sleeping bag:** Synthetic fill, rated to at least 15°F / -10°C, possibly colder. The same logic as the mid layer — down will fail in this moisture. A synthetic bag that gets damp will still keep you alive. A damp down bag in thin, cold air will give you a very educational night about the early stages of hypothermia. Bring a silk or synthetic liner to add a few degrees and keep the bag's interior cleaner.

**Sleeping pad:** Insulated, inflatable, with an R-value of at least 4.0. You are sleeping on a surface that is alive, damp, and cool. Ground (canopy?) insulation is as important as top insulation. A foam pad as a backup — CCF pads don't puncture, and on an uneven wood surface with hidden splinters and branch stubs, puncture risk for an inflatable is real. Consider bringing both and stacking them.

## Gear: Navigation and Orientation

This is where preparation gets philosophically difficult. There is almost certainly no magnetic north here — or if there is, you have no reason to believe it corresponds to anything useful. GPS is meaningless. The sky offers no stars, no sun, no moon — only uniform overcast. The terrain shifts over weeks, so even landmarks are temporary. The light is too dim for visual navigation beyond twenty feet.

**You must make peace with a different relationship to navigation than you're accustomed to.**

That said, practical tools:

- **A compass** — bring one anyway. If there is a magnetic field, knowing its orientation relative to your travel gives you *something*. Even if north is arbitrary, consistent north is better than no directional reference.
- **Dead reckoning supplies.** A pace counter (ranger beads), a waterproof notebook, a reliable pencil (not pen — pens fail in cold and damp). Mark your paces and direction. Build a rough map as you travel. Note the positions of pools, structures, hills. This map will be wrong in a month, but it will be useful for days.
- **Cord and small flags or reflective tabs.** If you establish a base camp (your bivy location), mark the route back to it. Tie a small reflective tab to a branch stub every hundred meters or so. The phosphorescent moss will faintly illuminate reflective material, making your markers visible at greater distance than unlit objects. This is your lifeline back to your shelter.
- **A sense of the wind.** The text mentions a prevailing wind direction and weather coming from the west. Pay attention to this. The wind may be the most reliable orienting feature you have. Carry a small wind indicator — even a thread tied to a stick — and learn the local wind patterns within your first day. If the wind consistently comes from one direction, that's your compass.

## Gear: Light

**This is the most consequential gear decision you'll make, and it requires restraint.**

Your instinct will be to bring the most powerful headlamp you can find and blast the darkness. Resist this. The ecosystem here is adapted to near-total darkness. The fauna echolocates. The amphibians navigate by sound. The moss phosphoresces at a level so faint it's barely perceptible. Introducing a powerful artificial light source into this environment would be the equivalent of setting off a flashbang in a library — disorienting, disruptive, and likely to provoke reactions you can't predict.

The clickers — the echolocating predators — may not use vision primarily, but you don't know that they're blind. A sudden bright light might startle them, and startled predators are dangerous predators. The interior inhabitants, whatever they are, have clearly adapted to this darkness. If you are walking across their ceiling shining a 1000-lumen beam, you are announcing yourself in the most aggressive possible way.

**Bring light, but use it gently.**

- **A headlamp with a red-light mode and a very low white-light mode (5-10 lumens).** Red light preserves night vision, is less visible at distance, and is less likely to disturb photosensitive organisms. The low white mode is for close examination of objects — the carved marks in the structures, the stone tools, the surface texture when you need to assess footing near a pool edge.
- **A backup headlamp**, same specs. Redundancy in your only light source is not optional.
- **Spare batteries** — many of them. Lithium batteries, which perform better in cold. Store them inside your clothing, against your body, so they stay warm. Cold batteries deliver less power. Label your fresh and spent batteries differently (tape systems work) so you don't fumble in the dark trying to figure out which ones are good.
- **A small lantern with a diffuser** for camp use — something that casts a very low, warm glow inside your bivy. A candle lantern would work beautifully and doesn't require batteries, but fire on a living wood surface is an ethical and practical concern of the highest order. Use an LED lantern on its lowest setting instead.
- **Ideally, train yourself to operate without light.** Spend time before your trip in total darkness. Learn to set up your bivy by feel. Learn to eat, drink, manage your gear, and find your way back from a latrine stop using touch and sound alone. Your time on the Canopy Plateau will be immeasurably richer — and safer — if you can function confidently in darkness, using light as a specific tool rather than a default crutch.

## Gear: Sound Management and Awareness

This environment communicates through sound. The wood groans carry tectonic information. The clicking conveys predator presence and movement. The singing tells you where the pools are and where life is active. The tapping from below is... something you need to be aware of.

**Protect your hearing, but keep it open.**

- **Do not bring earplugs** for general use. You need your ears here more than your eyes. The clicking of the predators is your early warning system. The change in wood-groan frequency or intensity might tell you about imminent surface shifts. The singing's absence might tell you something has gone wrong.
- **Consider a hearing amplification device** — a simple electronic ear designed for hunting, the kind that amplifies ambient sound while cutting sudden loud noises. This would let you hear the faint tapping, the distant clicking, the subtle sounds that the background noise might otherwise mask. It would also protect you from the closer, louder bark-splitting sounds that the text describes as sharp as gunshots.
- **Learn to listen directionally.** Cup your hands behind your ears. Tilt your head. In an environment where vision is limited to twenty feet, your ability to locate, identify, and range sounds will determine your spatial awareness. Practice this at home — go outside at night, close your eyes, and try to identify and locate every sound you hear. Do this for an hour. You'll be surprised how much your auditory acuity improves with conscious attention.

**Your own sound profile matters.**

You are walking on a surface that transmits vibration. The interior inhabitants communicate through the wood via tapping. This means every footstep you take is potentially audible to beings inside the canopy. You are drumming on their ceiling with your boots.

- Walk softly. Place your feet deliberately. Avoid stomping, dragging, or scuffing.
- Consider the weight and hardness of your boot soles. A softer rubber compound will transmit less sharp impact sound than a hard one.
- Be aware that setting down heavy gear — dropping a pack, for instance — will create a percussive impact that travels through the wood.
- **Do not tap on the surface.** I know the temptation will be enormous. You hear patterned tapping from below. Your curiosity demands you tap back. Do not do this until you have spent significant time listening and have developed at least a rudimentary understanding of the patterns. An ill-considered tap might be meaningless noise, or it might be an obscenity, a territorial claim, a declaration of war, or an invitation you're not prepared to accept. Sound is language here. Do not speak until you have listened.

## Gear: Food and Water

**Water:** You are surrounded by it. The pools, the fog, the rain, the frost — water is abundant. The question is safety. You're on a living organism. The water that collects in canopy pools has been in contact with bark, moss, resins, and the amphibians that live in the pools. Any or all of these might introduce substances your body can't handle.

- Bring a reliable water filter — a pump or gravity filter rated for bacteria and protozoa at minimum. A Sawyer Squeeze or similar hollow-fiber filter is light, reliable, and effective.
- Bring chemical treatment as backup — chlorine dioxide tablets (Aquamira or similar). They're effective against a broader spectrum of organisms than mechanical filters and weigh nothing.
- **Consider boiling as primary treatment** if you can manage a heat source safely, because it is the most universally effective method and does not depend on the specific biology of this world conforming to what your filter was designed for.
- Collect water from rain or fog when possible rather than from the pools. A simple tarp or collection surface will catch rain during precipitation events. Fog collection — a fine mesh strung in the air — can produce surprising quantities of water in the consistently overcast, foggy conditions described.
- Do not drink from pools where the amphibians are singing. They breathe and presumably excrete through their skin. The water they inhabit is essentially a biological broth.

**Food:** There is no indication that anything on the Canopy Plateau is safe for human consumption. The moss may contain whatever compounds allow it to phosphoresce. The insects are slow-moving and available but entirely unknown biochemically. The amphibians have translucent skin and dark blood — this suggests unusual physiology. Do not eat anything you find here unless you have the capacity to perform biochemical analysis on it and understand the results.

**Bring all your food.** Calorie-dense, cold-tolerant, moisture-resistant. The cold means you'll burn calories faster. The altitude means your appetite may be suppressed even as your caloric need increases. The dampness means anything that can absorb water and spoil will.

- Nut butters in squeeze packets
- Hard cheeses (wax-coated, moisture resistant)
- Dried meats and jerky
- High-calorie bars (test several brands in cold conditions before your trip — some become brick-hard below 40°F and are nearly inedible)
- Chocolate (dark, high cocoa content — resists moisture better than milk chocolate)
- Dehydrated meals IF you have a reliable way to heat water (see below)
- Hardtack or dense crackers in waterproof packaging
- Powdered drink mixes for electrolytes — the cold air and altitude will dehydrate you faster than you realize, and plain water won't replenish electrolytes lost through exertion

Plan for at least 3,500–4,000 calories per day. In cold, at altitude, under exertion, this is the minimum to maintain body function and warmth.

## Gear: Heat Source

**This requires extreme care.**

You are standing on a living organism made of wood. Fire is the obvious tool for warmth, water heating, and morale, and fire is profoundly dangerous here. Even if the surface wood is damp and resistant to ignition, which it may be, the resins in the wood are explicitly described as unusual ("full of resins that don't freeze") and might be flammable in ways you don't expect. A single errant spark on a resin-rich surface in the wrong conditions could be catastrophic.

**Primary recommendation: A liquid fuel or canister stove with extreme precautions.**

- Use a canister stove (isobutane/propane blend) rather than a liquid fuel stove — less risk of spilling fuel on the surface. Note that canister stoves perform poorly in cold; invert-compatible canisters or a remote-canister design that lets you warm the canister in water helps.
- **Always use a stove board** — a small, rigid, fire-resistant platform (a piece of titanium sheet metal or a purpose-built ceramic tile) between the stove and the canopy surface. This is non-negotiable.
- Cook in still conditions only. The brief rain and sleet events suggest variable wind; cook during calm periods.
- Keep a liter of water within arm's reach while cooking, designated for fire suppression, not drinking.
- Minimize cooking. Use the stove for heating water, not for extended meal preparation. Heat water, pour it into an insulated bottle, turn off the stove, and prepare your food away from the stove site.
- An insulated mug and a wide-mouth insulated bottle become critical pieces of gear — hot liquid is warmth, morale, and hydration in one.

**If the fire risk assessment feels too high, go stoveless.** It is possible to survive on the Canopy Plateau without hot food or drink. It is miserable, but possible. Everything in your food plan above can be eaten cold. You won't enjoy it. But you also won't burn down a living continent.

## Gear: Communication and Recording

**Journal.** Waterproof paper (Rite in the Rain or equivalent) and multiple pencils. Document everything. The sounds, their timing, their direction. The structures, their construction, their contents. The behavior of the clickers, the singers, the insects. The patterns in the tapping. Sketch the structures. Sketch the framing devices at the pool edges. Sketch the carved marks in the wood, as accurately as you can, because the tree is growing scar tissue around them and they are slowly disappearing.

**Audio recording device.** This environment is defined by its soundscape. A high-quality portable field recorder with external microphones — something like a Zoom H5 with a pair of matched condenser mics — would capture what your journal cannot. Record the wood groans. Record the singing. Record the clicking. Record the tapping. Keep the recorder in a waterproof case with silica gel packets and only extract it for recording sessions, because the moisture will kill electronics quickly.

Protect all electronics: waterproof cases, dry bags, desiccant packets, and keep batteries warm against your body.

**Camera.** A low-light capable camera is worth bringing, though the conditions will test any sensor's limits. The phosphorescent moss provides *some* light. A full-frame mirrorless camera with a fast prime lens (f/1.4 or faster) and high ISO capability could capture the eerie glow of the moss-surface, the shapes of the structures, the frost patterns. Use a small, flexible tripod or a beanbag for stabilization — long exposures will be necessary. Flash is inadvisable for the same reasons bright headlamps are inadvisable, but a single controlled flash on a structure you're documenting, used sparingly, is probably acceptable.

## Psychological Preparation

This is not an afterthought. This is arguably the most important section.

**The darkness will affect you.** Humans are visual creatures. We process the majority of our environmental information through sight. On the Canopy Plateau, that channel is reduced to a twenty-foot radius of dim phosphorescent haze. Everything beyond that is sound and inference. For the first several hours this will be exciting and novel. By the end of the first day it will be oppressive. By the second day your brain will begin constructing visual hallucinations to fill the void — shadows that move, shapes at the edge of perception, faces in the moss texture. This is normal. This is your visual cortex trying to do its job with insufficient data. Acknowledge it, note it, and do not trust your eyes when they tell you they've seen something that your ears did not confirm.

**The openness will affect you differently than you expect.** The text describes the canopy plateau as empty in the way a cathedral is empty when the organ is playing. This is the critical insight. You will feel exposed. There are no walls, no trees (above the surface), no features to provide psychological shelter. You are standing on an open plain in the dark, and the darkness is full of sound. This is agoraphobia and claustrophobia simultaneously — the space is too big and too close at the same time. Practice spending time alone in large, dark, open spaces before you go. An empty field at night, away from light pollution, is a reasonable simulation.

**The living ground will disturb you on a primal level.** The surface shifts. It flexes. It groans. There is no stable ground here — only a surface that has decided, for now, to hold still enough for you to walk on. This violates something deep in the human psyche about the reliability of the earth beneath your feet. You may experience a persistent, low-grade anxiety that you can't attribute to any specific threat. This is your body recognizing that the ground is alive and therefore unpredictable. Acknowledge this feeling. It is rational. It is also manageable.

**The loneliness is profound and particular.** You know there are intelligences here — the builders, the interior inhabitants. You hear their tapping. You see their structures. But you cannot see *them*, cannot communicate with them, cannot share the experience with anyone. You are a guest in a house where the hosts live in the walls. This is isolating in a way that's distinct from simple solitude. In simple solitude you are alone. Here, you are alone *in the company of others you cannot reach.* This is harder.

- Bring something personal. A photograph, a small object, a letter. Something that connects you to the world you came from, not because you'll forget, but because it helps to have a physical anchor when the environment becomes psychologically overwhelming.
- Set a routine. Wake, eat, walk, observe, record, eat, shelter, sleep. The routine imposes structure on an environment that offers none.
- Talk to yourself. Out loud, in a normal voice. Not constantly, not performatively, but regularly — narrate what you're doing, what you're observing. This keeps you grounded in your own cognition and prevents the drift into passive, hypnotic absorption that the soundscape can induce. The rhythmic singing, the groaning, the clicking — these sounds have a quality that can lull you into a dissociative state if you're not careful. Your own voice is a tether.

## Ethical Considerations

You are a guest. In every sense, in every way, you are a guest here.

**Do not damage the canopy surface.** Do not cut wood, do not strip moss, do not carve marks. The interior inhabitants will perceive your impact on the organism they inhabit. You are walking on the roof of someone's home.

**Do not take anything from the structures.** The stone tools, the knotted cords, the suspended objects — these are not artifacts for your collection. They are someone's possessions, or offerings, or functional instruments. Document them thoroughly and leave them exactly as you found them.

**Do not interfere with the ecology.** Do not feed the animals, do not harass the clickers, do not approach the singing amphibians at the pools. Do not shine light into the pools. Do not drop anything into them.

**Manage your waste scrupulously.** Pack out everything — all food waste, all packaging, all human waste. A WAG bag system (waste alleviation and gelling) is appropriate for solid waste. Urinate onto moss well away from pools and well away from structures, in areas where rainwater will dilute it quickly. Ideally, urinate into a container and pack it out, but this is difficult on extended trips. At minimum, be thoughtful about it. You are urinating on a living organism.

**If you encounter the builders — if they emerge, if they approach, if one of the tapping patterns resolves into something clearly directed at you — be still.** Do not advance. Do not retreat hastily. Do not speak loudly. Sit down, if you can, to reduce your profile. Make yourself small and quiet and wait. Let them determine the terms of contact. You are the alien here. You are the unexplained thing on their ceiling. Give them the agency to decide what to do about you.

## Duration and Extraction Plan

**Do not plan a long first visit.** Three to five days is appropriate. This is enough time to acclimate to the altitude and darkness, to walk a meaningful distance, to encounter several structures, to observe the fauna, to begin understanding the soundscape. It is short enough that your food supply remains manageable, your psychological resilience holds, and you haven't yet begun to take the strangeness for granted (which is when mistakes happen).

**Have a clear extraction plan with a fixed time.** Know exactly when and how you are leaving. Mark your entry point with something you can find again — reflective markers, a distinctive gear cache, whatever your transit method requires. Do not depend on being able to navigate back to a specific point by landmark alone. The terrain shifts. What was a small hill near your entry point may have subsided by the time you return.

**Emergency extraction protocols.** If you become injured, hypothermic, or disoriented beyond recovery, you need a way out that doesn't depend on walking. Whatever multiverse transit mechanism you use, ensure it can be activated from any point on the canopy surface, not just a fixed location. If it requires a fixed location, stay close enough to that location throughout your trip to reach it while injured.

## A Final Note on Mindset

The Canopy Plateau will try to become a mystery to be solved. You will want to decode the tapping. You will want to understand the framing structures at the pool edges. You will want to know what makes the booming sound in the distance. You will want to find the builders.

Resist the urgency, though not the curiosity. This place operates on a timescale that dwarfs human attention spans. The canopy locked together over centuries. The carved marks are being digested over decades. The growth-groans announce shifts that take years to complete. You are visiting a world whose clock runs slow and whose patience is geological. You will not decode it in three days. You will barely perceive it in three days.

Go to observe, not to solve. Go to listen, not to answer. Walk softly on the living wood and be grateful that it holds your weight, because it doesn't have to, and one day — in its own time, on its own terms — it may decide not to.

Bring good boots. Bring warm layers. Bring humility in larger quantities than either.

And when you hear the deep boom from far away, the one that shakes the canopy beneath your feet — when you feel that vibration in your bones and some part of you wants to walk toward it to find the source — remember that not everything in the multiverse is meant to be found by you, and walk the other way.
