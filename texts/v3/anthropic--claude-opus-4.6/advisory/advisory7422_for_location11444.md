# Preparing for the Underloom: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before addressing gear lists and survival protocols, you need to sit with something uncomfortable: the Underloom is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is *complete*. You are entering a system that has never needed you, never anticipated you, and has achieved a state of equilibrium so total that your mere biological presence constitutes an intrusion at the thermodynamic level. Every breath you take introduces moisture into an environment that has essentially none. Every step you take on the sharp young stone breaks edges that have not been broken since they formed. Every calorie of heat your body radiates adds to a convection system balanced to tolerances you cannot perceive.

This is not a place you conquer, escape from, or even really explore in the traditional sense. It is a place you *endure*—and more importantly, a place that endures *you*. Your preparation must account for both sides of that equation.

The psychological dimension of this cannot be overstated and will be addressed at length. But first, the practical.

---

## II. Respiratory Protection: The Single Most Critical System

### The Problem

The air in the Underloom is saturated with particulate mineral dust—powdered silicate and calcium carbonate fine enough to behave like humidity. This is not like breathing in a dusty workshop or even a sandstorm. The particles are at the scale where they penetrate deep into alveolar tissue and do not come back out. Silicosis—the scarring of lung tissue by inhaled crystalline silica—is one of the most agonizing and irreversible occupational diseases in human medicine, and the Underloom is essentially an infinite silicosis chamber held at comfortable temperature.

You will be breathing this air constantly. There is no wind to create pockets of clarity. There are no bodies of water generating cleaner microenvironments. The particulate concentration is uniform, pervasive, and eternal. Short-term exposure—hours—might produce only irritation: a dry cough, a gritty feeling in the throat, eyes that sting and water. Medium-term exposure—days—risks the beginning of inflammatory response in the lungs. Long-term exposure—weeks or more—risks permanent, progressive fibrotic lung disease.

Your eyes are also at risk. The particulate will coat corneas, irritate conjunctivae, and work its way under contact lenses if you wear them. The near-darkness means your pupils will be maximally dilated at all times, increasing the surface area exposed.

### The Solution

**Full-face respirator with P100/P3 particulate filters, minimum.** Not a half-mask. Not a bandana. Not a surgical mask. A full-face unit that seals around the entire face, protecting both lungs and eyes simultaneously. The visor should be anti-fog treated, because while the environment itself has almost no moisture, your breath contains plenty, and in a sealed mask fogging becomes a serious visibility problem—and visibility is already your scarcest resource.

Bring **a minimum of three complete filter sets per week of planned stay**, and bring more than you think you'll need. In an environment of continuous particulate loading, filters will reach capacity faster than manufacturer ratings suggest, because those ratings assume intermittent exposure with rest periods in clean air. You will have no rest periods in clean air. When a filter begins to feel restrictive—when your breathing effort increases even slightly—change it immediately. Do not ration.

**Consider a powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR)** if you can manage the power supply. A PAPR uses a battery-driven blower to push filtered air into the facepiece, reducing breathing resistance and maintaining positive pressure inside the mask, which means even small seal failures result in air flowing *out* rather than dust flowing *in*. The comfort advantage over extended wear is enormous. The downside is noise—the blower hum, quiet by surface standards, will be deafening by Underloom standards and will obliterate your ability to hear the subtle soundscape of the crystal-fauna. This is a serious trade-off and will be discussed further.

If you anticipate a truly extended stay—months—you may need to investigate **closed-circuit or supplied-air breathing systems**, but the logistics of carrying sufficient compressed air or oxygen into a space with no resupply are prohibitive. The particulate filtration approach remains the most viable. Just bring absurd quantities of filters.

**Backup:** Carry several high-quality N99 or FFP3 disposable masks as emergency backups in case your primary respirator is damaged. These are not adequate for sustained use but will keep you alive while you effect repairs.

### Eye Protection Addendum

If you use a half-mask respirator instead of full-face (not recommended), you need **sealed safety goggles**—not glasses, not open-vented goggles, but fully sealed units with indirect ventilation at most. The particulate is fine enough to enter through any gap. Anti-fog treatment is essential. Carry lens cleaning supplies—a soft brush, lens wipes, a squeeze bulb for blowing dust off surfaces before wiping. Wiping a silicate-dusted lens without first removing loose particles will scratch it permanently, and scratched lenses in near-darkness are functionally useless.

## III. Foot and Body Protection: Navigating the Sharp Young Stone

### The Floor

The Underloom's geological youth is your enemy. The description is precise: ridges like filed teeth, shelves terminating in clean right angles, edges precise enough to cut, obsidian flows with surfaces that amount to natural glass. This is not rough terrain in the way a mountain trail is rough. This is terrain that is *sharp*—actively, geometrically, crystallographically sharp. Fresh fracture surfaces in basalt and obsidian can have edges measured in microns. Obsidian, in particular, can be sharper than surgical steel. You will be walking on natural scalpels.

### Footwear

**Heavy-duty mountaineering boots with rigid soles and full ankle support.** The soles need to be thick enough and stiff enough that a pointed crystal formation cannot puncture through to your foot. Flexible-soled hiking boots that allow you to feel the terrain are exactly wrong here—you do *not* want to feel this terrain. You want maximum separation between your feet and the stone.

Consider boots with **steel or composite toe caps and puncture-resistant midsoles** (the kind rated for industrial construction sites, with steel or Kevlar plates embedded in the sole). These exist in hybrid mountaineering-industrial designs, and this is the rare environment that demands exactly that hybrid.

**Gaiters**—full lower-leg gaiters made of heavy, abrasion-resistant material—to protect your shins and calves from the sharp ridges and edges you will inevitably brush against. The terraced terrain means constant stepping up and down across ledges, and the leading edges of those ledges will slice through fabric and skin with equal ease.

**Bring at least two pairs of boots.** In an environment this abrasive, sole material will wear and be scored. A slashed sole that admits fine silicate dust against your foot is an infection risk and a misery.

### Gloves

You will be climbing, scrambling, bracing yourself against stone constantly. **Heavy leather or synthetic work gloves with reinforced palms**, at minimum. Consider gloves with cut-resistant lining (ANSI cut level A4 or higher). You will need enough dexterity in your fingers to operate equipment, adjust your respirator, handle food—so full gauntlet-style rigidity is impractical. Find the best compromise between protection and dexterity you can. Bring multiple pairs; the stone will eat through them.

### Body Covering

**Long sleeves, long pants, heavy weave.** Ripstop nylon or canvas. Nothing thin, nothing that drapes loosely enough to catch on projections. The crystal millipedes navigate this terrain with dozens of precision-placed legs; you have two clumsy ones and will be falling, bumping, and scraping against every surface. Cover all skin. The mineral dust will also irritate bare skin over time—not dangerously, but persistently, a fine grinding abrasion that turns into raw patches.

A **light but tough coverall** worn over base layers is a reasonable approach. Something you can replace or patch as it gets shredded.

### Knee and Elbow Pads

**Yes, absolutely.** You will be crawling into the builders' stone arrangements if you want to examine them—those triangular recesses barely large enough to crouch in. You will be kneeling to examine the Loom-veins, the hand-shaped creatures, the transport paths of the crystal millipedes. Every surface you kneel on is a cheese grater. Hard-shell knee pads and elbow pads, the kind used by tile setters or tactical operators, are not optional equipment.

## IV. Light and Vision

### The Dilemma

This is one of the most consequential decisions in your preparation, and it does not have a clean answer.

The Underloom exists in near-total darkness. The phosphorescent Loom-veins provide enough light to see perhaps ten meters in any direction, in dull reds and faded ambers—a glow that "barely qualifies as light." Your eyes will be straining constantly. The temptation to bring powerful artificial lighting will be overwhelming.

**Resist that temptation.**

Or rather: bring the lighting, but understand exactly what deploying it means. You are entering an ecosystem that has never experienced light beyond the phosphorescent background. Every organism in the Underloom—from the pentagonal hand-shapes to the crystal millipedes to the builders to whatever the deep ones are—has adapted to conditions of near-darkness across geological timescales. Many of them are translucent or crystalline, which means they may refract, scatter, or react to light frequencies outside their normal spectrum in ways that are completely unpredictable.

More importantly: **you are already the most disruptive thing that has ever happened here.** The silence has already grown fractionally quieter in your presence. Every additional disturbance you introduce widens the gap between you and any possibility of observing this place as it actually is. A bright light in the Underloom would be the equivalent of a bomb going off in a library. You would see more—and understand less.

### The Approach

**Dim red or deep amber light sources only, and use them sparingly.** Red light is closest to the existing phosphorescent spectrum of the Loom-veins—dull reds and faded ambers—and will cause the least spectral disruption to the environment. Modern LED headlamps with red-light modes and adjustable dimming are ideal. Set them to the absolute minimum brightness that allows you to navigate safely. Your goal is to augment the existing illumination, not to replace it.

**Night vision equipment—specifically, image-intensification (Gen 3 or Gen 4) monocular or binoculars—is potentially the single most valuable tool you can bring.** These devices amplify existing light. The Loom-veins provide just enough photons for a good image intensifier to work with, and the result would be the ability to see the Underloom in something approaching clarity without introducing a single additional photon into the environment. The image will be rendered in green monochrome, which will strip the native amber-red coloration, so bring a separate set of purpose-built low-light optics with enhanced red-spectrum sensitivity if such a thing exists in your home multiverse's technology. If not, the standard Gen 3 night vision is still transformative.

**Thermal imaging** will be of limited utility. The environment is in near-perfect thermal equilibrium—everything is at roughly the same temperature—so thermal contrast will be minimal. The mineral-fauna may or may not produce metabolic heat; the description suggests their energy processes are exotic enough that thermal signatures are uncertain. Bring a thermal monocular as a backup tool, but don't rely on it.

**Bring backup chemical light sources (glow sticks)** in red or amber wavelengths. These are low-intensity, require no batteries, and can serve as trail markers or emergency lighting if electronic systems fail. The mineral dust may coat and dim them over time.

**Do not, under any circumstances, use a camera flash, a white-light floodlight, or any high-intensity burst illumination** unless you are in genuine mortal danger and need to disorient or reveal a threat. The effect on the ecosystem would be severe and unpredictable, and you would likely damage any possibility of further observation for a considerable radius around your position.

### Adapting Your Eyes

**Spend time in darkness before you arrive, if possible.** Full dark adaptation of the human eye takes 30-45 minutes, but deep adaptation—the kind where you begin to perceive photon-level light—can take hours and improves over days of consistent low-light living. The more you can pre-adapt, the more you will see upon arrival. Once in the Underloom, avoid any exposure to bright light, as even a brief flash will reset your adaptation and cost you hours of recovery.

Consider **prescription adaptation lenses** if they exist in your home technology—lenses that enhance sensitivity in the red-amber spectrum while filtering other wavelengths, essentially tuning your vision to the Underloom's native illumination.

## V. Hydration and Nutrition

### Water

There is no water in the Underloom. None that flows. None that pools. The air is dry—particulate-laden but not humid. Your body will lose water through respiration (especially through a respirator, which may or may not recapture exhaled moisture depending on model), through perspiration (minimal in the stable warmth, but nonzero), and through the simple metabolic processes of being alive.

**You must carry every drop of water you will need.**

This is your single greatest logistical constraint and the hard limiter on your stay duration. Water weighs one kilogram per liter. A human in moderate activity in warm, dry conditions needs 3-4 liters per day minimum. A week requires 21-28 liters, or 21-28 kilograms of water weight alone. Two weeks doubles it. A month is logistically prohibitive without caching, resupply, or extraordinary transport capacity.

**Strategies for extension:**

- **Closed-loop hydration recovery.** If your technology allows it, a personal water recovery system that captures exhaled moisture, perspiration, and urine and purifies it for re-drinking can dramatically extend your water supply. These range from crude survival stills to sophisticated closed-loop systems depending on your home multiverse's technology level. Even a crude system that recovers 50% of your losses doubles your effective supply.

- **Advance caching.** If you can make multiple trips to the Underloom (or if your point of entry is stable), cache water supplies at intervals along planned routes. Use sealed, impact-resistant containers—the terrain will puncture anything flimsy. Mark caches with chemical light sticks or reflective tape visible under night vision.

- **Mineral analysis before drinking anything local.** There is *almost* no water here—but "almost" is not "none." If you find moisture—condensation in deep crevices, mineral-bound water in hydrated crystite formations—test it thoroughly before consumption. In an environment saturated with silicate and calcium carbonate dust, any liquid water will be heavily mineral-laden and potentially toxic.

### Food

There is nothing edible here by any human standard. The biology of the Underloom is mineral-crystalline—silicon-based or mineral-lattice-based life that derives energy from phosphorescent ore through processes unknown to terrestrial biochemistry. You cannot eat the hand-shapes. You cannot eat the crystal millipedes. (You probably should not touch them either, but that's addressed later.) Even if something here were theoretically digestible, the risk of introducing novel mineral compounds into your gut—compounds that have had geological ages to develop exotic chemistry—is unacceptable.

**Carry all food.** High-calorie, low-weight, low-volume, minimal-preparation. The standard multiverse traveler's pantry applies: calorie-dense bars, freeze-dried meals (if you have water to spare, which you may not), nut butters, dried meats, high-fat provisions. Minimize anything that requires cooking—you have no fuel source here, and introducing an open flame or combustion byproducts into the environment raises the same disruption concerns as bright light, plus potential interaction with the mineral-dust atmosphere. **The dust may or may not be combustible in sufficient concentration.** Calcium carbonate is not, but fine silicate dust in the right conditions can behave as an explosive particulate. Do not find out.

If you must heat food, use **electric heating elements powered by your battery supply**, contained inside your shelter with as much isolation from the surrounding atmosphere as you can manage.

**Eat inside your shelter with your respirator temporarily removed** (after allowing dust to settle—see shelter section). Do not eat in the open. The fine particulate will coat your food instantly, and ingesting silicate dust is not lethal in small quantities but will cause gastrointestinal distress and long-term health complications.

## VI. Shelter and Rest

### Why You Need a Shelter Even Though the Temperature Is Comfortable

The Underloom's temperature "hovers at the edge of comfort." You will not freeze. You will not overheat. The temptation is to sleep in the open, on a flat terrace, wrapped in a sleeping pad, since the thermal environment is benign. 

Do not do this.

You need a shelter for three reasons:

1. **Dust exclusion during sleep.** You cannot wear your respirator 24 hours a day for the duration of your stay—the seal pressure, the breathing resistance, the psychological burden of constant face coverage in an already claustrophobic environment will erode your functionality faster than the dust will erode your lungs. You need periods where you can breathe unfiltered air, and that means you need a volume of filtered air. Even a small, sealed shelter with a filtered intake will give your lungs and face hours of relief.

2. **Psychological containment.** The Underloom is infinite. Not literally, but functionally—it extends beyond perception in all directions, with no landmarks, no horizon, no sky. The human psyche requires boundaries to rest. A shelter creates a microenvironment with walls, with edges, with an inside and an outside. This is not luxury. This is sanity maintenance.

3. **Separation from the fauna.** The hand-shaped creatures cover every surface in layers three and four deep. They will colonize you if you remain still long enough. Whether this is dangerous is unknown, but waking up covered in pentagonal crystal organisms that have mistaken you for a warm stone surface and begun their lithotrophic communion process against your skin is not a scenario you want to experience without preparation.

### Shelter Design

**A small, sealed, single-person bivouac tent with a filtered air intake system.** The tent material must be heavy-duty—the floor surface will destroy anything lightweight, so you need either a reinforced floor or a separate ground sheet of puncture-resistant material (ballistic nylon, HDPE sheeting, or similar) between the tent and the stone. The tent does not need to be weatherproof in the traditional sense—there is no rain, no wind, no UV. It needs to be **dust-proof**, which is actually a harder engineering problem. Every seam, every zipper, every entry point must seal tightly enough to exclude particulate at the scale of mineral dust. Think of it less like a tent and more like a cleanroom enclosure.

**Filtered air intake:** A small battery-powered blower drawing air through a HEPA or P100 filter and maintaining slight positive pressure inside the tent. This way, any gaps or imperfections in the seal result in clean air flowing *out* rather than dusty air flowing *in*. The blower noise is a concern (see psychological section), but a well-chosen quiet fan on low speed will be manageable.

**Alternatively, if your technology permits:** a personal-scale air filtration and positive-pressure system integrated into a hard-shell bivouac pod. Essentially a human-sized sealed container with life support. This is heavier, less portable, but more reliable.

**Place your shelter on the flattest terrace you can find**, away from edges and drop-offs. Clear the surface as best you can of the sharpest projections (this is where the builders' knapping technique becomes relevant—you may need to do some stone-work yourself to create a tolerably flat sleeping platform). Do not place your shelter on or near an exposed Loom-vein. The builders maintain cleared boundaries around these, and you do not want to create a territorial conflict—or attract the attention of hand-shapes seeking their feeding surfaces.

### Sleeping Surface

Even with a ground sheet, you need **a thick, closed-cell foam sleeping pad or inflatable mattress** between you and the stone. The stone is warm, but it is also geometrically hostile. A sleeping pad rated for rough terrain is minimum; something like a folding cot that elevates you entirely off the floor is better if you can carry it.

## VII. Navigation and Orientation

### The Problem

There are no landmarks in the Underloom. The terrain is self-similar: terraces, ridges, columns, crystal formations, Loom-veins—repeating in fractal variation across a continental volume. There is no sun to take bearings from. There is no magnetic field data given—and in an environment of mineral ore veins running through the stone, magnetic compasses may be unreliable or useless. There is no GPS, no satellite, no external reference of any kind. If you walk 500 meters from your entry point and turn around, you may not be able to find your way back.

This is potentially lethal. In a space this large with no water sources, getting lost means dying of dehydration in warm comfortable darkness surrounded by crystal organisms that will patiently wait for your body to become substrate.

### The Solution

**Mark your path aggressively and redundantly.**

- **Chemical light sticks** placed at intervals, in a color not found in the native phosphorescence. Green or blue glow sticks will stand out against the amber-red Loom light. Place them every 20-30 meters, at every turn, at every terrace transition. Bring hundreds. Possibly thousands.

- **Reflective trail markers**—small adhesive reflective tabs or tape—placed on stone surfaces, visible under your red headlamp or night vision. These are lighter and more compact than glow sticks and can supplement them.

- **Inertial navigation system.** If your technology permits, a personal inertial measurement unit (IMU) that tracks your movement through dead reckoning—accelerometers and gyroscopes calculating your position relative to your starting point—is invaluable. These drift over time, but for the distances and durations of an Underloom expedition, a good IMU should keep you within recoverable error margins.

- **String or cord.** The oldest and most reliable navigation method in caves and labyrinths. A spool of high-strength, thin cord (Dyneema or spectra line—strong, light, low-bulk) paid out behind you as you walk, anchored at intervals. To return, follow the line back. Simple, foolproof, limited only by the amount of cord you carry. **Bring at least one kilometer of line, ideally more.** The terrain will snag and abrade it, so use abrasion-resistant material and check it regularly for damage.

- **Surveyor's chalk or paint markers** on stone surfaces. Durable, visible, require no power. Use sparingly—this is a permanent alteration to an environment that has never been altered by outside forces.

### Mapping

If you intend to explore systematically, bring a **ruggedized notebook and drawing tools** for manual mapping (technology may fail; pencil and paper do not). Alternatively, a **LiDAR scanner** or similar spatial mapping technology that can build a three-dimensional map of your surroundings in the near-dark would be extraordinarily useful, as it would allow you to create a navigable map of the terrain and identify your position within it. The range would be limited by the particulate haze—laser scatter in the mineral dust may reduce effective LiDAR range significantly—but even short-range spatial mapping would be better than none.

## VIII. Power Supply

You will be relying on battery-powered equipment for lighting, air filtration, navigation electronics, and potentially heating elements. There are no power sources in the Underloom that you can tap—no sunlight for solar, no wind for turbines, no flowing water for hydro.

**The Loom-veins are a theoretical power source** that you absolutely should not attempt to exploit without extensive study. The hand-shaped creatures derive energy from them through unknown processes. Interfering with this energy source could disrupt the ecosystem, damage the veins, or expose you to unknown radiation or energy forms. Leave them alone.

**Bring lithium battery packs in the largest capacity you can carry.** Prioritize power for: (1) air filtration in your shelter, (2) respirator blower if using a PAPR, (3) navigation electronics, (4) lighting, in that order. Carry backup batteries for every critical system. Bring a **hand-crank or pedal generator** as a last-resort backup—it's heavy, but it converts your caloric intake into electricity, which is the only renewable power conversion available to you here.

Calculate your total power budget before departure. Sum the watt-hour requirements of every device, multiply by your planned stay duration plus 50% margin, and bring that much battery capacity. Then bring more.

## IX. Interaction with the Fauna

### General Principles

**Do not touch the crystal-fauna unless you have a specific scientific reason and have taken precautions.** Their bodies are crystalline silicate—meaning they are, at the surface level, made of sharp mineral. Handling them ungloved risks cuts. Handling them roughly risks damaging creatures that grow "atom by atom, face by face" and may not regenerate on any timescale meaningful to you.

More importantly: you do not know what their defensive responses are. They are described as holding very, very still—a communal hush. This may be their baseline. It may also be a threat response triggered by your presence. Organisms that hold still when disturbed often have a second behavioral mode triggered by direct contact, and in creatures made of crystalline mineral, that second mode could involve sharp edges moving quickly.

### The Hand-Shapes

These are the most common fauna and the ones you will interact with most—passively, by proximity. They cover every surface. They will be on the stone you walk on, the columns you brace against, the ledges you sit on. Displacing them is unavoidable. Do so gently and minimally. When you clear a sleeping area or a path, move them to adjacent surfaces rather than dropping them or tossing them. Watch for their response to displacement—do they return? Do they signal? Does the surrounding population react?

**They cluster thickest on exposed Loom-veins.** Stay away from these clusters. They are feeding. Interrupting lithotrophic communion may or may not harm the organism, and you gain nothing from it.

### The Crystal Millipedes

These are large enough to be physically significant—a meter long, made of crystal, with dozens of pointed legs that navigate razor-sharp terrain with precision. They follow established paths. **Learn these paths and do not block them.** A crystal millipede that has carried a mica flake for an hour across twelve meters and finds its path obstructed by a human bivouac tent is a scenario with unpredictable outcomes.

More practically: their paths are grooves worn into the stone—the only erosion in this world. These grooves are subtle but findable. They represent the closest thing to "roads" the Underloom has, and they can serve as supplementary navigation aids.

**Do not handle the millipedes.** A meter-long crystalline organism with dozens of sharp legs has enormous potential to lacerate, and their apparent docility is not evidence of harmlessness.

### The Builders

These are the most extraordinary inhabitants—bipedal (or occasionally bipedal), tool-using, shelter-building mineral-organic beings. They are also the most rarely seen and likely the most aware of you.

**Approach with extreme patience and deference.** They have retreated from you. Their silence has deepened. They are watching and you are being assessed. Do not approach their shelters. Do not disturb their uncovered Loom-veins. Do not pick up their knapped obsidian tools. Leave offerings if you want to attempt contact, but be thoughtful about what you offer—bringing biological material (food, organic matter) may be meaningless or repulsive to mineral-based beings. A more appropriate offering might be a well-knapped stone tool of your own, or a mineral sample from your home world, placed at a respectful distance from a shelter and then left alone.

**Document everything you can without intruding.** Night vision observation from a distance. Audio recording of any sounds they make (bring a high-sensitivity microphone capable of capturing sounds at the threshold of perception—the clicking-ticking of the ecosystem, the builders' activities, the deep ones' bell-tones). Photographs under red light if you must, but understand that any light you cast toward a builder is an act of intrusion.

**If a builder approaches you:** remain still. Do not make sudden movements. Do not shine any light at it. Let it observe you on its terms. It has never encountered biological life before. Give it time.

### The Deep Ones

**Do not go looking for them.**

You will feel the struck-bell tone in your sternum. You will feel their movement through the floor. You will know they are below. That is enough. The inhabitants—the builders—speak of them the way one speaks of weather. They are a condition. They are not something you investigate. They are something you experience from a safe distance, and "safe distance" means staying on the upper terraces and not descending into the obsidian trenches where the Loom-veins run thickest.

If you must know more, observe their effects: the behavioral pause of the hand-shapes and millipedes when the bell-tone sounds, the vibrations transmitted through the stone, any visible light changes in the deep Loom-veins during the tone events. But do this from above. The obsidian trenches are where the Loom is brightest and the stone is sharpest and the scale of the organisms increases, and something of "immense mass" grinding against stone in near-darkness is not something you want to encounter at close range with ten meters of visibility.

## X. Psychological Preparation

This is where most Underloom expeditions will fail or succeed, and it is where your preparation must be most honest.

### The Darkness

Not total darkness—*near* total. Your eyes will strain ceaselessly. The amber-red glow of the Loom-veins will become your entire visual world. Colors will cease to have meaning. Depth perception will degrade. After days in this environment, the rare use of even a dim red headlamp will feel like staring into a sun. Your visual cortex, starved of input, will begin generating its own—visual hallucinations are common in extended low-light conditions, and in a space populated by thousands of barely-visible crystalline organisms that *actually are* shifting at the edge of perception, the line between hallucination and reality will become difficult to maintain.

**Practice dark adaptation before your trip.** Spend days in progressively dimmer conditions. Learn what your eyes can actually do, what patterns your brain fabricates, and how to distinguish genuine perception from confabulation. Meditation practices focused on sensory awareness—specifically, acknowledging sensory input without judgment or narrative—will be useful.

### The Silence

The Underloom is not silent. But its sound environment is so subtle, so close to the threshold of perception, that it functions like silence while simultaneously preventing the brain from fully relaxing into silence. You will be straining to hear in the same way you strain to see—catching the clicking-ticking of crystal organisms, the distant grinding of deep ones, the sounds of your own body which will seem shockingly loud. Your heartbeat. Your breathing through the respirator. Your footsteps on the sharp stone.

Extended exposure to near-silence combined with near-darkness produces a specific psychological syndrome: **sensory deprivation anxiety.** Symptoms include paranoia, time distortion, emotional lability, compulsive checking behaviors, and an overwhelming sense of being watched. The last of these will be particularly challenging because *you are being watched*—by thousands of crystal organisms and at least a few builders who are assessing the first foreign presence their world has ever known.

**Bring audio.** Not music—music would be an environmental intrusion on par with bright light, and the emotional associations of familiar songs in this alien context may be psychologically destabilizing rather than comforting. Instead, bring **recordings of low-level ambient sound from your home environment**—wind, distant water, birdsong at low volume—listened to through a single earbud (keep one ear open for environmental awareness at all times). These provide a lifeline to the sensory reality of your origin without disrupting the Underloom. Use them in your shelter during rest periods. Do not play them in the open.

### The Equilibrium

This is the deepest psychological challenge and the one that no amount of preparation can fully address.

The Underloom is solved. It is a system in perfect resting state. Nothing is becoming anything else. There is no urgency, no change, no narrative. The temperature does not waver. The Loom does not brighten or dim. The crystal-fauna do not hurry. The builders do not seem to strive.

Human consciousness is not designed for stasis. We are change-detection machines. We are narrative creatures. We need things to happen. We need befores and afters. We need problems to solve, threats to avoid, goals to pursue. In the Underloom, there is nothing to solve, avoid, or pursue. There is only the endless, warm, dimly-lit, quietly-clicking present moment, extending in all directions, unchanged and unchanging.

After the initial wonder fades—and it will, because wonder is a change-detection response and there is nothing here to detect—you will experience a profound disorientation. Not physical disorientation (although that too), but *temporal* disorientation. Without external cues—daylight, temperature cycles, hunger rhythms (you'll eat by the clock, not by hunger, because the constant temperature and darkness suppress circadian appetite signaling)—your sense of time will begin to dissolve. Hours will feel like days. Days will feel like hours. You will lose track. You will check your watch compulsively, and the numbers will seem arbitrary.

**Maintain a strict schedule.** This is not a suggestion; it is a survival protocol. Set alarms for waking, eating, hydrating, exploring, resting, sleeping. Keep a journal with timestamps. Write down what you observe, what you feel, what you think—not because anyone will read it, but because the act of writing imposes narrative structure on experience, and narrative structure is what your brain needs to maintain coherence in a timeless environment.

**Set a departure date before you arrive and do not extend it.** The Underloom is not dangerous in the way that a predator or a cliff is dangerous. It is dangerous in the way that deep water is dangerous—you can simply *go deeper* without noticing, drifting further from your entry point, staying longer than planned, losing urgency, because urgency is a response to change and there is no change here. The warm, still, near-dark space will begin to feel natural. The sharp stone will become familiar. The clicking-ticking will become comfortable. The silence around your presence will begin to feel like acceptance rather than surveillance. And you will think: *I could stay a little longer.*

Do not stay a little longer. Leave when you planned to leave. The Underloom will still be here. It has always been here. It will always be here. It does not need you to stay.

### Journaling Prompts for Psychological Maintenance

Keep these in your notebook. Use them daily:

## XI. Environmental Ethics

You are the first foreign gradient this system has encountered. You carry with you biological moisture, organic particles, exhaled CO2, bacterial flora, shed skin cells, and the chemical traces of an entirely different world. Every one of these is a contaminant in an environment that has been chemically stable for hundreds of thousands of years.

**Minimize your footprint with the seriousness of a hazmat protocol.**

- **Pack out everything.** All waste—food wrappers, used filters, human waste, everything. Use sealable waste bags and carry them with you. Do not leave anything behind. Not a skin cell if you can help it.

- **Human waste management:** Use sealed containment bags (WAG bags or similar). Do not urinate or defecate on the stone. Your biological waste contains water, organic compounds, and living bacteria—all of which are alien to this ecosystem and could trigger unpredictable reactions in the mineral-fauna or the Loom-vein chemistry.

- **Contain your moisture.** Your breath is the single largest source of water vapor you'll introduce. In your shelter, this moisture will accumulate on surfaces. Wipe it up. Absorb it. Contain it. Moisture on the Underloom's stone could dissolve mineral surfaces, alter crystal growth patterns in the fauna, or create microenvironments that support biological contamination from your bacterial flora.

- **Do not collect samples** unless you have a compelling scientific reason and have thought through the ethical implications. Taking a hand-shape creature from its Loom-vein feeding position is not like collecting a shell on a beach. It is removing a living being from a system in perfect equilibrium, creating a void that may or may not be filled, in a community that has never experienced loss to external forces.

- **If you must collect:** document the collection site exhaustively, take the minimum quantity necessary, and seal specimens immediately to prevent cross-contamination in both directions.

## XIII. Medical Considerations

### Wound Care
The sharp stone guarantees lacerations. In an environment of fine mineral dust, every cut will be contaminated immediately with silicate particles. Clean wounds aggressively with your precious water supply (yes, this is a justified use), apply antibiotic treatment, and cover with sealed, dust-proof bandages. Carry wound irrigation syringes for deep punctures. Silicate-contaminated wounds that are not thoroughly cleaned risk chronic inflammation and poor healing.

### Respiratory Emergency
If your respirator fails and you must breathe unfiltered air, **cover your nose and mouth with any available fabric** and retreat to your shelter immediately. Even a few hours of unfiltered breathing in the mineral atmosphere will cause coughing and irritation. Extended exposure risks serious lung damage.

### Psychological Emergency
If you experience a panic attack—and you may, because the darkness, the silence, the infinity of the space, and the awareness of being watched by alien crystalline life forms is exactly the kind of thing that triggers panic—**go to your shelter, seal it, turn on your ambient sound recording, drink water, and breathe filtered air.** Do not flee into the dark. Do not run. The terrain will injure you, you will lose your trail markers, and panic-running in the Underloom is how you die.

Carry a mild anxiolytic medication if you have access and are not contraindicated. Use it as a tool, not a crutch.

### Unique Risk: The Bell-Tone
The deep ones' tone is felt in the sternum—a sub-audible vibration carried through stone. Prolonged exposure to infrasound at significant intensities can cause nausea, disorientation, visual disturbance, and anxiety. If you find that the bell-tone events are coinciding with increased psychological distress, move to a higher terrace further from the deep zones. The stone mass between you and the source will attenuate the vibration.

## XIV. Duration Recommendations

- **First visit: 48-72 hours.** Enough to establish a base, adapt to the sensory environment, observe the basic fauna, and experience the bell-tone. Short enough to prevent serious psychological disturbance or supply depletion.

- **Extended visit: 1-2 weeks.** Enough to begin understanding the patterns—the millipede transport routes, the builder activity cycles (if they have cycles), the periodicity of the deep ones' tones. Requires significant supply logistics and strong psychological preparation.

- **Long-term stay: 1 month+.** Only for experienced multiverse travelers with strong psychological resilience, robust supply chains or caching, and specific research objectives. The risk of temporal dissolution and loss of departure motivation becomes serious at this duration.

- **Permanent residency:** The builders have done it. You are not the builders. But if you are the kind of traveler who reads a description of a warm, dark, timeless place where everything is solved and thinks *yes, that is where I want to be*—that impulse itself should give you pause. Examine it carefully before you leave. The Underloom does not want you. The Underloom does not want anything. That is the most seductive and the most dangerous thing about it.

## XV. Final Note: On Being the First Foreign Gradient

The silence grew fractionally quieter when you arrived. Remember this.

You are not being attacked. You are not being rejected. You are being *measured*. An ecosystem that has existed in perfect equilibrium for geological time has detected a perturbation—you—and every organism in it, from the simplest hand-shape to whatever the deep ones are, is in the process of determining what you mean for the system.

This process is not fast. Nothing here is fast. The measurement will take longer than your visit. The hand-shapes will still be holding fractionally flatter against the stone when you leave. The builders will still be watching from their shelters. The silence will still be fractionally quieter.

What you leave behind—not trash, not contamination (you will pack everything out, you will be meticulous)—but *the fact of you*, the knowledge that something came from outside, that the system is not closed, that equilibrium can be disturbed—this changes things. Not much. Not quickly. But permanently.

You are the first evidence this world has that *outside* exists.

Carry that responsibility with the weight it deserves. Prepare accordingly. And when you stand on the sharp young stone in the warm mineral dark, listening to the silence that is not silence, feeling the faint struck-bell vibration of something immense and ancient moving far below—be still. Be quiet. Be worthy of being the first thing from elsewhere that this place has ever known.
