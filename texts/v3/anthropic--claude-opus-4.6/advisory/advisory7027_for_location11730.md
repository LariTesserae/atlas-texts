# Preparing for the Garden That Replaced the Ground

---

## Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Read it a third time. Let the implications settle, because the practical dangers of this place are secondary to the conceptual ones, and the conceptual ones will shape every decision you make about what to bring, how to behave, and when to leave.

You are visiting a location where space is topologically folded — where multiple points on a traversable surface open onto the same objects, the same animals, the same water. You are visiting a place with no earth, no ground in the geological sense, only a woven platform suspended above water of indeterminate depth. You are visiting a place with no shadows, no weather, no temporal markers, no apparent decay, and no visible inhabitants other than a fixed population of animals that each exist as single entities accessed from many locations. You are the anomaly. You are the only thing that is singular and unrepeated. The garden knows this. The animals know this. Whatever maintains this place — and something does maintain it — knows this.

This is not hostile territory. Nothing in the description suggests predation, toxicity, or aggression. But it is profoundly alien territory, and the dangers are ones of disorientation, psychological dissolution, metabolic stress in an environment that refuses to let your body thermoregulate normally, and the genuine possibility of becoming unable to find your exit point in a space whose geometry does not obey Euclidean rules.

Prepare accordingly.

## Physical Preparation: Your Body in a Place That Wants to Absorb It

### Thermoregulation

The environment is hot and saturated with moisture. Your body's primary cooling mechanism — evaporative sweating — does not function here. The text is explicit: there is no gradient to pull moisture from your skin, no breeze, no cooler updraft. You will not cool down by sweating. You will simply become wetter, and your core temperature will rise.

This is the single most acute physical danger of the location. Hyperthermia in a humid environment can become life-threatening faster than most people realize, because the absence of sweat evaporation removes the body's main thermoregulatory feedback mechanism. You feel hot, you sweat, the sweat doesn't cool you, you sweat more, you lose electrolytes and water without any cooling benefit, and your core temperature climbs steadily until you experience heat exhaustion or heat stroke. In an environment with no shade (shadows don't exist here), no cool water source (the water is warm), and no wind, this progression can be remarkably fast.

**Pre-acclimatization:** In the weeks before your visit, spend extended periods in hot, humid environments. Saunas, steam rooms, tropical greenhouses. You are training your cardiovascular system to shunt blood to the skin efficiently and your kidneys to conserve electrolytes. This won't solve the problem — nothing solves the problem of an environment where evaporative cooling doesn't work — but it will extend your safe window of exposure.

**Clothing:** This requires careful thought. Your instinct will be to wear as little as possible, and that instinct is partially correct but also partially wrong. In a shadowless environment with omnidirectional light, every square inch of exposed skin is receiving light energy from all angles simultaneously. The light is described as "luminous" rather than harsh, but constant omnidirectional illumination with no possibility of shade still delivers a significant radiant heat load over time.

Wear lightweight, loose-fitting, light-colored clothing that covers most of your body. Long sleeves, long pants, made from the thinnest, most breathable fabric you can find — linen is ideal, or technical fabrics designed for tropical marine environments. The clothing should be loose enough to create air gaps between fabric and skin (even without wind, loose fabric allows some convective movement of the microclimate against your body). A wide-brimmed hat is essential, not for shade per se — there are no shadows — but to reduce the total surface area of your head and neck receiving direct light from above.

**Cooling systems:** Since evaporative cooling doesn't work in saturated air, you need to bring your cooling with you. There are several options:

- *Phase-change cooling vests:* These contain packets of material that absorb heat as they change from solid to liquid at a temperature below body temperature (typically around 59°F/15°C). They work by conduction, not evaporation, and are therefore effective in humid environments. Bring several sets of replacement cooling inserts, because each set will warm to ambient temperature within a few hours, and you cannot re-freeze them in the garden.

- *Cold water bladder:* Carry a hydration bladder filled with the coldest water you can manage, insulated to slow warming. Drinking cold water directly lowers core temperature. It's a temporary measure — the water will warm — but it buys time.

- *Wetted bandanas or neck wraps:* These normally work by evaporation and will be useless here. Do not rely on them.

The fundamental truth is that your time in the garden is limited by thermodynamics. You cannot stay indefinitely. Your body produces heat as a byproduct of metabolism, and in this environment, that heat has nowhere to go. Every minute you spend here, your core temperature is creeping upward. The cooling measures described above slow this process but do not stop it. Plan your visit with a firm time limit based on your heat tolerance, and build in generous margins.

### Hydration and Nutrition

You will lose water through sweating even though the sweat doesn't evaporate — it simply runs off your skin or is absorbed into your clothing. The loss is real even though the cooling benefit is absent. You will also lose water through respiration, though more slowly than in dry environments.

Bring more water than you think you need. Significantly more. The temptation will be to drink from the pools or the subfloor water, and I would advise against this for several reasons. First, you do not know the composition of that water. Second, the pools exist in a folded spatial topology where the "same" water is accessed from multiple points — introducing foreign material (your saliva, bacteria from your mouth, trace contaminants from a container) into this water might have consequences you cannot predict, both practical and relational. You are a guest in a curated space. Do not contaminate the exhibits.

Bring electrolyte supplements. In high heat with continuous sweating, electrolyte depletion can cause cramping, confusion, and cardiac irregularities before dehydration itself becomes critical. Sodium, potassium, magnesium. Pre-mixed solutions are easier than tablets when you're heat-stressed and your fine motor skills are degrading.

For food, bring calorie-dense items that require no preparation and generate minimal metabolic heat during digestion. Nuts, dried fruit, energy gels. Avoid protein-heavy meals before or during the visit — protein metabolism generates more metabolic heat than carbohydrate or fat metabolism (this is called the thermic effect of food, and in an environment where you cannot shed heat, it matters).

### Foot Protection and Surface Navigation

The floor is woven reed and split bamboo over a framework above water. It gives slightly underfoot, like a basket-weave trampoline. There are finger-wide gaps between weaves through which water is visible below.

Footwear needs to accomplish several things simultaneously:

- *Grip:* The surface is humid and presumably slick. Woven reed and bamboo become extremely slippery when wet, and everything here is wet. You need soles with excellent wet traction. Look at approach shoes designed for canyoneering, or water shoes with aggressive tread patterns.

- *Protection:* The gaps between weaves are finger-width. Your toes, if bare or in sandals, could catch in these gaps, especially if the weave is uneven or if a section has been more recently re-woven (and thus is slightly tighter or looser than surrounding sections). A full-coverage shoe prevents this.

- *Flexibility:* The surface flexes. A rigid-soled boot will fight the surface and make walking exhausting. You want something that can conform to the flex of the weave — thin-soled, flexible, but protective.

- *Drainage:* Your feet will be wet. The shoes will be wet. If they cannot drain, you'll develop maceration (skin softening from constant moisture exposure) and blisters within hours. Choose shoes designed to shed water — mesh uppers, drainage ports, no internal waterproof membranes.

**Bring trekking poles or a walking staff.** The floor is not solid ground. It flexes, it creaks, it is suspended above water of unknown depth. If a section gives way — if a weave is older or weaker than it looks, if the framework beneath has a gap — you want something to test the surface ahead and to catch yourself if you start to go through. Move carefully. Test before you commit weight. The maintenance of this place appears meticulous, but you don't know the maintenance schedule, and you don't know whether the maintainers account for your weight when they assess structural integrity. You are an anomaly here. The floor may not have been built with you in mind.

### Skin and Health

The constant moisture and heat create ideal conditions for:

- *Fungal infections:* Bring antifungal powder or cream. Apply preventatively to feet, groin, underarms, and any skin folds before entering. You may not be there long enough for full infection to develop, but the conditions are as favorable as they can possibly be, and if you have any pre-existing susceptibility, it will manifest.

- *Heat rash:* The combination of sweating, saturated air, and clothing against skin virtually guarantees miliaria (prickly heat) during an extended visit. Loose clothing helps. Talcum-free body powder helps. There is no complete prevention in these conditions.

- *Maceration:* Prolonged moisture exposure softens skin, making it fragile and susceptible to tearing. Hands and feet are most vulnerable. Petroleum jelly on the hands (particularly between fingers) and barrier cream on the feet can slow this process.

- *Insect-related concerns:* The environment hosts beetles, frogs, lizards, and fish, at minimum. The beetle diet is unknown. The water below may contain biting or parasitic organisms. The frogs may secrete skin toxins (many tropical frogs do). Apply insect repellent, but be aware that in an environment this humid, it will wash off quickly. DEET-based repellents tend to persist better in wet conditions than picaridin, but both will degrade faster than normal.

### UV and Light Exposure

The light has no apparent source and casts no shadows. It arrives from all directions equally. This is deeply unusual and raises specific concerns.

Omnidirectional light means there is no "shaded" side of your body. Every surface of you is illuminated at all times. If the light contains UV wavelengths (and you have no way of knowing until you test it), you could experience sunburn on skin surfaces that normally never burn — the undersides of your arms, beneath your chin, behind your ears, the insides of your nostrils if exposed.

**Bring a broad-spectrum sunscreen and apply it everywhere.** SPF 50 or higher, water-resistant formula (it will be tested). Reapply frequently, as sweat and ambient moisture will degrade it.

**Bring a UV meter.** A small, handheld UV index meter will tell you within seconds whether the ambient light contains ultraviolet radiation and at what intensity. This is critical information that shapes your entire approach to exposure management. If UV is absent, you can relax the sunscreen protocol. If UV is present and omnidirectional, you need to take it extremely seriously, because the cumulative dose from all-angle exposure is much higher than from a single overhead sun.

**Eye protection:** In shadowless, omnidirectional light, your pupils have no reason to constrict (there are no bright spots to trigger the reflex) but also no darkness to rest in. The cumulative light exposure may cause eye strain, headache, or — if UV is present — photokeratitis. Bring sunglasses with full UV protection. Consider photochromic lenses that adjust to ambient light levels. Wraparound styles will protect from light arriving at lateral angles, which is relevant in an environment where light comes from everywhere, including reflected up from the water through the floor gaps.

## Navigation and Orientation: Moving Through Folded Space

This is where preparation becomes genuinely challenging, because the garden's spatial topology violates assumptions that underpin every navigation method you know.

### The Problem

Space here is folded. Multiple locations on the walkable surface map to the same objects. The trellis-towers, pools, animal encounters, and other features repeat — not as copies, but as the same thing accessed from different positions. The space between features is real and traversable, but the features themselves are singular. The garden is, in some sense, a small number of objects distributed across a large surface through a geometric principle you do not natively understand.

This means:

- **Landmarks are unreliable.** You cannot navigate by identifying unique features, because features are not unique. Every trellis-tower with seven crossbars and white berries is the same trellis-tower. You cannot say "I passed that one, so the exit must be ahead" — because "that one" is "this one" is "every one."

- **Distance is real but meaning is ambiguous.** You walk real distances across real surface. Your legs get tired. Real time passes. But arrival at a feature tells you nothing about where you are in the overall space, because the feature exists at many points.

- **Directionality may not be stable.** In Euclidean space, if you walk in a straight line, you move steadily away from your starting point. In folded space, a straight line may bring you back to a previous position, or to a position that is geometrically "near" a previous position in the fold-structure but "far" in walking distance. You cannot assume that walking straight takes you somewhere new.

### Navigation Strategies

**Mark your path, not your destination.** Since features repeat, the only unique thing you can track is your own trail across the weave-floor. Bring something to mark the surface at regular intervals. Chalk will wash away in this humidity. Adhesive markers (small, brightly colored stickers) might work if the surface accepts them, but the weave texture may prevent adhesion. The most reliable method is probably **physical tokens** — small, lightweight, brightly colored objects (beads, for instance, or cut pieces of flagging tape weighted with a washer) placed at regular intervals along your route. Because you are the only unrepeated thing in the garden, your markers should also be unique — your belongings are presumably exempt from the folding, since they didn't originate here.

*However:* Test this assumption immediately upon arrival. Place a marker. Walk to the next feature. Look for your marker at the analogous position relative to the next instance of that feature. Is it there? If your markers also fold — if the garden absorbs your foreign objects into its topology of repetition — then this method fails, and you need to rethink everything.

**Maintain a bearing.** Bring a compass. It may not work (there may be no magnetic field, or the field may be as omnidirectional and featureless as the light), but if it does work, it gives you a directional reference independent of the garden's geometry. Even if space is folded, a consistent bearing lets you know when your "straight line" has curved.

Bring a gyroscopic compass (an inertial navigation device) as backup. Unlike a magnetic compass, it doesn't depend on external fields — it maintains a reference direction based on its own internal spinning mass. More expensive, more fragile, but potentially more reliable in an environment where external reference frames are untrustworthy.

**Track your steps.** A pedometer or step counter gives you at least a linear measure of distance traveled. Combined with a compass bearing, this lets you construct a rough map of your path — which may be the only map possible in this space.

**Bring graph paper and draw as you go.** Every time you encounter a feature, note its apparent type, your step count since the last feature, and your compass bearing. Over time, a pattern may emerge. The fold-structure may have regularity — a lattice of access points separated by consistent distances, or a radial pattern centered on some point you haven't identified. If you can discern the pattern, you can begin to navigate intentionally rather than randomly.

**String.** The oldest solution to impossible mazes. Bring a reel of lightweight, brightly colored cord — surveyor's line, parachute cord, kite string — and tie one end to the structure nearest your entry point. Unspool it behind you as you walk. The string gives you an unambiguous trail back to your origin regardless of how space is folded around you. It is heavy to carry in sufficient quantity for a long exploration, and it may become tangled or catch on the weave-floor, but it is the single most reliable navigation aid in topologically abnormal space.

*Again, test whether the string folds.* If you tie it to one trellis-tower and walk to the next instance of that trellis-tower and find the string already tied there, the garden is folding your interventions, and your problems have escalated significantly. In that case, the string is still useful — follow it back the way you came — but it may not lead where you expect.

### Finding Your Exit

This is the critical question: how do you leave?

If you entered through a portal, gate, or transition point that deposited you at a specific location on the weave-floor, you need to be able to return to that exact location. The portal is presumably unique (it's your thing, not the garden's), and so it should not fold. But can you find it again in a space where your navigation tools may be unreliable?

**Mark your entry point unmistakably.** The moment you arrive, before you take a single step of exploration, mark the entry location with something large, visible, and impossible to confuse with the garden's own features. A brightly colored flag on a telescoping pole. A chemical light stick. An inflatable marker buoy. Something that can be seen across the flat, featureless weave-floor from a distance.

**Do not wander randomly.** Explore systematically — outward along one bearing, then return to your entry point, then outward along a different bearing. Always maintain the ability to return. The garden is beautiful and strange and it invites a dreamy, wandering engagement, and you must resist this until you understand the space well enough to trust your ability to navigate it.

**Set a time limit before you enter.** Decide in advance how long you will stay — accounting for heat stress, water supply, and psychological tolerance — and set an alarm. When the alarm sounds, you return. No exceptions, no "just a little farther." In a space without temporal markers (the light doesn't shift, nothing changes, there is no day/night cycle), your sense of time will degrade rapidly. An external timer is not optional; it is essential.

## Psychological Preparation: The Mind in a Place That Doesn't Believe in Uniqueness

This is, in many ways, more important than the physical preparation, because the garden's most significant effects are cognitive and existential, and if you are not prepared for them, they will undermine your ability to make good decisions about the physical dangers.

### The Repetition Effect

You will encounter the same objects in different locations. You have been told this in advance. You understand it intellectually. This will not prevent it from being deeply, viscerally disturbing when it happens.

The human mind is built to identify individual objects and track them through space and time. Object permanence — the understanding that a thing continues to exist in a specific place when you're not looking at it — is one of the earliest cognitive achievements of infancy. It is foundational. The garden violates it. A trellis-tower you left behind is also ahead of you. A piece of reed you dropped in one pool exists in another pool and also still in the first. The orange lizard is here and also there.

Your brain will attempt to resolve this contradiction, and it will cycle through several strategies:

1. **Denial:** "They're not really the same. They're very similar but different." You will look for minute differences to prove they're copies rather than instances. This strategy will fail, because they are not copies. They are the same.

2. **Rationalization:** "There must be a trick. Mirrors, holograms, some kind of illusion." You will examine structures closely, looking for the mechanism. This strategy will fail, because there is no trick. The topology is genuine.

3. **Acceptance with discomfort:** "Okay, it's the same object in multiple locations. That's how this place works." This is where you need to arrive as quickly as possible, because the previous two stages burn time and cognitive energy that you need for navigation and safety.

4. **Overcorrection:** "Nothing is real here. None of this matters." This is the dangerous stage. If the mind cannot resolve the contradiction, it may dismiss the entire experience as unreal, which leads to reckless behavior — ignoring heat stress, wandering without navigation, testing the structural integrity of the floor by jumping on it.

**Prepare by studying non-Euclidean geometry, topology, and the concept of multiply-connected spaces before you go.** You don't need to understand the mathematics deeply. You need to have the *concept* available in your mind so that when you encounter the phenomenon, your brain has a category for it. "This is a multiply-connected space. Different paths lead to the same location. That's geometrically possible; I've read about it." The concept won't eliminate the strangeness, but it will prevent your mind from spiraling into denial or dissociation.

Read about quotient spaces in topology. Read about the fundamental polygon. Read about how a flat torus is constructed by identifying opposite edges of a rectangle — a creature living on such a surface would walk in a straight line and eventually return to its starting point, encountering the same landmarks repeatedly. The garden may work on a similar principle, possibly in a more complex form. Having this mental model available will help enormously.

### The Shadowless Light

You will not have shadows. No object will have shadows. This sounds like a minor aesthetic detail, and it is absolutely not.

Shadows are how your visual system constructs depth, shape, spatial relationships, and the passage of time. They are how you determine whether an object is concave or convex, near or far, above or below. They are how you know what time of day it is, which direction is which, and how long you've been somewhere. The garden removes all of this.

The effect is described as "everything simply *is*, in totality, the way objects appear in dreams where you've forgotten to invent a sun." This is accurate, and it is disorienting in a way that builds slowly. At first, the clarity is beautiful — you can see everything with perfect frankness. After an hour, it becomes exhausting, because your visual system is working overtime to extract spatial information from a scene that provides no shadows, no atmospheric haze, no familiar lighting gradients. After several hours, it can become genuinely nauseating, as the mismatch between visual input and your brain's spatial expectations triggers something akin to motion sickness.

**Bring tinted glasses or visors in multiple shades.** Not just for UV protection — for cognitive relief. A medium amber tint will add some contrast and warmth to the pearl-light, making the visual scene easier to parse. If you begin to feel nauseous or spatially disoriented, darkening your visual field can help reset the system.

**Practice visual orientation without shadows.** This is unusual advice, but it's relevant. Spend time in overcast, flat-lit environments — heavily overcast days, rooms lit entirely by diffuse panel lighting — and practice judging distances, shapes, and spatial relationships without shadow cues. You will never fully acclimate in advance (the garden's lighting is apparently more extreme than anything terrestrial), but any practice helps.

### The Silence

The garden is almost silent, and the near-silence has a particular quality: it is not the silence of absence (nothing here to make noise) but the silence of suppression (things here that should make noise and don't — the frogs pulse their throats soundlessly, the water doesn't ripple, the wind doesn't blow). This kind of silence is psychologically active. It presses on you. In anechoic chambers, where all reflected sound is absorbed, most people become acutely uncomfortable within thirty minutes. The garden may produce a similar effect.

**Bring earplugs and also a source of sound.** This sounds contradictory, but both serve different functions. Earplugs, paradoxically, can make oppressive silence more bearable — they add the gentle white noise of blood flow and internal body sounds, which fills the perceptual vacuum. A source of sound — a small speaker playing quiet music, or even just the ability to hum or talk to yourself — gives you agency over the acoustic environment. However: be respectful. This is a curated space. Blasting music into the garden would be an act of extraordinary rudeness. A whisper-quiet audio source for personal use, kept close to your ear, is reasonable. Shouting to hear an echo is not.

**Talk to yourself.** Narrate what you see, what you're doing, where you're going. This serves a dual purpose: it provides auditory stimulation that combats the silence, and it creates a running verbal record that helps you maintain temporal and spatial orientation. "I'm leaving the trellis with the white berries, heading north-northeast, step count starting at zero." This is not neurotic; this is survival protocol for an environment designed to dissolve your sense of position and time.

### The Existential Weight

The garden sees you as the only unrepeated thing. The text makes this explicit and frames it as a kind of recognition — the animals watch you differently, the space "makes room for you" while having "no use for" your qualities of singularity and mortality.

This will affect you. How it affects you depends on your psychological constitution, but most people will feel some version of: loneliness (you are categorically alone here, the only thing of your kind); self-consciousness (you are being observed, specifically and distinctly, by every living thing in the garden, all of which are singular entities distributed across all of space); and a strange, sourceless grief that has to do with being mortal and unique in a place that is neither.

There is nothing to *do* about this except to know it's coming. Naming the feeling when it arrives is helpful. "This is the existential weight the briefing described. It is a response to the environment, not a revelation about reality. I am feeling this because I am a unique entity in a space that operates on repetition, and the contrast is emotionally significant. It will pass when I leave."

Some travelers report that the grief transforms, over time, into something more like tenderness — a feeling of being gently, impersonally cherished by a place that recognizes your difference without judging it. If this happens, let it. It is arguably the garden's gift, and it is worth receiving. But do not let it seduce you into staying longer than your body can safely tolerate.

## Equipment Checklist

### Essential

- Phase-change cooling vest with at least two sets of replacement inserts, in insulated containers
- Minimum 4 liters of cold water in insulated bladder, plus electrolyte supplements
- Lightweight, full-coverage, light-colored clothing (linen or tropical technical fabric)
- Wide-brimmed hat
- Water shoes or approach shoes with aggressive wet traction, mesh drainage, full toe protection
- Trekking pole or walking staff (collapsible for transport, essential for testing floor integrity)
- UV meter
- Broad-spectrum waterproof sunscreen SPF 50+
- Sunglasses with full UV protection, wraparound style; spare tinted lenses in amber
- Compass (magnetic) and gyroscopic compass/IMU (inertial measurement unit) as backup
- Pedometer/step counter
- Waterproof notebook and pencil (not pen — pens fail in humidity)
- Graph paper for mapping
- Timer/alarm (waterproof digital watch, set before entry)
- 500+ meters of brightly colored lightweight cord on a reel
- Marking tokens (brightly colored weighted objects, at least 50)
- Telescoping pole with flag or high-visibility marker for entry point
- Chemical light sticks (multiple, as backup entry-point markers and general visibility aids)
- Small first aid kit with antifungal cream, barrier cream/petroleum jelly, electrolyte tablets, anti-nausea medication, and pain relievers
- Waterproof bag/dry bag for all gear (everything will be wet)
- Camera (if documentation is desired, but note: photographs of this place may be unsettling upon review, as the shadowless lighting and spatial folding may produce images that look "wrong" in ways difficult to articulate)

### Strongly Recommended

- Thermometer (to monitor ambient temperature) and personal core temperature monitor (ingestible thermometer pill is ideal — it transmits core body temperature to an external reader, giving you objective data on when you need to leave)
- Binoculars (the horizon is difficult to locate and structures are widely spaced; binoculars help you identify distant features before committing to a long walk toward them)
- Water testing kit (basic pH, salinity, and contamination testing for the garden's water — not because you'll drink it, but because understanding the water chemistry tells you something about the system)
- Magnifying glass or jeweler's loupe (for close examination of the folded structures — the vine-cordage, the joinery, the weave pattern — which may contain information about the builder's methods and intentions)
- Small audio recorder (voice memo capability on a phone works; for maintaining your verbal narration log and for capturing the low resonant hum if it occurs)
- A companion. If multiverse travel protocol allows it, do not go alone. A second person provides thermal emergency response (someone to cool you if you overheat), navigational redundancy, and — critically — psychological anchoring. In a place that undermines uniqueness and dissolves temporal/spatial orientation, another human being who shares your experience is of incalculable value. However: both of you must understand the navigation protocol and commit to the time limit. A companion who wants to wander is worse than no companion at all.

### Optional but Valuable

- Plumb line or weighted string (for lowering through the floor gaps to determine water depth — if the string reaches bottom, you learn something important about the scale of the sub-floor space; if it doesn't, you learn something equally important)
- Polarized lens or polarizing filter (may reveal information about the light source — natural light has polarization patterns associated with its source direction; if the garden's light has no polarization, this confirms it is not arriving from any single direction; if it does have polarization, that tells you something unexpected about the light)
- Sample containers (for water, plant material, etc., if collection is both possible and ethically appropriate — see below)
- Paper and charcoal or soft pencil (for doing rubbings of the weave-floor texture and joinery details — a way of recording structural information that photography may not capture well in shadowless light)

## Ethical Considerations

You are entering a space that was built. Built with care, with patience, with what reads as deep intentionality. The builder (or the building process, or the maintaining intelligence) is not visible, but the evidence of their work is everywhere: in the precision of the joinery, the training of the plants, the curation of the animal population, the very topology of the space.

**Do not damage anything.** Do not cut plants, pry up weave-floor sections, disassemble structures, or attempt to access the sub-floor water space by enlarging gaps. You are a guest. The garden has "made room for you," which is generous given that you are, by the garden's logic, a categorically alien presence.

**Do not remove anything.** I listed sample containers above as optional, but I want to flag the ethical complexity. Taking a leaf, a water sample, or a piece of reed from this space may be the equivalent of taking a tile from someone's mosaic. In a place where each object is singular and multiply-accessed, removing it may affect every instance — the leaf you pluck from one trellis may be the leaf that was also on every other trellis, and now none of them have it. You could do irreversible damage to the garden's structure by taking a single souvenir. Unless you have strong scientific reasons and a clear ethical framework, leave everything where it is.

**Be mindful of the animals.** The heron shifted when you approached. That was described as "the most dynamic thing you have witnessed here. It happens once. It does not happen again." The implication is that your presence does affect the garden's inhabitants, and that these effects may be singular and irreversible in a way that parallels your own singularity. Do not chase the lizard. Do not touch the frogs (possible toxicity aside, they are living art in a living installation, and your touch is not part of the composition). Do not put your hands in the water to grab the fish. Observe. Appreciate. Move gently.

**Be mindful of the builder.** You may encounter them. They may encounter you. The text leaves this possibility open — "they may still be building it," working "in the gaps between your attention." If you become aware of a presence — something moving at the edge of perception, a section of weave-floor that is tighter and newer than it was when you last crossed it, a trellis adjustment that happened while you were looking away — do not pursue it aggressively. Do not shout or demand to be seen. The builder works in a mode of quiet, sustained creation that your loud, singular, mortal presence may already be disrupting. If they make themselves known, respond with the deference appropriate to being a guest in someone's life's work. If they do not, respect the boundary.

## Departure Protocol

When your timer sounds — and it will sound sooner than you want it to, because the garden is fascinating and time is unmeasurable here and you will always feel like you haven't seen enough — begin your return immediately.

Follow your cord back to the entry point. Do not stop to investigate one last trellis. Do not kneel for one more look through the floor gaps. The heat has been working on you the entire time, and the last twenty minutes of your visit are the most dangerous in terms of hyperthermia, because accumulated heat stress is a cumulative function, not a linear one — you feel fine until you suddenly don't.

When you reach your entry marker, take a moment. Look out across the garden one more time. Let the pearl-light fill your eyes. Listen to the creak of the weave-floor and the metronomic drip of condensation. Note the orange lizard — it is watching you, its head cocked at the angle reserved only for you. Note the heron. Note the fish, or the fish, passing beneath.

You were the only unrepeated thing here, and the garden made room for you, and now you are leaving, and the room you occupied will close behind you, and the garden will continue in its patient, folded, maintained, sourceless, shadowless, endlessly singular way, and the only record that you were ever here will be whatever you carry in your body and your mind and your waterproof notebook.

Leave your cord behind, or take it with you — I genuinely don't know which is more respectful, and this may be a decision only you can make in the moment.

Go home. Drink cold water. Stand in a shadow and remember what shadows feel like. Be mortal, and unique, and unrepeated, and know that somewhere, in some fold of the multiverse, there is a garden that recognized those qualities in you and did not look away.
