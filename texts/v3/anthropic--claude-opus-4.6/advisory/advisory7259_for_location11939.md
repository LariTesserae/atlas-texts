# Preparing for the Hanging Gardens of the Abyssal Spires

---

## First: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack a single piece of equipment, before you calibrate a single instrument, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation will fail if it is rooted in the wrong assumptions.

You are not visiting an underwater cave. You are not doing a deep-sea dive. You are entering a stable, self-contained world that has existed in a state of completion for geological time, a place where you will be submerged in total darkness at crushing depth, surrounded by living acoustic architecture, and where you will be—by many orders of magnitude—the most disruptive thing to have entered the environment in millennia or perhaps ever. Every choice you make in preparation must be weighed against two imperatives that will sometimes conflict: keeping yourself alive, and not destroying what you came to witness.

Let me be direct about something. Many travelers approach extreme environments with a conqueror's mindset—the right gear, the right plan, the right extraction protocol, and the place becomes a problem to be solved. The Abyssal Spires will punish that mindset, not through hostility but through indifference. This place does not care about you. It was complete before you arrived. The danger is not that it will attack you; the danger is that you will bring noise into a world of music, light into a world that has never needed it, urgency into a world that operates on timescales your nervous system cannot comprehend, and in doing so you will experience the place as chaos when it is in fact the most ordered environment you have ever entered. Your preparation must include preparing *yourself*—your mind, your expectations, your sensory habits—as much as your equipment.

---

## The Pressure Question

Let's deal with the engineering problem first, because if you get this wrong nothing else matters.

The description tells us several things. The spires were once the highest mountains in their world. They are now deeply submerged, with the peaks still far below the surface—so far below that there is a *noticeable* pressure differential as you ascend the spires themselves. This means the base of the spires is at extreme abyssal depth, likely equivalent to several thousand meters of water column, and even the peaks, the highest terraces, exist at a depth that would be lethal to an unprotected human body.

You need a pressure solution that allows you to exist at these depths for an extended period—and I mean extended. This is not a place you visit for an hour. Crossing from one spire to another takes an hour for a drift-eel, which is native to the medium and moves with metabolic efficiency you cannot match. To actually experience this place—to feel the harmonic transitions, to enter the chambers, to drift between the ghost-shapes of the spires—you need days. Possibly weeks.

There are broadly two approaches, and your choice between them will define the entire character of your visit.

**Option one: a rigid pressure vessel.** A personal submersible, a hardsuit, something that maintains surface pressure around your body while you exist at depth. This is the safer engineering choice. It eliminates decompression concerns entirely. It lets you carry your own atmosphere, your own warmth, your own sensory instruments. Many travelers will default to this.

I am going to argue against it, or at least ask you to think very carefully about what it costs you.

A rigid vessel separates you from the water. That sounds like a feature, and from a survival standpoint it is. But the Abyssal Spires are a world of *pressure and vibration*. The tower-kelp harmonics are felt in the bones. The drift-eels navigate by pressure change. The sessile colonies feed on acoustic resonance. If you are inside a rigid shell, you will *hear* the harmonics through your hull—attenuated, filtered, transformed by the resonant properties of your vessel rather than the resonant properties of the water and the stone. You will feel pressure changes as instrument readings rather than as bodily sensations. You will be looking at this world through a window when the world is not a visual one. It would be like visiting a perfume garden in a sealed hazmat suit and trying to appreciate it through a chemical spectrometer. You would get data. You would miss everything.

**Option two: saturation diving at extreme depth.** This means breathing a carefully calibrated gas mixture at ambient pressure, allowing your body tissues to equilibrate with the surrounding water pressure, and existing in the medium itself—skin exposed to the water, bones exposed to the vibrations, body exposed to the pressure gradients that everything here navigates by. This is vastly more dangerous. It requires extensive pre-dive preparation, a complex gas management system, thermal protection that does not rely on dry insulation, and a decompression protocol that will take *days* when you finally ascend. But it puts you in the water. It makes you, however briefly and imperfectly, a body in the medium, subject to the same physics as everything else that lives here.

My recommendation: a hybrid approach, and I'll describe it in detail.

## The Hybrid Rig

What you want is a system that keeps you alive at depth while exposing as much of your sensory surface as possible to the medium.

**Breathing system.** At the depths we're discussing, you are looking at a hydrogen-helium-oxygen mixture, or possibly a more exotic trimix calibrated for the specific pressure range. Standard heliox will work at the shallower reaches—the upper terraces of the spires—but if you intend to descend to the base, to the deep chambers where the sessile colonies grow, you may need to step through multiple gas mixtures during your descent. Carry a closed-circuit rebreather with multiple gas canisters and an automated blending system. The rebreather is critical not just for efficiency but for *noise*. Open-circuit scuba produces bubbles; bubbles produce sound; sound in this environment is not background—it is the fundamental medium of navigation and ecology. Every bubble you release is, to the drift-eels and the mantle-rays, a localized acoustic event with no analog in their experience. A rebreather produces no bubbles. Silence is your first courtesy to this world.

**Thermal protection.** The water is cool—the text describes it as feeling like the inside of a stone jar. Not freezing, not icy, but persistently, pervasively cool in a way that will leach your body heat over hours and days. A standard wetsuit will not suffice for the duration you need. What you want is a thin-profile heated undersuit—a mesh of low-power heating elements against the skin, powered by a compact cell, covered by a minimal neoprene layer for hydrodynamic smoothness and some passive insulation. The key word is *thin*. You do not want bulk. Bulk changes your hydrodynamic profile, which changes the pressure signature you present to every organism around you. You want to be as close to a soft body in the water as you can manage. The heating elements should be adjustable by zone—you will want more warmth at your core and less at your extremities, because your extremities are your sensory interface with the environment and some degree of thermal exposure will actually help you feel the faint temperature gradients that mark different zones of the spire ecology.

**Pressure exposure.** Here is where it gets philosophically interesting. Full saturation means your entire body is at ambient pressure, which means your tissues are loaded with dissolved gas, which means decompression is a serious concern. But it also means your bones conduct sound from the water directly, your skin feels pressure changes directly, your proprioception functions in relation to the actual medium rather than in relation to an artificial atmosphere inside a shell. For the Abyssal Spires, this matters enormously. I recommend full saturation. Accept the decompression cost. It is the price of actually being present in this world.

To manage saturation safely over a multi-day visit, you will need a **habitat module**—a rigid pressure vessel, yes, but one you return to for rest, gas management, nutrition, and sleep, not one you inhabit while exploring. Think of it as a base camp. Anchor it to one of the mid-level terraces of a spire (gently—more on this below) and use it as your starting and ending point for each excursion. The habitat maintains ambient pressure internally, so there is no decompression cost for entering and exiting it; you simply swim in, seal the hatch, and you're in a dry space at the same pressure as the surrounding water. The gas mixture inside can be optimized for rest and recovery. You sleep here. You eat here. You process your experiences here. But you do not explore from inside it.

**Mobility.** You will be tempted to bring a propulsion unit—a DPV, a thruster pack, something to move you efficiently through the vast distances between spires. Resist this temptation, or at least use it sparingly and with extreme care. Any mechanical propulsion system produces noise, vibration, and water displacement on a scale that is alien to this environment. The drift-eels move by sinusoidal undulation so slow it appears meditative. The mantle-rays ride currents that are barely currents. The entire ecosystem is calibrated to a world of minimal water movement. A thruster pack in this world is a screaming engine in a monastery.

Instead: learn to move as the water moves. Bring a pair of long, supple, oversized free-diving fins—not rigid performance fins, but something wide and flexible that lets you generate thrust with a slow, full-body undulation rather than a rapid kick. Practice this movement extensively before you arrive. You want a kick cycle of three to four seconds, a long glide between each stroke, and a body position that is as hydrodynamically clean as possible. You will move slowly. Accept this. Everything here moves slowly. Your impatience is your own problem; the world will not accommodate it.

For crossing the open water between spires—the void spaces where there is nothing but black water and fading harmonics—you may use a DPV at its lowest power setting, but only in the open water, never near the spires themselves. And even then, be aware that the sound it generates will propagate through the water for a considerable distance and will be perceived by every organism within range as something unprecedented and inexplicable.

## Sensory Preparation

This is where most travelers will fail, and it is where your preparation must be most rigorous.

You are a visual creature. Your dominant sense, the one your brain allocates the most processing power to, is sight. You are entering a world of total darkness. Not dim. Not murky. *Total*. The bioluminescent moss provides a glow so faint it does not register as light in any meaningful sense. You will see nothing. For the entire duration of your visit, unless you activate an artificial light source (which you should not—more on this shortly), you will see nothing.

This means you must shift your sensory hierarchy, and you must do it *before you arrive*. This is not something you can figure out on the fly in the dark at crushing depth.

**Darkness adaptation training.** Spend a minimum of two weeks before your visit in progressively increasing periods of total darkness. Start with four hours a day. Extend to eight, then twelve, then full days. During these periods, practice ordinary activities: eating, moving through a space, handling equipment, resting. Your goal is not to develop echolocation (you will not, your neurology does not support it at any useful resolution) but to accomplish two things. First, to reduce the panic response that total darkness triggers in visual creatures—the low-grade, persistent anxiety that says *something is wrong because I cannot see*. You need to extinguish this response completely, because in the Abyssal Spires it will drain your psychological reserves within hours and leave you unable to function. Second, to elevate your awareness of non-visual sensory channels: touch, pressure, proprioception, and especially *hearing*.

**Auditory training.** This is critical. The Abyssal Spires are a sonic environment of extraordinary richness and subtlety, and you will navigate by sound more than by any other sense. Before your visit, train your ears.

Learn to identify intervals. The tower-kelps produce chords—specific harmonic relationships. A minor third. A perfect fifth. If you cannot distinguish these by ear, you will not be able to tell one spire from another, and you will become disoriented in the void between them. Take a basic course in ear training, the kind a music student would take. Learn to identify intervals, to hear the beating of slightly mistuned notes, to perceive the difference between a major and minor third without visual reference. This will be the single most practically useful skill you bring to the Spires.

Beyond interval recognition, practice listening to *infrasound*—to sounds at the very bottom of the human hearing range, below 20 Hz, where audition blurs into tactile perception. The tower-kelp fundamentals may lie in this range. You will feel them as much as hear them. Spend time with a good subwoofer and infrasound generator, eyes closed, learning to attend to vibrations in your sternum, your jaw, the bones of your skull. Learn to discriminate between slightly different infrasonic frequencies by feel. This is your compass. This is your map.

**Tactile and pressure sensitivity.** In the water, at ambient pressure, your skin becomes a sensory organ of much greater utility than you normally grant it. Practice attending to water pressure changes: spend time in still water (a sensory deprivation tank is ideal, if you can find one large enough to move in) and have an assistant create subtle currents and pressure changes while you, in darkness, try to identify their direction and magnitude. This is what the drift-eels do with their lateral lines, and while your skin is a crude instrument by comparison, it is better than you think.

## What Not to Bring

**Do not bring bright lights.** This is the hardest discipline for visual creatures, and the one I must be most emphatic about. The organisms of the Abyssal Spires have never experienced light above the faintest bioluminescent shimmer. Their tissues—the gorewood, the moss, the integument of the drift-eels, the velvety sound-absorbing skin of the mantle-rays—have developed in total darkness. Exposing them to a dive light, even a dim one, is not equivalent to turning on a lamp in a dark room. It is equivalent to nothing in their experience. You do not know what it will do. It may cause photochemical damage to tissues that have no photoprotective pigments. It may trigger panic responses in fauna that have no flight behavior for this stimulus, leading to injury as they collide with stone and plant structures in an environment they normally navigate with perfect competence. It may disrupt the feeding rhythms of the sessile colonies, which respond to vibration, by introducing a stimulus that confuses their sensory processing in ways you cannot predict.

More than this: light will ruin the experience *for you*. If you turn on a light, your visual cortex will seize dominance of your attention, as it always does, and you will see—what? Stone. Plants. Water. The shapes of things. And you will stop listening. You will stop feeling the pressure changes. You will stop attending to the harmonics. You will see the Abyssal Spires the way a deaf person sees an orchestra: the forms are there, but the thing itself—the actual phenomenon, the actual beauty—is inaudible to you because you are looking instead of listening.

Bring a light for genuine emergencies only—equipment failure, injury, situations where visibility is necessary for survival. Keep it in a sealed pouch. Do not use it otherwise.

**Do not bring sonar or acoustic instruments.** This should be obvious but I will say it anyway. Active sonar in the Abyssal Spires would be an act of violence—a massive, alien acoustic pulse injected into an environment that is structured around specific, stable, ancient harmonic relationships. The sessile colonies are powered by sound. The drift-eels navigate by sound. The entire ecosystem is acoustically coupled. Active sonar would be heard across multiple spires, would disrupt navigation, would overwhelm the feeding mechanisms of the colonies, would do the acoustic equivalent of setting off a flashbang inside a cathedral during vespers. Do not.

Passive acoustic recording is fine, even desirable. Bring hydrophones—good ones, with a frequency response that extends well below 20 Hz into the infrasonic range. Bring a recording system with enough storage for days of continuous capture. The acoustic environment of the Spires is perhaps the most remarkable thing about them, and a high-quality recording, properly made, may be the most meaningful artifact you bring back.

**Do not bring food that produces waste.** Your metabolism is an import into a closed nutrient cycle. Everything you eat, you will eventually excrete, and your excretions will introduce organic compounds that are foreign to this food web. Use nutritional pastes and calorie-dense rations that produce minimal solid waste. Carry a waste containment system and remove everything you produce. Leave nothing.

## What to Bring

**A bone-conduction audio system.** Not headphones—bone-conduction transducers mounted against your mastoid processes, connected to your hydrophone array, with adjustable gain and frequency filtering. This system allows you to amplify and process the ambient sound without blocking your ears, without introducing artificial sound into the environment, and with the ability to shift frequencies from the infrasonic range up into the range of comfortable hearing. When you are in the void between spires and the harmonics are faint, you can boost. When you enter a resonant chamber and the sound is dense enough to vibrate your teeth, you can attenuate. This will be your single most important piece of equipment after your breathing system.

**A tactile mapping device.** Since you cannot see, and since active sonar is forbidden, you need a passive way to build a spatial model of your surroundings. The best option is a body-mounted array of passive pressure sensors—essentially an artificial lateral line, inspired by the drift-eels—that translates nearby water displacements into a tactile readout on your skin. Several multiverse-grade sensory augmentation systems offer this. The output is typically delivered as a pattern of gentle vibrations on a belt or vest: objects to your left produce a buzz on your left side, objects above produce a buzz on your shoulders, and so on. The resolution is crude compared to a drift-eel's natural system, but it is enough to tell you when you are approaching a stone surface, when a gorewood fan is within reach, when something large is moving in the water nearby. Combined with the acoustic information from your bone-conduction system, it gives you a workable, if low-resolution, picture of your immediate environment.

**A tether and beacon system.** For your habitat module. You need to be able to find your way back to base after an excursion, in total darkness, in a featureless water column. A thin, neutrally buoyant guideline running from your harness to the habitat is the simplest solution. For longer excursions—crossing to other spires—use a set of passive acoustic beacons that emit a tone so faint it falls below the threshold of ecological significance but that your bone-conduction system can detect and amplify. Calibrate these carefully. The tone must not overlap with any tower-kelp harmonic, or you will create interference in the natural acoustic landscape. Choose a frequency that falls in a gap between the natural harmonics. Finding such a gap will require initial passive recording and analysis before you set up your beacons.

**A dosimeter for dissolved gas saturation.** At ambient pressure, you are accumulating dissolved gas in your tissues continuously. The rate depends on your depth, your gas mixture, your activity level, and individual physiology. A transcutaneous sensor that continuously monitors tissue gas loading and calculates decompression obligation in real time is not a luxury; it is a necessity. You must know, at all times, what your decompression cost would be if you had to ascend. In an environment where you may lose track of time (and you will—there is no light cycle, no social cue, nothing but the unchanging harmonics to mark the passage of hours), it is easy to extend your exposure beyond safe limits without realizing it.

**Minimal recording equipment.** Besides the hydrophones, bring a compact, high-sensitivity pressure logger that records the ambient pressure at your position over time—this gives you a detailed record of your depth profile and movements. A temperature logger, similarly. And if you are technically inclined, a passive chemical sampling system—small cartridges that absorb dissolved minerals and gases from the water as you move through different zones, which can be analyzed later to map the chemical gradients that the gorewood and moss feed on. These are lightweight, silent, non-disruptive instruments that produce rich data without affecting the environment.

**A journal. A physical, waterproof journal and a graphite stylus.** You will want to write. Not type, not dictate—write, in darkness, by feel, on pages you cannot see. The act of writing by hand in total darkness is a different cognitive process than any other form of recording. It forces you to slow down, to crystallize your thoughts before committing them, to accept imprecision and overlap and the literal illegibility of your own marks. It matches the pace of this place. And when you surface and read your notes—scrawled, overlapping, half-illegible—they will contain things that a dictated log would not. The handwriting pressure will vary with your emotional state. The spacing will reflect your spatial awareness. They will be, in their imperfect way, the most honest record of your experience.

## Behavioral Protocols

**Move slowly.** I have said this, but I will say it again with more specificity. Your normal swimming pace in open water is approximately 1 to 1.5 knots. In the Abyssal Spires, halve that. Then halve it again. You want to move at a pace that a drift-eel would find unremarkable—a gentle, steady glide, with long pauses, and a willingness to hang motionless in the water for minutes at a time when something interesting is happening acoustically. The temptation to hurry is real, especially when crossing the void between spires, where the darkness and emptiness trigger a deep mammalian unease that whispers *move, get somewhere, get to shelter*. Resist. You are not in danger in the void. There is nothing there to harm you. The unease is a surface instinct that has no referent here. Let it pass. Move slowly.

**When you arrive at a spire, pause.** Do not immediately begin exploring. Hang in the water at the outer edge of the moss-glow zone—the point where the ghost-shape of the spire first becomes perceptible—and spend at least thirty minutes simply existing there. Listen. Feel the chord. Let your pressure sensors map the gross shape of the spire face in front of you. Let the drift-eels, if there are any nearby, register your presence and decide you are not interesting. You are large for this environment—larger than anything except a mantle-ray—and you are producing a pressure signature that is novel. Give the local fauna time to assess you and dismiss you. If you rush in, you will push water ahead of you, disturb the gorewood filaments, scatter the grazers, and the spire face you wanted to experience will be in a state of disruption caused by your own arrival.

**Enter the chambers with extreme care.** The sessile colony chambers are the most sensitive and the most extraordinary spaces in the Spires. The sound inside them is unlike anything in human experience—a dense, choral, vibration-rich acoustic environment that is also, simultaneously, the feeding mechanism of a vast colonial organism. When you enter a chamber, you are entering the interior of a living body, in a sense. The colony-lattice fills the space. It is close but—if you are careful—will not touch you. Move through the lattice the way you would move through a room full of sleeping children: with total awareness of every part of your body, every fin stroke, every exhalation. The lattice branches are calcified but not stone-hard; they can be broken by careless contact, and a broken branch represents a loss that the colony will take years or decades to repair in this slow-growing world.

Once inside, be still. Float neutrally. Let the sound wash over and through you. The bone-conduction system may be overwhelming here; consider turning it off and relying on your natural hearing and bone-conducted vibration. This is the heart of the Abyssal Spires experience. This is what you came for. The harmonic density in these chambers—the layered, converging, resonating tower-kelp chords amplified and shaped by the stone and the living lattice—has been described by the few travelers who have experienced it as the closest thing to a genuine encounter with the numinous that the multiverse offers. I am not in a position to confirm or deny that. I am in a position to tell you that the experience will change something in you, and that you should be prepared for that change to be disorienting and, possibly, permanent.

**Be aware of the mantle-rays.** They are not aggressive. Nothing here is aggressive in the way you understand the term. But they are large, and they move silently—their sound-absorbing integument makes them acoustically invisible in a world where everything else is acoustically present. Your pressure sensors should detect the water displacement of a mantle-ray at close range, but "close range" may be closer than you'd like. If you feel a sudden, broad pressure change—a smooth wave of displacement coming from one direction, consistent with a large flat body passing nearby—go still. Do not make sudden movements. The mantle-ray is not hunting you; you are far too large and far too strange. But a startled mantle-ray in an enclosed space near fragile gorewood fans or colony-lattice branches could cause significant environmental damage. Let it pass. It will.

## Psychological Preparation

I have saved this for last because it is the most important and the hardest to prescribe.

The Abyssal Spires are a completed world. You are visiting a place that has finished becoming. Nothing here is in transition, in crisis, in the process of dramatic change. The ecology is at equilibrium. The geology is at terminal form. The harmonics have not changed in centuries. For a human being—a creature defined by change, by narrative, by the expectation that things will happen and that time will be marked by events—this is profoundly disorienting. You will wait for something to happen. Nothing will happen. The tower-kelps will hum. The grazers will rasp. The drift-eels will thread their courses. And this will continue, unchanging, for as long as you are there.

This will produce, after the initial wonder fades, a deep restlessness. And after the restlessness, if you stay, something else: a quiet, a settling, a recalibration of your internal clock to match the timescale of the place. This is what you are here for. Not the spectacle—there is no spectacle, nothing to see—but the *recalibration*. The experience of existing, for a time, inside a system that is complete, that does not need you, that was here before you and will be here after you, and that is beautiful not because it is dramatic but because it is *finished* and *still going*.

Prepare for this by adjusting your expectations. Do not arrive with a checklist. Do not plan to visit a certain number of spires or chambers. Do not set objectives. Set a duration—five days is the minimum for the experience to take hold, ten is better, two weeks is what I would recommend if your gas supply and decompression schedule allow—and then surrender to the pace of the place. Let the harmonics structure your time. When a chord calls you, follow it. When the void opens before you, cross it slowly. When a chamber presents itself, enter it and be still.

You will, at some point during your visit, experience what many travelers report as the *dissolution moment*: a period, usually in a colony chamber or in the convergence zone between several spire harmonics, when the boundary between your body and the water seems to soften, when the vibrations in your bones feel less like an external stimulus and more like something you are participating in, when the darkness stops being the absence of sight and starts being a medium as rich and navigable as light ever was. This is not a mystical experience, or rather, it is not *only* a mystical experience—it has a physiological basis in the interaction of infrasound with human neurology, in the effects of prolonged darkness on visual cortex repurposing, in the deep mammalian dive reflex that slows your heart and redirects blood to your core and brain. But the phenomenology of it—what it *feels like*—is that you have become, briefly, a native of this world, a body in the harmonic landscape, a soft thing in the dark water, held by sound.

Let it happen. Do not fight it. Do not analyze it while it is happening. You can analyze it later. In the moment, let the ancient music hold you.

## Departure

When your time is up—when your dissolved gas loading says it is time to begin decompression, when your supplies are calculated for the ascent—leave slowly. Leave as you arrived: moving gently, disturbing nothing, trailing your guideline, swimming upward through the pressure gradient with long decompression stops during which you will hang in the dark water and listen to the harmonics fading below you, one chord at a time, each one quieter and fainter as you rise, until the last spire falls below your sensory range and you are in open water, alone, rising toward a surface this world has never known, carrying in your bones the memory of a sound that will not stop.

The decompression will take days. Use them. Write in your journal. Listen to your recordings. Begin the slow process of re-becoming a creature of light and air and change.

You will not fully succeed. Part of you will stay in the dark water. That is the cost of visiting a completed world: you come back incomplete, carrying an absence shaped exactly like a chord you can no longer hear.

It is worth it.
