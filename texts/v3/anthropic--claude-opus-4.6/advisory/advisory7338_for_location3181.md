# Preparing for the Risen City of Keth Anoul: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## I. Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before we discuss gear lists and cognitive preparation techniques, you need to sit with something uncomfortable: Keth Anoul is not a destination in the way you have understood destinations before. It is a place that operates on principles of discontinuous spatial transformation, non-Euclidean geometry, and ontological ambiguity. You are not visiting ruins. You are not exploring a city. You are entering a *process*—one that is indifferent to your presence at best, and responsive to it in ways you cannot predict at worst.

The temptation will be to approach this like a challenging wilderness environment or an unusual architectural site. Resist that temptation. Keth Anoul is both of those things, but it is also something that doesn't have a clean analogue in most of the multiverse. The closest comparison might be entering the interior of a living organism whose body is made of stone and whose metabolism operates through spatial reconfiguration rather than chemistry. You are not in danger the way you would be in danger from a predator or a collapsing structure. You are in danger the way a blood cell might be in danger inside a body that doesn't know it's there—subject to processes that are not hostile but are vastly larger than you and entirely unconcerned with your continued coherence.

Plan accordingly.

## II. Physical Preparation

### A. Altitude and Thin Air

The city occupies an entire mountain range at what appears to be extreme elevation. The air is described as thin—thin enough to notice, to feel the inadequacy of each breath, though not so thin as to be immediately incapacitating. Think of it as roughly equivalent to 4,000–5,500 meters (13,000–18,000 feet) in a standard Earth atmosphere, though the comparison is imperfect because this atmosphere may not have standard composition.

**Pre-acclimatization:** Spend at minimum two weeks before your crossing at progressively higher altitudes in a baseline-Earth-equivalent world. If your transit method allows it, stage through a high-altitude world first—spend three to five days at 3,000 meters, then three to five days at 4,000. Your body needs time to increase red blood cell production and adjust ventilation response. There is no shortcut for this. Altitude sickness in Keth Anoul would be catastrophic, not because the symptoms would be worse than elsewhere, but because your capacity to make sound decisions is the single most important thing you will carry, and hypoxia destroys judgment before it destroys anything else.

**Supplemental oxygen:** Bring it. A lightweight closed-circuit rebreather system is ideal—it recycles exhaled gas, scrubs CO2, and meters in oxygen from a small tank, giving you far more duration per unit of carried weight than open-circuit systems. Carry enough for at minimum 72 hours of continuous use, though you should plan for the possibility that your stay will be longer than intended (more on this below). The rebreather also has the advantage of adding a small amount of moisture to your breathing gas, which matters enormously given the absolute dryness of the environment.

If a rebreather is unavailable, carry multiple small oxygen cylinders with a demand-valve regulator. Use oxygen judiciously—not continuously, but when you feel cognitive function degrading, when you need to make decisions, when you sleep. Ration it. You don't know how long you'll be there.

**Pharmaceutical support:** Acetazolamide (Diamox) is your first-line altitude medication—begin taking it 24 hours before arrival, 125–250mg twice daily. It accelerates acclimatization by promoting bicarbonate excretion and shifting your ventilatory response. Dexamethasone is your emergency medication for cerebral edema symptoms (severe headache, confusion, ataxia)—carry it but hope not to use it. Nifedipine for pulmonary edema symptoms if needed. **Note:** Some multiverse pharmacies carry adapted versions of these drugs; confirm compatibility with your specific physiology before departure.

### B. The Dryness

This is not ordinary dryness. The description is explicit: the dryness is *ontological*. Water hasn't merely evaporated from Keth Anoul—it has been subtracted from the possibility space. This has profound implications for your body, which is approximately 60% water by mass and relies on water for every metabolic process.

**Hydration strategy:** You must carry all of your water. There is no water to be found, filtered, or condensed from the environment. None. The air holds no moisture. The stone holds no moisture. Plan for a minimum of 3–4 liters per day, more if you're exerting yourself significantly. For a planned 72-hour visit, that's 9–12 liters minimum, weighing 9–12 kilograms. This is heavy. Accept it. Dehydration in this environment will not follow the normal progression—you won't get thirsty and then uncomfortable and then impaired over the course of hours. The absolute dryness may pull moisture from your body faster than you expect through respiration and transdermal loss. The air you breathe in is perfectly dry; the air you breathe out carries moisture from your lungs. Every breath is a net water loss. In thin air, you breathe faster, so you lose more.

**Water containers:** Use sealed, insulated containers. Wide-mouth bottles for easy drinking. A hydration bladder with an insulated tube for hands-free sipping while walking. And here is an important consideration specific to Keth Anoul: **keep your water on your person at all times.** Do not set a water container down and walk away from it, even briefly. In a city that rearranges itself between your glances, anything you set down may not be where you left it—not because it moved, but because the stone beneath it was replaced, the space it occupied was reconfigured, the avenue shifted. Your water could end up inside a wall, or in a room that no longer has a door, or at a point in the canyon system that is topologically unreachable from where you stand. Keep everything critical attached to your body.

**Moisture barriers:** Apply a heavy occlusive moisturizer to all exposed skin before entering and reapply every few hours. Petroleum jelly on lips, nostrils, the corners of your eyes. Consider wearing a lightweight balaclava or face covering with a moisture-retaining layer over your nose and mouth—a simple damp cloth (re-dampened periodically from your water supply) can help humidify inspired air and reduce respiratory water loss. Protective eyewear—sealed goggles, not just sunglasses—will help prevent your corneas from drying, though the dim light means you won't need UV protection.

**Nasal care:** Carry saline nasal spray or gel. Your nasal passages will dry and crack in this environment, and cracked nasal mucosa bleeds, and blood is water you can't afford to lose.

### C. Temperature Management

The cold is described as non-biting but persistent and incremental. This is insidious. A sharp cold prompts you to put on a jacket. An incremental cold, the kind that settles into you slowly over hours, can lower your core temperature significantly before you register the danger, especially when combined with altitude-induced metabolic changes and dehydration (which impairs thermoregulation).

**Clothing system:** Layer for sustained moderate cold without wind. There is no wind in Keth Anoul, which is actually helpful—wind chill is not a factor. You need insulation and moisture management.

- *Base layer:* Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking fabric against the skin. Not cotton. Cotton holds moisture and in a no-wind environment it will still cool you through evaporation once it's damp with sweat.
- *Mid layer:* Lightweight fleece or synthetic insulation. Something you can easily add or remove as needed.
- *Outer layer:* A soft shell or light wind jacket. Not for wind (there is none) but as an additional insulation layer and to contain body heat. A hard shell rain jacket is unnecessary—there is no rain, no precipitation of any kind.
- *Extremities:* Thin gloves that allow dexterity. A warm hat—you lose significant heat through your scalp. Warm socks, doubled if your boots allow it.

**Thermal monitoring:** If you have access to a core temperature monitoring device (ingestible sensor pill, temporal artery monitor), use it. Check your temperature every few hours. If it drops below 36°C (96.8°F), take active warming measures immediately—eat calorie-dense food, add layers, do light exercise, use a chemical heat pack against your torso. Hypothermia impairs judgment, and in Keth Anoul, impaired judgment is potentially fatal in ways that have nothing to do with cold.

### D. Nutrition

There is nothing to eat in Keth Anoul. No plants, no animals you'd want to catch (or could catch—they move by discontinuity), no water to cook with. The grey-violet crystalline films are of unknown composition and should absolutely not be consumed.

**Calorie needs:** At altitude, in cold, while walking with a heavy pack, you may burn 4,000–5,000 calories per day. Carry calorie-dense, water-free or low-moisture foods: nuts, nut butters, hard cheeses, dried meats, energy bars, chocolate, hard candy. Avoid foods that require water to prepare. Avoid foods that require significant chewing if your jaw muscles are tight from cold. Simple sugars for quick energy, complex fats and proteins for sustained energy.

**Eating schedule:** Force yourself to eat on a schedule—every 2–3 hours—whether you feel hungry or not. Altitude suppresses appetite. The strangeness of the environment may further suppress your desire to eat. Eat anyway. Your body is burning fuel to maintain temperature and oxygen saturation, and if you fall into caloric deficit, your cognitive function will degrade.

### E. Footwear and Movement

The avenue is fitted stone—smooth, precise, hard. Canyon walls are natural and carved stone. There may be stairs (the tower with the door on the avenue has a staircase). Everything is dry—no slippery surfaces from moisture, but stone on stone can be slick, especially if the soles of your boots are smooth.

**Boots:** Sturdy hiking boots with aggressive rubber soles (Vibram or equivalent), ankle support, and insulation. Not trail runners—the surfaces are hard and unforgiving, and you need ankle protection for uneven stone and potential changes in floor level (because the city rearranges, a flat avenue could become stepped without warning—more on this below). Break in your boots thoroughly before arrival. Blisters in this environment, where you have limited water and no way to wash a wound, could become a serious problem.

**Trekking poles:** Strongly recommended. They provide stability on stone surfaces, help with balance near canyon edges, reduce knee strain on descents, and probe the ground ahead of you. In a place where the floor plan changes between glances, having poles that let you test whether a surface is actually where it appears to be is a genuine safety measure.

## III. Equipment and Gear

### A. Navigation (Or the Futility Thereof)

Let's be honest about this: you cannot navigate Keth Anoul in any conventional sense. The city rearranges its own topology. Streets redirect. Distances don't map to visual relationships. The geometry is non-Euclidean—the avenue curves left in both directions simultaneously, buildings that are across a canyon also have doors on your street. A map would be obsolete before the ink dried.

**What you can do instead:**

- **Breadcrumb strategy (with caveats):** You might think to mark your path—chalk marks on stone, small cairns, flagging tape. This will fail. The stones rearrange. Your marks will end up on surfaces you've never touched, in locations you've never visited, or inside walls. However, there *may* be value in marking continuously and observing what happens to your marks. If the city redistributes your chalk marks, the pattern of redistribution might tell you something about the logic of its reconfiguration—which sections change most frequently, whether marks placed in certain locations persist longer, whether the city treats your marks as part of itself or as foreign objects. This is intelligence gathering, not navigation.

- **Tether strategy:** A more promising approach. Carry a long spool of thin, strong cord—Dyneema line, 1mm diameter, is incredibly strong relative to its weight. Anchor one end at a fixed point (more on finding fixed points below) and pay out line as you explore. If the city rearranges the space around your cord, you will see the cord respond—it may go taut as the distance between two points changes, it may go slack, it may thread through a window that wasn't there before. The cord gives you a physical connection to a known point that is harder for spatial reconfiguration to erase than a chalk mark. It may also reveal something about *how* the reconfiguration works—whether it preserves physical connections or severs them, whether it respects continuous objects or treats them the same as discontinuous ones.

  **Critical caveat:** The cord may also lead you astray if space itself is being reorganized. Following a cord back through non-Euclidean space might take you somewhere other than your starting point. Use it as one data point, not as your sole lifeline.

- **Fixed points:** The description mentions that the city changes *between observations*—when you look away and back. It also says that if you stare at a section without blinking, it remains still. This suggests that *observation pins things in place.* This is extremely important. It implies that a continuously observed location may be stable. If you can find or create a fixed reference point—something you can observe without interruption, or something the city itself treats as stable—you have an anchor. Candidates: the grey-violet crystalline films *might* persist more reliably than bare stone (they seem to occupy a different category of thing than the building material). The creatures *might* occupy fixed points in whatever spatial logic the city uses (they seem to operate by its rules). Your own body and equipment are presumably fixed points relative to themselves, though you should verify this.

- **Mental mapping:** Keep a running mental model of your surroundings, but hold it loosely. Note landmarks, but accept that they will change. Note *categories* of landmarks—types of building, patterns of window arrangement, the depth and direction of canyons—and track whether these categories persist even when specific instances change. The city may have regularities at a higher level of abstraction than individual structures.

- **Compass/GPS:** Almost certainly useless in any specific sense, but carry a compass anyway. If it behaves normally, that tells you something about the world's magnetic field. If it behaves abnormally—spinning, pointing inconsistently, pointing in a direction that doesn't match visual references—that also tells you something. Information is survival here.

### B. Lighting

The ambient light is dim ochre, constant, with no day-night cycle (the silicate dust ceiling doesn't move, and there's no visible sun). You'll need supplemental lighting, especially for interior spaces—those tens of thousands of dark windows suggest a vast unlit interior.

**Primary:** A reliable headlamp with multiple brightness settings and a red-light mode. LED, lithium batteries. Carry spare batteries—at minimum enough for 100 hours of use. In the cold, lithium batteries perform far better than alkaline. Keep spare batteries warm against your body.

**Secondary:** A small handheld flashlight for directed illumination—shining into windows, down staircases, across canyons. Something with a focused beam that reaches at least 200 meters.

**Tertiary:** Chemical light sticks. No batteries to die, no bulbs to break, no switches to fail. They also can't be turned off, which is useful—you can leave one glowing in a room and see if it's still there when you come back, testing whether the city rearranges occupied interior spaces the same way it rearranges exterior ones.

**A note about entering buildings:** The description says the buildings have interiors—rooms behind rooms, staircases, chambers going back into the mountain. Entering these spaces means moving through a structure that may reconfigure while you're inside it. A doorway you entered through may become wall. A room you're standing in may change dimensions. An interior staircase may gain or lose flights. The risks of interior exploration are categorically different from the risks of walking the avenue, and I will address them in detail in the safety section. For now: carry lighting, but think very carefully before using it to go inside.

### C. Recording Equipment

You will want to document what you see. More importantly, documentation may be one of your most powerful tools for understanding the city's behavior.

**Cameras:** Bring multiple. A digital camera with a high-capacity memory card, a backup camera, and if possible a film camera (the mechanism is purely mechanical/chemical and may behave more reliably in whatever field the city generates). Time-lapse photography is essential—set up a camera to take a photo every few seconds and leave it pointed at a section of wall or avenue. The resulting sequence will show you the reconfiguration in ways your eyes can't, because it will capture the *before* and *after* of changes that happen between your blinks. This is data. This is the closest thing to a map you're going to get.

**Video:** Continuous video recording may be even more valuable. If the city only changes when not observed, and a camera is a form of observation, does continuous video recording prevent the change? Or does the city only respond to conscious observation? This is a testable question with profound implications for your safety strategy. Set up a video camera pointed at a section of the city and watch the feed rather than the city itself. Does the section change on the feed while you're watching the screen? Does it change when you look away from the screen? These experiments should be among the first things you do upon arrival.

**Audio recording:** Carry a sensitive audio recorder. The silence is described as near-total, with only a low tonal hum and occasional distant clicks. A high-quality recorder with gain boosted may pick up sounds below your hearing threshold. The hum may have a frequency, a pattern, a rhythm. The clicks may correspond to reconfigurations. If you can establish a relationship between sounds and spatial changes, you gain an early warning system.

**Notebooks:** Bring physical notebooks and pencils (not pens—ink may behave unpredictably; graphite is more robust). Write constantly. Describe what you see. Draw. Sketch building profiles. Note timestamps (from your own clock—see below). When you re-read your notes and they describe a building that no longer exists or a street layout that has completely changed, that discrepancy is information. Your notes are a record of previous states that the city itself does not preserve.

### D. Timekeeping

There is no day-night cycle. The light doesn't change. Without external temporal cues, your circadian rhythm will drift and your subjective sense of time will become unreliable within days or possibly hours.

**Watches:** Carry at least two independent timekeeping devices. A mechanical watch (wind-up, no battery, no electronics to be disrupted by whatever the city does to physics). A digital watch as backup. Check them against each other regularly—if they diverge, one of them is being affected by local conditions, and you need to know which.

**Scheduled rest:** Force yourself onto a sleep/wake schedule tied to your watches, not your fatigue level. 16 hours awake, 8 hours rest (you may not sleep well, but lie down and rest). Without this discipline, you will lose track of time entirely, and losing time in a place where time may already be functioning abnormally is dangerous.

### E. Shelter and Sleep System

There is no weather in Keth Anoul—no wind, no precipitation, constant temperature. You don't need a tent for weather protection. But you need something for sleep, and the question of *where* to sleep is one of the most critical safety decisions you'll make.

**Sleeping pad:** Closed-cell foam, thick. The stone is cold and will sap heat from your body all night if you lie on it without insulation. A foam pad doesn't rely on air (which doesn't move here and may not inflate normally) and can't be punctured.

**Sleeping bag:** Rated to at least -5°C (23°F). The temperature is described as "not freezing" but during sleep your metabolism drops, your activity level drops to zero, and the persistent chill will feel colder. Down fill if the environment is truly as dry as described (down loses insulation when wet, but there is no moisture here); synthetic fill as a hedge against uncertainty.

**Bivouac location:** This is the hard part. Where do you sleep in a city that rearranges itself when you're not looking? When you sleep, you're not observing *anything*. The stone beneath you could rearrange. The walls around you could reconfigure. You could wake up in a room that has sealed itself, or on a ledge above a canyon that wasn't there when you lay down.

**Strategies:**
- Sleep on the avenue, in the open, away from building walls and canyon edges. Give yourself as much buffer space as possible. If the avenue shifts, you want to be in the center where you're unlikely to end up embedded in a wall or over an edge. Questionable whether this works—the avenue is part of the city.
- Sleep on natural bedrock, if you can find any. If there are portions of the mountain that are exposed, unworked, natural stone—not shaped or fitted by the city—they *may* be exempt from reconfiguration. This is a hypothesis, not a guarantee. Look for places where the city's construction clearly abuts natural rock, and camp on the natural side of the boundary.
- The buddy system: if you're traveling with a companion (strongly recommended—see section VII), take shifts sleeping. The waking partner watches the sleeper's immediate environment. If observation pins space in place, a human observer staying awake and watching the ground around the sleeper may prevent reconfiguration of that specific area. This is the best option if the observation hypothesis holds.
- Accept the risk. You will need to sleep eventually. You cannot stay awake indefinitely, especially at altitude. After every reasonable precaution, there remains an irreducible risk that the city will rearrange around you while you sleep. Acknowledge this. Make your peace with it. Then sleep anyway, because the alternative—sleep deprivation at altitude in an alien environment—is a guaranteed path to cognitive failure and death.

### F. Rope and Climbing Gear

You are on a mountain ridge above canyons of tremendous depth. The city descends along canyon walls. Bridges exist but may relocate or disappear. If you need to move vertically or if the avenue deposits you somewhere unexpected due to spatial reconfiguration, climbing gear could save your life.

**Bring:**
- 60 meters of dynamic climbing rope (lightweight, 8.5mm)
- A climbing harness
- A belay device
- A selection of passive protection: nuts, hexes, cams that can be placed in stone cracks
- Carabiners, slings, quickdraws
- A pair of approach shoes or sticky-rubber climbing shoes (in addition to your hiking boots)

**Note on anchors:** Any protection you place in a crack may be redistributed by the city's reconfiguration. A cam placed in a crack in a building wall might find itself in a featureless surface that no longer has a crack, or in a crack that has changed dimensions. Place protection in natural stone when possible, and always be aware that your anchors may be less reliable than they appear. Test every anchor with body weight before committing to it. Back up every anchor with a second independent anchor whenever possible.

**Note on the canyon edges:** The description mentions approaching the edge and looking down through hundreds of meters of dim air. Do not approach canyon edges without being roped to an anchor well back from the edge. The edge itself may reconfigure. A stable lip of stone could become an overhang or a vertical face between one observation and the next.

## IV. Cognitive and Psychological Preparation

This section may be the most important in this guide.

### A. The Observation Effect

The central strangeness of Keth Anoul—that it changes only when not observed—creates a psychological trap. Once you understand that your attention is the only thing holding your environment stable, the temptation is to *never stop looking*. To stare at the ground beneath your feet, the walls beside you, the canyon edge, with desperate fixity, trying to prevent the city from changing around you.

This will break you. Humans cannot maintain focused visual attention indefinitely. We blink. We fatigue. We get distracted. And the knowledge that every lapse in attention allows the world to shift creates an anxiety feedback loop: you watch harder, you fatigue faster, you lapse more, the world changes more, you watch harder. This spiral leads to hypervigilance, panic, and cognitive shutdown.

**Preparation:**

- **Meditation practice.** Specifically, practice with open awareness—the ability to maintain a broad, relaxed attention without fixating on any one thing. You are not trying to *control* the city by watching it. You are trying to maintain equanimity while the city does what it does. Start a daily meditation practice at least three months before your trip. Vipassana or Zen traditions are particularly useful for cultivating non-reactive awareness.

- **Accept the change.** Before you arrive, before you see a single rearranged stone, make a decision: you will not try to prevent the city from changing. You will observe it. You will note the changes. You will use them as information. But you will not fight them. The city has been doing this for longer than you have been alive—possibly longer than your species has existed. Your observation is not a weapon; it is a candle in a cave. Accept what it illuminates, and accept the darkness beyond it.

- **Practice with controlled disorientation.** Before your trip, deliberately expose yourself to disorienting environments. Sensory deprivation tanks. Maze experiences. Virtual reality environments with non-Euclidean geometry (there are several good ones available in technologically advanced baseline-Earth-adjacent worlds). The goal is to build familiarity with the sensation of spatial confusion so that when it hits you in Keth Anoul, it doesn't trigger a panic response. You want your brain to say "ah, I recognize this feeling—this is disorientation" rather than "SOMETHING IS WRONG."

- **Journaling and verbal processing.** Talk to yourself. Narrate what you're seeing. If you're with a companion, discuss your observations frequently. The act of verbalizing your experience helps your brain process it and reduces the accumulation of unprocessed strangeness that leads to psychological decompensation. There is no embarrassment in talking to yourself in an empty city. The alternative—silent accumulation of impossible observations—is much worse.

### B. The Silence

The near-total silence of Keth Anoul will work on you in ways you may not anticipate. Humans are social animals who evolved in noisy environments. Prolonged deep silence, combined with the other stressors of this environment—altitude, cold, dehydration, spatial disorientation, the uncanny activity of the city—can produce auditory hallucinations, paranoia, and dissociative states.

**Preparation:**
- Bring music. An audio player with earphones, loaded with familiar, comforting music. Use it sparingly—you need to be able to hear the environment (the hum, the clicks, any other sounds)—but when the silence becomes oppressive, twenty minutes of music can reset your psychological state.
- Bring earplugs. Counterintuitive, but in deep silence, your hearing becomes hypersensitive, and you begin hearing your own body—heartbeat, blood flow, joint sounds—which your brain may interpret as external sounds, leading to false pattern detection and anxiety. Earplugs can paradoxically reduce the subjective experience of silence by blocking body sounds and giving your auditory cortex something consistent to process (the slight hiss of the earplug material).

### C. The Creatures

The dark, cat-sized entities that move by discontinuity and the larger pale thing on the canyon wall are unknown quantities. You don't know if they're dangerous. You don't know if they're intelligent. You don't know if they're part of the city, symbiotes, parasites, or visitors like yourself.

**Guidelines:**
- Do not approach them. Observe from a distance.
- Do not attempt to communicate with them unless they communicate first.
- Note their behavior. Do they appear in the same locations? Do they seem to observe you? Do they react to anything you do?
- The pale thing—the large entity on the canyon wall—elicits a particular quality of attention in the description. It is the only pale thing in a world of dark stone. It is very large. It vanished when not observed. Treat this entity with extreme caution. If you see it again, observe it continuously for as long as possible. Do not turn your back on it. Do not allow it to be behind you and unobserved. This may be overcautious, but in an environment where things change when not watched, an entity of unknown capability that disappears the moment you look away deserves every precaution you can give it.

### D. The Temptation to Enter Buildings

You will want to go inside. The dark windows beckon. The staircases invite. The nautilus-shell depth of rooms behind rooms behind rooms promises revelation—answers about who built this city, how it works, why it persists.

This is extraordinarily dangerous.

On the avenue, you are in open space. If the city reconfigures around you, you have room to react—the canyon edge might move closer, but you have buffer space; a wall might appear, but you can walk around it. Inside a building, you are enclosed. The walls, ceiling, floor, and doorways are all part of the city's mutable structure. A room could seal you in. A corridor could extend infinitely. A staircase could become a pit. And all of this could happen in the moment between one blink and the next.

If you choose to enter buildings despite this—and you might, because some questions can only be answered from the inside—take the following precautions:

- Never go deeper than one room from an exterior opening.
- Keep the exterior opening in your line of sight at all times.
- Trail your cord from an exterior anchor point.
- Have a companion watch the exterior opening from outside while you enter.
- Limit interior explorations to ten minutes maximum.
- Before entering, observe the building continuously for at least thirty minutes. Note any reconfigurations. Attempt to establish a baseline frequency of change for that specific structure.
- If anything changes while you're inside—anything at all, any wall, any doorway, any dimension of the room—exit immediately. Do not investigate the change. Leave.

### E. Time Distortion

The description mentions that time is hard to track without a moving sun, and that "hours or days" might pass without clear distinction. This might be purely subjective—a consequence of the unchanging light and the disorienting environment. Or it might be objective—time itself may flow differently in Keth Anoul, or your perception of it may be altered by whatever field or force drives the city's reconfiguration.

**Preparation:**
- Use your watches religiously. Note the time in your journal with every entry. Create a temporal log.
- Count steps. A human walking pace is roughly 100 steps per minute. Count your steps and compare to elapsed time on your watches. If the ratio drifts—if you count 600 steps but only 3 minutes have passed, or 10 minutes have passed—note it.
- Set regular alarms on your digital watch: every hour, an alarm goes off. This creates a temporal scaffold that doesn't rely on your subjective sense of passing time.
- If you suspect time dilation or compression, do not panic. Adjust your water and food rationing calculations to reflect *watch-time* rather than *felt-time*. Your body operates on physiological time, which may or may not track with watch-time, but watch-time is at least consistent and measurable.

## V. Transit Strategy: Getting In and Getting Out

### A. Entry

How you arrive in Keth Anoul depends on your multiverse transit method. If you use a gate, portal, or fixed crossing point, establish it on natural rock outside the city proper if at all possible. Do not let your entry point be within the city's built environment. If the city reconfigures around your portal anchor, you might not be able to find it again, or it might be moved to a location you can't reach.

If your transit method is less precise—if you shift between worlds at whatever location corresponds to your current position—then study the geography before arriving. You want to arrive on the ridgeline, on the avenue, in the open, not inside a building and not on a canyon wall. Check the parallels carefully.

### B. Marking Your Exit

This is the most critical logistical challenge of the entire trip.

If the city rearranges itself, and your exit point is within the city, it may be moved. You need a way to find it again.

**Options:**

- *Fixed external exit:* If you can place your exit portal on natural rock outside the city, and if you can establish a line of sight or a physical connection (your cord) between the city and the exit point, you have the best chance of return. The cord may be rearranged within the city, but if the natural rock is truly exempt from reconfiguration, the anchor point should remain stable.

- *Homing device:* If your transit method supports it, carry a homing beacon or recall device that functions independently of spatial configuration. Something that pulls you back regardless of where you are in the city's folded geometry. Test it before you enter. Test it frequently while you're there.

- *Time-limited visit:* Set a hard extraction time before you enter. "I will leave after 48 hours on my watch, regardless of what I have or haven't discovered." This prevents the insidious expansion of "just a little further, just a little longer" that this environment will absolutely encourage. The city is infinitely explorable. You are not infinite. Set a limit. Honor it.

- *Dead man's switch:* If you're working with a support team in another world, establish a protocol: if they don't hear from you by a certain time, they come in after you, or they activate a recall, or they collapse the portal. This requires communication capability across the transit boundary, which may or may not be possible depending on your method.

### C. The Possibility of Not Getting Out

Address this before you go. There is a non-trivial probability that you will enter Keth Anoul and be unable to leave. The geometry is non-Euclidean. The topology changes. Linear progress may be illusory. Your exit point may be moved, destroyed, or rendered unreachable by spatial reconfiguration. You may walk for days and end up where you started—or nowhere you recognize.

If you cannot accept this risk, do not go.

If you can accept it, plan for an extended stay. Carry more water and food than you think you need. Carry materials for building a more permanent shelter (a lightweight bivouac sack, a space blanket, extra insulation). Carry a water recycling kit if such a thing exists in your technical context—a system for reclaiming water from urine and breath condensate. In the ontological dryness of Keth Anoul, such a system may not function normally (if water has been subtracted from the possibility space, can you create new water from hydrogen and oxygen?), but it's worth carrying as a hedge.

## VI. Interaction Protocols

### A. With the City

Keth Anoul may or may not be sapient. It behaves like a process, but processes can have intelligence. The reconfiguration may be purposeful. It may be responsive to your presence. The description notes that the city rearranges itself "for no one, or for you, or for the difference between the two."

**Approach the city as though it is aware of you** until you have strong evidence otherwise. This means:

- Be respectful of the space. Do not deface, chip, or sample the stone without consideration. If the stone is part of a living process, damaging it could provoke a response.
- Observe the city's behavior toward you. Does the avenue lead you somewhere? Do doors open in your path? Do bridges form when you need to cross a canyon? If the city is facilitating your movement, follow where it leads—cautiously, with your wits about you, but follow. It may be trying to show you something.
- Conversely: does the avenue dead-end? Do doors close? Do bridges disappear? If the city is impeding your movement, stop. Retreat to your last stable position. Try a different direction. Do not force passage through a space the city is closing to you.
- Try communicating. This sounds absurd—talking to a stone city—but the city communicates through spatial change. You might communicate through spatial action: stacking stones, arranging pebbles (if you can find loose ones), walking in deliberate patterns. Whether the city can perceive or interpret your actions is unknown, but the cost of trying is low and the potential benefit is enormous.

### B. With the Creatures

As noted above: observe, don't approach. If a creature appears near you—within ten meters, say—remain still. Let it observe you. If it shows any sign of intentional interaction (moving toward you between discontinuities, appearing repeatedly in your path, orienting toward you in a way that suggests communication), respond cautiously. Slow movements. Non-threatening posture. Mirror its behavior if possible—stillness for stillness.

The pale entity on the canyon wall deserves special mention. Given its size (house-sized) and its disappearance, it may be something qualitatively different from the smaller creatures. It may be something you do not want to interact with at all. Trust your instincts. If you feel fear when you see it—the deep, visceral, evolutionary fear that says *this is a predator*—treat it as a predator until proven otherwise. That fear exists for a reason, even across worlds.

### C. With the Grey-Violet Films

Leave them alone unless you are desperate. They are the closest thing to life in the city (besides the creatures), and they may serve a function you don't understand. If you must interact—say, to test whether they're biological—take the smallest possible sample from the most abundant patch you can find, and observe whether the city's reconfiguration behavior changes afterward. If it does, put the sample back if you can.

## VII. Companionship

**Do not go alone if you can avoid it.**

A companion in Keth Anoul is not merely a comfort or a safety measure—they are a cognitive anchor. When the world rearranges itself and your sense of spatial reality degrades, another person confirms what you saw, contradicts your hallucinations, remembers what you've forgotten, and watches what you can't watch. They extend your circle of observation. They take watch while you sleep. They carry extra water and food. They talk to you in the silence.

An ideal team is three people—one to explore, one to observe the explorer's surroundings, one to rest. Minimum is two. More than four is probably counterproductive—the logistical burden of food and water increases faster than the benefit of additional observers.

**Choose your companions carefully.** You need people who are:
- Psychologically resilient. Not fearless—you want people who feel fear appropriately—but capable of functioning under sustained uncertainty and strangeness.
- Physically fit and altitude-experienced.
- Observant and articulate. The ability to describe what you're seeing, precisely and quickly, is a survival skill here.
- Compatible under stress. People you trust. People you've been in difficult situations with before.
- Uninclined to recklessness. The city invites exploration, and reckless curiosity in Keth Anoul is how people disappear.

## VIII. What You Are Trying to Learn (And Why It Matters)

Before you go, articulate your purpose. Not because the city cares about your purpose, but because you need a framework to organize your observations, a criterion for deciding when you've learned enough to leave, and a reason to resist the city's seductive pull toward infinite exploration.

Possible research questions:
- **What drives the reconfiguration?** Is it random? Is it purposeful? Does it follow a pattern that repeats? Is it responsive to observers or independent of them?
- **What is the relationship between observation and stability?** This is perhaps the most important empirical question. Test it rigorously with cameras, continuous observation, timed intervals of looking and not-looking. The answer has implications not just for Keth Anoul but for the nature of reality in this branch of the multiverse.
- **Who built Keth Anoul?** Were they human? Are they gone? Are they the creatures? Are they the city itself?
- **What happened to the water?** "Subtracted from the possibility space" is a description, not an explanation. Is this a local phenomenon? Does it extend beyond the city? Is it spreading?
- **Can the city's spatial manipulation be understood, predicted, or utilized?** If space here is mutable—if adjacency is negotiable—could you learn to navigate by the city's rules rather than Euclidean rules? Could you, in effect, learn to *think like the city*?

Choose your questions. Pursue them methodically. Record everything. And when your time limit arrives—when your watches say it's time to go—go.

## IX. A Final Note on Mindset

The Risen City of Keth Anoul is not hostile. Nothing in the description suggests malice. The city is not trying to trap you or kill you or absorb you. It is simply *being what it is*, a vast lithic process operating on principles you don't understand, in a space that doesn't obey the rules you grew up with, in a silence that has outlasted civilizations.

The danger is not the city. The danger is you—your assumptions, your need for the world to be stable and navigable and comprehensible, your panic when it isn't, your exhaustion, your dehydration, your altitude sickness, your reckless curiosity, your despair.

Go in humility. Go in curiosity. Go with good boots and full water bottles and a companion who will tell you when it's time to leave. Watch the city do what it does, and let yourself be amazed without needing to understand. Understanding may come later, or never.

The city was here before you. It will be here after you. It does not need you to solve it. It only needs the next configuration, and the next, and the next.

You need to come home.

Pack accordingly.
