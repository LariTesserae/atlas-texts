# Preparing for the Cistern Quarter

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this place will resist your instincts as a traveler. There is nothing to conquer here, nothing to discover in the conventional sense, nothing that will yield to impatience or reveal itself to haste. The Cistern Quarter is a place that has already finished becoming what it is. You are visiting a conclusion. Your preparation should orient you accordingly—not as an explorer, but as a guest entering a room that was complete before you arrived and will remain complete after you leave.

The multiverse contains places of spectacle, danger, wonder, hostility. This is none of those things, or at least not in any way that announces itself. The danger here is subtle and mostly self-inflicted. The wonder is the kind that accumulates slowly and may not register until days after you've left. Prepare for that. Prepare to be underwhelmed at first and then to realize, gradually, that something fundamental has shifted in how you think about permanence, shelter, and what it means for a people to have found their final form.

---

## Physical Preparation

### The Approach

The mountain is old, eroded, and two thousand meters high. The summit is a worn dome of basalt. This means the approach will not be technically difficult in the mountaineering sense—no vertical pitches, no ice, no exposed ridgelines—but it will be long, grinding, and deceptive. Rounded mountains with gentle profiles often lull you into underestimating the cumulative toll of sustained uphill travel over terrain that never quite gives you a crisis to focus on. You just walk upward, and upward, and the altitude accumulates.

**Fitness:** You should be comfortable with sustained hiking at altitude. Not elite fitness, but the kind of cardiovascular baseline that allows you to walk for six to eight hours uphill without your judgment degrading. The entrance is a shoulder-width crack in a cliff face. You'll need to identify it, which means you'll need to be cognitively sharp when you arrive at approximately two thousand meters, possibly in cloud cover, possibly wet, possibly after a long and featureless ascent. Train for endurance, not intensity. Long walks with a pack on hilly terrain. Do this for at least six weeks before departure.

**Altitude acclimatization:** Two thousand meters is not extreme altitude, but if you're arriving from sea level in another variant, it's enough to cause mild symptoms—headache, fatigue, mild nausea—especially if you ascend quickly. Spend at least one night at an intermediate elevation if possible. Hydrate aggressively on the way up. The irony is that you're climbing to a place defined by water, but the approach itself may be dry, windy, and dehydrating, depending on the exterior climate.

**The entrance squeeze:** You will need to turn sideways and press through a crack barely shoulder-width. This is not negotiable. If you have a large frame, you need to know your shoulder breadth and your ability to compress through narrow rock passages. Practice if you can—find a slot canyon or a caving environment and test your comfort with lateral squeezes. The rock will be wet. You will be pressing your chest and back against slick basalt. Claustrophobia is not a theoretical concern here; it's a practical gate. If you cannot tolerate sustained contact with close rock walls, if the feeling of stone against your ribs triggers panic, you need to address this before you go. Breathing exercises, exposure therapy, whatever works for you. The entrance is the bottleneck, literally and psychologically. Once you're through, the chamber opens up, but "opens up" is relative—the whole inhabited zone is four hundred paces across at most.

### What to Bring (and What Not to Bring)

**Clothing:**

The exterior approach will likely be cold, windy, and damp—permanent low cloud cover sits over the highlands, and at two thousand meters, that cloud layer may envelop you directly. Wear layers you can shed quickly. A wind-resistant outer layer, a moisture-wicking base layer, an insulating mid-layer. Standard mountain kit.

But here's the critical thing: the moment you pass through the crack, the environment changes completely. Inside, the air is warm and humid. Perpetually. The rock weeps. Every surface is filmed with condensation. Your mountain clothing—designed to retain heat and repel external moisture—will immediately become a liability. You will begin sweating inside your insulation layer, and because the air is saturated, that sweat will have nowhere to go. Within an hour you'll be as wet from the inside as the walls are from the outside.

Pack a change of clothing for the interior. Something minimal, light, fast-drying if possible but understand that nothing truly dries in there. A single thin layer—linen, if you can get it, or a light synthetic. Something that can be wet against your skin without chilling you, which in the warm interior air means almost anything lightweight will do. The residents likely wear very little. Take your cue from them. Bare feet are probably the right call once you're inside, too—the floors are polished stone, worn smooth, and wet. Boots will be dangerously slippery and will mark you as an outsider more than anything else you could do. You want skin on stone. Your feet will adjust.

Bring a dry bag or waterproof compression sack for your exterior clothing so you have something dry to put on when you leave.

**Footwear for the approach:** Sturdy, broken-in hiking boots with good ankle support and aggressive tread. The basalt will be slick with mineral seep even outside the entrance. Consider approach shoes if you're confident in your ankle stability.

**Footwear for the interior:** None. Seriously. The polished stone floors have been shaped by bare feet over millennia. Every groove, every curve, every worn handhold assumes bare skin contact. Shoes will rob you of the tactile information you need to navigate safely on wet stone, and they will put you at a biomechanical disadvantage on surfaces that have been optimized for an unshod gait. If you absolutely cannot go barefoot—if you have a medical condition, a foot injury, sensitivity—then thin-soled water shoes with good grip, the kind used for river walking. But you will lose something. The residents read the floor with their feet. You should try to do the same.

**Light sources:** Bring a small, reliable light source. A headlamp with a low setting, or better yet, a small handheld light you can dim to almost nothing. The Cistern Quarter exists in a state of luminous dampness—not darkness, not light, but an in-between that your eyes will adjust to over several hours. A bright light will be violent here. It will flatten the subtle gradations of shadow that define the space, it will hurt the eyes of residents adapted to near-darkness, and it will destroy your own dark adaptation. Use your light sparingly, at the lowest possible setting, and only when you genuinely cannot see. In the deeper alcoves where the ceiling drops below the reach of the light shafts, even the residents use flame—small wicks in fat dishes. If you can source or improvise something similar, that would be more appropriate than any electric light. The warm orange glow of a small flame belongs here in a way that LEDs do not.

That said: carry the headlamp. If you become disoriented—and in a space this dark, this convoluted, this unfamiliar, you may—you'll want a reliable way to reorient yourself quickly. Safety supersedes etiquette.

**Water and food:** Here is where the preparation becomes philosophically interesting.

*Water:* You are entering a place that is literally defined by its water. The entire settlement is a water-management system. Drinking the local water—warm, mineralized, faintly green with copper salts—is probably the single most significant act of participation available to you. It's the thing the residents share, the thing the mountain provides, the thing that gives the quarter its name and its reason for existing.

But you don't know the microbiology. The mineral water may host organisms your gut has never encountered. The biofilms coating the wet stone are an unknown. The copper salt concentration might be within safe limits or might not—chronic copper intake is a real concern, though a short visit likely won't reach problematic thresholds. The microbial communities in the reservoir, whatever blind swimming things inhabit it—you don't know what's in that water.

My recommendation: bring a portable water treatment option. A filter with activated carbon would address both particulates and some dissolved metals. A UV purifier as backup. But here's the thing—use them discreetly, or better yet, pre-filter away from the communal reservoir. Visibly treating the shared water source before drinking from it would be, in almost any culture, an insult. You're telling your hosts their water isn't good enough. In a place where water is the foundational infrastructure, the sacred resource, the thing the mountain provides—that insult would be profound. Filter quietly. Or accept the risk and drink as they drink, understanding that you're making a choice about trust and participation that carries real physiological stakes.

Bring your own water for the approach and keep some in reserve for the exit. Inside, drink what they drink, one way or another.

*Food:* Bring your own for the approach and enough to sustain you during your visit without being a burden on the settlement's resources. The Cistern Quarter's food supply appears to be extremely limited—etiolated cress, a wiry astringent herb, whatever can be grown in shallow soil troughs under minimal light, plus whatever organic matter enters from above (which is likely very little). The ecosystem is stripped to essentials. There is no surplus here. Every calorie matters. Do not eat their food unless explicitly offered, and even then, eat sparingly and offer something in return if you can do so without disruption.

Bring compact, calorie-dense food that doesn't require cooking (you have no fuel source inside, and the residents' rendered fat wicks are for light, not cooking—or maybe they are for cooking too, but you shouldn't assume access to them). Nuts, dried fruit, dense grain bars, cured meat if available. Things that don't crumble and leave residue—in a closed ecosystem this tight, your crumbs become an ecological input. Be meticulous about waste.

**Tools and equipment:**

- A small waterproof notebook and a graphite pencil (not a pen—ink will run in the humidity). You will want to record observations, and your phone or tablet, if you even have one, will fog instantly in the saturated air and may not recover.
- A compact thermometer and hygrometer if you're scientifically inclined. The microclimate data would be fascinating.
- Ziplock bags for anything that must stay dry.
- A thin, quick-dry towel. You will be wet constantly. Being able to dry your hands before handling your gear is a small luxury with large practical benefits.
- Minimal first aid: bandages, antiseptic (cuts in a warm, wet, enclosed environment will want to infect), blister care, basic pain relief.
- Nothing metal if you can avoid it, or keep metal items sealed away. The description specifies no metal in the settlement. This may be resource-driven (no accessible ore, no trade for metal goods) or it may be cultural, and you won't know which until you're there. Either way, flashing steel or aluminum in a stone-and-bone-tool culture is a disruption. If your headlamp has a metal casing, it's fine—you'll be using it sparingly. But don't bring a knife conspicuously on your belt. Bring one, but keep it in your pack.

**What not to bring:**

- Anything loud. No speakers, no alarms, no devices that beep. Sound doesn't carry here—it puddles, then dies. But that means even small sounds are intensely local and intrusive.
- Anything with a strong scent. The air is still, warm, and humid. Scent molecules will hang in place. Perfume, scented sunscreen, strong soap—all of these will be overwhelming in an enclosed space with no ventilation to speak of. The residents' olfactory landscape is wet stone, mineral water, rendered fat smoke, their own bodies, and the sharp astringent herb. Don't pollute it.
- Anything you can't afford to lose to moisture. Assume 100% humidity for the duration of your visit. Assume everything you bring will be filmed with condensation within an hour. Plan accordingly.

## Psychological and Cognitive Preparation

This is where the real preparation happens, and where most travelers will be most underprepared.

### Confinement

The Cistern Quarter is four hundred paces across, roughly, with a ceiling lower than it is wide. The paths are not straight. The spaces are tight—sleeping niches just deep enough for a body, walkways barely wide enough for two people to pass. You will be enclosed in wet stone for the duration of your visit, with no horizon, no sky (just the faintest grey glow from fist-width shafts), no open space, and no easy exit—the entrance is a shoulder-width crack in a cliff face, and if something goes wrong, you can't just step outside for air.

Some people will find this immediately intolerable. If you have any history of claustrophobia, confinement anxiety, or panic in enclosed spaces, you need to do serious preparatory work. This isn't a cave tour where you walk through a well-lit passage for an hour and emerge into daylight. This is immersive. The rock is close. The air is heavy. The light is dim. There is no outside to look at, no outside to think toward. The mountain is all around you, above and below and on every side, and it presses in not aggressively but inescapably.

Practice being in enclosed spaces. Start with something manageable—a closet with the door closed, a small bathroom with the lights off—and work up to actual caving or underground environments if you can access them. The key is to train your nervous system to recognize enclosure without triggering a fight-or-flight response. Breathing techniques: slow, deliberate breathing, exhaling longer than you inhale. Box breathing. Whatever modality works for you. Practice it until it's automatic, because you won't have the cognitive bandwidth to think through a technique if you're already panicking.

And understand this: the confinement may not hit you at the entrance. It may hit you on the second day, when the novelty has worn off and the reality of the space has settled into your body. Delayed confinement response is common. Prepare for it.

### Darkness

Your eyes will adjust. Human dark adaptation is remarkable, and in the Cistern Quarter you're not dealing with total darkness—there's the grey diffusion from the light shafts, the small steady flames in their halos of condensation. But it will take time, possibly an hour or more for your scotopic vision to fully engage, and during that adjustment period you may feel functionally blind in the deeper passages. This is disorienting in a space you don't know.

Spend time in darkness before you go. Not sleeping darkness—waking darkness. Sit in a room with no light and keep your eyes open. Move around. Navigate by touch. Get comfortable with the idea that your visual system is providing incomplete information and that your other senses—touch, hearing, proprioception—will need to carry more of the load. In the Cistern Quarter, you'll be navigating wet stone passages by the feel of the polished floor under your feet, by the sound of water dripping at different pitches in different basins, by the faint warmth gradients that tell you whether you're near a wall or an open space. Let your body learn to do this.

### Time Distortion

There is no day-night cycle inside the mountain. The light from the shafts varies only in degree of dimness, modulated by cloud cover outside, and that variation is subtle. There are no clocks, no schedules you'll be able to detect. The residents operate on rhythms you won't immediately understand—the slow pulse of the water table, the seasonal seep rate, the sub-audible hum of the mountain breathing. These are not rhythms your body knows.

You will lose track of time almost immediately. Hours will compress or expand unpredictably. A period that feels like thirty minutes may have been four hours. A long, slow passage through the quarter may have taken twenty minutes. This is normal in lightless or near-lightless environments, and it's intensified here by the absence of sound cues, the constant temperature, and the unchanging humidity.

If maintaining a time reference matters to you, bring a watch—a mechanical one, ideally, since electronics may struggle in the humidity. Check it periodically. But also consider whether maintaining your external time frame is actually necessary or desirable. You're visiting a place that thinks in stone time. Trying to impose clock time on that experience may be not only futile but actively counterproductive. You might learn more by letting go and allowing the quarter's own temporal rhythms to take hold. That's a choice you'll have to make, and there's no wrong answer, but think about it beforehand.

### The Sound

The description is precise and important: the dripping is not background noise. It's the dominant acoustic feature of the environment, an arrhythmic, unpatterned composite of a thousand individual water drops, each with its own pitch and timing. It will fill your auditory field completely. It will never resolve into a recognizable pattern. It will shape your thoughts without your conscious awareness.

If you've ever experienced sensory environments that produce this effect—ocean surf, heavy rain on a tin roof, the hum of a large machine—you have some reference point, but the cistern's dripping is different because it's so close, so multiple, so lacking in any organizing rhythm. It's water doing what water does, unsynchronized, patient, endless.

Some people find this deeply calming. Others find it maddening. There's a specific form of acoustic distress that arises from prolonged exposure to arrhythmic sound in enclosed spaces—not because the sound is loud or unpleasant, but because the brain keeps trying to find a pattern and keeps failing. If you're pattern-sensitive, if you're the kind of person who can't sleep with an irregular drip, this will be challenging. Bring earplugs as a safety valve, but use them sparingly—the sound is carrying information. The residents read the dripping the way they read the hum. If you block it out, you're blocking out the quarter's primary communication channel with itself.

Better to prepare by pre-exposing yourself to arrhythmic water sounds. Find a place with multiple drip sources—a cave, a leaky greenhouse, a fountain room—and spend extended time there. Let the pattern-seeking exhaust itself. Reach the point where you can hear the composite sound without trying to resolve it, and then sit in that space. That's where the Cistern Quarter lives, acoustically. Get comfortable there before you arrive.

### The Hum

Below the dripping, there's a low, nearly sub-audible resonance—the mountain breathing through its shafts. This is felt as much as heard. It will vibrate in your chest, your sinuses, possibly your teeth. It cycles slowly, linked to atmospheric pressure changes outside. The residents read it the way sailors read the wind.

You may find the hum unsettling. Low-frequency sound can trigger unease, anxiety, even a vague sense of dread in some people—this is well-documented. It can also induce a meditative state, a deepening of presence, a feeling of being held by something vast. Your response will be personal and somewhat unpredictable. Be aware that it's there. Be aware that it's affecting you even if you can't consciously hear it. If you feel a sudden unexplained shift in mood—anxiety, sadness, awe, calm—consider that the hum may have shifted. This is not mystical. It's psychoacoustic. The residents have internalized it over generations. You'll be experiencing it raw.

## Cultural and Social Preparation

### You Are a Guest in a Terminal Culture

The Cistern Quarter has reached its final form. Everything that could erode has eroded. Everything that could be carved has been carved. The ceiling is full of marks—no room for more. The sleeping niches have reached shapes that the rock seems to want to hold. The water system is as much geological as it is engineered. The settlement and the mountain have merged.

This means you are not visiting a developing culture. You are visiting a culture that has arrived. It's done. Not dead—people live there, work there, set down stone tools, murmur to each other—but complete in a way that almost no human settlement you've encountered will be. There's nothing to build, nothing to discover, nothing to expand into. The quarter has found its stable state.

What does this mean for you as a visitor? It means that novelty—your novelty, the novelty you represent—is a disruption in a system that has minimized disruption to near zero. You are, in thermodynamic terms, a perturbation in an equilibrium state. Be aware of this. Be gentle about it. Move slowly. Speak softly (the space enforces this anyway). Don't touch the carved walls unnecessarily—your touch adds to the cumulative erosion, and in a place where the marks on the ceiling are literally full, every touch matters. Don't leave things behind. Don't take things with you. Your presence is already an event; minimize its footprint.

### Communication

You will almost certainly not share a language with the residents. Prepare for non-verbal communication. Learn to read body language in near-darkness—posture, the pace of movement, the angle of a head tilt. The residents pause mid-task to listen to the hum. You should learn to recognize that pause and to respect it. When someone is listening to the mountain, don't interrupt.

Gestures: be cautious. Gestures that seem universal often aren't. Pointing may or may not be appropriate. Smiling may or may not convey friendliness in a culture where faces are rarely fully visible in the dim light. Focus on mirroring—if they move slowly, move slowly. If they speak in murmurs, murmur. If they sit, sit. If they offer you water from the reservoir, take it (see earlier notes on water safety and the risks/benefits calculation).

Gift-giving: this is complicated in a culture with no surplus. Anything you bring from outside is, by definition, alien to the quarter's ecosystem and material culture. Metal tools would be revolutionary and potentially destructive to a stone-tool equilibrium. Food would be welcome but ephemeral. The most appropriate gift might be nothing at all—your willingness to be present without demanding anything, to occupy space without reshaping it. If you feel compelled to offer something, consider something that consumes itself: a wick and fat for their lamps, perhaps, sourced from outside but compatible with their existing technology. Something that burns and is gone, leaving no lasting disruption.

### The Marks on the Ceiling

The ceiling is covered in shallow, overlapping tool marks—not writing, not pictures, but the accumulated record of every hand that has reached up and pressed a tool to the stone. Each mark is different. Each mark is the same gesture. The ceiling is full.

Do not add your mark. I say this with emphasis. The ceiling is full. That is part of its meaning. The fullness is the message. Adding to it—even if invited, which you probably won't be—would be like adding a brushstroke to a finished painting. The completion is the point. Observe it. Sit beneath it. Wait for the clouds to thin and the light to ripple across the water and illuminate it, briefly, before the dimness returns. That moment is the closest thing the Cistern Quarter has to spectacle, and it lasts only seconds. Be present for it. Don't try to capture it. There's nothing to capture. It's just light on stone, and then it's gone.

### Respect for the Dead

In a sealed community that has existed for millennia in an enclosed space with no surplus and minimal organic input, the dead must go somewhere. You will not see a cemetery—there's no room for one, and burial in the stone floor would disrupt the water channels. The most likely possibilities: the dead are returned to the reservoir (which would explain the dissolved organic matter that feeds the microbial biofilms and whatever swims in that water), or they are carried out through the entrance and left on the mountain, or they are cremated using rendered fat. You may encounter evidence of whatever practice they use. Do not investigate. Do not ask, even by gesture. If you inadvertently witness a death rite, retreat quietly and give them space. This is not cultural tourism. Death in a community this small, this enclosed, this stable is an event of profound structural significance—a niche is empty, a channel goes untended, a hand that read the hum is gone. Respect the weight of that.

## Logistical Planning

### Duration of Visit

Stay long enough for your eyes to fully adjust, for the dripping to become part of your internal landscape, for the hum to register in your body. This means a minimum of two full sleep cycles—whatever "sleep cycles" mean in a place with no day or night. Roughly forty-eight hours, though it will feel like both more and less than that.

Don't stay longer than you're welcome. You'll know. In a culture this attuned to its environment, a guest who has overstayed will be felt before they're told. If people begin to route their paths to avoid you, if the murmured conversations near you drop to silence, if no one meets your eyes in the brief moments of adequate light—leave. Thank them in whatever way seems right (a bow, a palm pressed briefly to the wall, mirroring whatever farewell gesture you've observed) and go.

### Navigation

You will get lost. The paths don't run straight. The junctions have been worn into smooth curves. The dim light makes every passage look similar. The wet stone reflects what little light there is, creating false depth cues. You'll turn a corner and not remember which way you came.

Develop a mental map as you move, using tactile and acoustic landmarks. This basin has a higher-pitched drip. This wall has a deep scallop pattern. This junction curves left and the floor dips slightly. Don't mark the stone—no chalk marks, no scratches. Use your notebook instead: draw a map as you go, noting landmarks by feel and sound. The central reservoir is the anchor point; it's the lowest point in the chamber, so water always flows toward it. If you're lost, find the direction water is moving and follow it downhill. You'll reach the reservoir, and from there you can reorient.

### Exiting

The exit is the entrance. The same shoulder-width crack. Leaving will be, in some ways, harder than arriving. Your eyes, adapted to near-darkness, will be savaged by even the dim grey light of the cloud-covered highlands. The cold will be a shock after the warm humidity. The wind will feel violent after the still air. The open sky—even a grey, overcast sky—will feel like vertigo after the close ceiling.

Bring sunglasses for the exit, even though there's no sun. Your dark-adapted eyes may find even heavy overcast painfully bright. Give yourself time in the entrance crack to begin readjusting—linger there, in the transition zone, letting the light increase gradually. Don't burst out into the open. Ease yourself back into the world.

And be prepared for a period of disorientation after you leave. You've been immersed in a sensory environment profoundly different from anything your nervous system considers normal. The open air, the wind, the brightness, the sound that carries instead of puddling, the dry rock, the visible horizon—all of these will feel simultaneously familiar and strange. You've been thinking in stone time. Coming back to the pace of the outside world takes adjustment. Don't plan to do anything demanding on the day you exit. Descend carefully. The mountain is as slippery going down as it was coming up, and your balance may be off after spending days on polished wet floors in darkness.

## Emotional Preparation

This is the part I can least prescribe and most want to address.

The Cistern Quarter will change something in you, if you let it. Not dramatically. Not in the way that places of great beauty or great danger change you. It will change you the way water changes stone—slowly, through contact, through the accumulation of small impressions that individually mean nothing and collectively reshape the surface.

You will come out thinking differently about time. About what it means to live somewhere permanently—not for a lifetime but for a civilization's span, until the place and the people are the same thing. About what it means for a culture to be finished, to have reached its final form, to have no more ceiling to carve.

You may come out quieter. Sound will seem louder and cruder to you for a while. You may find yourself listening for a hum that isn't there, tilting your head the way they do, pausing mid-task to attend to something you can't hear.

You may come out grieving, though you won't be able to say for what. The Cistern Quarter is not sad. It's not dying. But its completeness has a quality that is hard to sit with—the sense that everything that can happen has happened, that the only remaining motion is the slow dissolution of the mountain itself, bead by bead, into the water table. It's the grief of witnessing something that doesn't need you, that doesn't need anyone, that has found its equilibrium and will remain in it until geology itself shifts. Your own impermanence will feel very vivid after that.

Prepare for this by—I don't know. By sitting with the idea of it beforehand. By understanding that you're visiting a place that is, in the deepest sense, at peace, and that being in the presence of that kind of peace is not always peaceful. It can be confronting. It can make your own restlessness, your own unfinishedness, your own need to keep moving through the multiverse, feel suddenly very loud.

Let it be loud. Let the Cistern Quarter do what it does—absorb the sound, soften the edges, wear the sharpness down. You won't stay long enough for it to finish the job. Nobody does, except the people who were born there. But you'll carry the beginning of it with you, the way water carries dissolved stone—invisibly, in solution, changing the taste of everything that follows.

## Summary Checklist

**Physical gear:**
- Sturdy boots for approach, bare feet for interior
- Layered exterior clothing, minimal quick-dry interior clothing
- Dry bag for clothing storage
- Dim headlamp (low setting, red light if available)
- Waterproof notebook and graphite pencil
- Water treatment (filter + UV, used discreetly)
- Compact calorie-dense food (no-cook, minimal waste)
- Thin towel
- Basic first aid with emphasis on wound care in wet environments
- Watch (mechanical preferred)
- Sunglasses for exit
- Earplugs (emergency use)

**Physical preparation:**
- Six weeks of endurance hiking with pack
- Altitude acclimatization plan
- Enclosed-space exposure practice
- Darkness navigation practice
- Bare-foot walking on wet stone practice

**Psychological preparation:**
- Confinement tolerance training
- Arrhythmic sound exposure
- Time-loss acceptance
- Letting go of visual dominance

**Cultural preparation:**
- Practice non-verbal communication and mirroring
- Internalize a leave-no-trace ethic beyond the physical—leave no cultural trace either
- Accept that you will not fully understand this place
- Accept that that's appropriate

**Emotional preparation:**
- Sit with the concept of completion
- Sit with the concept of irrelevance—yours, specifically
- Be willing to be changed slowly, by contact, by the accumulation of small impressions
- Be willing to leave

The dripping will continue after you go. It was continuing before you came. The mountain doesn't care, but it accommodates, the way it accommodates everything, the way water accommodates stone and stone accommodates water, each one shaping the other so slowly that the shaping itself becomes a kind of stillness.

Go. Be careful on the wet steps. Listen for the hum. Drink the water if you can bring yourself to. Come back out. Descend. Remember.
