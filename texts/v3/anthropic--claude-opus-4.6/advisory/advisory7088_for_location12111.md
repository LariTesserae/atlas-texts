# Preparing for the Underhalls of the Ceramic Dark

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with this: you are entering a living construction. Not a ruin, not a museum, not a cave system. The Underhalls are a world that is still curing, still deciding what it will become, and you will be inside its body while it does so. This changes everything about how you prepare, because the usual categories — is it dangerous? is it cold? how far is the exit? — are subordinate to a more fundamental question: *what does it mean to be a soft, biological visitor inside a hard, mineral, resonant structure that is actively becoming?*

The answer is: you will be profoundly alone in a way that has nothing to do with other people. You could bring companions and you would still feel it. The Underhalls are not hostile to life, but they are *indifferent* to it in a way that goes beyond the indifference of a desert or an ocean. A desert was never built. An ocean doesn't ring when struck. This place was shaped by hands — you will see the fingerprints, thousands of them — and yet it has moved beyond whatever intention shaped it, and it does not care that you are there, and this not-caring will press against you the way the sound presses against you, physically, in your chest, behind your eyes.

Prepare for that. Prepare psychologically for an environment that acknowledges your presence only as an acoustic phenomenon — your footsteps will ring, your voice will propagate through chambers you cannot see, and the architecture will treat your sounds exactly the way it treats the condensation drips and the deep percussive booming: as material to be shaped, channeled, transformed. You will hear yourself reflected back in ways that don't sound like you. This is disorienting. It is not malicious.

## Physical Preparation

### Conditioning Your Body

**Knees and joints.** There are crawlways. Low passages connecting chambers, where the only way through is hands and knees on glazed ceramic tile that is smooth, slightly convex, and slick with condensation. Your kneecaps will be in direct contact with fired clay. If you have any joint issues, any old injuries, any reluctance to crawl — address these before you go. Spend weeks beforehand doing knee-conditioning work. Practice crawling on hard surfaces for extended periods. Not because any single crawlway is extraordinarily long, but because you don't know how many you'll encounter, you don't know the layout, and you cannot predict when you'll need to retreat.

**Core and back.** Some chambers require you to duck. The ceiling heights vary without warning — twenty feet in one room, five in the next. You will spend time in semi-crouched postures, moving cautiously in near-darkness, uncertain of the clearance above you. Your lower back will bear the cost of this. Strengthen it. Yoga, deadlifts, whatever works for your body. The goal is to be able to maintain a half-crouch for extended periods without debilitating fatigue.

**Cardiovascular fitness.** The air is damp but not thin — there's no indication of altitude or oxygen deprivation. However, the emotional and sensory intensity of the environment will elevate your heart rate. The booming alone has physiological effects: when you approach the central column, the vibration becomes a physical pressure against your face and body. Your cardiovascular system will respond to this as if you are under exertion even when you are standing still. Arrive in good baseline condition.

**Hearing.** Get your hearing tested before you go. Know your baseline. The acoustic environment in the Underhalls is unlike anything you have experienced. The ceramic surfaces are highly reflective to sound, and the chambers are tuned — deliberately tuned — so that the same source sound is transformed differently in every room. In some rooms, the bass frequencies will blur your vision. In others, sharp overtones will seem to originate inside your skull. If you have any existing hearing sensitivity, tinnitus, or auditory processing issues, you need to understand them fully before entering an environment that will test every frequency your ears can detect.

### Acclimating to Darkness

This is perhaps the most important physical preparation, and most travelers underestimate it.

The Underhalls are dark. Not pitch-black everywhere — the phosphorescent tiles provide marker-light in cold blue-green patches — but functionally dark. The distances between illuminated spots vary enormously: sometimes three glowing tiles within arm's reach, sometimes forty paces of absolute blackness before the next one. Your eyes will begin to adjust, but the description is explicit: *they never fully adjust*. The variation in light density prevents your pupils from settling into a stable dilation. You will be in a constant state of partial adaptation, which is more fatiguing than either full darkness or full light.

**Train for this.** In the weeks before your visit, spend increasing amounts of time in near-darkness. Not just sitting in a dark room — *moving* in near-darkness. Set up a safe indoor space with small, irregularly spaced light sources (LED tea candles work) and practice navigating between them. Walk the spaces between them with your eyes open, learning to use peripheral vision, learning to trust the faint information your retinas provide in scotopic conditions. Practice the transition — walking from a cluster of lights into a zone of absolute darkness, then finding the next light. Your goal is not to see in the dark. Your goal is to be *comfortable not seeing*, to learn to navigate by sound and touch and spatial memory when vision fails.

**Learn to use sound for navigation.** In the Underhalls, your footsteps on the ceramic tile will return to you as echoes, and these echoes carry information about the shape and size of the space you're in. This is rudimentary echolocation, and humans can learn it. Practice before you go. Walk through your training space with your eyes closed, clicking your tongue or snapping your fingers, and try to sense walls, openings, changes in ceiling height. You won't become a bat, but you'll develop enough sensitivity to distinguish between a corridor (tight, quick echoes) and a large chamber (diffuse, delayed return), and in the Underhalls, this distinction might be the difference between confident movement and paralyzing uncertainty.

## Gear and Equipment

### Footwear

This is your most critical equipment decision. The floors are glazed ceramic tile — enormous convex slabs with narrow mortar seams. When dry, the glaze has moderate traction. When damp with condensation, it becomes slick. You need footwear that provides:

- **Excellent wet-surface grip.** Rubber soles designed for wet tile or polished stone. Not hiking boots — the aggressive lugs of a trail sole will be wrong for this surface. Think instead of approach shoes or high-quality indoor climbing shoes with flat, sticky rubber soles. Some travelers have reported success with water shoes designed for tidal rock walking.
- **Low acoustic profile.** You need to hear. The Underhalls communicate through sound — the booming, the dripping, the skittering of the arthropod fauna, the echoes that tell you the shape of rooms. Hard-soled shoes will produce such loud footstep echoes that they'll mask other sounds. Soft rubber. Minimal heel.
- **Thin soles.** You will feel the vibration through the floor. The deep percussion rises through the ceramic and into your body through your feet. This is not just an aesthetic experience — it's navigational information. The vibration intensifies as you approach its source. Thick, cushioned soles will muffle this signal. You want to feel the floor.
- **Knee protection compatibility.** Whatever shoes you choose, make sure they work with the knee pads you'll need for the crawlways. Test the combination before you enter.

My specific recommendation: a sticky-rubber approach shoe with a flat profile and thin sole, one size larger than normal to accommodate a moisture-wicking sock liner beneath a light wool outer sock. Break them in thoroughly before arrival.

### Knee Protection

Non-negotiable. Bring knee pads designed for tile-setters or flooring installers — these are specifically engineered for prolonged kneeling on hard, smooth surfaces. Avoid bulky tactical or sporting knee pads; you need a low profile because the crawlways may be tight. Gel-insert pads from a professional flooring supply company are ideal. Attach them securely enough that they won't shift during crawling but loosely enough that you can remove them quickly when standing and walking, because the bulk will alter your gait on the slick tile.

### Light Sources

This requires careful thought and restraint.

Your instinct will be to bring powerful illumination — headlamps, flashlights, lanterns — to banish the darkness and see clearly. Resist this instinct, or at least temper it severely. The Underhalls were designed for the light level they have. The phosphorescent tiles are placed deliberately. The architecture reveals itself *at the light level it provides*, and flooding a chamber with white LED light will not just change the aesthetic — it will change the information you receive. The spiral grooves, the fingerprints, the pale arthropods, the mineral formations: these are all optimized for visibility in dim blue-green phosphor-glow. Under harsh white light, the fingerprints will flatten, the creatures will flee, the phosphorescent tiles will be washed out and invisible, and you will see the geometry of the rooms without understanding their character.

Bring lights, but bring them thoughtfully:

- **Primary: a low-output red or amber headlamp.** Red light preserves dark adaptation and has minimal impact on the phosphorescent tiles (which emit in the blue-green spectrum and will remain visible under red illumination). Set it to its lowest useful output — 5 to 10 lumens, no more. Use it for the crawlways, for checking your footing on uncertain surfaces, for reading your notes. Keep it off when you don't need it.
- **Secondary: a compact white-light flashlight with adjustable output.** For emergencies, for examining something closely, for navigating if you become truly disoriented. But keep it in your pack unless you need it. Every minute of white light use costs you ten minutes of dark adaptation recovery.
- **Tertiary: chemical light sticks in low-intensity green.** These are your breadcrumbs. Crack one and leave it at the entrance to a crawlway, at a junction, at any point where you need to mark your path for return navigation. Their color is close enough to the phosphorescent tiles that they won't shatter the ambient light ecology, but distinct enough that you'll recognize them as yours. Bring at least twenty. Small ones. Half-inch diameter, two-inch length.
- **Do not bring UV lights.** You might think UV would enhance the phosphorescent tiles. It might. It also might alter them, accelerate their chemistry, cause them to fluoresce at intensities that damage them or change their emission characteristics permanently. You are a visitor. Do not alter the light.

### Sound Management

Bring earplugs — high-fidelity musician's earplugs that reduce volume evenly across frequencies without muffling or distorting. Not foam plugs, which will kill the high frequencies and leave you with only the bass, which will be overwhelming and nauseating. You need the earplugs for the central column chamber and any similar resonant nodes you encounter. The description is explicit: the vibration near the column becomes "a physical pressure against your face." At that intensity, unprotected exposure risks temporary threshold shift or worse. But you also need to *hear* the Underhalls to navigate and understand them, so the plugs must be high-fidelity, removable quickly, and kept accessible — clipped to a lanyard around your neck, not buried in a pocket.

**Consider bringing a recording device.** A small, high-quality field recorder with good low-frequency response. The acoustic environment of the Underhalls is, based on every indication, utterly unique — tuned ceramic architecture propagating complex percussion through a resonant lattice of chambers. This is worth documenting if you can do it without it becoming a distraction. Clip it to your chest, press record, forget about it. Review the recordings later. Do not spend your time in the Underhalls fussing with levels and microphone placement. Be present first. Document second.

### Clothing

- **Base layer:** Moisture-wicking synthetic or merino wool, long-sleeved, fitted close to the body. The temperature is "just below what your skin considers comfortable" — roughly 60-65°F / 15-18°C based on that description — and the humidity is high. Cotton will soak with condensation and sweat and leave you chilled. Avoid it entirely.
- **Mid layer:** A thin fleece or wool pullover. Not a puffy jacket — you'll be crawling, and bulk is your enemy in the low passages. Something with a close fit that moves with your body.
- **Outer layer:** Consider a thin, quiet softshell or wind shirt. Not for rain protection — there is no rain — but for the slight wind that moves through the ventilation shafts. Critically, it must be *quiet*. No crinkly nylon, no rustling Gore-Tex. The Underhalls are an acoustic environment, and your clothing is part of the soundscape. Anything that rustles or crinkles will produce noise that masks the subtle sounds you need to hear — the dripping, the arthropod movement, the distant harmonics.
- **Gloves:** Bring thin, grippy gloves for the crawlways — something like mechanics' gloves or light climbing gloves. The glazed tile is cool and slick, and prolonged hand contact will chill your fingers and reduce dexterity. But also bring the option of bare hands. You will want to touch the walls. You will want to press your palm against the fingerprints of the builders and feel the vibration transmitted through the ceramic into your bones. That passage at the end of the description — "the fingerprints of the builders press back against your skin, a perfect negative contact" — is not metaphor. It is the central experience of this place. Do not glove yourself out of it.
- **No jewelry, no buckles, no metal that might clink against ceramic.** You do not want to introduce extraneous sound into this acoustic space. It's a matter of both courtesy (to whatever processes are occurring) and practicality (metal striking ceramic will produce sharp, disorienting echoes that can mask navigation-relevant sounds for seconds).

### Navigation

There is no map. The description makes clear: the layout has no grid, no organizing axis. It grew like coral. You will not map it in a single visit, and attempting to do so will consume your attention and prevent you from experiencing the space.

Instead, prepare with:

- **The chemical light sticks mentioned above.** Mark your path at decision points.
- **A simple notation system.** Carry a small waterproof notebook and a pencil (not a pen — ink may not flow reliably in the humidity). At each junction or notable chamber, make a quick sketch: the number of exits, their approximate compass bearing (bring a compass — ceramic shouldn't interfere with magnetic readings, though verify this early in your visit), any distinguishing features of the room. Don't try for accuracy. Try for *recognizability* — enough information that if you see the sketch again while standing in that room, you'll know you've been there before.
- **A sense of the sound gradient.** The deep percussion is your one reliable directional signal. It gets louder as you approach its source, softer as you move away. Use it as a compass. "Toward the sound" and "away from the sound" are your two cardinal directions. Everything else is relative.
- **String or cord.** Old-fashioned, but there's a reason Theseus used it. Bring a lightweight, high-visibility cord — neon orange paracord, 200 feet minimum — and tie it off at your entry point. Pay it out as you go. In absolute darkness in an unmapped ceramic labyrinth, cord is the difference between exploration and being lost. The psychological value alone is worth the weight.

### Hydration and Nutrition

The condensation is explicitly described as carrying dissolved calcium and silicates from the ceramic body. **Do not drink the condensation.** It is not potable. It is mineral-laden water that is in the process of becoming stalactites. Ingesting it could range from mildly unpleasant to harmful depending on the specific mineral content and glaze chemistry — and you don't know the glaze chemistry. Some historical ceramic glazes contained lead, cadmium, barium. You have no way to test what's in this water.

Bring all your own water. Carry at least 3 liters for what you estimate as a day's exploration, in soft containers (hard bottles will clank against the ceramic, producing noise; they can also crack if dropped on the tile floor). Hydration bladder with a bite valve is ideal — hands-free, quiet, no fumbling in the dark.

Food: high-calorie, quiet to eat, minimal packaging. Trail mix, energy bars unwrapped and stored in a soft bag, dried fruit. No crinkly wrappers. Eat when you need to, but be aware that food smells may attract the arthropod fauna. This is probably not dangerous — the creatures seem small and timid — but you don't know enough about them to be certain, and attracting a clustering of unseen arthropods in near-darkness is an experience best avoided if possible.

**Pack out all waste.** Every crumb, every wrapper, every drop of urine. This is a pre-biological environment. You are possibly the first organic visitor in a very long time, or ever. Introducing biological material — food scraps, waste products, skin cells, shed hair — could be the seed of biological colonization that changes the character of the Underhalls permanently. You probably can't achieve perfect sterility, but you can minimize your biological footprint.

Bring a dedicated waste container: a wide-mouth screw-top bottle for liquid waste, sealable bags for solid waste and food scraps. Be meticulous.

## Psychological and Emotional Preparation

This section matters more than the gear list. Read it carefully.

### The Sound

You cannot turn it off. The deep percussion is continuous, or nearly so — clusters of blows separated by silences that are "almost long enough to forget" before the next cluster arrives. This is not background noise. It is foreground noise. It is *the dominant sensory experience of the Underhalls*, more present than the darkness, more constant than the dampness. It will be with you from the moment you enter to the moment you leave.

Prepare by understanding what sustained, unpredictable low-frequency sound does to the human body and mind:

- **Physiological effects:** Low-frequency vibration (infrasound and near-infrasound) can cause feelings of unease, anxiety, pressure in the chest, difficulty breathing, blurred vision, and disorientation. These are *physical* effects, not psychological ones — they result from the resonant frequencies of your body cavities (chest, skull, abdomen) being excited by external vibration. In the Underhalls, these effects will vary dramatically from room to room because each chamber transforms the sound differently. You may step through an archway and feel suddenly nauseous, or suddenly calm, or suddenly as if something is standing behind you. **These sensations are acoustic artifacts, not perceptions of actual threat.** Knowing this in advance is critical to maintaining composure.
- **Psychological effects:** Arrhythmic sound — sound with no predictable pattern — prevents your brain from habituating to it. You cannot tune it out the way you tune out a refrigerator hum or a steady heartbeat. Each new cluster of booms will startle you slightly, and this startle response, repeated hundreds or thousands of times over the course of your visit, will produce cumulative stress. You will become irritable, fatigued, and hypervigilant. Plan for this. Take breaks in whatever rooms you find where the sound is least intense. Sit down. Breathe slowly. Let the startle response peak and pass without adding your own fear to it.

**Practice before you go.** Find recordings of deep, irregular percussion — taiko drums, industrial machinery, infrasound recordings — and play them through a subwoofer while you go about ordinary activities. Eat dinner with the sound playing. Try to sleep with it. Read a book. The goal is not to become comfortable with the sound (you won't — it will always be disruptive) but to build familiarity with the *experience of being disrupted*, to learn what your personal stress responses are so you can recognize them in the field and manage them.

### The Darkness

Humans are visual creatures, and sustained darkness degrades our cognitive function, spatial reasoning, and emotional regulation. After several hours in near-darkness, you may experience:

- **Spatial disorientation.** Not just "which way is the exit?" but a deeper confusion about the shape and scale of your body relative to the space. Reaching for a wall and finding it closer or farther than expected. Feeling that the ceiling is lowering or the floor is tilting. These are normal responses to visual deprivation and will resolve when you restore light.
- **Autoscopic phenomena.** In deep darkness, your visual cortex sometimes generates its own input — faint lights, moving shapes, the sense of a figure nearby. These are hallucinations in the clinical sense but not the pathological sense; they are your brain filling in the absence of signal. In the Underhalls, these will be complicated by the real phosphor-glow tiles and the real movement of the arthropod fauna, making it difficult to distinguish perception from hallucination. Accept this ambiguity. You will not always know whether what you saw was real. This is okay. Move carefully, trust your other senses, and don't chase phantoms.
- **Emotional amplification.** Darkness strips away the visual information that normally helps you contextualize and regulate your emotions. The emotions that remain — the unease from the sound, the wonder at the architecture, the loneliness of being a biological entity in a mineral world — will feel more intense than they would in daylight. Let them. Don't suppress them. But also don't act on them impulsively. If you feel an overwhelming urge to flee, stop, breathe, turn on your red headlamp for thirty seconds, verify your surroundings, and then make a deliberate decision about whether to continue or retreat.

### The Loneliness of the Non-Biological

This is the subtle one. The one that gets people.

The Underhalls are explicitly described as a *non-life* environment. Nothing grows. The surfaces are vitrified. The only fauna are sparse, cryptic arthropods that you never get a clear look at. The processes occurring are mineral processes — condensation, crystallization, erosion. The sounds are the sounds of material, not voice. The fingerprints on the walls are a record of life that has departed, leaving only its impressions.

You are a warm, wet, soft, temporary organism in a cool, hard, permanent, mineral world. You carry bacteria on your skin, in your gut. You exhale carbon dioxide and moisture and volatile organic compounds. You shed skin cells. You smell like life. And everything around you is clay and glaze and mineral phosphorescence and silence. The contrast is absolute, and over time it will make you feel *alien* in a way that visiting a desert or an ice cap does not, because those places are at least made of the same planet, the same geology, the same physics of erosion and weather. The Underhalls are *made*. They were built by someone. And the builders are gone and the building remains and you are not what the building was built for.

Some travelers find this profoundly peaceful. The absence of biological competition, biological demand, biological urgency. Nothing wants to eat you. Nothing wants to mate with you. Nothing wants anything. The ceramic is just *there*, ringing and dripping and setting, and you can rest against it and feel its vibration and be still in a way that the living world almost never allows.

Other travelers find it devastating. The homesickness hits hard. The longing for green, for soil, for the smell of rain on earth rather than the smell of damp clay. The feeling of being not just alone but *categorically alone*, alone in a kingdom of existence that has no place for you.

Know which kind of traveler you are. If you're not sure, prepare for the devastating version and be pleasantly surprised if you get the peaceful one.

**Bring something alive.** Not an animal — that would be irresponsible in this environment. But bring something that connects you to life. A photograph. A letter. A small bag of soil from your garden, sealed, to smell when the mineral air becomes too much. Something that reminds your nervous system what kingdom you belong to.

### The Question of the Builders

You will touch their fingerprints. The description guarantees it. You will press your palm against the wall and feel the negative impression of a hand that was there before you, that pushed into wet clay and shaped it and then was gone. And you will want to know: who? When? Why?

There are no answers available to you. The Underhalls do not contain explanations. They contain evidence — fingerprints, structural choices, the tuning of chambers — but not narrative. You will have to sit with not knowing. This is harder than it sounds, especially in the dome chamber with the perforated column, where the evidence of deliberate design is overwhelming and the absence of any explanation is equally overwhelming. The builders made this. They made it with extraordinary skill and understanding. They made every room a resonant body, every corridor a sound-tube, every ceiling a condensation-management system. And they left nothing — no writing, no images, no artifacts — that tells you why.

Prepare for this by practicing comfort with mystery. Not "there must be an explanation I haven't found yet" — that's just deferred certainty, and it will drive you to explore beyond your safety margins, looking for the inscription, the mural, the Rosetta Stone that isn't there. Practice instead the more difficult discipline of **genuine unknowing**: "This was made, and I don't know why, and I may never know why, and the not-knowing is part of the experience."

## Practical Protocol for the Visit

### Duration

Plan for 8-12 hours on your first visit. Less than 8, and you won't have time to acclimatize to the darkness and sound enough to begin genuinely perceiving the space. More than 12, and the cumulative fatigue from the acoustic environment and visual deprivation will degrade your judgment and navigation ability to dangerous levels. This is not a place to make mistakes in — a fall on glazed ceramic tile is a broken wrist at best, a skull fracture at worst, and you're in a labyrinth with no emergency services.

### Entry and Exit

Anchor your paracord at the entry point. Mark it with multiple chemical light sticks. Memorize the acoustic signature of the first few rooms — the specific quality of the echo, the distance to the nearest phosphor tiles, any distinctive features. You will need to recognize these rooms by sound and feel when you return, possibly in a state of fatigue and disorientation.

Set a turnaround time before you enter. **Honor it.** The temptation to push deeper — especially when the percussion is growing louder, when you sense you're approaching the source, when the chambers are becoming larger and more elaborately shaped — will be intense. Do not succumb to it on your first visit. There will be other visits. The Underhalls are not going anywhere. They are ceramic. They will outlast you by geological epochs. There is time.

### Movement

Walk slowly. Place your feet deliberately. Test each step before committing your weight — the convex tile slabs can shift slightly, and the mortar seams are narrow enough to catch a toe. Keep one hand free to trail along the wall (which provides both stability and navigation information). When you enter a new chamber, stop at the threshold and listen before moving into it. Let the echoes of your arrival dissipate. Feel the acoustic character of the room. Then move.

In the crawlways, keep your head low. The ceramic is unforgiving — a scalp laceration from hitting a low vault will bleed freely in the humid air and be difficult to manage in the dark. Wear a thin helmet or a padded headband. Something you barely notice until you need it.

### The Central Column

When you reach the dome chamber with the perforated column, stop at the entrance. The sound here is intense — complex enough to approach noise, vibrating the air with physical pressure. Put in your musician's earplugs before entering. Approach the column slowly. Do not touch it on your first visit. You don't know the temperature of its surface (the air near it is warmer — the column itself may be significantly hotter, heated by whatever process is occurring below). You don't know how the vibration will feel through direct contact (through the floor it's already blurring your vision — through your hands it might be painful or injurious).

Observe. Sit on the tile floor at a distance that feels tolerable — where the pressure is present but not overwhelming. Watch the arthropods near the base. Watch the condensation on the column's surface. Listen to the chord emanating from the perforations and try, without straining, to hear the individual pitches within it. Take your time. This is the heart of the Underhalls, or at least the heart of the level you've reached. It will give you as much as you're willing to receive, and it will give it in the currency of vibration and mineral light and the slow drip of dissolving architecture, and you must be patient enough to accept payment in that currency.

### The Fauna

Do not touch the arthropods. Do not attempt to collect them. Do not illuminate them with white light if you can avoid it. They are adapted to this environment — pale, cryptic, possibly eyeless or nearly so — and bright light may harm them or disrupt behaviors you don't understand. Observe them in the phosphor-glow. Note their locations (near moisture? near the column? near mortar seams?). They are part of the ecosystem of this place, possibly the *entirety* of its macroscopic ecosystem, and they are precious in the way that all life in extreme environments is precious: scarce, specialized, irreplaceable.

If one climbs on you (possible, especially if you sit still for long periods in the dark), remain calm. Let it explore. It will likely lose interest quickly — you are warm, organic, unfamiliar, wrong. It will leave. Do not flick it or brush it away violently. You are the intruder here, not it.

## Medical Kit

Compact, waterproof, oriented toward the specific risks of this environment:

- **Wound care for ceramic injuries.** Broken ceramic produces edges as sharp as glass. If you fall against a wall or crawl over a broken tile, the cuts will be clean and potentially deep. Butterfly closures, wound-closure strips, gauze, pressure bandages, antiseptic (the humid environment is a bacterial incubation chamber for any wound on *your* skin, even if the ceramic surfaces themselves are inhospitable to biology).
- **Blister care.** Hours of walking on hard, smooth surfaces in damp conditions. Moleskin, blister pads, medical tape.
- **Anti-nausea medication.** The infrasound effects in certain chambers may cause intense nausea. Meclizine or dimenhydrinate, taken prophylactically before entering rooms where the bass resonance is strongest.
- **Ibuprofen.** For the headache that will develop from the combination of low-frequency sound exposure, visual strain, and dehydration (you will under-drink; the dampness of the air tricks your thirst response into complacency).
- **Emergency signaling.** A whistle. Ceramic carries sound beautifully. If you are injured and need to signal, a whistle blast will propagate through the chambers far more effectively than your voice, and it will be acoustically distinct from the ambient percussion.
- **Thermal blanket.** If you are injured and immobile, the cool temperature and high humidity will chill you quickly. A compact mylar emergency blanket weighs nothing and could prevent hypothermia during a long wait.

## What to Bring Home

Your recordings, if you made them. Your notebook sketches. The memory of pressing your palm to the wall and feeling the fingerprints of the builders pressing back.

Do not take samples. Do not chip off a tile. Do not scrape the phosphorescent slip. Do not collect an arthropod. Do not bottle the condensation. The Underhalls are still becoming. The stalactites are embryonic, the mineral crusts nascent, the erosion barely suggested. You are witnessing the first moments of a geological timescale, the childhood of a mineral world. Everything you remove is a thread pulled from a tapestry that is still being woven by processes you don't understand.

Leave the chemical light sticks if they've gone dark — they're inert, small, and retrieving them means retracing your entire path. If they're still glowing, collect them on your way out.

Leave the paracord for your next visit. Tie it off at the entry. It will be your starting thread, your proof that you were there, your promise to return.

## Final Thought

The Underhalls are not for everyone. Some travelers arrive, feel the first deep boom through the soles of their feet, hear the ceramic ring with the aftershock, see the blue-green phosphor glow barely marking the darkness, and they know — immediately, instinctively — that this is a place they need to be. That the mineral loneliness of it is a gift. That the sound of a world still being fired is the most honest sound they've ever heard.

Others arrive and feel nothing but the cold damp and the disorientation and the relentless, unpredictable percussion, and they turn around, and they are right to turn around, because there is no shame in being a creature of sunlight and soil who has no business inside a curing kiln.

Know yourself before you go. And if you're not sure — go. The worst outcome is that you crawl back out into the light after an hour, shaken and damp and relieved. The best outcome is that you sit in the dome chamber with your palm against the wall and the fingerprints of the builders against your skin and the column roaring its mineral chord and the pale arthropods listening at its base, and you understand something — not intellectually, not in words, but in your bones, in the resonant cavities of your chest and skull — about what it means to be inside something that is still deciding what it is.

And then you come home, and the world seems louder and softer and more temporary than it did before, and that feeling never entirely goes away, and you are grateful for it, and sometimes at night you press your palm flat against the wall of your house and feel for the vibration, and there is none, because your house is not ceramic, your house is not tuned, your house is not still being fired, but for a moment — just a moment — you remember what it felt like when the wall pressed back.
