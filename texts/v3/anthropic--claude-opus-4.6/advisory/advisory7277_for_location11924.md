# Preparing for the Terrace Settlement at Breath-of-Stones

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before you pack a single item, sit with the description of this place and understand something fundamental: you are visiting a location that is, in geological terms, an infant. The plateau is perhaps fifty years old. The rock is still warm. The mountain beneath it is still growing. This is not a ruin, not a monument, not a place that has settled into the comfortable stability of age. It is a living process that you will be standing on top of, and the people who live there have organized their existence around that fact. Your preparation must account for this in ways that go beyond the merely physical.

You are also visiting a place whose primary feature is sound—pervasive, inescapable, physically felt sound that the settlement has been architecturally designed to shape and focus. Many travelers underestimate what this means for prolonged exposure. You need to prepare your body, your mind, and your expectations for an environment where sound is not background but foreground, where it will press against your sternum and vibrate in the arches of your feet and make the condensation on your skin tremble. This is not a place you will experience primarily through your eyes.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude and Respiratory Conditioning

The settlement sits in the cloud layer of an actively growing mountain. The description tells you the air is cool and wet, that the light when the sun breaks through is high-altitude light—clean, precise, free of haze. You are likely at a significant elevation, somewhere in the range where atmospheric pressure is meaningfully reduced and the oxygen content of each breath is lower than at sea level.

Begin cardiovascular conditioning at least six to eight weeks before your visit. Running, cycling, sustained hiking with elevation gain—whatever builds your aerobic base. If you have access to altitude simulation equipment (hypoxic tents, altitude masks with verified performance), use them during the last three to four weeks to begin acclimatization. If you're coming from sea level, plan intermediate stops at progressively higher elevations. Spend at least two to three days at a moderate altitude before ascending to the plateau. Acute mountain sickness is not romantic and it is not interesting; it is a splitting headache, nausea, disorientation, and an inability to appreciate anything about where you are.

The persistent moisture in the air will interact with the altitude in ways you should anticipate. Wet air at altitude can feel paradoxically harder to breathe—not because it contains less oxygen per unit volume (it actually contains slightly less dry air per breath since water vapor displaces nitrogen and oxygen), but because the sensation of moisture in your airways can trigger a feeling of breathlessness that compounds altitude discomfort. Practice breathing exercises. Slow, deep, diaphragmatic breathing. You will need this skill doubly when the Breath is pressing against your chest.

### The Sound and Your Body

This requires dedicated preparation and most travelers neglect it.

The Breath is described as loud enough to feel in your sternum, capable of blurring vision at its focal points, producing subsonic pulses felt as pressure changes in your ears. This is not a sound you listen to. It is a sound that enters you through your skeleton, through the fluid in your inner ear, through the resonant cavities of your chest and sinuses. Prolonged exposure to low-frequency sound—particularly infrasound below 20 Hz—can cause a range of physiological effects: nausea, disorientation, a vague but persistent sense of unease or dread, visual disturbances (the blurring mentioned in the description is likely caused by resonant vibration of the eyeballs at certain frequencies), fatigue, and in some people, a profound emotional response that can range from euphoria to weeping to a deep, sourceless anxiety.

You cannot fully prepare for this, but you can reduce the shock of it.

**Earplugs:** Bring high-fidelity earplugs—the kind designed for musicians, which reduce volume evenly across frequencies without muffling the sound. These are not for constant use; using them constantly would be both disrespectful to the place and self-defeating, since the sound is the entire point. But you need the option to attenuate the Breath when you first arrive, when you encounter the focused courtyard where it becomes "almost unbearable," and during sleep. Standard foam earplugs will muffle the higher harmonics and leave the low-frequency components untouched, which can actually make the experience more disorienting, not less, because you'll feel the vibration without the contextualizing sound. Invest in proper filtered earplugs. Bring at least two pairs.

**Bone conduction awareness:** Understand that earplugs will not block the bone-conducted component of the sound. The vibration traveling through the stone, into your feet, up through your skeleton, and into your skull will bypass your ear canals entirely. There is no way to block this short of not standing on the stone. Accept this. Prepare for it mentally. The first night will be the hardest, because you will try to sleep and discover that the Breath is not just around you but inside you, coming up through whatever surface you are lying on, and no earplugs will change this.

**Pre-exposure:** If possible, spend time before your trip in environments with sustained low-frequency sound. Stand near large waterfalls. Visit industrial sites with heavy machinery. Attend an organ concert in a large cathedral and sit near the pipes during the lowest bass notes. Find a sound therapy practice that uses Tibetan singing bowls or gong baths and attend a session, paying attention not to the "spiritual" framing but to the physical sensations—the vibration in your chest, the way your vision softens, the pressure changes in your ears. None of these will replicate the Breath, but they will give your nervous system a reference point so that the experience at Breath-of-Stones is not entirely unprecedented.

**Vestibular readiness:** The subsonic and infrasonic components of the Breath may affect your balance. The vestibular system in your inner ear is sensitive to pressure changes and low-frequency vibration. Some travelers report a mild, persistent sense of vertigo during their first day or two on the plateau—not spinning, but a feeling that the ground is subtly uncertain, that your sense of vertical is slightly off. If you are prone to motion sickness or inner ear sensitivity, consult with a physician about vestibular exercises or, if appropriate, medication. Scopolamine patches designed for seasickness may help with the worst of it, though they bring side effects (dry mouth, drowsiness, dilated pupils) that may compound the altitude discomfort.

### Thermoregulation and Moisture

You will be wet. Constantly. Not from rain—from condensation. The cloud layer sits on the plateau for much of the day, and moisture will condense on your skin, your clothing, your gear, everything you own. This is a persistent, low-grade dampness that will not dry because the humidity is near saturation much of the time. When the sun breaks through, things dry rapidly in the high-altitude UV, but these windows may be brief and unpredictable.

**Clothing:** Merino wool, from skin outward. Not cotton—cotton absorbs moisture, holds it against your skin, and in the cool air at altitude, it will chill you steadily and relentlessly. Synthetics are acceptable as a second choice but tend to develop odor quickly, and in a settlement this small and intimate, you want to avoid giving unnecessary offense. Merino regulates temperature, wicks moisture, resists odor, and continues to insulate when damp. Bring:

- Three to four sets of base layers (long-sleeve tops, leggings). You will rotate through them as they dampen.
- A mid-layer of heavier merino or a merino-synthetic blend fleece.
- A wind-resistant outer shell that is breathable. Not waterproof—a fully waterproof membrane will trap your own perspiration against the already-damp base layers and make things worse. You want something that breaks the wind (which can be strong at the plateau's edge, where the updrafts and the Breath interact) while allowing vapor to pass through. A soft-shell jacket is ideal.
- For the moments of direct sun: the UV at this altitude will be fierce. A lightweight, loose-fitting long-sleeve shirt with UPF protection, and a wide-brimmed hat that ties securely against the wind.
- A warm hat for evenings and mornings in the cloud layer, when the temperature drops and the dampness cuts.
- Gloves. Thin merino glove liners. Your hands will be cold and wet much of the time, and if you want to touch the stone—feel its warmth, trace the wedge-marks, understand the craftsmanship—you will want the option to do so without your fingers going numb.

**Footwear:** This is critical. The stone is wet, it is smooth (unweathered, remember—no centuries of roughening), and in places it is warm. You need footwear that grips wet, smooth stone without slipping. Approach shoes with sticky rubber soles—the kind used for scrambling and light rock climbing—are ideal. Break them in thoroughly before you arrive. The residents wear fiber sandals and presumably have callused feet and a lifetime's familiarity with the stone; you have neither. Bring the approach shoes. Also bring a pair of camp sandals or lightweight shoes for rest periods, but understand that any footwear you bring will be perpetually damp.

Consider the vibration dimension of your footwear choice. Thicker, more cushioned soles will dampen the bone-conducted Breath that comes up through the stone. This might sound appealing but may actually disorient you more—you'll feel the vibration in your chest and skull but not in your feet, creating a disconnection that some travelers find deeply unsettling. Thinner soles let you feel the full spectrum. This is more intense but more coherent. Choose based on your own sensitivity, but I would lean toward thinner soles for the truest experience of the place.

**Sleeping arrangements:** The description does not mention roofed structures. The settlement is walls and open space—doorways without doors, alcoves without contents. You may be sleeping in the open, on stone, in cloud moisture, with the Breath vibrating through your sleeping surface. Bring:

- A closed-cell foam pad, or better yet, an inflatable pad with good R-value. This serves double duty: insulation from the warm-but-still-hard stone, and some (minimal) dampening of the vibration. A foam pad will transmit more vibration than an inflatable; an inflatable pad with an air chamber will isolate you slightly. Choose according to your preference.
- A sleeping bag rated for temperatures lower than you expect. Damp conditions rob insulation of its effectiveness, and night temperatures at this altitude in persistent cloud cover could drop significantly. A bag rated to just above freezing, with a hydrophobic down fill or synthetic insulation, would be prudent.
- A bivvy sack or ultralight shelter tarp. Not a tent—a tent would be both impractical (where would you stake it on solid stone?) and inappropriate (you would be enclosing yourself in a cocoon that separates you from the sound-architecture of the settlement, which is the entire reason for being here). A bivvy or tarp provides rain protection if the clouds thicken beyond mere condensation, while leaving you acoustically present.
- A silk or merino liner for the sleeping bag, to add warmth and manage moisture next to your skin.

## Gear and Equipment

### What to Bring

**Water and food:** Water is abundant but exists only as condensation. You will need a way to collect it—a lightweight vessel with a wide mouth that you can set in the shallow depressions where water pools, or a clean cloth that you can drape over surfaces and then wring into a container. The water will be exceptionally pure—condensed from cloud moisture onto stone that is decades old and has had no agricultural runoff, no industrial contamination, no biological material beyond mosses and ferns. It may have a faint mineral taste from the volcanic rock. It is likely safe to drink without treatment, but bring a UV purifier or filter straw as a precaution, particularly for water collected near the breathing vents, which may carry mineral aerosols from below.

Food is harder. The settlement's residents appear to eat a paste or porridge made from the plateau's plants. You do not know these plants, do not know their nutritional content or their compatibility with your biology, and should not rely on them as a primary food source, though you may be offered them and should accept out of courtesy (see Cultural Preparation below). Bring calorie-dense, moisture-resistant food sufficient for your stay plus emergency reserves. Nuts, dried meats, hard cheeses sealed in wax, energy bars, dried fruit. Nothing that requires cooking unless you bring fuel—there is no wood on this plateau, no combustible material beyond dried moss and lichen, which you should absolutely not burn (see Ethical Considerations below). A small canister stove with fuel sufficient for heating water for warm drinks would be valuable for morale and warmth during the cold, damp evenings.

**Notebook and writing instruments:** Not electronics—a paper notebook, ideally one with waterproof or water-resistant pages (such as Rite in the Rain or similar products). A graphite pencil, not a pen—ink will smear in the persistent moisture, graphite will not. You will want to record things. The sound changes with the hour, with the wind, with some deeper geological rhythm. You will want to map the settlement's acoustic architecture—which courtyard produces which effect, where the harmonics focus, where they cancel. You will want to sketch the wedge-marks in the stone, the growth patterns of the ferns, the four-toed track in the moss. A camera is worth bringing but will struggle with the conditions (moisture on the lens constantly, flat light in the cloud layer, and no photograph can capture the sound, which is the dominant sensory experience). A notebook is primary. A camera is supplementary.

**Sound recording equipment:** If you are serious about documenting this place, bring a field recording setup. A matched pair of small-diaphragm condenser microphones will capture the audible components of the Breath. But much of what makes the Breath distinctive is below the threshold of normal audio recording. If you can obtain or borrow a measurement microphone and recorder capable of capturing infrasound (down to 1 Hz or below), do so. This is specialized equipment—consult with acousticians or geophysicists who work with volcanic monitoring, as they use similar tools. Protect all electronic equipment against moisture with silica gel packets and waterproof cases, and be aware that the vibration transmitted through the stone may affect sensitive electronics left resting on surfaces. Suspend microphones on shock mounts. Keep recorders in padded cases, off the ground.

**A tuning fork or pitch pipe:** A known reference pitch. When you are immersed in the Breath for hours, your perception of pitch and interval will drift. A fixed reference will help you characterize what you're hearing. Bring one in the A440 range and one lower if possible.

**Illumination:** If the settlement has no roofed structures, nights will be ambient-lit by whatever celestial light penetrates the cloud layer—which may be very little. A headlamp with a red-light mode (to preserve night vision and minimize disruption) and spare batteries. A small candle lantern if you want warmer light for evening writing. The settlement's residents may have their own lighting practices; observe and follow them.

**First aid:** Standard high-altitude kit: ibuprofen for headaches (altitude and sound-pressure headaches are likely), acetazolamide (Diamox) if prescribed by your physician for altitude prophylaxis, blister supplies (wet feet on stone are a blister factory), anti-nausea medication, oral rehydration salts, broad-spectrum antibiotic in case of infection from cuts on sharp stone edges (the rock is described as sharp-edged, unsmoothed—you will cut yourself at some point), and wound closure strips or surgical glue. Tweezers for splinters—both stone and wood, from the tool handles or the plants' woody stems. Bring more bandages and wound care supplies than you think you need; wounds heal slowly at altitude and in persistent moisture.

### What Not to Bring

**Powered speakers or sound-generating equipment:** Do not bring anything that produces sound into this settlement beyond your own voice and perhaps a single reference pitch instrument. The entire settlement is a precisely tuned acoustic instrument built over decades. Introducing foreign sound would be both disrespectful and destructive to what makes this place what it is. This includes music players, radios, notification sounds on devices, and anything with an alarm that beeps. If you need an alarm, use a vibrating one.

**Heavy equipment, climbing gear for the vent:** You will look at the central breathing vent and feel the pull to descend. The warm air rising from it, the complexity of the sound emanating from its depths, the implication that the settlement's people may use it as a passage to interior spaces—all of this will call to you. Do not descend into the vent without explicit invitation and guidance from the residents. The thermal dynamics are unpredictable, the geology is young and potentially unstable, and you have no knowledge of what is below. The people who descend (if they do) have a lifetime of understanding of this specific system. You have hours.

**Fire-making equipment beyond a small canister stove:** There is nothing to burn here that you can ethically burn. The mosses, lichens, and ferns are the first life colonizing infant rock—they are the beginning of soil, the beginning of an ecosystem. The woody-stemmed bushes in the corners are survivors clinging to stone. Burning any of them would be a desecration. Bring contained fuel or bring none.

## Mental and Emotional Preparation

This is the section most travelers skip, and it is the section that determines whether your visit is transformative or merely endurable.

### The Sound Will Change You

I don't mean this mystically. I mean it neurologically and psychologically. Sustained exposure to low-frequency sound, particularly sound with complex harmonic content that shifts over time, has documented effects on consciousness. Your brain will entrain to the Breath. This is not a choice you make; it is a physiological response. Neural oscillations—the rhythmic electrical activity of your brain—tend to synchronize with dominant external rhythms, particularly low-frequency ones. After several hours on the plateau, you may notice changes in your thought patterns, your emotional baseline, your perception of time.

Common experiences reported in analogous environments (near large waterfalls, in cave systems with resonant acoustics, in anechoic chambers where the absence of sound makes your own heartbeat and blood flow audible and dominant):

- **Time dilation:** Hours may feel like minutes, or minutes like hours. Bring a watch. Check it regularly until you calibrate your sense of time to the plateau. The changing quality of light through the cloud layer will help if you learn to read it, but on heavily overcast days you may lose all sense of the hour without a timepiece.

- **Emotional surfacing:** The Breath may bring up emotions that have no apparent source or connection to your present circumstances. Grief, joy, longing, tenderness, a sense of profound smallness or profound belonging. These are real feelings; they are also, at least in part, artifacts of what the low-frequency vibration is doing to your autonomic nervous system and your vagus nerve. You do not need to interpret them as messages or revelations. You also do not need to dismiss them as mere artifacts. They are both. Let them pass through you like the Breath passes through the stone.

- **Altered pattern recognition:** Your brain, bathed in complex sound, may begin to hear patterns that are not there—voices in the harmonics, words in the drone, rhythmic structures that emerge and dissolve. This is pareidolia, the same mechanism that makes you see faces in clouds. It is your brain's pattern-recognition machinery running in overdrive because it has been given a rich, ambiguous input. Enjoy it. Do not trust it. The Breath is not speaking to you. The mountain is not trying to tell you anything. It is doing something much more interesting than that: it is being itself, and your consciousness is doing what consciousness does in the presence of something vast and ongoing—it is trying to make narrative out of phenomenon. Notice this tendency. It will teach you more about your own mind than any message would.

- **Sleep disruption:** The Breath does not stop at night. You will sleep inside it. Some travelers adapt within one or two nights. Others find it impossible to achieve deep sleep for the duration of their stay and must manage with light, broken rest supplemented by napping during the day. The earplugs will help. Accepting the sound rather than resisting it will help more. The Breath is remarkably steady; it does not have sudden changes in volume or character. It is, in sleep terms, the most consistent white noise imaginable—except it's not white noise, it's deeply structured sound, and your sleeping brain may engage with that structure in ways your waking brain filters out. Some travelers report extraordinarily vivid dreams. Others report a state that feels neither like sleep nor wakefulness but something between—a restful, receptive suspension. If this happens, do not fight it. It may be the closest you get to understanding how the settlement's residents relate to the Breath.

### Solitude and Presence

The people of Breath-of-Stones may not appear during your visit. The description makes clear they are present—tools left out, food residue, recently worn sandals—but they may choose to remain unseen, in the mountain's interior or in parts of the settlement you do not enter. You should prepare for the possibility that you will spend your entire visit alone with the Breath.

This is not a failure. It may be a gift. It may also be a test—not in the sense of a trial you must pass, but in the simpler sense that the residents may be observing how you behave when you believe you are unobserved. Do you treat the space with care? Do you touch the unfinished wall? Do you pick up the tools? Do you take stones, lichen, ferns as souvenirs? Do you attempt to descend the vent? Do you shout into the courtyards to hear how they reshape your voice? Your behavior during apparent solitude may determine whether and when they choose to make themselves known.

If they do appear, be still. Be quiet. Do not rush toward contact. These are people who have organized their lives around a sound; they likely have a relationship with silence and speech that is very different from yours. Observe what they do. If they work on the stone, watch. If they sit and listen, sit and listen. If they offer food, accept. If they ignore you, be at peace with being ignored. You are a visitor to an instrument that is being played across decades. Your presence is a temporary note in a very long composition.

### The Vertigo of Newness

Perhaps the most psychologically challenging aspect of Breath-of-Stones is its newness. We are accustomed, as travelers, to visiting places that are old—ancient ruins, weathered landscapes, cities with centuries of accumulated human presence. Age gives us a framework. It tells us where we are in time. It gives surfaces a patina that our eyes read as stability, permanence, trustworthiness. A weathered stone wall says: *I have stood. I will stand.*

Nothing at Breath-of-Stones says this. The stone is unweathered. The crystal faces are sharp and glittering. The rock is warm. The mountain is still growing. The settlement is unfinished and will never be finished. The Breath is changing pitch across decades. You are standing on something that is in the process of becoming, and there is no guarantee about what it will become. The plateau could continue to extend. It could stop. The vents could shift, closing old ones and opening new ones, rendering the settlement's careful acoustic architecture obsolete. The mountain could produce a new volcanic event that destroys the plateau entirely. Everything here is contingent, provisional, mid-process.

This can produce a subtle but pervasive anxiety in visitors—a feeling of instability, of groundlessness, that has nothing to do with physical danger and everything to do with the absence of the temporal anchors we use to feel safe in a place. Prepare for this by practicing, before your trip, a tolerance for incompletion. Visit construction sites. Sit with unfinished projects. Read the first chapter of a book and put it down. Begin a drawing and stop. Get comfortable with the feeling of something that is not yet what it will be. This is the fundamental condition of Breath-of-Stones, and if you cannot make peace with it, the place will be merely uncomfortable rather than revelatory.

## Ethical Considerations

### Leave Nothing, Take Nothing

This is standard wilderness ethics magnified by the specificity of this place. The settlement is an instrument. Every block, every wall, every surface has been placed with acoustic intention. Moving a stone—even accidentally knocking a loose block—could alter the sound in a courtyard in ways that the builders have spent years calibrating. Walk carefully. Touch gently. Set nothing on the walls or benches that could shift position.

Do not take samples of the stone, the moss, the lichen, the ferns. The ecosystem is young, still establishing itself on infant rock. Every organism here is a pioneer, a first-arrival that is building the soil and the biological community that will eventually make this plateau a mature ecosystem. Removing even a small amount of moss disrupts a process that is measured in decades.

Do not leave food waste, human waste, or any material on the plateau. Pack out everything. If you must relieve yourself, do so at the plateau's edge, downslope, away from the settlement and away from the breathing vents—introducing organic matter into the vent system could alter the chemistry of the exhalation, potentially affecting both the Breath and whatever thermal/geological processes operate below.

### The Tools

You will see the chisels, the mallets, the rope. Do not use them. Do not move them. Do not examine them more than superficially. These are the residents' working tools, left where they were set down, and their placement may have significance—practical, cultural, or both—that you cannot read. The chisel lodged in the half-split block is mid-task. The person who placed it there will return to that exact stroke. Disturbing it would be like turning the page of a book someone is reading.

### Recording and Documentation

Record the sound freely—it is the mountain's sound, not the people's property. But if and when residents appear, do not record them, photograph them, or sketch them without evident permission. Do not record conversations. Do not attempt to notate or transcribe any vocal practices, songs, chants, or speech patterns they may use in relation to the Breath. These may be sacred, proprietary, or simply private. Observe, appreciate, and keep your documentation of the human element to your own written reflections.

## Practical Logistics

### Duration of Stay

Plan for a minimum of three days and nights on the plateau to allow for altitude acclimatization, acoustic acclimatization, and the slow process of learning to perceive the settlement's acoustic architecture. The first day will be overwhelming and you will understand almost nothing. The second day the Breath will begin to differentiate—you will start to hear it not as a single sound but as a complex system of interacting sounds shaped differently by each space. The third day, if you are attentive and lucky, you will begin to understand why the walls are where they are.

If you can stay longer—a week, two weeks—the experience deepens in ways that are apparently non-linear. There are reports from long-term visitors of eventually perceiving the Breath not as sound but as a kind of spatial information, a three-dimensional map of air movement and thermal dynamics rendered audible. Whether this represents a genuine perceptual shift or a fatigue-induced alteration of consciousness is a question I cannot answer. Probably both.

### Approach and Access

The description does not specify how to reach the plateau, but it juts from the shoulder of an actively growing mountain. The approach will involve mountain travel—likely steep, likely on young, potentially unstable volcanic terrain. Hire a guide if guides exist in this multiverse variant. If not, bring topographic awareness and move slowly. Young volcanic rock can be deceptively fragile—it looks solid but may contain gas voids, cooling fractures, and unweathered surfaces that have not yet been tested by weight or weather. Test every foothold. Trust nothing until it has borne your weight.

The sharp-edged, unsmoothed rock faces where the plateau meets the mountainside suggest a technical scramble or short climb to access the plateau surface. Bring a pair of lightweight approach-climbing gloves to protect your hands on the sharp stone during the final approach.

### Emergency Preparedness

You are at altitude, on a geologically active mountain, in persistent cloud cover that may limit visibility and complicate any evacuation. The nearest help is likely far below and far away. Carry:

- A personal locator beacon appropriate to the multiverse you're traveling in (verify that the signaling technology functions in this variant—do not assume).
- Sufficient food and water purification capacity for twice your planned stay.
- Knowledge of descent routes.
- A clear agreement with someone not on the mountain about when you are expected to return and what action to take if you do not.

The geological activity beneath the plateau is, as far as we can tell, gentle and stable—thermal differentials driving air exchange, not magmatic eruption. But you are not a geologist, you have not studied this specific system, and the residents' decades of intimate knowledge cannot be transmitted to you in a three-day visit. If you feel the stone become noticeably hotter underfoot, if the Breath suddenly changes character (much louder, much quieter, different pitch, sulfurous smell), or if you hear cracking sounds from beneath the surface rather than from the plateau's edges, move to the plateau's edge immediately and prepare to descend. These may indicate a change in the subsurface thermal regime. They may indicate nothing. You cannot know. Err on the side of caution.

## A Final Note on Readiness

There is a kind of preparation that cannot be done in advance. It can only be done in the moment, on the plateau, when the Breath is in your bones and the cloud is on your skin and the stone is warm under your feet and the lichen is swaying in a wind that is not wind but exhalation.

The preparation is this: willingness to be small.

You are visiting a place where the primary actor is the mountain. The primary voice is geological. The primary timescale is one that renders your life a brief pause between breaths. The settlement's builders understand this—they have built not a monument to themselves but an ear for the mountain, an instrument that makes audible what the stone is already doing. They have subordinated their architecture to a sound they did not create and cannot control, only shape, temporarily, until the Breath shifts and they must reshape again.

To be ready for Breath-of-Stones, practice being the ear and not the voice. Practice listening without interpreting. Practice standing in a place and feeling it without needing to name what you feel. Practice being unfinished, because everything here is unfinished, and the settlement will teach you that unfinished is not a deficiency but a condition of being alive, of being still in the process of becoming what you are, which is the same process the mountain is in, the same process the stone is in, the same process the Breath is in as it finds its way through the vents and the walls and the courtyards and your chest and out into the cloud and back again.

Go. Listen. Be careful with the stone. Leave when it is time, and carry the sound home in your sternum, where it will live for a long while, humming quietly, like a memory of something the earth once said to you in a language neither of you fully understood.
