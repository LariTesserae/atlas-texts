# Preparing for the Nursery at Gant's Threshold

## A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## First: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before a single item goes into your pack, you need to sit with something uncomfortable. The Nursery is not a hostile location in the way that most dangerous multiverse sites are hostile. Nothing there is trying to kill you. Nothing is hunting you. There are no recorded accounts of direct physical harm befalling visitors. And yet every account I've encountered from travelers who have spent time there describes a particular kind of *aftermath* — a lingering disquiet that lasts days or weeks, a sense that the ground beneath their feet in their home reality is somehow less certain than it was before, that the solidity of familiar things has become a polite convention rather than a fact.

This is the real danger of the Nursery: not injury, not entrapment, but *ontological erosion*. You are going to spend time in a place where reality has not yet committed to being anything in particular, where the fundamental material truth of the ground under your feet is an open question being answered in multiple contradictory ways simultaneously. Your nervous system evolved to operate in a reality that has already decided what it is. At the Nursery, you will be asking it to function in a place that hasn't. The preparation that matters most is not physical — it's cognitive and psychological. Everything else follows from this understanding.

## Psychological Preparation

### Grounding Exercises (Start Three Weeks Before)

I mean this literally and figuratively. You need to develop, before you arrive, a robust and practiced sense of your own physical reality — your weight, your temperature, the feeling of your own skeleton inside your body, the sensation of your feet against a surface. These are the things the Nursery will make ambiguous, not by altering them but by surrounding them with a context where ambiguity is the norm.

**Daily practice:** Stand barefoot on a single, known surface — stone, wood, concrete, soil — for ten minutes. Close your eyes. Feel the texture. Feel the temperature. Feel your weight pressing down and the surface pressing back. Name what it is. Say it aloud: *This is limestone. This is oak. This is packed earth.* You are training your proprioceptive system to have a strong default, a home frequency, so that when the Nursery starts introducing competing signals, your body has something to return to.

**Variation:** Do this on multiple surfaces in sequence, moving from one to the next, consciously registering the transition. The Nursery's ground is a mosaic of competing materials; you need your body to be fluent in the experience of *this surface ends and that surface begins* so that the blurred, phasing boundaries in the Nursery don't cause the deep unease that comes from ambiguity your body can't parse.

### Developing Tolerance for Irresolution

The human mind is a resolution engine. It encounters ambiguity and it *resolves* — it decides, it categorizes, it commits. The Nursery is a place where resolution is not available. The floor is simultaneously obsidian and packed earth and something your visual cortex cannot process. The structures are simultaneously beginnings and ruins. The creature is simultaneously animal and mineral and process. If you walk in with your normal resolution reflexes fully engaged, you will exhaust yourself in the first twenty minutes, because your brain will be trying to *decide* what things are, over and over, and the Nursery will refuse to let those decisions stick.

**Practice:** In the weeks before your visit, spend time with optical illusions — not the fun, recreational kind, but the genuinely uncomfortable ones. The Necker cube. Rubin's vase. Binocular rivalry displays if you can get access to them. But here's the key: *don't try to resolve them*. Sit with the ambiguity. Let the figure-ground relationship flicker. Let the cube face both directions. Practice the sensation of *not deciding*, of letting two contradictory perceptions coexist without your mind snapping to one. This is the cognitive posture you'll need at the Nursery.

**Advanced practice:** Visit a construction site — a real one, in your home reality — at the earliest possible phase, when the foundation is being laid and the building is just a suggestion in concrete and rebar. Sit with it. Try to see it as *not yet a building* rather than as a building that isn't finished. The distinction matters. "Not finished" implies a known endpoint. "Not yet" implies an open question. The Nursery is the latter.

### Preparing for the Quiet

This is the one that catches people off guard. Many multiverse travelers prepare for danger, for strangeness, for alien physics. Very few prepare for prolonged exposure to deep quiet in a context of extreme strangeness. The combination is uniquely taxing. The quiet of the Nursery is not peaceful — it is *suspended*. It is the quiet of enormous forces in equilibrium, and your animal brain will read it as the quiet before something happens, and it will keep you in a state of low-grade alert the entire time you're there. Your adrenal system will be gently, persistently activated. Over an hour, this is draining in a way that active danger is not.

**Practice:** Meditate in unfamiliar spaces. Not your usual meditation spot — go somewhere you've never been, somewhere that feels slightly wrong or slightly charged, and sit in the silence there. An empty parking garage at midday. A basement you don't know. A clearing in a forest that's too quiet. Train yourself to sit in *strange quiet* without your system escalating to full alert. You won't be able to fully replicate the Nursery's quality of silence, but you can build some tolerance for the combination of stillness and unease.

### Preparing for the Creature

You will see it. Every account confirms this. And every account describes the same thing: a long, held moment of eye contact with something that is not an animal looking at you with the awareness of something that is not intelligence. This moment is described universally as one of the most disorienting experiences at the Nursery, more unsettling than the phasing ground or the crystallized air column or the semiotic doorframe. There is something about being *regarded* by a process — being noticed by something that has no mind but has *attention* — that strikes at a very deep part of the human psyche.

You cannot prepare for this in any practical sense. But you can prepare for it emotionally by accepting, in advance, that it will happen, that it will be profoundly strange, and that the appropriate response is to hold still, hold the gaze for as long as it lasts, and let the creature leave on its own terms. Do not follow it. Do not attempt to touch it. And do not, under any circumstances, attempt to follow it down the staircase or into the ground. Not because anyone knows what would happen — but because the fact that no one knows what would happen is, in this context, reason enough.

## Physical Preparation

### Clothing

The ambient temperature is approximately 3°C below the temperature on the approach side of the wall, and it holds steady — no wind, no variation, just a flat, persistent cold. Dress for standing still in cold, damp conditions for one to two hours, because that's what you'll be doing. You won't be hiking. You won't be running. You will be standing, crouching, occasionally taking very careful steps across a terrain of unpredictable surfaces. Your clothing needs are specific:

**Base layer:** Merino wool or synthetic moisture-wicking fabric. The condensation cycle of the Nursery's ground means the air holds more moisture than you'd expect. You will not be sweating much — the cold and your stillness will prevent that — but the ambient humidity will settle on you, and cotton will make you miserable within thirty minutes.

**Mid layer:** Fleece or light insulated jacket. Nothing too bulky — you'll be moving through spaces as narrow as sixty centimeters between structures, and snagging your clothing on the bruise-bricks or the fractal pipe branches is a real possibility. Snug, warm, low-profile.

**Outer layer:** A thin, water-resistant shell. Not for rain — there is no rain at the Nursery — but for the condensation. The crystallized air column produces persistent moisture at its base, and if you crouch near it (you will; everyone does), you'll get damp. The shell also provides a psychological benefit: a defined boundary between you and the environment. At a place where boundaries are under negotiation, a clear physical envelope around your body is more comforting than you'd think.

**Footwear:** This requires serious thought. The ground at the Nursery includes compacted earth, polished stone, brushed steel, ceramic tile, and surfaces that defy categorization. Some are slick with condensation. Some have slight textures that will catch at soft rubber. Some are *warm* when the ambient temperature is cold, which can cause sole materials to behave unpredictably. You need:

- A sole with moderate, even tread — nothing too aggressive (it'll catch on tile edges and the etched steel), nothing too smooth (you'll slip on the condensation-wet basalt)
- Ankle support — the proprioceptive confusion near the boundaries is real and physical, not just perceptual. Travelers report micro-stumbles, moments where the ground seems to be a centimeter higher or lower than their foot expected. Ankle support prevents this from becoming a sprain.
- Insulation from below — several ground surfaces are significantly colder than ambient, and at least one (the programmable-matter gel) has been reported to actively draw heat. A thick, insulating sole is not optional.

I recommend a mid-weight hiking boot with a Vibram sole, well broken in. Not new boots. Boots your feet know. Boots that feel like *your ground* when everything else is someone else's.

**Gloves:** Bring thin, tactile gloves — the kind climbers or photographers use. You will want to touch things. Everyone touches things at the Nursery. The impulse is nearly irresistible: you'll want to press your palm against the bruise-bricks to confirm their warmth, run your fingers over the etched doorframe, test the surface of the programmable-matter gel with a fingertip. Thin gloves allow tactile exploration while providing a membrane between your skin and surfaces whose material properties are not fully understood. The filaments in the mineral clay, in particular, are best not touched with bare skin — not because they're known to be harmful, but because the faint pulsing light they emit operates at a frequency that several travelers have reported feeling *in their bloodstream* for hours after contact, a rhythmic, twelve-second throb that is physically harmless but psychologically maddening.

**Head covering:** A warm hat, and separately, if you are sensitive to subsonic frequencies, consider quality earplugs or noise-isolating earbuds (without music — you do not want to mask the Nursery's soundscape, you want to attenuate it). The pervasive subsonic hum is felt as much as heard, and it enters partly through the bones of the skull. A hat won't stop it, but the earplugs can reduce the audible-frequency components enough to make the experience less oppressive.

### What to Carry

**Minimal pack.** The spaces between structures are tight, and you will be navigating by intuition as much as by sight. A large backpack will catch, snag, and generally make you miserable. A small chest pack or a thigh-mounted pouch is ideal — something that keeps your essentials accessible without extending your profile.

**Contents:**

- **Water.** 1 liter minimum. The cold and the quiet will mask your thirst, but the low humidity on the approach side and the strange condensation dynamics inside mean you'll dehydrate without noticing. Do not drink the condensation from the crystallized air column. I don't care that it looks clean. It precipitated out of solidified atmosphere in a location where matter is in an unresolved state. Bring your own water.

- **Calorie-dense food.** A few energy bars or similar. Not because you'll be there long enough to need a meal, but because eating something familiar — tasting something known, feeling the texture of a food you recognize — is a powerful grounding technique when the Nursery's ambient strangeness starts to accumulate. Halfway through your visit, stop. Eat a bite of something. Taste it. Let your body remember what *resolved* material reality tastes like.

- **A notebook and pencil.** Not a phone, not a tablet, not any electronic device (see below). A physical notebook. Paper. A pencil, specifically — ink pens have been reported to behave erratically near the programmable-matter gel patches, either flowing too freely or not at all, as though the ink's viscosity is being interrogated by the substrate. Pencil graphite, being a simple carbon lattice, seems to be below the threshold of whatever material-curiosity the Nursery's surfaces exhibit. Write down what you see. Not because you'll forget — you won't; the Nursery is deeply memorable — but because the act of *describing* is an act of *resolving*, and periodically forcing yourself to commit an observation to language is a way of exercising your resolution faculties so they don't atrophy during your visit. Also, your notes will be valuable later, when you're trying to explain this place to someone and discovering that language becomes strangely slippery around the memories.

- **A small, familiar object.** A coin. A key. A stone from your garden. Something you've held a thousand times, something whose weight and texture and temperature are written into your muscle memory. Keep it in your pocket. Touch it periodically. This is your ontological anchor. When the floor is flickering between states and the doorframe is suggesting a threshold to somewhere that doesn't exist yet and the creature is looking at you with the attention of a process that has no mind, reach into your pocket and close your hand around this object and feel its *thereness*, its committed, resolved, single-state *thereness*. It will help more than you expect.

- **A watch.** Mechanical, not digital. Time at the Nursery has that thick, sluggish quality, and your internal clock will become unreliable within the first fifteen minutes. You need an external reference, and it needs to be mechanical because (see below) electronic devices are not reliable here. A wind-up watch with a sweeping second hand is ideal. The second hand serves a dual purpose: it tells you how much time has passed, and its continuous, visible movement is a reassurance that time *is* passing, that you are not stuck in the Nursery's slow, suspended almost-stasis. Glance at it. Watch the second hand sweep. Feel time moving. This matters.

### Electronic Devices

Leave them on the approach side of the wall. All of them.

This is not because the Nursery is known to destroy electronics — the reports are more complicated than that. What happens is: devices brought into the Nursery *work*, mostly, but they work *differently*. Phones have been reported to receive text messages in scripts that match the doorframe's semiotic-static characters. Camera sensors produce images that contain more visual information than they should — details the photographer doesn't remember seeing, structures that weren't in the frame, ground surfaces in states that the photographer's eyes never registered but the sensor apparently did. GPS units report coordinates that don't correspond to any terrestrial location and occasionally report *negative* coordinates, implying a position inside the Earth or in a spatial framework that uses a different origin point.

One traveler brought a laptop and attempted to write their observations in real time. The text they produced was coherent for the first twenty minutes. After that, the word processor began auto-completing their sentences with material that the traveler did not type and does not remember thinking — descriptions of structures that weren't present, accounts of changes that hadn't happened yet (but in two cases, *did* happen in the subsequent hour). The traveler describes the experience as "my laptop started taking its own notes."

The devices are not broken when you retrieve them. They return to normal function within minutes of leaving the Nursery. But the data they collected inside is... unreliable in ways that are more unsettling than simple corruption would be. It's *too coherent*. It makes *too much sense*. It describes a version of the Nursery that is more resolved than the one you experienced, as though the devices, lacking the human capacity for tolerating ambiguity, resolved the contradictions on their own, and not necessarily in the same direction as consensus reality.

Pencil and paper. Mechanical watch. Your own eyes and ears and hands. That's it.

## Navigation and Movement

### Getting There

The freestanding concrete wall is located in a flat, cold lowland. The specific lowland varies by account — it has been accessed from at least four different locations across three known realities, always in flat, cold terrain, always as a wall that shouldn't be there. The consistent features are: the wall is concrete, it stands alone, it has a gap the width of a doorway, and the air through the gap is colder and smells of graphite and wet chalk. If you are in the right kind of terrain and you see this wall, you have found Gant's Threshold.

The approach side is mundane. Flat ground, cold air, an ordinary sky. Note the conditions carefully before you step through. Note the temperature. Note the quality of the light. Note the feel of the ground. These observations become your *baseline* — the reality-state you will be returning to, and the contrast against which you will measure the Nursery's strangeness. The clearer your baseline, the more effectively you can notice and manage the shifts on the other side.

### Moving Inside

Walk slowly. I cannot emphasize this enough. The ground is a mosaic of incompatible surfaces with different friction coefficients, different temperatures, and different structural behaviors. Some surfaces are rigid. Some have a faint give. The programmable-matter gel patches are solid enough to walk on but produce an uncanny sensation — not softness, exactly, but a feeling that the surface is *deciding* to be solid in response to your weight, and might decide otherwise. Move at the pace you would use on an icy sidewalk: deliberate, flat-footed, with your weight centered over your hips and your steps short.

Test each surface before committing your weight. Use the toe of your boot. Feel for temperature changes, for unexpected give, for the faint vibration that some surfaces produce. The tessellated ceramic tiles are generally reliable — firm, flat, stable — and they're common enough that you can often route your path from tile patch to tile patch. The brushed steel sections are slippery when condensation is present. The basalt is reliable but cold. Avoid stepping directly on the filament-threaded mineral clay; the filaments are delicate, and while there's no evidence that damaging them causes any consequence, there's also no evidence that it doesn't, and in a place this dense with ontological potential, caution about disturbing active processes seems wise.

### Do Not Approach the Boundaries

The edges of the Nursery — wherever they are — produce proprioceptive confusion. This is not a subtle effect. Travelers who have attempted to reach the boundary describe a growing sense that the distance they're covering is not the distance they think they're covering, that each step is being *edited* — sometimes longer, sometimes shorter — in a way that makes forward progress feel like an asymptotic approach to a limit they can never reach. Some travelers have experienced this as nausea. Others as a profound spatial anxiety, a sense that they are simultaneously too close to the edge and impossibly far from it. None have reported reaching a definitive boundary.

Stay in the interior. The density of structures there provides landmarks (such as they are) and the ground, while contradictory, is at least *assertive* — it's trying to be something, which gives it a solidity that the ambiguous boundary zones lack.

### The Doorframe

You will encounter the freestanding doorframe with the etched characters. You will feel the temptation to walk through it. The description is accurate: the landscape through the frame looks the same but shifted slightly toward blue, with the ground about four centimeters lower. The gut feeling that walking through the frame is a different action than walking around it is reported universally and consistently.

My recommendation: do not walk through the frame on your first visit.

This is not a prohibition. Several travelers have stepped through the frame and reported no dramatic effect — they found themselves on the other side, in what appeared to be the same Nursery, and stepping back through the frame returned them to their starting point, or at least to a starting point so similar as to be indistinguishable. But. There are subtler reports. One traveler noted that after stepping through the frame, the creature appeared almost immediately, as though the act of using the threshold had drawn its attention. Another reported that the subsonic hum shifted in pitch — still subsonic, but differently subsonic — and took several minutes to return to its prior frequency after she stepped back through. A third reported that the colors of the bruise-bricks were different after the transit — shifted by a shade, from gray-violet to something closer to gray-blue — and never shifted back during the remainder of their visit.

These are small differences. Maybe meaningless. But the Nursery is a place of small differences that may not be meaningless, and the doorframe is explicitly a *threshold* in a location where the concept of *threshold* seems to carry genuine ontological weight. Until you understand the Nursery better — until you've visited multiple times and developed an intuitive sense of its rhythms — treat the doorframe as something to observe, not to use.

### The Staircase

The seven-step staircase descending into the ground is fascinating and draws every visitor. The varying materials of the steps, the hovering force-plane, the multi-state floor at the bottom — it's the most concentrated expression of the Nursery's nature, a vertical cross-section through its layered potential.

Descend no further than the fifth step on your first visit. Here's why:

The first four steps are made of stable, resolved materials — stone, concrete, aerogel, and a conventional alloy. They're strange, but they're *committed to being what they are*. The fifth step — the hovering force-plane — is the transition point. Below it, the materials become less resolved. The sixth and seventh steps (accounts vary on what they're made of; they may not be consistently anything) exist in a state of higher ambiguity, and the floor at the bottom is actively flickering between states. The deeper you go, the more unresolved the environment becomes, and the more your proprioceptive and perceptual systems have to work to maintain coherence.

More importantly: the creature was seen descending the staircase and sinking into the floor at the bottom. The floor *accepted* it. You are not the creature. You are a visitor made of resolved matter with committed physics, and there is no reason to believe the unresolved floor at the bottom of the staircase would accept you in the same way. What it might do instead is an open question, and the Nursery is not a good place for open questions to which you are the experimental test.

Stand on the fourth or fifth step. Look down. Observe the flickering floor. Feel the force-plane tingle under your boot. Note the way the staircase seems to continue, implied, beyond the seventh step — that suggestion of an eighth step forming in the multi-state floor. Then climb back up. That's enough. That's more than enough.

## Duration

One hour. Maybe ninety minutes. That's your target for a first visit.

This is not about physical safety — you could probably stay longer without acute harm. It's about cognitive endurance. The Nursery exerts a constant, low-level demand on your perceptual and interpretive faculties. Everything you look at requires more processing than normal because everything you look at is *more than one thing*. Your brain will be working harder than you realize, running parallel interpretations of every surface, every structure, every sound, trying to resolve contradictions that cannot be resolved, and the fatigue from this accumulates below the threshold of awareness until it suddenly crosses it, at which point you'll feel a profound, heavy exhaustion — not sleepy, but *thick*, as though your thoughts have taken on the Nursery's sluggish temporal quality.

When you notice this — when your thoughts start to feel slow and syrupy, when you realize you've been staring at the mortar between two bruise-bricks for an indeterminate time without any sense of the minutes passing — it's time to leave. Check your mechanical watch. Note how long you've been there. Eat a bite of your familiar food. Touch your anchor object. Then walk back to the gap in the wall, step through, and stand on the other side for at least ten minutes before going anywhere else.

The return to baseline is usually smooth but sometimes disorienting. The ordinary ground on the approach side may feel aggressively, almost painfully *singular* — so committed to being one thing that it seems loud, garish, over-determined, the way a quiet room seems to ring with silence after prolonged noise. This passes. Give it a few minutes. Drink some water. Let your senses recalibrate.

## What to Do If Something Changes While You're There

It probably won't. The Nursery changes at geological timescales, and an hour is nothing to it. But "probably" is not "certainly," and there are a few scenarios to prepare for:

**If a new structure appears:** Stay still. Don't approach it. A nucleation event — the formation of a new structure from the dense ontological substrate — has never been directly observed, but the theoretical energy involved is significant. If you see something forming, the safest position is away from it, still, not drawing attention. Whether the Nursery can *pay attention* is debatable. Treat it as though it can.

**If the ground beneath you changes state:** Step off it, calmly, onto the nearest stable surface (ceramic tile is your friend). The ground-state changes described in accounts are usually slow — a surface shifting from one material to another over minutes or hours — but the phase-through effect, where two ground surfaces occupy the same space, has been described as "sudden" by one traveler, meaning it may have happened over seconds rather than minutes. If the surface you're standing on begins to feel different — warmer, colder, softer, more ambiguous — move to an adjacent surface and observe from there.

**If the quiet changes:** This is the one that warrants the most attention. The Nursery's quiet is a feature, not an absence — it is the sound of enormous forces in equilibrium. If the quality of the quiet changes — if the subsonic hum shifts, if the dripping from the pipe increases or ceases, if you hear a sound you haven't heard before — stop whatever you're doing and assess. A change in the sound environment may indicate a shift in the balance of competing nucleation processes, and while this is probably not dangerous to a visitor, it represents a state change in the Nursery itself, and state changes in a location saturated with ontological potential are events you want to observe from a position of stability, not while climbing a staircase made of seven different materials.

**If the creature approaches you:** Hold still. Do not reach for it. Do not crouch down to its level. Let it look at you. Let the eye contact happen. If it moves toward you rather than past you, remain still and allow it to approach. One traveler reports that the creature paused at their feet, rested its ceramic-tessellated head against their boot for approximately four seconds, and then moved on. The traveler describes the sensation as "being bookmarked." What this means is unclear. The traveler reported no lasting effects, except that on their second visit to the Nursery, six months later, the creature appeared almost immediately upon their entry, as though it remembered them. Or as though the Nursery did.

## After the Visit

### Immediate Aftermath (First 24 Hours)

You will be tired. More tired than the physical activity warrants. Sleep will come easily but may be strange — several travelers report dreams characterized by *material specificity*, dreams in which the texture and temperature and composition of surfaces are vivid and detailed in a way they never normally are. You may dream of tiles, of bricks, of cold polished stone, of surfaces flickering between states. These dreams are not distressing, exactly, but they're *dense*, and you may wake feeling as though you've been doing cognitive work in your sleep. You have been. Your brain is processing and filing the Nursery's contradictions, trying to find a schema that accommodates them. It will settle down within a few days.

Eat well. Eat foods with strong, definite flavors and textures. Crusty bread. Sharp cheese. Tart fruit. Your senses need recalibration after the Nursery's gray, cold, ambiguous palette, and strong sensory input helps.

### Short-Term Effects (First Week)

You may experience periodic moments of what I'll call *material hyperawareness* — sudden, vivid perceptions of the specific material composition of whatever you're touching or standing on. You'll be walking across a tiled floor and abruptly become intensely, almost uncomfortably aware that these tiles are *ceramic*, fired at a specific temperature, glazed with a specific compound, grouted with a specific cement, and that all of these are *choices* that could have been otherwise. This is the Nursery's residue. It's showing you what you always knew but never attended to: that the material world is the result of decisions, and decisions imply alternatives, and alternatives are always present, always pressing, even in a reality that has long since committed to a single outcome.

This effect fades. By the end of the first week, surfaces will go back to being what they are without insistently reminding you of what they could have been. Enjoy the interlude, if you can. It's a rare gift — a brief period of heightened sensitivity to the *made-ness* of the material world, the sheer improbability of everything being this particular way rather than some other way. Some travelers describe this as the most valuable part of the experience.

### Long-Term Effects

If you visit once, there are no documented long-term effects beyond a persistent, detailed memory of the place and a slight, permanent increase in attention to material surfaces. You will notice textures more. You will register the composition of floors, walls, objects with more awareness than you did before. This is minor. It's a recalibration, not a pathology.

If you visit repeatedly — and many travelers do; the Nursery is, despite everything, *compelling* in a way that's hard to articulate — the effects deepen. Repeat visitors describe a growing sense of the Nursery as a *place they know*, with the attendant emotional weight of familiarity. They begin to notice changes between visits — the moss has extended, the mortar has shifted, the eighth step has become marginally more real — and they feel, about these changes, something very like the feeling of watching a child grow. This is the emotion the name evokes, of course. It is a nursery. Something is growing there. Something is, very slowly, being born. And repeated visitors develop a relationship with that process that is difficult to categorize and, by some accounts, difficult to relinquish.

I know of one traveler who has visited the Nursery over forty times across a decade. She describes the place with the specific, patient attention of someone describing a garden they've tended for years. She can tell you which structures have grown, which have receded, where new nucleation events have occurred and what they suggest about the direction of the Nursery's slow self-determination. She says the creature recognizes her now — that it comes to her within minutes of her arrival and walks beside her for a portion of her visit before descending its staircase and vanishing. She says the Nursery is, in her estimation, about six percent more coherent than it was when she first visited, and that if she had to guess, she'd say it's becoming something with a lot of water in it. A harbor, maybe. Or a bathhouse. Or a reservoir. Something with water.

She could be wrong. She probably is. The Nursery may be inscrutable on human timescales, its eventual form — if it has one — unknowable from inside a single lifetime's observations.

But she goes back. Every few months, she goes back, and she walks slowly through the dense, cold, quiet space, and she notes what's changed, and she touches the bruise-bricks with her gloved hand, and she listens to the chime that has already ended, and she watches the condensation drip from the fractal pipe, and she stands on the fourth step of the staircase and looks down at the floor that hasn't decided what it is.

You'll understand, when you've been there. You'll understand why she goes back.

## Final Note

The Nursery at Gant's Threshold is not a spectacle. It will not awe you in the way that vast or violent multiverse locations awe you. It will not produce great stories for other travelers over drinks. It is small, cold, quiet, and slow, and the strangeness it offers is not dramatic but *textural* — a strangeness of surfaces, materials, and the unresolved space between what things are and what they might become.

What it will do, if you let it, is change the way you understand *ground*. Not metaphorical ground — literal ground. The surface of reality. The material fact of the world under your feet. You have always walked on ground that knew what it was. After the Nursery, you will know that this knowledge is not inevitable, that somewhere there is a place where the ground is still making up its mind, and that the process by which it decides is slow, and patient, and beautiful in a way that has no name, and that you were, for one cold and quiet hour, standing in the middle of it, feeling it think.

Go. Be careful. Be slow. Be quiet. And when the creature looks at you, look back.

It's the least you can do for a place that hasn't been born yet.
