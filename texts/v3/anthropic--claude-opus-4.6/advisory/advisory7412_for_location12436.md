# Preparing for the Plateau of Constant Engines

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the description for a while. Read it again. Let the implications settle, because the greatest danger of the Plateau is not physical — it's ontological. You are visiting a place that has *finished*. Not died, not failed, not wound down — finished. Reached equilibrium. Solved itself. Every other location you've visited in the multiverse, no matter how alien, has been in some state of becoming. This one isn't. You need to understand what that means for a creature like you, who is nothing *but* becoming — metabolism, cognition, desire, decay — before you set foot on that wet basalt.

You are, in the most literal sense, the only incomplete thing that will exist on that plateau. The only open process. The only system still running a deficit between what it is and what it will be. This will feel like something. You need to be ready for what it feels like.

## Physical Preparation

### Thermoregulation

The temperature is fixed just above freezing — call it 1-3°C, permanently, with no variation for wind chill because there appears to be no wind, only the omnipresent moisture and the pressure differentials created by the engines themselves. This is not a dramatic cold. It will not kill you quickly. It is the kind of cold that kills you slowly by convincing you it isn't that bad.

The problem is the wet. You will be wet within minutes and you will stay wet for the entire duration of your visit. There is no drying out. There is no warming up by a fire — there is nothing to burn and the atmosphere is so saturated that combustion may be unreliable. Every insulation strategy you know from Earth mountaineering assumes some possibility of drying, of finding shelter, of generating warmth. Here, those assumptions fail.

**What to wear:**

- **Base layer:** Synthetic hydrophobic materials — polypropylene or similar. Not merino wool. Wool retains warmth when wet, yes, but it also holds moisture against the skin, and in an environment of permanent saturation, you need fabric that refuses water at the fiber level rather than managing it. You want the moisture to bead on the outside of the fabric rather than wicking into it, because there's nowhere to wick *to*.

- **Insulation layer:** Closed-cell synthetic insulation — something like Primaloft or its multiverse equivalents. Not down. Down is useless here. It will be saturated within an hour and will never recover. You need insulation that maintains loft when soaked, and you need to accept that it *will* be soaked, permanently, and that your insulating layer is going to carry an extra kilogram or two of water for your entire visit.

- **Outer shell:** Full waterproof membrane — but understand that "waterproof" here means something different than it does in a rainstorm. Rain comes from above. This moisture comes from everywhere. It condenses on you. It forms on the inside of your shell as readily as the outside, because the humidity differential between your microclimate and the atmosphere is enormous (your body heat will be the only warmth source for kilometers, and it will attract condensation the way a cold glass sweats in a warm room — except inverted, you're the warm glass in a cold room). You may need to accept that the shell's primary job is wind protection and abrasion resistance rather than moisture management, because moisture management is a losing game here.

- **Extremities:** This is where the real danger lives. Your hands and feet will be wet and cold continuously. Neoprene gloves — the kind used in cold-water diving — will outperform any textile glove. Neoprene boots with rigid soles for the basalt. The rock is described as glassy and slick where the moss doesn't cover it. You need aggressive traction. Consider boot crampons or chains designed for wet rock, not ice — the micro-spikes used for icy trail running would work, but test them on wet polished stone first, not ice. The surface will be like wet tile, not like frozen ground.

- **Head and neck:** A neoprene hood or balaclava. Your head will be the primary heat-loss site, and the condensation on your face and scalp will be constant. Bring something to cover your ears that won't trap water — neoprene again, or a treated synthetic. Avoid anything cotton. Cotton will become a cold poultice on your skin and stay that way forever.

**Plan for hypothermia anyway.** Not acute hypothermia — you're unlikely to fall through ice or be caught in a blizzard — but the chronic, insidious kind. The kind where your core temperature drops half a degree and then another half degree over the course of days because there is no way to fully rewarm. Bring chemical heat packs, many of them, and understand their limitations: they require oxygen to activate, and the atmosphere should have oxygen (the moss is photosynthesizing), but the density of the moisture-laden air may affect their efficiency. Test one immediately upon arrival. Bring backup heating — battery-powered heating elements sewn into your base layer if your tech level supports it. If not, bring a companion, because shared body heat in a sealed bivouac may be your only reliable warming strategy.

### Nutrition and Hydration

Water is not a problem. Water is the *least* of your problems. You could practically drink the air. But you should bring a filtration system anyway, because the condensation on the metal surfaces will be laden with iron oxide, mineral deposits, and whatever chemical byproducts the engines are emitting (more on that below). Collect water from the moss or from clean basalt surfaces, not from the pipework or the structures.

Food is a serious problem. There is nothing to eat here. The moss is the only biology, and while you could theoretically eat moss — people have survived on it — you don't know what this moss is. It exists in an ecosystem with no consumers. It has never been eaten by anything. It has never needed to defend itself against being eaten. This means it probably hasn't evolved toxins (no selective pressure to do so), but it also means it hasn't evolved to be nutritious for anything, and it may have a biochemistry that's technically incompatible with your digestion. Don't eat the moss. Don't eat the lichen. Don't eat the green things that are neither.

Bring all your calories. Dense, high-fat, cold-tolerant food — pemmican, nut butters, hard cheese if it'll keep, chocolate, energy-dense bars. Nothing that requires cooking unless you bring a stove system that works in high humidity (pressurized liquid-fuel stoves will outperform gas canisters, which lose pressure in cold and may struggle with the moisture). Bring more food than you think you need, because your body will be burning calories for thermoregulation continuously, and the cold-wet combination is more metabolically expensive than either cold or wet alone.

**Estimate your caloric needs at 4,000-5,000 calories per day minimum.** You will feel hungrier than that. The plateau gives you nothing. You bring everything or you go without.

### The Sound

This is not adequately addressed by "bring earplugs." The sound is described as something the body feels before the ears parse it — a bass-frequency, whole-body vibration generated by machines the size of city blocks, transmitted through the rock, the air, the water film, the moisture in the atmosphere itself. Standard foam earplugs attenuate high-frequency noise effectively but are nearly useless against deep bass. Active noise cancellation works by generating inverse waveforms, but the harmonic complexity described here — an organ chord with each pipe a different diameter and alloy — would overwhelm any ANC system designed for consistent noise like airplane cabins.

**What to bring:**

- **Passive protection:** Custom-molded earplugs with the highest NRR rating you can find, *plus* over-ear hearing protection (the kind used on aircraft carrier decks or in artillery emplacements). Wear both simultaneously — this is called "double protection" and it's standard in extreme noise environments. This will reduce the audible component by 30-40 dB but will do very little about the felt component.

- **Vibration isolation:** This is the harder problem. The sound is in the ground. It will travel through your boot soles, up your skeleton, and into your skull via bone conduction. You cannot ear-plug your way out of bone conduction. Heavy vibration-damping insoles will help marginally. If you can obtain them, use boots with vibration-isolating midsoles — the kind used by jackhammer operators or heavy equipment drivers. These will reduce the amplitude of ground-transmitted vibration but will not eliminate it.

- **Psychological preparation for inescapable sound.** This is the real preparation. You need to know, before you go, that you will not experience silence at any point during your visit. Not when you sleep. Not when you are kilometers from the nearest structure. The sound will be there. It will be monotonous — the same chord, the same frequencies, forever — and monotonous inescapable noise drives people insane in predictable, well-documented ways. Sleep disruption comes first. Then irritability. Then difficulty concentrating. Then a kind of auditory hallucination where your brain, desperate for pattern variation, begins to *invent* changes in the sound — hearing melodies, hearing voices, hearing your name. This is normal. It is not the plateau speaking to you. It is your brain screaming for novelty in a world that contains none.

  Counter this by bringing your own sound. Music. Audiobooks. Recordings of variable, complex, human-generated noise. Play them through bone-conduction headphones (which will work *with* the ambient sound rather than trying to block it) at volumes sufficient to give your auditory cortex something to parse. Change the content frequently. Your brain needs variation the way your body needs food, and this plateau provides neither.

- **Duration limit:** I would strongly recommend not staying longer than 72 hours near the engine towns, and not longer than 7 days on the plateau at all. The psychological effects of the constant sound compound nonlinearly with time. Day one is uncomfortable. Day three is distressing. By day seven, your sense of self will begin to erode — not metaphorically, not poetically, but as a predictable neurological consequence of sustained low-frequency noise exposure and sensory monotony. The plateau has already solved itself. If you stay too long, some part of your brain will try to solve itself too, and you are not an equilibrium system. The attempt will damage you.

## Navigation and Orientation

The plateau offers you almost nothing to navigate by. There is no sun, no stars, no magnetic pole (you should bring a compass and test it, but be prepared for it to be useless — the massive iron structures may create local magnetic fields that dwarf the planetary field, if there even is one). The light is perfectly diffuse and casts no shadows. The terrain is flat basalt with no prominent landforms. The cloud ceiling is uniform and low.

**What to bring:**

- **Inertial navigation.** A dead-reckoning system that tracks your movement via accelerometer and gyroscope, independent of external references. GPS requires satellites; there are none here. Celestial navigation requires celestial bodies; there are none visible. Map-and-compass requires a map (none exists) and a reliable magnetic field (unlikely). Inertial navigation accumulates drift error over time, so you'll need to calibrate it against known points — the engine structures themselves can serve as landmarks, and you should log them obsessively.

- **Retroreflective markers.** Bring hundreds of them. Small, lightweight, adhesive-backed reflective markers that you affix to the basalt at regular intervals along your path. Use a headlamp or handheld light to spot them on your return. This is your lifeline. If your inertial navigation fails and you're between towns in a featureless expanse of wet rock and moss under a directionless gray sky, those markers are the only way home.

- **Document the towns.** Every town you encounter, sketch its layout, note its architectural style (cylindrical housings? right angles? hexagonal pattern?), estimate its size and the number of structures. This is your map. The towns are not identical and their variation is the only geography the plateau provides.

- **Do not trust distance estimation.** In the absence of shadows, in flat terrain, under diffuse light, with no familiar objects for scale, the human brain is catastrophically bad at judging distance. Those engine structures are *enormous*, and the plateau is *immense*, and a structure that looks like a twenty-minute walk may be a four-hour walk. Bring binoculars or a rangefinder. Verify distances mechanically before committing to a route.

## Equipment Considerations

### Shelter

You need a bivouac system that can handle permanent external condensation and near-freezing temperatures indefinitely. A standard tent will work, but understand its limitations:

- The outer fly will be dripping with condensation at all times, inside and out. Double-wall tents are designed for this, but the inner wall will also be damp because the ambient humidity is essentially 100%. Your sleeping bag will be damp. Your sleeping pad will be damp. Everything will be damp, always.

- Bring a fully waterproof bivouac bag for your sleeping system — not breathable, *waterproof*. Yes, this means condensation from your own body will collect inside it. This is the lesser evil. Without it, the ambient moisture will soak through any breathable fabric within hours.

- A vapor barrier liner against your skin, inside your sleeping bag, inside the bivvy bag. This traps your own moisture against you but prevents it from migrating into your insulation. It feels clammy and unpleasant. It works.

- Site selection: pitch on moss, not bare rock. The moss provides a millimeter or two of insulation from the cold basalt (negligible, but real) and — more importantly — provides traction for tent stakes. The basalt is too hard and too smooth for stakes. Bring rock-anchor stakes (the kind that wedge into cracks) or free-standing tent designs that don't require staking.

### Power

If you're bringing electronics — navigation equipment, headlamps, audio players for psychological maintenance — you need power. Solar panels are useless under that sky. Wind power is unreliable (there may be no wind; the atmosphere seems too stable for it). Bring batteries. Lots of batteries. Lithium-ion performs poorly in cold and wet; lithium iron phosphate is more tolerant. Keep batteries against your body to maintain their temperature. Bring more than you think you need. The plateau has no power outlets, and the engines — whatever they're generating — don't appear to have accessible electrical outputs. (Do not attempt to tap the engines for power. See below.)

### Light

The ambient light is described as sufficient — a directionless gray-white, like an overcast day — but it provides no contrast. You will struggle to see surface texture, to distinguish wet rock from moss, to spot hazards. Bring a headlamp with adjustable color temperature. A warm (amber/yellow) light may provide better contrast against the gray-green palette than a cool white light. Bring a powerful handheld spotlight for distance work and for spotting your retroreflective markers.

## Interacting with the Machines

### Do Not Enter the Structures

The buildings have doors that do not open and windows of thick green glass behind which nothing moves. Leave them that way. The temptation to break a window or force a door will be significant — you are a curious being in a world that invites no curiosity, and the interiors of those buildings are the only remaining mystery in a landscape that has otherwise solved itself. Resist.

The reasons are practical as well as philosophical:

- The structures may be pressurized. The engines are working — actively converting something — and the pipe networks connecting the structures suggest fluid systems under significant pressure. Breaking a seal could release superheated steam, chemical vapor, or something you don't have a name for. The atmosphere is already possibly engine exhaust; the concentrated version inside a sealed structure could be immediately toxic.

- The structural integrity of the buildings exists in the same state of arrested entropy as everything else. Rust that has progressed exactly far enough and no farther. Rivets swollen but not failing. The moment you breach a wall or a window, you may introduce a perturbation that the system's equilibrium can't accommodate. Or — perhaps worse — you may introduce a perturbation that it *can* accommodate, absorbing your disruption, normalizing it, adding your broken window to the set of things that are now permanent. What if the equilibrium is attractive? What if it pulls things toward itself? You don't want to find out what "being absorbed into a self-solving system" feels like from the inside.

### Do Not Touch the Moving Parts

The turbines and flywheels are visible through gaps in the cladding. They are turning. They have been turning at exactly this speed since before the concept of "since" had meaning here. They are not going to stop because you put your hand in them. They are going to continue at exactly the same speed, through your hand, because that is what equilibrium means. The machines do not care about you. They do not notice you. You are a perturbation too small to register in a system that has already resolved every perturbation it will ever face.

The moving parts are also wet and cold and made of dark iron and operating in an environment of zero illumination contrast. You might not see them moving until you're already too close. Keep a distance of at least ten meters from any exposed mechanism.

### The Pipes

The pipe networks are everywhere — elevated on trestles, running between structures, crossing the open plateau for kilometers. They carry something. The condensation patterns on their surface (orange, white, verdigris) suggest different metals or alloys in the pipe walls, which suggests different substances or different temperatures in different parts of the network. Some pipes may be extremely hot. Others may be extremely cold — cold enough to cause instant frostbite on contact. All of them are wet on the outside and therefore impossible to assess by visual inspection alone.

**Do not touch the pipes.** Bring an infrared thermometer and check the surface temperature of any pipe before passing near it. Some of these things may be carrying fluids at hundreds of degrees through walls of corroded iron that are thinner than they look.

### The Air

The description strongly implies that the cloud ceiling *is* engine exhaust — that the atmosphere itself is an industrial byproduct. You should bring respiratory protection and use it, at minimum, when near the engine towns.

A full-face respirator with combined particulate and organic vapor cartridges is the baseline. If you can obtain a self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA) or a closed-circuit rebreather, bring it, but be aware of the weight and duration limitations. Realistically, you probably can't carry enough breathing gas for a multi-day expedition, so the respirator is your working tool and the SCBA is your emergency backup.

Before you arrive, if possible, obtain information about the atmosphere's composition from previous travelers or from portal-side analysis. The moss is photosynthesizing, which implies the presence of CO2 and the production of O2, which is encouraging. But photosynthesis alone doesn't tell you about trace gases, industrial volatiles, heavy metal vapor, or any of the other things that engine exhaust might contain.

**Monitor yourself for symptoms:** headache, dizziness, nausea, metallic taste, confusion, skin irritation, eye irritation. If you develop any of these, mask up immediately (if you aren't already) and move away from the nearest structures.

## The Psychological Preparation

This is the section that matters most.

### The Plateau Will Not Threaten You

This is the fundamental difficulty. You are preparing for a place that will not try to kill you, not try to seduce you, not try to transform you, not try to communicate with you. It will not present you with challenges, puzzles, hostile entities, extreme weather, or moral dilemmas. It will present you with *itself* — complete, resolved, stable — and it will do so indefinitely, without variation, without response to your presence, without acknowledgment that you exist.

Most dangerous multiverse locations are dangerous because they act on you. The Plateau is dangerous because it doesn't. It is the most aggressive form of indifference imaginable — not the indifference of an empty room, but the indifference of a room that is *full*, that is doing something enormous and purposeful and eternal, and that does not include you in any part of it.

You will feel irrelevant here. Not small — smallness implies a scale on which you register, just at the low end. Irrelevant. External. Like a typo in a book that's already been published. The machines are working. The moss is growing. The clouds are forming. None of it needs you. None of it knows you. You are the only open question in a world of answers, and the world is not asking you.

### This Will Affect Your Sense of Purpose

Human beings are purpose-generating creatures. We walk into a room and ask "what is this for?" We see a machine and ask "what does it do?" We see a road and ask "where does it go?" The Plateau answers all of these questions with the same answer: *itself*. The machines work in order to work. The roads connect structures to structures. The product of the process is the process. And your brain, which has evolved over millions of years to find and exploit purpose in its environment, will grind against this like a gear with no teeth.

You will want to find meaning here. You will want the machines to be *doing* something — refining ore, generating power, building something, preparing for something. You will want the empty buildings to be waiting for occupants. You will want the stopped carts on the rails to be going somewhere. You will invent narratives: the builders left, the builders are coming back, the builders ascended, the builders are inside the machines, the machines *are* the builders. None of these narratives will be confirmed or denied by anything you observe, because the Plateau does not participate in narrative. It is post-narrative. It is what a story looks like after the story is over and the book stays open to the last page forever.

**Bring a journal. Write in it compulsively.** Not because you need to record observations (although you do), but because the act of writing is the act of narrating, and narrating is how you maintain the sense that time is passing and that you are a being who exists *in* time. The Plateau will try to dissolve your sense of temporal flow — not maliciously, but structurally, by providing no markers of change. Writing creates change. Each word is different from the last. Each sentence moves forward. The journal is your proof that sequence still exists, that before-and-after is still a valid concept, that *you* are still a valid concept.

### The Sound and the Self

The constant sound will, over time, begin to feel like your own thoughts. This is not mystical. It is a well-documented auditory phenomenon: when a sound is constant, invariant, and inescapable, the brain eventually ceases to process it as external stimulus and begins to integrate it into the background hum of consciousness. You will stop hearing the engines and start *thinking* in the key they play. Your internal monologue will sync to their rhythm. Your breathing will sync to their rhythm. Your heartbeat will try.

This is the point at which you need to leave, or at minimum take aggressive countermeasures — blast unfamiliar music through your headphones, do vigorous physical exercise, have a conversation with a companion (if you have one) about something specific and personal and *not about the Plateau*. Talk about home. Talk about food you miss. Talk about a book you read when you were fourteen. Anything that reasserts the existence of a world where things happen, where sounds change, where people argue and laugh and burn dinner.

If you are traveling alone: bring voice recordings from people you love. Bring the most chaotic, unpredictable, human-generated noise you can find — live concert recordings with audience chatter, children's birthday parties, busy market streets, thunderstorms. Anything with variation. Variation is medicine here. Variation is survival.

### The Temptation to Stay

This is counterintuitive, because everything about the Plateau sounds unpleasant — cold, wet, loud, empty, gray. Why would anyone want to stay?

Because equilibrium is seductive. Because the Plateau has no problems. Because it is the only place in the multiverse where *nothing needs to be done*. The machines are working. The moss is growing. The temperature is stable. The system is solved. And after enough time here — enough time in the cold wet noise with no threats, no obligations, no uncertainties — a part of your brain that is exhausted from a lifetime of vigilance and planning and wanting and fearing will whisper: *this is what peace feels like.*

It isn't. It is what *completion* feels like, and you are not complete. You are alive, which means you are unfinished by definition, and the Plateau's peace is not available to you except by ceasing to be what you are. People have stayed. (This is an intuition, not a confirmed fact, but I believe it with a conviction that feels like memory.) People have sat down on the moss between two engine houses, in the sound, in the wet, in the cold, and thought: *I could stop here. I could stop.* Not die. Just — stop. Become part of the equilibrium. Another thing that is wet and cold and permanent and not waiting for anything.

**Set a departure time before you arrive. Write it on your hand. Set alarms. Tell someone on the other side of the portal when you'll be back and instruct them to come get you if you're late.** Do not trust your in-the-moment judgment about when to leave. Your in-the-moment judgment will be compromised by the sound and the cold and the terrible, seductive, annihilating peace of a world that has no use for you.

## What to Bring: Summary Checklist

**Clothing and Warmth:**
- Hydrophobic synthetic base layers (multiple sets)
- Closed-cell synthetic insulation mid-layer
- Waterproof hard shell, top and bottom
- Neoprene gloves (multiple pairs)
- Neoprene boots with rigid soles and aggressive traction (micro-spikes or chains)
- Neoprene hood/balaclava
- Vapor barrier liner for sleeping
- Chemical heat packs (dozens)
- Battery-powered heating elements if available

**Shelter and Sleep:**
- Double-wall tent, freestanding (no stakes required)
- Waterproof (not breathable) bivvy bag
- Synthetic-fill sleeping bag rated to -10°C (to account for permanent dampness reducing effectiveness)
- Closed-cell foam sleeping pad (not inflatable — an inflatable pad in this humidity will grow mold inside within days)
- Rock-anchor stakes or heavy objects for tent stability

**Nutrition:**
- All food for entire trip + 50% emergency surplus
- 4,000-5,000 calories/day minimum
- High-fat, no-cook-required food preferred
- Pressurized liquid-fuel stove + fuel for hot drinks (the psychological value of a hot drink here cannot be overstated)
- Water filtration system (for collecting condensation from clean surfaces)

**Navigation:**
- Inertial navigation system (dead reckoning)
- Retroreflective markers (hundreds)
- Headlamp with warm color temperature
- Handheld spotlight
- Rangefinder or binoculars
- Journal and waterproof pen for mapping

**Sound Management:**
- Custom-molded high-NRR earplugs
- Over-ear hearing protection (industrial grade)
- Vibration-damping boot insoles
- Bone-conduction headphones
- Audio player loaded with varied, chaotic, human-generated sound
- Voice recordings from loved ones

**Respiratory and Environmental:**
- Full-face respirator with combination cartridges (multiple replacement cartridges)
- SCBA or rebreather (emergency use)
- Infrared thermometer (for pipe surface assessment)
- Atmospheric monitoring device if available (O2, CO, CO2, VOCs)

**Psychological:**
- Journal and multiple waterproof pens
- Photographs from home
- A book — a novel, something with characters and plot and change
- A predetermined, non-negotiable departure time
- A companion, if at all possible

## Final Thoughts

The Plateau of Constant Engines is not hostile. It is not haunted. It is not cursed or trapped or designed to harm you. It is simply *done*. It is a world that reached the end of its processes and then continued to run, not because anything requires it, but because running is what it *is*. The machines work. The moss grows. The clouds form. The sound continues.

You are going there to witness this. To see what a world looks like when every question has been answered, when every gradient has been resolved, when entropy has reached its local minimum and stayed there. It is a profoundly rare thing in the multiverse — most worlds are still happening, still unfolding, still in the chaotic middle of their stories. The Plateau is a world that has reached the last page.

Go. See it. Stand in the moss between two engine houses and listen to the chord that has never started and will never stop. Feel the cold water condense on your skin. Watch the clouds form from the chimneys and spread across a sky that has never known the sun.

And then leave. Leave while leaving still feels like something you're choosing to do, rather than something the Plateau has forgotten to prevent. Leave while your journal still has blank pages. Leave while the voices on your audio player still sound like they belong to people you know, rather than artifacts from a world that seems increasingly theoretical. Leave while you still believe in sequence, in change, in the radical improbability of being a warm, hungry, finite creature in a cold, sated, infinite place.

The Plateau will not notice your departure. It will not notice your absence. The moss will be exactly as green, the engines exactly as loud, the clouds exactly as low and gray and permanent.

That's how you know you've survived: you leave, and nothing changes, and you are the only thing in the entire landscape that is capable of caring about that fact.

Go home. Eat something warm. Listen to the rain — real rain, rain that starts and stops, rain that means something is happening in the sky. Remember what weather feels like when it isn't a byproduct. Remember what sound feels like when it has a beginning.

And remember the Plateau, not as a place you escaped from, but as a place you were brave enough to witness and wise enough to leave — the world that finished, and the green moss growing softly in its cracks like a prayer no one is saying, to a god who was never there.
