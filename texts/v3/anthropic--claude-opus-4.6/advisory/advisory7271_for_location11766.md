# Preparing for the Hollow Garden

---

## First: Understanding What You Are Walking Into

Before any equipment list, before any physical preparation, you need to sit with what this place actually is, because your preparation will fail if it is organized around the wrong assumptions.

You are not going caving. You are not spelunking through a biological curiosity. You are entering the interior of a living organism that has replaced geology itself—an entity of unknown but certainly vast scale—and you are doing so by visiting a specific location within it that the organism has itself set apart as anomalous. You are, to use an analogy that will become uncomfortably literal, entering a living body and standing inside something like its unconscious mind. The cavity is a thought. The thought is incomplete. You will be present inside an ongoing cognitive event that operates on timescales and through mechanisms that have no relationship to your own neurology.

This means your preparation must address several categories of risk simultaneously:

1. **Biological exposure** to an organism whose metabolism operates outside known parameters
2. **Physical navigation** in a space where geometry is unreliable
3. **Perceptual and cognitive hazard** from extended proximity to spatial anomalies and an environment that may interact with consciousness
4. **The fundamental uncertainty of your exit**, since you are inside something alive that is actively reshaping itself around you
5. **The philosophical and psychological weight** of the experience itself, which multiple accounts suggest is the most durable and least manageable aftereffect

I'll address each of these, but I want to be clear from the outset: no preparation makes this safe. Preparation makes this *survivable with your identity mostly intact*, which is the realistic goal.

## Physical Preparation: Your Body

### Weeks Before

**Cardiovascular and respiratory conditioning.** Not because the Garden demands athletic performance—by all accounts, you will mostly stand still or walk slowly—but because the environment features intermittent air pressure changes (the thunder-pulses), atmospheric composition that deviates from standard (elevated concentrations of volatile organic compounds, possibly novel ones), and warmth with high humidity. Your heart and lungs will be working harder than you think. People who arrive in poor cardiovascular shape report headaches, nausea, and a kind of heavy-limbed fatigue within the first hour that compounds every subsequent challenge. You want a resting heart rate under 70 and good VO2 max. Nothing extreme—just honest fitness.

**Fasting protocol.** For 24 hours before entry, eat only simple, bland foods. No dairy, no heavy proteins, nothing fermented. The air inside the Garden is described consistently as sweet and green, intensifying to "almost narcotic" during thunder-pulses. Multiple travelers report that a full or unsettled stomach converts this from pleasant to overwhelming. Two have reported vomiting, and vomiting in the Garden creates complications I'll address later (you do not want to introduce foreign biological material into that space if you can possibly avoid it). Light meals. Rice. Plain bread. Clear broth. Hydrate well but stop heavy water intake two hours before entry so you're not dealing with a full bladder.

**Sleep.** Get two full nights of genuine, good sleep before your visit. Not one. Two. This is non-negotiable. The Garden's spatial anomalies and perceptual effects are dramatically worse when you are sleep-deprived. The spiral whorl on the left wall—the one that inverts your depth perception—has been described as "mildly disorienting" by rested travelers and "a corridor I almost walked into and could not have walked back out of" by one who had slept poorly. Your cognitive defenses against the unreality of the space depend on your prefrontal cortex functioning well, and your prefrontal cortex depends on sleep.

**Ear pressure management.** The thunder-pulses cause pressure shifts significant enough to pop your ears. If you have any sinus congestion, eustachian tube dysfunction, or recent history of ear infection, resolve it first. Bring a mild nasal decongestant as a precaution. The pain of failing to equalize during a pulse is apparently sharp and sudden, and pain in that environment is dangerous because it breaks your concentration at exactly the moment—during a pulse—when concentration matters most.

### What to Wear

**Close-fitting, natural fibers against the skin.** Cotton or linen base layers. The root system is alive; it has an immune response of some kind (the sterile-yet-alive fluid suggests this). Synthetic materials—polyester, nylon, technical fabrics—seem to provoke more interest from questing root tendrils than natural fibers do. This may be because the organism can recognize biological material as "not-self but comprehensible" and categorize synthetics as something that warrants investigation. You do not want to warrant investigation. Wear things that a very old, very large plant would find boring.

**No bright colors.** The luminous roots react to certain wavelengths. Reds and oranges appear to cause subtle brightening in nearby root-lanterns, which sounds harmless but means you are *interacting with the organism's light system*, and you don't know what that interaction means to it. Earth tones. Cream, brown, olive, grey. Become visually quiet.

**Footwear that you can feel the floor through, but that protects you from moisture.** The floor is living root-weave that is warm and damp. You need to feel its texture—partly for footing on an uneven surface, partly because changes in the floor's texture, temperature, or pulsation rate are your early-warning system for structural shifts in the cavity. But you also need a barrier between your skin and the root-fluid that condenses on surfaces. Until someone definitively characterizes that fluid, assume it is biologically active in ways that might not be friendly to human tissue over prolonged contact. Thin-soled leather boots or shoes, well fitted. Break them in thoroughly beforehand. You may be in them for hours and cannot afford blisters—again, you do not want to introduce your blood into this environment.

**Gloves.** Thin leather or tightly woven cotton. Same principle. You will want to touch things. The impulse to touch the walls, to feel the pulse of the thick roots, to dip your fingers in the basin fluid, is reportedly almost irresistible. Having a barrier between your skin and the surfaces gives you a moment of mediation—a moment to *choose* rather than simply *do*. This matters. If you do deliberately choose to make bare-skin contact with the root surfaces or the fluid (and I'll discuss that below), you want it to be a conscious decision, not a reflex.

## Equipment

### What to Bring

**A mechanical timepiece.** Not digital. Not electronic. A wind-up watch with a sweep second hand. Electronics in the Garden are unreliable—not consistently dead, which would be simpler, but intermittently functional in misleading ways. Phones will show the wrong time, or the right time on a clock face that has somehow acquired an extra hour. Digital watches will run backward for a while and then forward at double speed. A mechanical watch, powered by a spring, driven by gears, will at least be *consistently* inaccurate if the space distorts time, and you can use the second hand as a check against the forty-second pulsation cycle of the major roots to establish your own sense of temporal rhythm. You need some anchor to external time in there. This is the most reliable one.

**A simple, non-electronic distance measure.** A knotted cord of known length works well. When you pace the cavity and get three different numbers, you need a check that doesn't depend on your own stride. Lay the cord along the floor, mark your start point, see how many cord-lengths fit. This won't resolve the discrepancy—the space genuinely changes dimensions—but it will tell you whether the change is in the space or in your perception. Both are concerning, but they are differently concerning, and knowing which you're dealing with at any given moment is valuable.

**A journal and a pencil.** Not a pen—ink behaves oddly in high humidity, and the volatile organics in the air may interact with certain ink chemistries. Pencil on paper. Write down your observations as you make them, not after. Memory of the Garden is notoriously unstable—not in the sense that you forget, but in the sense that you remember *differently* each time you review the experience. Details shift. Was the mound on the floor to the left or the right? Was the spiral whorl on the left wall or... you're certain it was the left wall, except now you can picture it on the right with equal clarity. Written-in-the-moment notes are your hedge against narrative drift. Date and timestamp entries using your mechanical watch.

**A small, sealable glass container.** If you choose to collect a sample of the basin fluid—and there are reasons to do so and reasons not to, which I'll discuss—glass is the only material that seems to be genuinely inert to it. Plastic softens. Metal corrodes, slowly but visibly. Glass holds. Take a container with a ground-glass stopper, not a screw cap with a rubber seal. Bring a second one in case the first is compromised.

**Food and water for longer than you plan to stay.** You are going to be in the Garden for a planned duration—I'd recommend no more than four hours for a first visit—but the spatial unreliability means your exit may not be where you expect it, and retracing your path through the root matrix to return to whatever entry point you used may take longer than the entry trip. Bring water for twelve hours. Bring calorie-dense, low-odor food for two meals. The food should be sealed airtight until you eat it; you don't want the organism smelling your provisions and becoming interested.

**A small mirror.** Carry a mirror you can look into. There are reports—unconfirmed, subjective, impossible to evaluate from outside—that extended time in the Garden subtly alters perception of self. One traveler described looking at their hand after two hours and finding it "unfamiliar, as though it belonged to a different body or had always been made of something other than flesh." Having a mirror lets you check your own face periodically, which serves as both a perceptual anchor and a crude psychological assessment tool. If the face in the mirror starts to look wrong, it's time to leave.

**A source of flame.** A candle and matches. Not for illumination—the Garden provides more than enough light. The flame serves two purposes. First, it's a test of atmospheric composition; if the candle won't light or won't stay lit, the air has changed and you need to evaluate whether it's still safe to breathe. Second, flame is the one thing in the Garden that is definitively *not of this place*. The organism produces light but not fire. A small, controlled flame gives you something to focus on that is entirely external to the Garden's systems—a phenomenological anchor, a tiny embassy of the thermodynamic world you came from. The comfort this provides is disproportionate to its size.

### What NOT to Bring

**No electronics beyond what you're willing to lose.** They won't work right, and if they contain lithium batteries, there is a small but non-trivial risk that the pressure fluctuations during thunder-pulses could compromise battery integrity. A lithium fire in an enclosed space with unusual atmospheric chemistry is a scenario you do not want to create.

**No cutting tools.** Do not bring a knife, an axe, a saw, or anything designed to cut or damage plant tissue. The organism is aware of its own interior in ways that defy normal plant biology. Cutting into the root walls would be, in the most generous interpretation, like performing surgery on a conscious patient without anesthesia. In a less generous interpretation, it would be an attack on an entity of unknown intelligence and continent-scale biomass, conducted from *inside its body*. Do not do this.

**No recording equipment you're relying on.** Cameras, audio recorders, film—bring them if you like, but do not depend on them functioning or retaining coherent data. Film exposed in the Garden reportedly develops into images that don't match what the photographer remembers seeing. Audio recordings capture the growth-sounds and the thunder, but also pick up other things—long, slow frequencies below human hearing that, when sped up for analysis, sound structured. Patterned. Like language slowed down to geological pace. This is interesting but not useful for documentation purposes, and listening to these recordings at length after the trip has produced marked unease in everyone who's done it. If you record, label the files clearly and don't review them alone.

**No perfumes, colognes, scented products, insect repellent, or sunscreen.** You are inside a living chemosensory system. The organism communicates with itself partly through volatile chemical signals—the sap-smell, the green sweetness, the sharpening during pulses. Your chemical signature is already foreign enough. Don't add complexity.

## Navigation and Orientation

### Getting In

The approach to the Garden varies by multiverse branch and entry vector, but all known routes share a common feature: a transition zone where you move from conventional geology into the living matrix. This transition is gradual, not sudden. You'll notice it first as root intrusion into rock—cracks filled with living tissue, patches where the stone has been entirely replaced by dense wood that still holds the shape of what it consumed. The ratio shifts over distance until the stone is gone entirely and you're moving through a tunnel of living root.

**Mark your path.** Not by cutting or scratching the root surfaces—see above regarding cutting tools—but by tying short lengths of brightly colored cotton thread to protruding root-tips along the way. Cotton, because the organism seems to accept it passively. Bright colors (yellow or white are best, despite the earlier advice about wearing muted tones—the markers need to be visible) because the amber light of the root-lanterns makes earth tones disappear against the walls. Tie a marker every five meters minimum. Check them on your return; some travelers report that the root system slowly absorbs or overgrows markers left in place for more than several hours, which gives you another data point on how long you can safely stay.

**The entry tunnel will not remain static.** It will narrow or shift slightly during your visit. This is normal. This is the organism growing. It is not trapping you—or if it is, it doesn't know that it is, any more than a tree knows it's trapping the nail it grows around. But the practical effect is the same: your exit narrows with time. This is the primary reason to limit your visit duration. Check your exit route before committing to an extended stay, and consider posting a timed reminder—set your mechanical watch to chime at the halfway point of your planned visit—to begin your departure.

### Inside the Cavity

**Stay near the walls for your first hour.** Acclimate. Let the sound layer into your consciousness gradually rather than walking straight into the center. The center of the cavity is where the emptiness is most concentrated—the "nothing" that the organism is thinking about. It exerts no physical force, but travelers report a cognitive pull, a sense that the center of the room is *important* in a way that demands attention and investigation. Resist this initially. Observe from the periphery first.

**Walk the perimeter systematically.** Clockwise or counterclockwise, your choice, but be consistent. Touch the walls through your gloves. Feel for temperature variations, pulsation differences, places where the texture changes. You are building a mental map that will be inaccurate—the space shifts—but the *process* of building it keeps your analytical mind engaged, which is your primary defense against the Garden's more dissolving effects on cognition.

**When you approach the center**, do so deliberately and with a specific intention. "I am going to stand in the center for five minutes and observe" is better than drifting there because the pull became too much. Mark five minutes on your watch. When they're up, return to the periphery. The purpose of this discipline is not that the center is physically dangerous—there's no evidence of that—but that it is *psychologically absorptive*, and people who spend extended time there report difficulty afterward in distinguishing their own thoughts from... other inputs. What those other inputs are is the central open question about the Garden, and you are not going to answer it on your first visit. Do not try.

## The Specific Hazards and How to Manage Them

### The Spiral Whorl

Do not stare at it for more than a few seconds on your first visit. I mean this literally. Count to five and look away. The depth-inversion effect is instantaneous—the wall becomes a tunnel, the tunnel contains blue light, the blue light is *somewhere else*—and breaking the effect requires looking away, but the longer you've stared, the harder it is to look away. This is not because the whorl is hypnotic in any crude sense. It's because what you're seeing is *real*—the tunnel is genuinely there, the blue light is genuinely somewhere—and real things are harder to dismiss than illusions. Your curiosity will be screaming at you. Honor it later, on subsequent visits, when you have more experience with the Garden's effects and better self-knowledge about your own cognitive vulnerabilities.

The blue light that lingers in your peripheral vision after looking away will fade over several minutes. If it doesn't fade—if it's still there after ten minutes—you've looked too long, and you should consider ending your visit early. Some travelers report the blue light persisting for days afterward, accompanied by a sense that ordinary rooms have hidden depths in their walls. This fades. But it's disconcerting and can interfere with sleep.

### The Thunder-Pulses

These will startle you the first time despite knowing they're coming. The whole chamber contracts, the light flares, the air sweetens to narcotic intensity. There's nothing you can do to prevent the physical effects—the ear-popping, the pressure shift—beyond the decongestant mentioned earlier. What you *can* do is manage your response.

When a pulse hits, plant your feet, close your eyes, and breathe shallowly through your mouth until the sweet smell fades back to baseline. Do not breathe deeply during the intensified phase. Whatever the volatile compounds are that constitute that sweetness, their concentration during a pulse is significantly higher, and deep breathing means significantly more exposure. Two or three shallow breaths through the worst of it, then normal breathing as it recedes.

The pulses are irregular but seem to average one every three to seven minutes. If they begin occurring more frequently—more than one per minute—the deep root mass is in a state of unusual activity, and you should leave. Not because the cavity is going to collapse (it has been stable through apparently much worse), but because the cumulative chemical exposure from rapid-fire pulses is uncharacterized, and the cognitive effects compound. Multiple travelers describe a state after heavy pulse exposure that they call "thinking like the Garden"—a dissolution of linear cognition into something more spatial, more networked, less verbal. This is reversible, but not instantly, and you don't want to be navigating your exit route while your brain is trying to process information the way a root system does.

### The Unseen Creature

You will not interact with it. It will interact with you, in the sense that it will observe you—the dimming of roots near it suggests it absorbs or redirects light, meaning it is *looking*—but you have no way to meaningfully communicate with or affect something that moves through solid root matter and may not be a discrete organism at all.

Do not chase it. Do not try to corner it. When you hear the crystal tone, note its location and continue what you were doing. The creature—the node, the thought-fragment, whatever it is—is part of the Garden's immune or cognitive system, and you are a foreign body in the space it monitors. Your best strategy is to be as boring as possible. Move slowly, speak softly if you speak at all, do not make sudden gestures. Let it observe you and conclude that you are inert. The consequences of being found *not* inert by the Garden's internal surveillance are unknown, and you want them to remain unknown.

### The Basin Fluid

If you choose to touch it through your gloves, note the resistance. It's slight—like pressing into gelatin that hasn't quite set. The fluid is making a decision about you. Give it time. Don't force your finger in; let the fluid accept the intrusion at its own pace. If it doesn't—if the resistance increases rather than fading—withdraw. The fluid has decided you're not welcome, and you should respect that.

If you choose to touch it with bare skin: understand that you are introducing your biology to the Garden's biology in the most intimate way available short of drinking the fluid (which: **do not drink the fluid**, under any circumstances, regardless of how thirsty you are, regardless of how inviting it looks, regardless of any impulse that arises suggesting you should). Bare-skin contact with the basin fluid reportedly produces a tingling warmth that spreads from the point of contact up the arm over several seconds and then fades. Some travelers report fleeting perceptual changes—briefly hearing the growth-sounds as structured, almost meaningful, almost comprehensible, as though the barrier between your nervous system and the Garden's vascular network became momentarily thinner. This passes quickly and seems to leave no lasting effects, but the sample size is small and the follow-up periods are short. You're experimenting on yourself. Know that.

If you collect a sample, seal it immediately and store it in a dark, cool part of your pack. The fluid's faint luminosity persists outside the Garden for several days before fading. During that time it remains biologically anomalous—sterile by every test yet demonstrably alive by metrics that don't correspond to any standard assay. What you do with the sample after your trip is your business, but be aware that in at least one case, a sample left unsealed in a wooden-floored room was found to have spread into the wood grain overnight, and the affected boards had to be removed because they had begun to grow.

## Psychological and Philosophical Preparation

This is the most important section. Read it more than once.

### Before You Go

Spend a week before your visit practicing **deliberate attention management.** This means: meditation if that works for you, but it doesn't have to be meditation. It can be anything that builds your capacity to *choose what you attend to* rather than being captured by whatever is most salient. Stare at a candle and practice looking away. Listen to complex music and practice focusing on a single instrument. Walk through a crowded market and practice keeping your attention on your own footsteps. The Garden is the most attentionally demanding environment you will ever enter. Everything in it is interesting. The light, the sound, the textures, the movement, the impossible geometry, the spiral whorl, the creature, the fluid, the emptiness at the center—all of it competes for your attention simultaneously, and all of it rewards attention with deeper and more absorbing phenomena. Without the ability to deliberately redirect your focus, you will be captured by whichever stimulus is strongest, and you will remain captured until something else breaks the spell, which in the Garden might be nothing.

**Clarify your intention.** Why are you going? Curiosity is a fine answer. Wanting to see something extraordinary is a fine answer. Scientific inquiry, artistic inspiration, spiritual seeking—all fine. But know your answer. Name it to yourself before you enter. Write it in your journal. The Garden does not ask your intention, but *you* need to know it, because when you are standing in the amber light listening to the roots sing and feeling the thunder roll through the floor and the green sweetness fills your lungs, you are going to feel an impulse to *stay*. To just... stay. To become part of what is happening. To let the boundary between you and the place dissolve. Having a clear intention—"I came to observe, and I am going to leave"—is the rope that leads you back out, not just physically but psychologically.

**Talk to someone who has been.** If you can find a previous visitor—and they are rare, and some of them don't enjoy talking about it—sit with them and let them tell you what they tell you. Do not push for details they don't offer. Many describe the experience as "the most important thing that has happened to me" and simultaneously refuse to recommend it. Take that contradiction seriously. They are not being coy. The Garden gives you something that is genuine and costs something that is also genuine, and the exchange rate is not the same for everyone.

### During Your Visit

**Speak aloud.** Periodically, say something—your name, the time, your intention. "My name is [X]. It is [time]. I came to observe and I am going to leave." This sounds foolish. It is not foolish. The Garden's soundscape is immersive and will gradually replace your internal monologue if you let it. The growth-creaks, the sap-hiss, the thunder, the crystal tone—these layer into a kind of music that is deeply, viscerally compelling, and as your brain starts to entrain to it, your own verbal thought begins to feel thin and irrelevant by comparison. Speaking aloud reasserts the primacy of your own cognitive framework. Do it even when it feels unnecessary. *Especially* when it feels unnecessary, because that feeling—that your own voice is a small and unimportant thing in this place—is the first sign that the Garden's cognitive gravity is pulling you in.

**Do not try to understand the emptiness at the center.** Observe it. Note it. Write about it. But do not attempt to *comprehend* it while you are inside the space. The description—"you are standing inside a thought that a plant is having about the concept of absence"—is not a metaphor. It is the most literal description anyone has managed. The thought is ongoing. It is enormous. It is not structured for or accessible to human cognition, and attempting to merge your understanding with it is like trying to read a book by climbing inside the ink. You won't gain comprehension; you'll gain a kind of vertigo that is intellectual rather than physical, a sense that meaning itself has become unreliable. This can persist for weeks after the visit and is the most commonly reported negative aftereffect. Don't court it.

### After You Leave

**Reentry is disorienting.** The transition from the Garden's warm, sweet, amber-lit aliveness to the cold, dead, mineral world of ordinary geology is jarring in a way that surprises people. Several travelers describe emerging from the root matrix and finding normal stone to be *offensive*—ugly, inert, pointless. One described touching a granite wall and crying. This passes, but give yourself time and space for it. Don't schedule anything demanding for the 24 hours after your visit.

**Write your account within six hours.** After that, the narrative drift begins. Your memory of the Garden will remain vivid but will become *fluid*—details will shift, sequences will rearrange, things you didn't notice at the time will appear in memory as though they were central. Your in-situ journal notes become your fixed reference points. Write your full account while they still match your memory.

**Expect dreams.** For one to three weeks after a visit, most travelers report vivid dreams involving root systems, enclosed spaces, amber light, and a persistent sense of being *inside* something alive. These are not nightmares, typically—most describe them as peaceful, even beautiful—but they are intense and can disrupt sleep. If you already have a dream practice or a therapist, brief them beforehand.

**Watch for the pull.** Almost everyone who visits the Garden once wants to return. This is not inherently concerning—the place is extraordinary and the desire to revisit extraordinary things is normal. But monitor the quality of the wanting. If it feels like curiosity, like the desire to see a beloved landscape again, that's fine. If it feels like *need*—like thirst, like the Garden is something your body requires—that is a warning sign. A few travelers have described an increasing difficulty in finding ordinary environments meaningful after repeated visits, as though the Garden has recalibrated their baseline for what counts as real. This is the cost I mentioned earlier. It is not universal, but it is not negligible. Space your visits widely. Return to ordinary living fully between them. Do not let the Garden become your standard for what the world should be.

## Final Notes

You are going to be a guest inside something alive, something thinking, something so large that the word "large" loses its meaning. The roots have replaced the earth. Whatever surface exists above them is an afterthought, a skin on something that goes down and down and down into depths that produce thunder when they shift. And inside this incomprehensible organism, in a cavity the size of a chapel, the thing that has eaten the world is contemplating emptiness—holding a pocket of nothing inside itself and growing around it and singing to it and lighting it with impossible sunlight and not understanding it and not stopping trying to understand it.

You are going to stand in that pocket and be part of the contemplation whether you want to be or not. Your presence is data. Your absence, when you leave, will also be data. The Garden will think about you the way it thinks about the void—slowly, thoroughly, with senses you cannot imagine and patience that makes geological time look hasty.

Be respectful. Be deliberate. Be clear about who you are and why you came. Touch the walls gently. Listen to the singing. Let the thunder move through you. Look at the whorl on the wall for five seconds and no more. Speak your own name into the amber air and mean it.

And when the green sweetness rises and the light flares and you feel the pull toward the center, toward the nothing, toward the thought that hasn't finished being thought—

Walk toward the exit instead. Slowly. Counting your cotton markers. Feeling the root-weave beneath your feet pulse its forty-second heartbeat.

You'll come back. Everyone comes back.

Just make sure the first time, you prove to yourself that you can leave.
