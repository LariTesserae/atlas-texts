# Preparing for the Screaming Plateau

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before any gear list, before any practical consideration, you need to sit with what this place actually is. This is not a hostile environment in the way a volcano or a deep ocean trench is hostile — it will not kill you quickly or dramatically. It is hostile the way a river is hostile to a stone in its bed: slowly, constantly, without malice or pause. The danger here is not acute catastrophe. The danger is erosion — of your gear, your body, your hearing, your psychological stability, your sense of where the boundary lies between you and the world around you. The wind is not weather. The wind is the medium. You will be submerged in it the way a diver is submerged in water, and you need to prepare with that level of seriousness.

Read the description again. Read it carefully. Notice what it tells you about the former inhabitants: they did not conquer this place. They threaded it. They occupied gaps. They moved with it. Your preparation should be oriented around the same philosophy. You are not bringing equipment to defeat the plateau. You are bringing equipment to exist in its margins.

## Hearing Protection: The Single Most Critical System

This is not an exaggeration. This is the thing that will determine whether your visit is a profound experience or a medical emergency.

The description tells you the sound is "so constant and so total that it has become architecture." It tells you the wind produces frequencies low enough to vibrate stone, that horizontal vortices shift the pitch into the near-ultrasonic range, that you feel it in your teeth and inner ear fluid. This is not just loud. This is a complex, broadband acoustic assault that spans from infrasound through the audible spectrum into ultrasound, and it never stops.

**Layered hearing protection is non-negotiable.** You need:

**Layer 1: Deep-insertion custom-molded earplugs.** Have these made before you travel. Not foam plugs — custom silicone molds taken from impressions of your ear canals, with tuned acoustic filters that provide maximum attenuation (NRR 30+) while still allowing some perception of environmental sound. You need to hear enough to maintain situational awareness — the sudden silences the description mentions, the shift in wind direction, the sounds of the crevice creatures at night — but you need the raw decibel level brought down from tissue-damaging to merely overwhelming. Bring at least three pairs. The wind-driven grit will degrade them.

**Layer 2: Over-ear protection with active noise management.** Not consumer noise-canceling headphones — those work by generating anti-phase sound waves, and in an environment this acoustically complex and variable, they will fail unpredictably and may actually create dangerous resonance artifacts. Instead, bring passive over-ear muffs with high NRR (25+) that you can wear over the earplugs when conditions intensify. The combined NRR won't simply add together, but you should achieve effective attenuation of 40-45 dB with both layers, which may bring the plateau's roar from potentially damaging to merely very loud.

**Layer 3: Infrasound and ultrasound mitigation.** This is the part most travelers won't think about, and it's the part that may matter most for your long-term wellbeing. Standard hearing protection attenuates audible frequencies. The plateau also produces infrasound (below 20 Hz) and near-ultrasound (above 16 kHz), both of which bypass conventional ear protection and affect you through bone conduction, organ resonance, and direct pressure effects on the vestibular system. Infrasound at sufficient intensity causes nausea, disorientation, anxiety, a pervasive sense of dread, visual disturbances, and difficulty thinking clearly. The description's mention of feeling sounds "in your teeth, in the fluid of your inner ear" is almost certainly describing infrasonic effects.

There is no perfect protection against infrasound short of a sealed pressurized enclosure, which you won't have. What you can do:

- Bring a portable infrasound monitor (a low-frequency microphone with appropriate processing) so you at least know when levels are dangerous. Anything above 120 dB at frequencies below 20 Hz is cause for serious concern.
- Wear a padded cervical collar or high-necked garment that reduces transmission through the throat and chest to the inner ear.
- Plan to spend significant time in the deeper crevices where the narrow geometry may attenuate the longest wavelengths.
- Be prepared for psychological effects that feel irrational — sudden fear, paranoia, a sense of being watched, profound sadness. Recognize these as potential infrasonic artifacts, not accurate perceptions. Brief yourself on this beforehand so you can maintain rationality when it happens.

**Duration limits:** Even with all protection, do not plan to spend more than five to seven days on the plateau's upper surface. Cumulative acoustic exposure at these levels, particularly in the infrasonic range, produces effects that compound over time and may not be immediately apparent. Temporary threshold shift (reduced hearing sensitivity) is likely within the first 24 hours. If you notice tinnitus, muffled hearing, or a sense of "fullness" in your ears that persists after removing protection in a sheltered area, you need to descend immediately.

## Wind Protection: Clothing and Physical Shielding

The wind strips paint from metal. Think about what it will do to your skin.

**The grit problem.** The plateau surface is polished basalt, which sounds smooth, but the wind carries fine particulate matter — volcanic glass dust, fragments of organic material, mineral crystals freed from eroding rock. At the wind speeds described, even very fine particles become abrasive projectiles. Exposed skin will be sandblasted. Your eyes are particularly vulnerable. Any equipment with optical surfaces — lenses, screens, solar panels — will be degraded.

**Full-coverage clothing system:**

- **Base layer:** A moisture-wicking synthetic layer, full coverage from neck to wrists to ankles. The plateau's intermittent condensation means you will get damp regularly, and the wind will cool you by evaporation faster than you expect. Wool-synthetic blends are ideal — they maintain insulation when wet and resist the kind of mechanical abrasion that pure synthetics suffer from.

- **Mid layer:** Insulation appropriate for the temperature drop at night. The description suggests the temperature change is noticeable but not extreme — you're at 2,500 meters elevation, which in most Earth-like atmospheres means daytime temperatures perhaps 15-20°C cooler than the lowlands, with nighttime drops of another 10-15°C. A lightweight fleece or synthetic insulated layer should suffice. Choose something with a smooth exterior — textured fabrics catch the wind and create drag that will exhaust you.

- **Shell layer: This is critical.** You need a windproof outer layer that is also highly abrasion-resistant. Standard nylon rain shells will be shredded within hours. Look for fabrics designed for mountaineering in extreme conditions — heavy-denier ripstop nylon or polyester with reinforced panels, or better yet, a woven Dyneema or UHMWPE composite if available in your home universe's markets. The shell must seal completely: elastic or Velcro closures at wrists, ankles, and neck; a hood that cinches tight enough to leave only your eye protection exposed; no loose flaps, straps, or toggles that will catch the wind and be torn away. Double-zippered closures with storm flaps. Consider a shell that is one size larger than you'd normally wear to allow layering underneath without restriction.

- **Hand protection:** Full-coverage gloves with articulated fingers and reinforced palms. You will be gripping rock. The rock is polished but the edges of crevices are sharp. Wind makes fine motor tasks exhausting because your hands are constantly being pushed and buffeted. Consider fingerless gloves under full gloves so you can remove the outer layer for delicate tasks while maintaining some protection.

- **Foot protection:** Boots with aggressive sole tread for polished, potentially wet basalt — this is a surface with almost no friction when damp, and the description says it gets damp. Approach-shoe-style climbing footwear with sticky rubber soles would be ideal. Ankle support is important — the vortices can destabilize you, and a twisted ankle on a plateau with no rescue infrastructure is potentially fatal. Gaiters that seal over the boot tops to keep grit out of your footwear.

**Eye and face protection:** Sealed goggles, not sunglasses. Mountaineering goggles with anti-fog coatings and replaceable lenses. Bring multiple replacement lenses — the grit will scratch them. A balaclava or face mask under the goggles to protect the rest of your face. Consider a full-face shield for traversing the open plateau in the worst conditions — something like a clear polycarbonate visor that covers from forehead to chin and wraps around the sides. You will look ridiculous. You will also be able to see.

**Wind loading on your body:** You weigh, let's say, 70 kg. At the wind speeds implied by the description — sufficient to strip paint, to carry seeds at "terrifying speed," to flip a palm-sized beetle — you're looking at sustained winds of at least 100-150 km/h with gusts potentially much higher. A 150 km/h wind exerts roughly 140 kg of force per square meter on a flat surface. Your frontal cross-section is approximately 0.7 square meters. That's nearly 100 kg of force — more than your body weight. You can and will be knocked down. You can and will be pushed across polished rock. In the worst gusts, you could be killed by impact with a rock formation.

**Mitigation strategies:**

- Never cross open plateau unless you have identified your next wind-shadow and confirmed you can reach it.
- Use the crevice network. The former inhabitants did. The fissures are one to two meters wide and up to fifteen meters deep — even a few meters of depth dramatically reduces wind exposure.
- Carry a walking pole or, better, two — these function as outriggers for stability.
- Carry a length of dynamic climbing rope and a handful of basic rock anchors (cams, nuts, or pitons suitable for basalt cracks). In the worst conditions, you may need to anchor yourself to the rock and wait. The former inhabitants' shelters were all in wind-shadows for a reason.
- Move low. Crawl if necessary. Reduce your cross-section.
- During the localized vortices described — the ones that "spin up without warning" — drop flat and brace. Do not try to outrun them.

## Water Acquisition

There is water here, but it requires understanding and timing to collect.

The plateau has no standing water. No streams, no pools. All moisture exists as atmospheric condensation — it appears on rock surfaces during pressure drops, during the sunset resonance period, and intermittently throughout the day on leeward surfaces. The former inhabitants' shelter includes "a shallow basin cut into the floor that collects condensation drip from the overhang." This is your model.

**Condensation collection:**

- Bring lightweight, packable collection surfaces — thin sheets of material with hydrophilic coatings that encourage condensation to form and channel it into containers. Metal sheets work well if you can manage the weight; thin stainless steel or aluminum with micro-textured surfaces will collect condensation more efficiently than smooth surfaces.
- Position these in the wind-shadow of rock formations, angled to drain into containers.
- The description identifies the sunset period — approximately 40 minutes when the plateau becomes actively wet due to acoustic resonance and thermal effects — as the primary water event. Plan your collection strategy around this window. Have all collection surfaces deployed before the sun hits the horizon.
- During the sunset wet period, you can also collect water directly from rock surfaces using absorbent cloths that you then wring out. This sounds primitive. It works.
- The shallow basins carved by the former inhabitants will also collect during this period. If you camp in or near their structures, you can use these existing collection points.

**Water needs:** At this altitude, in this wind, with this level of physical exertion, you need at minimum 3-4 liters per day. The wind alone — its desiccating effect on your breathing and skin — accounts for an additional liter or two of water loss beyond what you'd expect at this temperature and elevation. You will be dehydrated before you feel thirsty.

**Carry-in water:** Bring enough water for your first 48 hours — six to eight liters minimum. This is heavy (6-8 kg) and you'll want to consume and lighten the load as you establish local collection. Water containers should be rigid or semi-rigid (the wind will deform and potentially rupture soft bladders) with secure closures (anything that can blow open will blow open).

**Water quality:** Condensation collected from rock surfaces will contain mineral dissolved from basalt (primarily silica, calcium, magnesium, iron) and potentially biological material from the crevice-garden ecosystems. The mineral content is likely harmless and may actually be beneficial. The biological content is unknowable — you are on another world, and the crevice organisms are uncharacterized. Filter and purify all collected water. A gravity filter with 0.1-micron pore size will handle particulates and most biological contaminants. UV purification as a secondary measure. Chemical treatment as a backup if your other systems fail, though be aware that if the local biology uses significantly different biochemistry, standard chemical treatments may be ineffective.

## Shelter Strategy

Do not bring a tent. Any freestanding structure will be destroyed.

Your shelter options are:

**1. The existing structures.** The former inhabitants' dry-stacked basalt shelters in the wind-shadows of eroded buttresses represent thousands of years of optimized design for exactly this environment. Use them. They are your best option by a significant margin. The description identifies one shelter in detail on the second terrace; there are others scattered across the plateau. Identify and map these before you commit to a camp location.

**2. The crevices themselves.** The stress fractures that lace the plateau — one to two meters wide, up to fifteen meters deep — provide natural wind protection that improves dramatically with depth. The crevice-gardens indicate biological habitability. The drawbacks: you'll be sharing space with whatever makes clicking sounds at night, the crevices may be difficult to enter and exit (especially if wind conditions change while you're inside), and drainage during the sunset wet period could create brief flooding in the deepest sections.

**3. Bivouac in wind-shadows.** If you cannot reach a structure or suitable crevice, you can survive by tucking into the leeward side of major rock formations, ideally ones with overhangs. Bring a bivouac sack — a single-person waterproof shell that you can climb into. Choose one in a dark color to absorb solar warmth during the day and one that is not so smooth that the wind can get under it and peel it off you. Weight it down with rocks. Accept that you will not sleep well.

**What to bring for shelter enhancement:**

- A compact sleeping system rated for approximately 0°C (to handle the nighttime temperature drop with a margin of safety). Down insulation is inadvisable — it will get damp from condensation and lose effectiveness. Synthetic insulation throughout.
- A sleeping pad with high R-value (4+). The basalt will conduct heat away from you efficiently, and you'll be sleeping directly on stone in most scenarios.
- Cord and webbing for rigging windbreaks. You won't create a tent, but you can rig a taut panel of heavy fabric across the opening of a crevice or supplement an existing wall to reduce wind intrusion.
- A groundsheet to keep you off the wet rock. The condensation is intermittent but frequent enough that anything in contact with the rock will get damp.

## Food and Cooking

**Do not plan to cook with an open flame on the open plateau.** The wind makes conventional cooking impossible and dangerous — a stove flame will be extinguished or wildly misdirected, and any fuel will be consumed at an extraordinary rate. Even inside the shelters, fire is problematic, though the description notes the former inhabitants' hearth had a cleverly designed flue system. If you can figure out how that flue works — and it was designed by people who "understood airflow intimately" — you might be able to maintain a small fire in one of their hearths. But don't count on it.

**Plan for cold food.** High-calorie, no-preparation-required nutrition:

- Nut butters, dense granola bars, dried meat and fish, hard cheeses (if they exist in your home universe's equivalents), dried fruit, chocolate, energy-dense trail mixes.
- Everything must be in secure, crush-resistant, wind-proof containers. Anything that can blow away will blow away. Anything that can be opened by a gust will be opened by a gust.
- Caloric needs will be significantly elevated — perhaps 4,000-5,000 calories per day. The wind resistance during movement is like walking uphill constantly. The cold exposure at night draws energy. The noise stress (yes, noise stress burns calories) takes its toll. Bring more food than you think you need. Then bring more.

**If you must cook:** Bring a sealed-canister stove system (like a JetBoil or equivalent) and use it only in the deepest wind-shadows or inside crevices. The fuel efficiency will still be poor, but you can heat water for warm drinks, which will be an extraordinary psychological comfort on your second or third day when the wind has worn your morale down to basalt. Hot drinks may become the thing that keeps you from leaving early. Budget fuel for two hot drinks per day.

**Regarding local food sources:** The crevice-garden plants are unknown. The animals are scarce and uncharacterized. Do not eat anything from this ecosystem without extensive testing. The speed-growing plants may use biochemistry that is actively hostile to your metabolism. The sealed, self-fertilizing flowers alone suggest reproductive chemistry that might be toxic. The beetle and the wingless bird-analog — you don't know what they are. Leave them alone.

## Navigation and Movement

The plateau is, by the description, enormous — a tableland that is called "the size of a country." You will not traverse it all. You need to be realistic about your movement range and plan accordingly.

**Visibility:** Generally good in daylight — the polished basalt reflects light, the sky is clear at this altitude, and you can see long distances. However, localized fog from condensation events can reduce visibility to near zero without warning, and the dust carried by vortices can create brief whiteout conditions. The description mentions clouds forming below the plateau, which means you may not be able to see the terraces below when planning a descent.

**Navigation tools:**

- Whatever mapping or positioning technology your home universe provides, bring it and bring backups. If you have access to satellite positioning, bring two receivers. If you have magnetic compass capability, bring one but be aware that the dense basalt may create local magnetic anomalies.
- Mark your path. Bring brightly colored surveyor's tape or equivalent and tie it to rock formations along your route. Make the ties extremely secure — loops through holes, wedged into cracks, weighted with stones. Anything simply tied to an exposed point will be stripped away by the wind.
- Map the crevice network. This is your road system. The former inhabitants used stone-lined paths in the fissures. The fissures connect. You can move significant distances through the crevice network with dramatically reduced wind exposure. But the network branches and intersects and it would be very easy to become disoriented in a system of channels that all look the same — dark basalt walls, green crevice-gardens, strips of sky above.
- Bring a handline — 30-50 meters of lightweight cord that you can use to probe crevice depth, lower yourself into channels, and create fixed handrail lines across exposed traverses.

**Movement pace:** On open plateau, into or across the wind, your speed will be perhaps 1-2 km/h at best, and you'll be exhausted. In the crevice network, sheltered from the worst wind, perhaps 3-4 km/h. Plan a maximum daily range of 8-12 km including all detours and rest stops. This means your realistic exploration radius from a base camp is 3-5 km in any direction.

**The terraces:** If you need to descend to lower terraces (for emergencies, exploration, or to reach the lowlands), the description says the steps are "hundreds of meters deep" and "sculpted into forms so smooth they look poured." This is a climbing problem. Smooth basalt, potentially wet, with hundreds of meters of exposure. Do not attempt to descend a terrace without proper climbing equipment — rope, harness, anchors rated for basalt, and the skill to use them. Going up is actually more dangerous than going down in these conditions because the wind will be pushing you backward off the face.

## Psychological Preparation

This section is not optional. It may be the most important part of your preparation.

**The sound will break you if you are not ready for it.**

Not in a dramatic way. Not all at once. It will break you the way the wind breaks the plateau itself — by never stopping, by being so constant that you forget it and then suddenly remember it, by occupying every space in your mind that silence normally occupies.

Here is what will happen:

In the first hours, the sound will be overwhelming. You will be consumed by it. It will dominate every thought. This is actually the easy phase, because novelty gives you energy and the intellectual fascination of the experience keeps you engaged.

In the first day, you will begin to adapt. The roar will recede into a background that you stop actively noticing. You will feel competent. This is dangerous, because you will also stop being as careful about hearing protection.

On the second day, the adaptation will begin to fail. Not because the sound gets louder, but because the cumulative neurological effects of constant high-decibel broadband noise start to manifest. You will become irritable for no clear reason. Your concentration will fracture. You will find it difficult to complete multi-step tasks. You may experience headaches that seem to originate behind your eyes. Your sleep — already compromised by the noise — will deteriorate further. You will begin to feel a pervasive, sourceless anxiety that is partly infrasonic effect and partly your nervous system's legitimate protest at the unending stimulation.

On the third day, if you are still on the plateau, you will reach a crisis point. This is where most travelers either leave or break through into a different relationship with the place. The sound, by this point, has become so deeply embedded in your sensory experience that your brain begins to process it differently — not as an external stimulus but as a feature of consciousness itself, like the hum of your own nervous system. This can be deeply disorienting. Some travelers report a sense of ego dissolution — the boundary between self and environment blurs, the constant vibration in your bones and teeth feels like *you* are vibrating, like *you* are one of the standing stones the wind plays through. This can be transcendent. It can also trigger panic attacks.

**Preparation strategies:**

- **Meditation or mindfulness practice.** If you have any experience with practices that teach you to observe sensory experience without identifying with it, use them. The plateau is, in a sense, an involuntary meditation retreat conducted in extremely challenging conditions. The ability to notice the sound without being consumed by it is exactly the skill that long-term meditators develop.

- **Intentional silence breaks.** Twice daily, find the deepest, most sheltered crevice you can access and layer every piece of hearing protection you have. Spend 20-30 minutes in maximum quiet. You will not achieve actual silence — the vibration comes through the rock itself — but the reduction in stimulus will allow your auditory system and your psyche to partially reset.

- **Social preparation.** If you are traveling alone, be aware that solitude amplifies every psychological effect described above. Conversely, if you travel with a companion, communication will be nearly impossible without technical assistance — even shouting is useless in a 150 km/h wind. Bring a pair of two-way radios with throat microphones and noise-isolating earpieces, or plan to communicate by written notes and hand signals. Pre-agree on a set of simple hand signals for essential messages: *I'm okay. I need help. Stop. Go. Come here. Danger. Let's go back.*

- **A decision framework.** Before you arrive, establish clear criteria for when you will leave. Not vague feelings — concrete, measurable criteria. Examples: *If I experience tinnitus lasting more than two hours, I descend. If I cannot sleep for two consecutive nights, I descend. If I become unable to follow my own navigation plan, I descend. If my hearing protection is compromised and I cannot repair or replace it, I descend immediately.* Write these down. Carry the written list. Refer to it daily. On the plateau, your judgment will be compromised by the very conditions you're trying to judge, so past-you needs to make the rules that present-you follows.

- **Something to anchor you.** A physical object, a photograph, a piece of music on a shielded playback device that you can listen to in your earpieces during silence breaks. Something from your life that is *not this place*. You will need reminding that other places exist. The plateau has a way of becoming the only real thing.

## Scientific Equipment (If You're Inclined)

The plateau is a staggering natural laboratory, and if you have any scientific inclination at all, you will want to document what you find.

**Recommended instruments:**

- **Anemometer and weather station:** Compact, ruggedized, capable of measuring wind speed, direction, temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure at high sampling rates. The pressure fluctuations alone — the barometric drops that trigger flower opening — would be worth studying. Anchor the station inside a crevice with the sensor head at a known height above the rim.

- **Acoustic recording equipment:** A high-quality microphone array with a frequency response from 1 Hz to 25 kHz (to capture both infrasound and the near-ultrasonic components) and a recorder with sufficient dynamic range to handle the enormous sound pressure levels without clipping. Windscreens will be destroyed immediately — mount the microphones inside perforated stone housings wedged into crevice walls. The recordings you bring back will be worth the entire trip. No one who has not been here will believe the sound. The recordings will be the proof.

- **Botanical sampling kit:** Small, sealable containers for plant specimens. A hand lens. A notebook (waterproof paper, pencil — ink pens will fail in the moisture and wind). The speed-growing vegetation and the drill-bit seeds deserve documentation. Collect carefully and minimally — you don't know the ecology well enough to know what damage collection causes.

- **Geological sampling:** A rock hammer and sample bags for basalt specimens. The "something older than basalt" description is tantalizing — if this rock predates the standard basalt classification, its mineral composition could be extraordinary. Collect from already-broken fragments rather than hammering fresh surfaces; the sound of your hammer will be lost in the wind anyway, but the ethics of defacing a surface that took millions of years to polish should give you pause.

- **Camera equipment:** Every camera you bring will suffer. Sand and grit in mechanisms, vibration damaging optics, moisture on lenses. Bring a weatherproof action camera as your primary — no moving parts, sealed housing, replaceable lens covers. Bring lens covers in quantity. Shoot video rather than stills; the plateau is a place of motion and the still images will fail to convey it. If you can manage it, time-lapse the sunset wet period from a fixed position in a wind-shadow. The sequence of stone going from dry to wet to dry in forty minutes, with the crevice-gardens visibly responding, will be among the most beautiful recordings you ever make.

## The Approach and Exit Strategy

The description implies you're arriving at the plateau's rim and working on its upper surface, but it doesn't describe how you got there. This matters enormously.

**The terraces are your highway.** The plateau rises from lowlands via a series of enormous stepped terraces, each hundreds of meters high. The wind accelerates as it climbs. This means your approach from the lowlands will involve progressively more extreme conditions — you can acclimate somewhat as you ascend, which is good, but you also need to recognize that each terrace crossed represents a deeper commitment and a harder retreat.

**Cache supplies on lower terraces.** As you ascend, leave sealed caches of water, food, and backup gear at each major terrace level. If you need to retreat — and you may need to retreat quickly — having supplies staged for the descent means you don't have to carry everything and you have resources even if you've lost gear.

**Know your descent route before you need it.** Scout and mark at least two routes from the upper plateau to the first lower terrace. Different wind conditions may make one or the other impassable.

**Weather patterns:** The description implies seasonal or at least cyclical variation in wind patterns. If any intelligence is available about these cycles from prior travelers or local observation, use it. Time your visit for the calmest period, if such a thing exists. Even a 20% reduction in average wind speed would dramatically improve your experience.

## What to Expect From Yourself

You will arrive full of awe and purpose. The plateau is genuinely one of the most extraordinary natural environments in the multiverse and you will feel, correctly, that you are witnessing something profound.

You will spend your first day struggling with the physical reality of the wind and the sound, but buoyed by the strangeness and beauty of everything you see — the polished black rock, the improbable green life in the crevices, the condensation ghosts.

You will spend your second day more settled, more competent, but also more tired. The wind fatigue is real. Every movement requires more effort than it should. Your body is fighting for stability constantly, even when you're standing still, and the metabolic cost is significant. You will eat more than you expected. You will drink more than you expected. You will sleep less than you need.

By the third day, the plateau will have become either your home or your enemy, and there may not be much space between those two states. You will have a moment — most travelers describe this consistently — when you are sitting in a wind-shadow, exhausted, deafened despite your protection, and the sunset wet period begins, and you watch the stone around you come alive with water, and the crevice-garden at your feet uncurls, and something clicks in the deep rock, and the light comes through an arch and paints a shadow that stretches for a kilometer, and the sound becomes music, actually becomes music, and you understand why the former inhabitants stayed.

And then it will pass, and the wind will howl, and you will be cold and tired and abraded and partially deaf, and you will want to leave.

Both reactions are correct. The plateau is both things. It is both of those places at once.
