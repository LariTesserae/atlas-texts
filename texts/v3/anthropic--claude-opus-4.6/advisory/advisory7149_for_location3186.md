# Preparing for the Canopy Plateau: A Comprehensive Guide for the Multiverse Traveler

---

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Let me be straightforward with you. This is not a hostile environment in the conventional sense. Nothing here is trying to kill you. But it is an environment that will slowly, patiently, and without malice dissolve the assumptions you carry about what a place is, what ground is, what direction means, and how long you've been somewhere. The danger of the Canopy Plateau is not violence. It is absorption. You must prepare not just your body and your gear but your *orientation*—your ability to remain a discrete being with intentions of your own inside a living system so vast and so ancient that it has become a kind of gravity.

Read the description again before you pack. Read it carefully. Notice what is not said as much as what is said. Notice that no one has measured the depth of the root-mass. Notice that no one has found the edge. Notice that the space inside is larger than the space outside. Notice that the easy path always leads *somewhere*, and that somewhere is chosen by the forest, not by you. These are not poetic gestures. These are the operational parameters of your destination.

## Mindset and Psychological Preparation

### The Problem of Duration

You need to decide, before you enter, how long you intend to stay. Write this number down. Write it on your arm if you have to. Tattoo it there if you're the cautious type. Because the Canopy Plateau does not offer you temporal landmarks. There is no sunrise. There is no sunset. There is no change in temperature, humidity, or light level that would allow you to distinguish Tuesday from Thursday, morning from afternoon, your third day from your thirtieth. The environment is a steady state. It will feel like a single endless moment that happens to contain walking, sleeping, eating, and walking again.

Humans orient themselves in time through change. When change ceases, time becomes a felt abstraction rather than a measured quantity. You will lose track. Everyone loses track. The description mentions six days; the traveler who wrote it may have been there for six days or sixty. They had no way to know, and neither will you.

**Bring at least two independent timekeeping devices.** Mechanical watches are preferable to electronic ones—not because electronics fail in the Plateau (there's no indication they do) but because a mechanical watch is something you can hear and feel, a tiny heartbeat of measured time strapped to your wrist that will anchor you when the forest's patient timelessness begins to erode your sense of duration. A digital display you have to look at is less useful than a ticking you carry in your body.

**Set alarms.** Every twelve hours, minimum. Something that will physically shake you out of the Plateau's rhythm and remind you: *you have been here this long, and you said you would leave after this long, and the difference between those two numbers is your remaining margin.*

### The Problem of Volition

The forest provides paths. The paths lead where the forest wants you to go. This is described as gentle, non-aggressive, more like a suggestion than a compulsion. Do not mistake gentleness for harmlessness. A river current is gentle too, right up until you realize you're two miles downstream from where you meant to be.

You need to practice, before you go, the art of *choosing difficulty*. The Plateau will always offer you an easy path. The easy path will always feel right. It will feel so right that choosing the hard path—climbing over roots, pushing through tangles—will feel not just physically arduous but *wrong*, the way walking against a crowd feels wrong, the way swimming upstream feels wrong. You will be tempted to rationalize: *the forest knows where I should go. The forest has been here longer than my species has existed. Who am I to resist?*

You are a traveler. You are passing through. That is who you are. And the forest's intentions, if it has intentions, are the forest's. They are not yours. Maintain the distinction.

**Practical exercise:** In the weeks before your visit, practice daily acts of deliberate inefficiency. Take the long way to work. Choose the harder trail. Sit in an uncomfortable chair when a comfortable one is available. You are training your mind to accept friction as the price of autonomy. On the Plateau, this training may be the difference between a traveler who explores and a traveler who is *conducted*.

### The Problem of Scale

The Plateau is a living system the size of a continent. You are a primate the size of a primate. The differential between you and your environment is not like the differential between a hiker and a mountain, or a sailor and the ocean. It is more like the differential between a bacterium and the body it inhabits. The Plateau may or may not be sapient. It is certainly *something*—aware, attentive, possessed of moods. And it will regard you with the same mild, incurious attention that you might give to a mote of dust drifting through a sunbeam.

This is psychologically difficult in a way that is hard to prepare for. Most environments that dwarf us are inert—deserts, oceans, ice fields. They don't *notice* us. The Plateau notices you. It just doesn't care very much. For some travelers, this produces a profound and destabilizing sense of insignificance. For others, it produces a seductive sense of comfort—the feeling of being held inside something alive, something warm, something that is aware of your existence and finds it acceptable. Both reactions are dangerous. The first can lead to despair and paralysis. The second can lead to a desire to stay.

**Bring something that matters to you.** Not something useful—something meaningful. A photograph, a letter, an object that carries emotional weight from your life outside. When the Plateau's vast indifference begins to erode your sense of self, or when its vast acceptance begins to feel like home, hold this object. Remember that you are a specific person with a specific history who exists in specific relationships with other specific people. You are not a component of the forest. You are a visitor.

## Physical Preparation

### Fitness

The walking is not strenuous in the traditional sense. There are no steep climbs, no scrambles, no exposed ridgelines. The root-mass surface undulates gently, and the paths (whether self-chosen or forest-provided) tend to be navigable without significant exertion. However:

- The humidity is constant and total. You will be wet at all times. Your body will struggle to thermoregulate through sweating because the air is already saturated. Heat management will be your primary physical challenge, even though the temperature is merely warm rather than hot.

- The footing is deceptive. The root-mass surface feels like earth but gives slightly. It is uneven on a micro-scale—ridges, gaps, soft spots where a compressed root has finally given way beneath a layer of accumulated detritus. Ankle injuries are likely if you're not careful. Knee strain from the constant slight instability of the surface is almost guaranteed over multi-day traversals.

- You will be walking for many days. There is no way to shortcut this. The Plateau is enormous and there is no transit system. Whatever you want to see, you walk to.

**Train for endurance over rough terrain in hot, humid conditions.** If you have access to tropical or subtropical environments, hike in them. If not, train on uneven surfaces (trail running on root-heavy forest paths is ideal) and acclimatize to heat and humidity as much as possible. Focus on ankle stability exercises—single-leg balance work, proprioceptive training on unstable surfaces.

### Pre-existing Conditions

If you have any respiratory conditions—asthma, COPD, chronic bronchitis—think carefully about whether this trip is advisable. The air is saturated with moisture, spores, pollen, volatile organic compounds from the trees, and particulate matter from the constant slow decomposition of organic material. It is not toxic, but it is *thick*. Healthy lungs will adjust. Compromised lungs may not.

If you have any fungal sensitivities, reconsider the trip entirely. The Plateau is, among other things, the largest fungal habitat you will ever encounter. Spores are omnipresent. Fungal bodies are embedded in every surface. The bioluminescence that lights the understory is fungal in origin. You will be breathing fungal spores with every breath for the duration of your stay. For most people this is harmless. For the fungally sensitive, it could become a serious medical issue in an environment where evacuation may take days.

If you have any psychological conditions that are exacerbated by sensory monotony—certain forms of anxiety, dissociative disorders, conditions where the loss of temporal landmarks could trigger episodes—prepare accordingly with your medical provider. The Plateau's unchanging environment is not just an inconvenience; it is a sustained assault on the brain's orientation systems. Discuss prophylactic medication if appropriate.

## Gear

### The Philosophy of Gear Selection

Pack light. Pack lighter than that. Then remove one more thing.

Every gram you carry, you carry on your back over uneven root-mass footing in near-total humidity for an unknown number of days. The Plateau provides water (more than you want) and, as we'll discuss, food (if you know what to look for). What it does not provide is shelter from itself, navigational aid, or a way out. Your gear should focus on these three deficiencies.

### Footwear

This is your single most important equipment choice. Get it wrong and the trip ends in injury.

You need a boot or shoe that offers:
- **Ankle support** without excessive rigidity. The root-mass surface requires constant micro-adjustment; a boot that locks your ankle completely will transfer all the instability to your knee.
- **Grip on wet organic surfaces.** The root-floor is perpetually damp and often covered in a thin film of algae or decomposing leaf matter. Standard lug soles designed for rock and dirt will slip. Seek out soles designed for wet wading or riverbed walking—softer rubber compounds with high surface-area tread patterns.
- **Drainage.** Your feet will be wet. Accept this. Do not try to waterproof your boots. Instead, choose boots that drain freely and dry relatively quickly (they will never fully dry, but some materials recover faster than others). Neoprene-lined wading boots are one option. Quick-dry hiking boots with mesh panels are another.
- **A sole thick enough to protect against puncture.** The root-mass surface occasionally presents sharp protrusions—broken root-ends, minerite calcified spurs, the edges of those incorporated stone structures. A thin sole risks puncture wounds, which in this environment will immediately become infection risks.

**Bring two pairs.** Alternate them daily. While one pair is on your feet, the other rides on your pack with maximum air exposure. This won't dry them, but it will slow the microbial colonization that turns wet boots into biohazard containers.

**Bring at least five pairs of socks.** Merino wool or synthetic wicking blends. Change socks at minimum twice a day, more often if you can. Foot rot is your most predictable medical threat. Carry foot powder and apply it liberally at every sock change. If you can source a medical-grade antifungal foot powder, so much the better.

### Clothing

Cotton will kill you. Not through cold—the Plateau is warm—but through chafing, fungal colonization, and the general misery of wearing a garment that has become a wet rag and will never be anything else again.

Wear synthetic quick-dry fabrics throughout. Long sleeves and long pants, despite the warmth. Your skin needs a barrier between it and the omnipresent moisture, spores, and unknown biological agents that coat every surface. A thin, breathable synthetic layer will serve this purpose without overheating you.

**Specific recommendations:**
- Lightweight synthetic long-sleeve shirts × 2
- Lightweight synthetic pants with articulated knees × 2
- Synthetic underwear × 5 (chafing in this humidity is inevitable; minimize it with good base layers and anti-chafe balm)
- A broad-brimmed hat. Not for sun—there is no sun—but for the constant dripping. Filtered rain cascading through a hundred meters of canopy is constantly falling on you. Having it fall on a hat brim instead of directly into your eyes and down your neck is a meaningful quality-of-life improvement.
- A lightweight, packable rain jacket. Not for waterproofing (you're already wet) but for those periods when the rain intensifies and the dripping becomes a genuine downpour. Even a jacket that wets through on the outside provides some insulation and prevents the mechanical erosion of your skin by constant water impact.
- **No gloves.** You need your hands for climbing, gripping, and feeling the texture of the surfaces you traverse. Bare hands are essential for reading the environment. However, carry a small tube of barrier cream and apply it to your hands twice daily. The moisture will macerate your skin; the barrier cream slows this.

### Shelter

You cannot pitch a tent on the root-mass. Stakes will not hold in compressed root material, and the surface is too uneven and too organic for most tent footprints. Even if you could pitch a tent, the humidity inside it would be identical to the humidity outside it within minutes.

**A hammock is your shelter.** The understory provides an infinite supply of anchor points—trunks, aerial roots, hardened lianas. A quality expedition hammock with an integrated bug net and a rain tarp is your sleeping system. Choose one designed for tropical use with maximum ventilation.

The bug net is not optional. Although the description doesn't mention biting insects specifically, the warm, wet, organic-rich environment is entomologically dense. The "insects, worms, soft-bodied larvae" mentioned as prey items for the amphibians came from somewhere, and wherever there are larvae, there are adults, and some of those adults bite.

Suspend your hammock at least a meter above the root-mass surface. This keeps you away from the surface-dwelling fauna (the pale amphibians, the boring beetles, whatever else moves along the root-floor at night) and allows air circulation beneath you, which is the closest you'll come to drying out.

**A sleeping pad or liner for the hammock** is recommended not for warmth but for comfort and moisture management. A closed-cell foam pad or a synthetic insulated liner creates a vapor barrier between your body and the hammock fabric, reducing the clammy contact that makes sleeping in total humidity so miserable.

### Navigation

This is where preparation becomes genuinely difficult, because the Plateau may not permit conventional navigation.

**The space inside is larger than the space outside.** Distances do not behave. Landmarks recur without circular travel. This is not a psychoactive effect or a disorientation illusion—it is a geometric property of the environment. Standard navigation assumes Euclidean space, and the Plateau does not provide Euclidean space.

That said, you should still carry navigational tools, because *some* navigation is better than *no* navigation, and the non-Euclidean properties seem to be subtle rather than dramatic:

- **A compass.** It may or may not work depending on whether the root-mass has its own magnetic properties (all that minerite calcification could plausibly affect local magnetic fields). Carry one anyway. If it works, it gives you directional consistency even if distances are unreliable. If it doesn't work, that's important information too.

- **A GPS unit or satellite communicator.** This depends on whether the Plateau exists in a universe where GPS satellites orbit overhead and whether signals can penetrate the canopy. Given that the canopy is opaque to visible light, it may well be opaque to radio frequencies. Test your GPS immediately upon entry. If it functions, it becomes your primary navigation tool, but **do not trust the distances it reports without independent verification.** If the space is non-Euclidean, a GPS may report internally consistent coordinates that don't correspond to actual traversal distances.

- **A pace counter (ranger beads) and a notebook.** This is your most reliable navigation system. Count your steps. Record direction changes. Note landmarks obsessively—distinctive tree formations, ruins, sinkholes, anything that stands out. Create your own map as you go. When the spatial anomalies manifest (a landmark appearing that you passed hours ago), document the discrepancy precisely: *Passed the ring of standing stones at step count 14,200 heading NNW. Encountered the same ring of standing stones at step count 22,700 heading NNW without turning. Apparent spatial fold.* This data may not help you navigate in the moment, but it establishes the pattern of the anomalies and may reveal a logic to them.

- **Flagging tape or biodegradable trail markers.** Mark your path. If the Plateau's spatial properties allow it, this gives you a breadcrumb trail to follow back out. If the spatial properties don't allow it—if your marked trail loops or discontinues—that tells you something about the nature of the folding and how it interacts with fixed markers.

**A critical note on navigation strategy:** The description states that the easy paths lead where the forest wants you to go. Your navigational effort should focus not on *following* the paths but on *mapping* them—understanding the forest's preferred routing and using that understanding to deduce the topology of the space. Where does the forest want you to go? Where does it *not* want you to go? The routes it discourages (the tangles, the difficult climbs) may be geometrically significant—edges of the spatial folds, boundaries between regions, or paths that lead toward the exterior.

If your goal is to *find the edge and leave*, you may need to systematically choose the difficult paths. The easy paths lead inward, toward sinkholes and ruins and ancient trees. The hard paths—the ones the forest doesn't offer—may lead outward.

### Water

You will not lack for water. The Plateau is saturated with it. The question is whether the water is safe to drink.

The rain-drip that filters through the canopy has been processed by a hundred meters of biological material. It is rich in tannins, organic compounds, dissolved minerals, and whatever chemical signals the trees are transmitting. It is probably not sterile. It may contain fungal spores, bacterial populations, and chemical compounds for which your body has no evolutionary preparation.

**Bring a high-quality water filter.** A pump filter with activated carbon is preferable to a UV purifier or chemical treatment, because your primary concern is not just pathogens but dissolved organic compounds. Activated carbon removes a broad spectrum of organic contaminants. Filter all water before drinking. Yes, even the rain-drip that looks clean. Especially the rain-drip that looks clean.

**Do not drink from the sinkholes.** The dark tannin water in the sinkholes is warm, stagnant, and home to organisms that are described in terms specifically chosen to be ominous. "Things move in them" is not a phrase that invites casual water collection.

The best water sources are the points where vascular leakage from exposed root-faces produces a steady seep. This water has been processed through the tree's own filtration systems—xylem transport is remarkably effective at filtering particulates—and while it will still contain dissolved organics, it is likely the cleanest water available. Collect from active seeps, filter through activated carbon, and drink.

**Bring electrolyte supplements.** You will be sweating constantly (even though the sweat can't evaporate), and the water you're drinking is mineral-rich but not necessarily rich in the specific minerals you're losing. Electrolyte tabs or powder added to your filtered water will prevent the gradual sodium/potassium depletion that leads to cramping, confusion, and cardiac arrhythmia.

### Food

This is a genuinely difficult problem. The Plateau is rich with biological material, but how much of it is safe for a human traveler from another universe to eat?

**The conservative approach: bring all your food.** Calorie-dense, lightweight, shelf-stable provisions for the duration of your planned stay plus a 50% emergency buffer. This is heavy and limits your trip duration but eliminates the risk of poisoning yourself with unfamiliar biochemistry.

**The moderate approach: bring base provisions and supplement with foraged food, cautiously.** The Plateau contains fruit, fungus, and soft inner bark that at least one species of local primate eats. This does not mean you can eat it. Alien biochemistry may contain compounds that are neutral to local fauna and toxic to you, or vice versa. However, the lemur-analogues are described as rough primate equivalents, which *slightly* increases the odds that their food sources are compatible with primate metabolism more broadly.

If you choose to forage, follow universal foraging protocol:
1. **Skin contact test.** Rub a small amount of the candidate food on the inside of your wrist. Wait 8 hours. If no reaction, proceed.
2. **Lip contact test.** Touch a small amount to your lip. Wait 8 hours. If no reaction, proceed.
3. **Tongue test.** Place a small amount on your tongue. Do not swallow. Wait 8 hours. If no reaction, proceed.
4. **Small ingestion test.** Swallow a very small amount. Wait 24 hours. If no digestive distress, the food is provisionally safe.

This protocol takes days to clear a single food item, so begin testing immediately upon entry if you plan to forage. Test multiple candidates simultaneously (on different skin patches) to parallelize the process.

**Foods most likely to be safe, in descending order of probability:**
- Fruit from the upper understory (most universal edibility across known biologies; sugars and organic acids tend to be broadly compatible)
- The soft inner bark eaten by the lemur-analogues (cellulose and starch; metabolically simple)
- Fungal bodies (highest risk; fungal biochemistry is wildly variable and the Plateau's fungi are ancient and deeply integrated into the ecosystem; some may contain psychoactive or neurotoxic compounds; proceed with extreme caution)

**Do not eat the fauna.** The pale amphibians, the insects, the things in the sinkholes—leave them alone. Animal proteins from alien biologies carry the highest risk of allergic reaction and toxicity, and the fauna of the Plateau are described as quiet and unobtrusive. Killing them for food in a place that may be semi-sapient and is certainly paying attention to you seems both ethically questionable and strategically unwise.

### Light

You will need supplemental light for close work—reading your notebook, treating injuries, examining the ruins—but not for general navigation. The understory's ambient twilight, augmented by the peripheral bioluminescence, provides enough light for walking and basic orientation once your eyes adjust.

**Bring a headlamp with a red-light mode.** Red light preserves your dark adaptation and is less likely to disturb the light-sensitive fauna. Use white light only when you must—for medical situations, detailed examination of objects, or emergencies.

**Bring extra batteries or a reliable rechargeable system.** A small solar panel is useless here (no sunlight reaches the understory). A hand-crank charger is the most reliable option for extended stays. Bring enough disposable batteries for your planned duration plus the 50% buffer.

A minor but important note: the bioluminescence is visible only in peripheral vision and vanishes when you look directly at it. This is consistent with rod-cell detection of low-level light (rods are concentrated in the peripheral retina; the fovea is cone-dominated). The bioluminescence is real and extremely faint. Using a bright artificial light will suppress your rod-cell sensitivity and render the bioluminescence invisible, which will make the understory feel darker and more claustrophobic even though you're technically adding light. Use artificial light sparingly. Let your eyes adapt. The Plateau's own light, strange as it is, is sufficient.

### Medical Kit

**Standard wilderness first aid kit, plus:**

- **Antifungal cream and powder** in generous quantities. You will develop fungal skin infections. This is not a possibility; it is a certainty. The environment is a fungal paradise, and your skin, perpetually moist and warm, is a perfect substrate. Treat early and aggressively. Apply antifungal powder to feet, groin, armpits, and any skin folds at every clothing change.

- **Broad-spectrum oral antibiotics.** Any wound, no matter how minor, will become infected in this environment. A scratch from a root-end or a blister from your boot is an open invitation to the microbial community that saturates every surface. Clean wounds immediately with antiseptic, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with waterproof bandages (which will not stay waterproof, so change them frequently). If a wound shows signs of infection despite topical treatment, begin oral antibiotics without waiting.

- **Antihistamines.** You are walking through an ecosystem that contains thousands of compounds your immune system has never encountered. Allergic reactions are likely. Carry both a fast-acting antihistamine (diphenhydramine or equivalent) and an epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis.

- **Anti-diarrheal medication.** Despite your best water-filtration efforts, your gut will encounter novel microorganisms. Gastrointestinal distress is likely at some point during an extended stay.

- **Moleskin, blister treatment, and foot care supplies** in quantities that seem excessive. Your feet are your transportation. A blister that would be a minor nuisance in a dry environment becomes a debilitating wound in this one.

- **A course of anti-parasitic medication.** Prophylactic or reactive, depending on your medical advisor's recommendation. The warm, wet, fauna-rich environment is a textbook parasite habitat.

- **Mental health medication** if prescribed. Bring more than you think you need. The Plateau's timelessness and spatial strangeness can exacerbate anxiety, depression, dissociative symptoms, and depersonalization. Do not go off your medications here of all places.

## Understanding the Non-Euclidean Properties

This requires its own section because it affects every aspect of your planning.

The description is careful: *"Not dramatically—not impossibly—but measurably, consistently."* This is not a funhouse. You will not walk through a door and find yourself in a different country. The spatial anomalies are subtle—a five-hour walk that should have covered ten kilometers covers twelve. A landmark that should be behind you appears ahead of you. The interior volume of the Plateau, integrated over your full traverse, exceeds what its exterior boundaries should allow.

There are several possible models for this:

**Model 1: Gradual curvature.** The Plateau's interior space has a slight positive curvature, like the surface of a sphere but in three dimensions. This would make straight-line travel subtly curved, would cause parallel paths to eventually converge, and would make the enclosed volume larger than a flat-space calculation would predict. If this is the case, there *is* an edge, but reaching it requires understanding the curvature and navigating accordingly.

**Model 2: Fractal folding.** The space is Euclidean but folded, with the fold points being the locations where landmarks recur. In this model, walking past a fold point seamlessly transitions you to a different region of space without any perceptible boundary. The total space is finite but larger than it appears from outside, like a crumpled piece of paper that fits inside a box smaller than its unfolded area.

**Model 3: Active spatial manipulation.** The Plateau—the living root-mass, the forest-organism—is generating the spatial anomalies in real time, adjusting the geometry of its interior in response to... something. Its own growth. Your presence. Both. In this model, the space is not fixed; it changes, and any map you make will be valid only for the time and circumstances of its making.

**Model 4: Perceptual distortion.** The space is normal. Your perception of it is being altered by chemical signals, fungal spores, or the low-frequency vibrations that the root-mass produces. You *think* the landmark recurred. You *think* the distances are wrong. In fact, you are mildly intoxicated by the environment and are navigating less accurately than you believe.

**My suspicion is that the truth involves elements of Models 1, 3, and 4.** The Plateau is old enough and large enough and *alive* enough to have developed genuine spatial properties that deviate from the norm, but you are also breathing its air and feeling its vibrations and subject to its chemical communications, which means your perception is not a reliable control. This is why mechanical measurement matters. Your pace count, your compass bearing, your flagging tape—these are objective anchors in an environment that will gently, patiently shift your subjective experience until you cannot distinguish what is real from what the forest is telling you is real.

**Plan for the possibility that you cannot navigate out.** This is not alarmism. This is contingency planning. If the spatial properties of the interior make systematic navigation to the edge impossible—if the easy paths lead inward and the hard paths fold back on themselves—you need a backup plan.

Options:
- A prearranged extraction. If you have a support team at your entry point, agree on a maximum duration. If you don't emerge by that time, they begin a search pattern along the plateau margin, making noise, using signals. You, inside, move toward any anomaly in the environment—any sound, light, or disturbance that doesn't fit the Plateau's baseline. This only works if the spatial anomalies don't prevent exterior signals from reaching the interior.
- A recall device appropriate to your multiverse transit method. If your transit technology allows you to return to your origin point from any location, the spatial anomalies may not matter—you can leave the Plateau without finding its edge. **Verify before entry that your transit method works from inside the Plateau.** Test it immediately upon crossing the boundary. Do not go further until you have confirmed you can leave.
- Acceptance. If you cannot navigate out and cannot transit out, you are a resident of the Plateau. This has happened before—the ruins suggest it. We will discuss this below.

## The Ruins: What They Mean For You

The ruins are not a tourist attraction. They are a warning, a comfort, and a mystery, in that order.

**The warning:** Someone was here before you. They were roughly human-sized. They built structures by hand, in stone. They are gone. The forest ate everything they left behind except the stone, and it is eating that too, slowly, by growing around and through it. Whatever civilization existed here was not sustained. It ended. The Plateau continued.

**The comfort:** Someone was here before you and *survived long enough to build.* They found food, water, shelter. They lived, probably for generations, in an environment that is alien to you but was apparently habitable for beings like you. If you become trapped, the Plateau can support human-scale life. It has done so before.

**The mystery:** The structures are not all shelters. The ring of standing stones with concave tops. The flat slab with its almost-repeating pattern of depressions. The lone wall running straight for thirty meters. These are not domestic architecture. They are... something else. Ritual spaces? Navigation aids? Attempts to interact with or influence the Plateau-organism? Instruments for measuring or manipulating the spatial anomalies?

**Do not disturb the ruins.** Examine them, document them, sit in them if you need shelter, but do not disassemble them, do not remove stones, do not alter their configurations. You do not know what they do. In a place where space does not behave normally and a continent-scale organism is paying attention to you, the last thing you want to do is disassemble a structure that might be performing a function you don't understand.

Pay special attention to the ring of standing stones with concave tops. The description says the tops are "worn concave as if by the repeated placement of something round." This sounds like it was used—regularly, over a long time. Whatever was placed on those stones was important enough to be a repeated practice. If you find spherical objects elsewhere in the Plateau—certain fruits, stones worn round by water, fungal bodies—note whether they fit the concavities. I'm not suggesting you recreate the practice. I'm suggesting you observe the correspondence and think about what it might mean.

The lone wall is particularly interesting. It runs perfectly straight for thirty meters. A perfectly straight line is a geometric assertion—a statement that *this* direction matters, that linearity is meaningful in this space. In a non-Euclidean environment, a straight wall is not just a wall. It may be a reference line, a way of testing whether space is behaving normally in a given area. Walk along it. Extend its line beyond both ends using your compass. See if the line remains consistent or if it curves in ways that reveal the local spatial geometry.

## Interacting with the Forest

### The Attention

You will feel it. The description is explicit: the Plateau becomes aware of you. This is not a hallucination. Whether it is literal sapient attention or a complex biochemical response to a novel organism in its body is, from your practical perspective, irrelevant. The experience is the same: a vast, slow, warm awareness settling onto you like a gaze.

**Do not panic.** The attention is not predatory. It is not hostile. It is curious in the way that something immeasurably old is curious about something transient—mildly, briefly, without urgency.

**Do not seek the attention.** Some travelers, upon feeling the forest's awareness, become fascinated by it and begin trying to deepen the contact—sitting still for hours, breathing in rhythm with the root-groans, entering meditative states to make themselves more receptive. This is how you stop being a traveler and start being a component. The forest's attention is like deep water: safe to swim in, dangerous to dive into, fatal to breathe.

**Do not flee the attention.** Running, panicking, or thrashing through the undergrowth in a desperate attempt to get away from the sensation will exhaust you, injure you, and accomplish nothing. The attention is not localized. You cannot outrun it because it is not coming from a point source. It is the ambient state of the entire environment becoming slightly more focused. It will pass. It always passes.

**The appropriate response is acknowledgment without engagement.** Feel the attention. Note it. Continue what you were doing. You are a visitor. You are passing through. The forest knows this. You know this. No further negotiation is necessary.

### The Paths

As discussed, the forest provides easy paths that lead where it wants you to go. Sometimes it wants you to see the ruins. Sometimes it wants you to visit a sinkhole or a particular ancient tree.

**Follow the paths sometimes.** Not always—maintaining your navigational autonomy is critical—but sometimes. The forest is showing you things. The things it shows you may be important. The ruins, the sinkholes, the ancient trees with their resonant internal chambers—these are features of the Plateau that you would not find on your own, because the understory is dense and disorienting and you would walk past them without knowing they were there.

Think of it as a guided tour offered by a host whose motives are opaque but whose knowledge of the territory is absolute. Accept the tour for a day. Then chart your own course for a day. Alternate. See where the forest leads you, and see where your own navigation leads you, and compare the two experiences.

**Keep detailed notes on where the forest-paths lead.** Over multiple days, a pattern may emerge. Is it leading you in a consistent direction? Toward a specific region? Away from a specific region? If it is consistently steering you away from something, that something is worth investigating—cautiously, on one of your self-navigated days.

### The Trees

The truly vast trees—the ones with trunks wider than plazas and internal chambers—are nodes in the Plateau's organism. They are, if not its brain, at least its concentrations. The description says the air inside their chambers is "different—warmer, resinous, still" and that it "hums with a frequency you feel in your teeth."

Entering these chambers is safe, as far as I can determine, but it is also an intimate act. You are entering the body of the organism that is the Plateau. The air inside is dense with its chemical communications. The vibration you feel is its internal processes operating at a scale you can perceive. Spending time in these chambers will intensify every aspect of the Plateau experience—the sense of attention, the emotional weather, the feeling of deep, slow, patient aliveness surrounding you.

**Limit your time in the tree-chambers.** An hour at most. Longer exposure to the concentrated chemical environment may have physiological or psychological effects that are difficult to predict. If you begin to feel drowsy, euphoric, or unusually reluctant to leave, leave immediately. These are signs that the chamber's atmosphere is affecting your cognition.

**Breathe through a cloth filter inside the chambers.** A bandana over your nose and mouth will not block the volatile compounds entirely but will reduce your intake and slow any dose-dependent effects.

### The Root-Groans

Once a day, more or less, the root-mass produces a deep, sustained groan as it adjusts under its own weight. The local fauna freezes when this happens. You should too.

Not because the groan is dangerous. It probably isn't—it's described as the sound of a structural adjustment, like a building settling. But the local fauna has evolved or adapted to treat it as a signal to stop moving. Respect their expertise. When the groan comes, stop walking, brace yourself against the nearest solid structure, and wait for it to pass. The root-mass surface may shift slightly during these events—not dramatically, but enough to open a gap you were standing on or close a passage you were walking through.

After the groan passes, reassess your immediate surroundings. Has anything changed? Has a new passage opened? Has your path been altered? Document any changes. These events may be the mechanism by which the forest redesigns its interior and reroutes its paths.

## The Fauna: Interaction Protocols

### The Pale Amphibians

Leave them alone. They are eyeless, live in the root-floor, and communicate with a slow exhalation that may be all they have. They are not dangerous, but they are also not interested in you, and handling them risks injuring creatures that are soft-bodied and adapted to a very specific micro-environment. If you encounter them on the surface, step around them. Their translucence is remarkable—take a moment to observe the shadows of their organs working, but do so from a respectful distance.

Do not eat them.

### The Lemur-Analogues

These are the closest thing to peers you will find on the Plateau. They are social, deliberate, intelligent enough to navigate the three-dimensional understory, and possessed of large, reflective eyes that will watch you with what feels like comprehension.

**Do not approach them.** Let them approach you, if they choose to. They may or may not be interested. If they approach, remain still and quiet. Do not make sudden movements. Do not attempt to feed them (you don't know what is safe for them, and offering food is a social act with implications you can't predict).

**Listen to their humming.** The low, resonant hum that vibrates in your chest—this is communication. You cannot understand it, but you can note when it occurs, how long it lasts, and what the group does afterward. If you are present for multiple humming events, you may begin to notice patterns. Does the humming precede movement? Does it follow the detection of something new (such as you)? Does it correlate with the root-groans?

The lemur-analogues' behavior may be your best source of information about the Plateau's state. They have been here far longer than you and have co-evolved with the forest-organism. If they become agitated, pay attention. If they move suddenly in a consistent direction, consider following at a distance. If they avoid a particular area, avoid it too.

### The Things in the Sinkholes

Do not go in the water.

The description is deliberately vague about what lives in the sinkholes, and that deliberate vagueness is itself informative. "Long, slow shapes that surface occasionally to breathe, if they breathe" is a description designed to communicate uncertainty and caution. These organisms are large enough to be visible as shapes in opaque water, they are patient, and they may have been here longer than the pools themselves.

Observe from the edge. Do not lean over the water. Do not dangle anything into the water—not a hand, not a stick, not a measuring line. If the forest-path leads you to a sinkhole, accept that the forest wanted you to see it, look at it, note what you see, and move on.

If you need to cross an area where sinkholes are concentrated, navigate around them with generous margins. The edges of sinkholes—the points where the root-mass collapsed to create the depression—may be structurally weak. Stay well back from the rim.

## Communication and Record-Keeping

**Keep a journal.** Write in it at every rest stop, at minimum. Record:
- Your step count since the last entry
- Your compass bearing
- A description of your surroundings, with particular attention to landmarks
- Any spatial anomalies (recurring landmarks, impossible distances)
- Any biological observations (fauna behavior, forest-path routing, root-groans)
- Your physical condition (hydration, foot condition, any injuries or symptoms)
- Your psychological condition (mood, sense of time, clarity of thought, any intrusive feelings of contentment or desire to stay)

The last item is not optional. The Plateau may influence your cognition subtly, through chemical signals, through the sub-audible vibration, through the sheer weight of its ancient, slow, patient presence. Monitoring your own psychology in writing gives you an external record that you can compare against your current felt experience. If your journal entries from day three say "I should leave soon" and your felt experience on day seven says "why would I ever leave, it's so peaceful here," the discrepancy is diagnostic. Trust the journal. Act on what it says, not on what you feel.

**If you are traveling with others:** Check in with each other frequently. Ask direct questions: "Do you want to leave? When do you want to leave? What day is it? How long have we been here?" Compare answers. If they diverge, someone's perception is being affected, and the affected person may not be able to tell from the inside. This is why solo travel on the Plateau is inadvisable, though I recognize that multiverse travelers often have no choice.

## Departure Planning

**Decide before entry how you will know it is time to leave.** A fixed number of days. A specific objective achieved. A specific symptom observed. Whatever your criterion, commit to it in writing, and honor it when it arrives, regardless of how you feel at that moment.

The Plateau is not a trap. It is not trying to keep you. But it is a place of profound comfort for certain temperaments—the quiet, the warmth, the steady dim light, the sense of being held inside something alive and vast and not unkind. People who are tired, who are grieving, who are lonely, who are running from something, may find the Plateau's ambient contentment almost unbearable in its sweetness. *Why leave? It is peaceful here. The forest provides. The path is easy.*

Because you are a traveler. Because you have a life elsewhere. Because the previous residents—the ones who built the ruins—stayed, and they are gone, and only their stones remain, and the forest grew through those too.

Leave while you still want to leave. If you reach the point where you don't want to leave, you have waited too long, and leaving will require an act of will that the Plateau's gentle, patient, ancient contentment will make almost impossibly difficult.

## A Final Word

The Canopy Plateau is one of the most beautiful places in the accessible multiverse. It is also one of the most subtle. It will not threaten you. It will not challenge you. It will simply be there—ancient, warm, dim, alive, patient—and it will let you walk through it, and it will show you things, and it will ask nothing of you, and that asking-nothing is the most dangerous thing about it.

Go. See it. Feel the root-mass give beneath your feet. Watch the bioluminescence pulse at the edge of your vision. Stand in the hollow of an ancient tree and feel the hum in your teeth. Listen to the lemur-things sing to each other in the green dark. Find the ruins and wonder who built them and what they were trying to do and whether they succeeded.

And then leave. Walk the hard paths, the ones the forest doesn't offer. Climb over roots. Push through tangles. Count your steps. Watch your compass. Trust your journal. Remember your name and where you came from and that there are people who expect you back.

The Plateau will not mind. It was here before you arrived, and it will be here after you leave, and the brief flicker of your passage through its body will settle into its memory the way a fallen leaf settles into the root-mass: gently, slowly, and then completely.
