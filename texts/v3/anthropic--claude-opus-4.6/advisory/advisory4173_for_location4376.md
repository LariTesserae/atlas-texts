# Preparing for the Cupola of Flies

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with the fact that this is not a destination in the conventional sense. No one built it for you. No one built it at all. It is an accident of infrastructure — a void that happened when systems too complex to fully model intersected and left a gap. Your visit is, by definition, unauthorized, not because someone forbade it, but because no one ever imagined anyone would be there. This changes the nature of preparation. You are not packing for a trip. You are packing for an intrusion into a space that functions perfectly without you and will be changed by your presence in ways you may not be able to predict or undo.

The multiverse traveler who treats the Cupola as a spectacle to consume will damage it. The one who treats it as a sacred space to revere will misunderstand it. It is neither. It is a working system — accidental, self-organizing, and in a state of constant renegotiation with its own infrastructure. Your preparation should be oriented toward a single goal: *be present without becoming a variable the system cannot absorb.*

## Physical Conditioning and Acclimatization

### The Climb

The Cupola sits at the top of a structure so vast its lower floors have their own weather. The description does not specify how you get there, and this is itself a preparation challenge. There is no elevator to an accidental room. There is no staircase designed to reach a void between systems. The approach will be through maintenance corridors, cable chases, ladder runs between equipment floors, and — in the final approach — through crevices in converging infrastructure barely wide enough for human shoulders.

Begin cardiovascular training at least six weeks before your visit. Not heroic training — you don't need to run marathons. You need sustained moderate output over long periods in awkward positions. Stair climbing with a loaded pack. Crawling drills. Time spent in confined spaces to calibrate your psychological tolerance. The final approach to the Cupola will require you to move through gaps between turbine housings, relay cabinets, and duct sheaths, likely on your belly or side for stretches, with hard metal on every side and no room to turn around if you change your mind.

The elevation is described as "absurd" — felt in the inner ear, producing shallow breathing despite pumped air. This suggests either genuine extreme altitude within a pressurized structure (where the pressurization is imperfect, designed for machinery rather than human comfort) or some quality of the structure's physics that produces analogous effects. Either way, spend time at altitude before your visit if you can. Acclimatize to breathing air that feels thin and slightly wrong. Practice slow, deliberate breathing patterns. Learn to recognize the early signs of hypoxia — tingling fingertips, mild euphoria, impaired judgment — because in a space where the air supply comes from a dozen competing systems none designed for human occupancy, oxygen levels may fluctuate in ways you cannot predict.

### The Grating

The floor is three overlapping gratings from different construction phases, none quite aligned. This is treacherous footing. The misalignment means there are gaps where your boot sole can catch, edges that don't line up where your weight can shift a panel, and layers that flex independently under load. Worse, the grating descends five or six meters into mechanical darkness below, which means a misstep is not just a stumble — it's a potential fall into infrastructure that has no interest in catching you.

Wear boots with thin, stiff soles — not hiking boots, which are too bulky, but something closer to approach shoes or technical climbing footwear. You want to feel the grating through your feet, to know when an edge is biting wrong, to sense the flex before it becomes a shift. Avoid anything with aggressive tread patterns that could catch in the mesh. Test your footwear on metal grating before you go — industrial catwalks, fire escapes, anything that lets you practice the particular awareness of walking on metal lattice over void.

Bring knee pads. Not the bulky construction kind — low-profile technical pads that you can wear under clothing. The observation position described in the text is a crouch at the edge of the space, and you may be there for hours. The grating will eat your knees without protection.

### Your Hands

You will touch things. You will need to brace against surfaces, grip edges, steady yourself on housings and cabinets. Every surface in the Cupola is metal — riveted, welded, bolted, caulked — with sharp edges, burrs, and bolt-heads protruding at unexpected angles. Bring thin, close-fitting gloves with good tactile sensitivity. Mechanics' gloves or thin leather work gloves. Not thick insulated gloves — you need to feel what you're touching, especially given the thermal variation (warm relay cabinets, cold inactive ones, the condensation-wet surfaces). Cut-resistant material is advisable. Carry a second pair in case the first gets wet.

## Atmospheric Preparation

### The Air

You are breathing the exhaust and intake of a dozen systems designed to service machinery, not lungs. One duct smells of ozone and lithium. Another carries something ferric. The air is pumped, processed, and delivered by systems that compete with each other, meaning local composition may shift moment to moment as the structure reconfigures below.

**Do not bring a respirator unless you are prepared to never take it off.** Here is the reasoning: a half-measure is worse than none. If you put on a respirator and then remove it because you feel fine, you'll be transitioning between filtered and unfiltered air repeatedly, which is more disorienting than simply acclimating. If you commit to wearing it the entire time, you lose the ability to smell the space, which is a significant perceptual channel — the ozone, the lithium tang, the ferric edge, the subtle organic note of the biofilm the flies cultivate. You also add the sound of your own breathing to a sonic environment where subtlety matters enormously.

My recommendation: bring a compact respirator with appropriate filters (combination organic vapor/particulate/acid gas — the lithium and ozone notes suggest possible lithium hydroxide or similar battery chemistry byproducts, and the ferric smell may indicate iron oxide particulates). Keep it accessible but do not wear it on entry. Monitor yourself. If you develop headache, metallic taste on the tongue, chest tightness, or unusual fatigue, put it on and do not remove it. If you feel fine after thirty minutes of breathing the space's air, you are probably fine for the duration of a visit — but keep monitoring.

Carry a small portable gas detector if you can obtain one rated for the compounds you expect. Ozone above 0.1 ppm is a concern for extended exposure. Carbon monoxide is always a concern in enclosed industrial spaces with active machinery. Oxygen displacement by nitrogen or other inert gases from pressurized systems is the invisible killer in spaces like this.

### The Particulate

The text describes "fine particulate of metal shavings and paint flakes and fly casings" settling on every surface. You will breathe this. The paint flakes are particularly concerning — industrial paint on infrastructure this old may contain lead, chromium compounds, or other heavy metals. The fly casings add biological particulate to the mix.

For a single visit of a few hours, the exposure is likely manageable. For repeated visits, it is not. If you plan to return — and the scuff marks suggest someone did, repeatedly — invest in proper respiratory protection for subsequent trips.

## The Flies: Understanding, Respect, and Practical Coexistence

### What They Are

They are not terrestrial flies. They are convergent on the fly form — small, six-limbed, winged — because the form works for what they do. Their ecology is entirely industrial: larvae eat paint, adults drink condensation and harvest iron-oxidizing bacterial biofilm. They are metallivores, or post-metallivores — organisms that have adapted to an infrastructure ecology the way terrestrial life adapted to hydrothermal vents or cave systems.

This means several things for you:

**They are not interested in you.** You are not food, not a thermal surface, not a source of condensation, not painted. You are an obstacle — a large, warm, mobile object that disrupts airflow patterns and occupies space. They will route around you the way they route around a new duct configuration. This is both reassuring and humbling.

**They may land on you.** Your body heat will register on their thermal sense. If you hold still near a warm relay cabinet, you may become thermally contiguous with it, and flies that would have landed on the cabinet may land on you instead, particularly on warm exposed skin or on dark clothing that absorbs the industrial floodlight. This is not an attack. It is a mapping error on their part, corrected quickly. Do not swat. Do not flinch if you can help it. Let them discover you are not infrastructure and leave.

**Their wings flash copper-green iridescence.** This will be beautiful and, in large numbers, disorienting. When the entire population lifts simultaneously — during a pressure reconfiguration — you will be inside a cloud of flashing light, thousands of signal-mirror wings catching the industrial floods. Prepare for this perceptually. It may trigger photosensitive responses in susceptible individuals. If you are prone to migraine with visual aura, or if you have photosensitive epilepsy, this space may not be safe for you.

**Their sound is subthreshold.** The papery, static-like whisper of ten thousand wing-pairs will sit just below your conscious hearing, and your brain will repeatedly reclassify it as silence. This is neurologically interesting and psychologically destabilizing over time. The sound is there. You will feel it more than hear it. It creates a sense of presence — of being surrounded by something alive and active — that never quite resolves into identifiable sound. After an hour, you may find yourself straining to hear something you cannot name. This is normal. Do not fight it. Let the perception stay liminal.

### Biosecurity — Yours and Theirs

**For you:** The flies' bacterial biofilm food source — iron-oxidizing bacteria — is not known to be pathogenic to humans in most variants, but you are in a multiverse. Variants exist that you have no immunity to. Do not touch the rust-lines. Do not touch the puddle. If a fly lands on you and you later touch the spot, wash your hands before touching your face. Carry antiseptic wipes. This is basic contact hygiene, not paranoia.

**For them:** You are a far greater biosecurity risk to the Cupola than it is to you. Your skin sheds bacteria constantly. Your breath carries moisture, CO2, and microbial load. Your boots carry whatever you walked through to get here. Every surface you touch, you inoculate with organisms from your own biome — organisms that may compete with, prey on, or simply confuse the iron-oxidizing bacteria that form the base of the Cupola's food web.

Minimize contact. Touch what you must for stability and safety, and nothing else. Do not sit on the grating if you can crouch instead — less surface area in contact. Do not set down food or water containers, and ideally do not eat or drink in the space at all (both to avoid introducing organic material and because of the air quality). If you spit, sneeze, or cough, direct it into a cloth you carry out with you.

The calibration wrench left on the grating — furred with the residue of a hundred egg-layings in its textured grip — tells you something: the flies *will* colonize what you leave behind. Anything you drop becomes infrastructure. Carry everything in; carry everything out.

## Equipment

### What to Bring

**Light:** Counterintuitively, you do not need a headlamp or flashlight for the Cupola itself — it is flooded with industrial light, status LEDs, and indicator sequences. But you need light for the approach, which will take you through maintenance corridors and cable chases that may not be illuminated. A compact, high-CRI headlamp with a red-light mode. Use red light in the final approach to avoid disrupting the flies' patterns before you've even arrived, though it's unclear whether their visual system responds to red wavelengths. Carry a backup light source.

**Documentation:** If you want to record what you see, bring a small camera with good low-movement capability. No flash — the industrial floods provide more than enough light, and a camera flash would be a massive perturbation to a population that navigates partly by light and thermal gradient. Video is better than still photography here, because the Cupola's essence is its constant change. A ten-minute unedited video of a single wall section during a thermal reconfiguration will tell you more than a hundred photographs.

Sound recording is even more valuable and even more difficult. Bring a high-sensitivity condenser microphone and a recorder with low self-noise. You are trying to capture sounds at the threshold of audibility — the papery wing-whisper, the individual scissoring of wing-pairs, the ticking of cooling metal, the metronomic condensation drip — against a background of subliminal electrical drone. This is a technical challenge worthy of dedicated preparation. If audio recording matters to you, test your equipment in an electrical substation or server room first, to understand how it handles the electromagnetic environment and the particular frequency range of industrial hum.

**Notebook:** Physical. Paper and pencil, not pen — pen ink may interact unpredictably with the humidity and condensation, and a dropped pen is another colonizable artifact. A small, hard-backed notebook you can write in while crouching. The observations you want to make here are ecological and temporal — what changes, when, in response to what. The flies' redistribution patterns encode information about the structure's state. If you watch long enough and take notes, you will begin to read the Cupola as the flies read it: a thermal-pneumatic-electromagnetic weather system in constant flux.

**Water:** Carry your own. Do not drink the condensation. Carry water in a sealed container that you open, drink from, and reseal without spilling. Dehydration is a real risk — the pumped air is dry from most of the competing systems, and you will lose moisture through breathing faster than you expect.

**Time:** Bring a watch. Not your phone — a watch, mechanical or with a simple digital display. The Cupola has no external referents for time. There is no daylight, no dimming, no cycle of activity and rest that you can perceive. The light never changes. The flies never sleep (or if they do, it is in shifts invisible to you). You will lose track of time, and this matters because the approach route is long and demanding, and you need to leave before fatigue makes the descent dangerous.

### What Not to Bring

**Do not bring food.** Organic crumbs, oils, sugars — all potential contaminants in a metallivore ecosystem. Eat before you arrive and after you leave.

**Do not bring magnets.** The electromagnetic environment is complex and functionally important — the flies respond to field shifts. A magnet in your pocket is a local perturbation.

**Do not bring insect repellent.** The chemical compounds would be alien to this ecosystem. The consequences are unpredictable and potentially devastating.

**Do not bring unnecessary metal tools.** Anything you drop joins the ecosystem. Anything with a painted or rubberized grip becomes a food source. Carry what you need for safety on the approach route and nothing more.

## Psychological Preparation

This is the section that matters most, and it is the hardest to write concretely.

### The Scale Problem

The Cupola sits at the top of a structure so large that its lower floors have their own weather and it has "forgotten this room exists." You are inside something immense and purposeful — but you do not know its purpose. You may never learn it. The structure extends below you and to every side, and the relays clicking and the ducts reversing and the pressure waves propagating upward are all evidence of decisions being made, somewhere, by something — routing decisions, thermal management decisions, maintenance protocol decisions — and none of them have anything to do with you or anything you understand.

This can produce a specific kind of vertigo that is not physical but existential. You are inside a machine. The machine is doing something. You are irrelevant to what it is doing. The flies are not irrelevant — they have found a niche, built an ecology, integrated themselves into the machine's thermal and chemical output — but you are just visiting. The machine does not know you are there. The flies don't care that you are there. You are, for the duration of your visit, perhaps the only conscious observer of a system that has never needed one and does not benefit from one.

Some travelers find this liberating. Some find it crushing. Know which you are before you go. If you need to feel that your presence matters — that you are a participant rather than a witness — this space will be difficult for you. If you can sit comfortably with being irrelevant, this space will be one of the most extraordinary things you ever experience.

### The Observation Trap

The scuff marks on the grating tell you that someone came here repeatedly, crouched, and watched. The calibration wrench tells you they set down a tool and forgot it — or chose not to retrieve it. This space is hypnotic. The constant reconfiguration — the shifting thermal map, the rippling population waves, the panels opening and closing, the puddles forming and evaporating — creates an endlessly novel visual field that never resolves into predictability. Your pattern-recognition systems will fire constantly, finding order, losing it, finding new order. This is deeply compelling to the human brain, and it does not habituate. An hour feels like fifteen minutes. Two hours feel like thirty.

Set a time limit before you enter. Stick to it. The descent is long, and fatigue-related accidents in industrial infrastructure are the prosaic way extraordinary journeys end.

### The Silence That Isn't

The sound environment of the Cupola will work on you in ways you may not consciously recognize. The fly-whisper sitting at the threshold of hearing, the electrical drone, the metronomic drip, the intermittent clicking of relays — this is not silence, but your brain will process it as silence because none of it crosses the threshold into identifiable, nameable sound. The result is a paradoxical sense of both deep quiet and deep presence. You are "alone" in "silence" inside a space teeming with life and sound.

Extended exposure to this perceptual state can produce something that experienced meditators will recognize: a dissolution of the boundary between observer and environment. You stop feeling like someone *in* the Cupola and start feeling like something *of* it — another warm body, another thermal variable, another element the flies route around. This is not dangerous, but it is disorienting, and it can make it difficult to decide to leave. When your watch tells you it's time, leave. You can come back.

### The Reconfiguration Moment

You will, at some point during your visit, experience a full systemic reconfiguration — the moment when a deep decision propagates upward as a pressure wave, every duct breathes inward simultaneously, and the entire fly population lifts from every surface at once. You will be standing inside a cloud of ten thousand copper-green bodies in bright, sharp industrial light, every wing flashing, the air full of that subthreshold whisper suddenly risen to something your ears can almost catch, and the space will feel like it has inhaled, like it is holding its breath, waiting to see what it has become.

There is no way to prepare for this. I tell you about it so that when it happens you will recognize it, and instead of flinching or ducking or freezing, you will hold still and breathe and *watch*. The flies will not hit you. They are better navigators than you are. They will flow around you like water around a stone, and for a few seconds you will be the only still thing in a room full of ten thousand small lives making ten thousand simultaneous decisions, and then the ducts will disagree again, and the flies will descend, and everything will be different, and you will understand — in your body, not your mind — what it means for a space to be alive.

## Ethical Considerations

### The Question of Intervention

You will notice things that seem wrong. A duct that wheezes suggests a blockage that could reduce airflow to a section of the population. A flickering LED suggests a failing circuit. The cracked duct seam that provides the condensation puddle could be sealed "properly," and part of you — the human, tool-using, problem-solving part — will itch to fix things.

Do not fix things. The Cupola exists because of failures, accidents, and deferred maintenance. The cracked duct provides the only water source. The misaligned gratings create the oxidation the flies feed on. The junction box that was opened and never resealed provides the electromagnetic readout the system uses for... whatever it uses it for. Every "flaw" is load-bearing. You do not understand the system well enough to improve it, and the flies, who have been reading this space for longer than you've known it exists, have already optimized around every imperfection.

### The Question of Removal

Do not take specimens. Not a dead fly, not a paint flake, not a scraping of biofilm, not a drop of condensation. You do not know what is native to this system and what might be invasive to yours, and you do not know what removing even a small element might cascade into. The calibration wrench was *added* to the system and has been integrated — egg-layings in the textured grip. Even removing the wrench now would be a subtraction from the ecosystem. Leave everything as you find it, minus the footprints and knee-prints you cannot avoid making, which the particulate will cover and the flies will colonize soon enough.

### The Question of Reporting

Should you tell anyone about this place? This is a genuine ethical question with no clear answer. If the structure has maintenance personnel, and if they become aware of the Cupola, they might seal it, clean it, fumigate it — standard procedure for an "infestation" in critical infrastructure. The flies and their entire ecology would be destroyed, and a pocket of extraordinary emergent complexity would be reduced to another sealed void in the machine.

On the other hand, if the structure is reconfiguring in ways that the flies have learned to read and you have not, the Cupola might contain information — encoded in flight patterns, population distributions, thermal response times — that could be genuinely valuable to whoever operates the structure. The flies are, in a real sense, a monitoring system. They are the only occupants of this space who actually understand what the structure is doing, moment by moment, decision by decision.

I cannot resolve this for you. But I will say: the scuff marks suggest that whoever came before you chose not to report. The wrench suggests they were maintenance personnel — someone with legitimate access to the structure's upper reaches, someone who carried calibration tools, someone who could have filed a work order. They chose instead to come back, and crouch, and watch. Draw your own conclusions.

## The Departure

Leave the way you came. Move slowly in the final crevice, where the Cupola narrows to the maintenance gap. Check that you've dropped nothing — run your hands over your pockets, your pack, your gear loops. Anything left behind becomes part of the ecosystem.

As you withdraw through the crevice, you will pass through the boundary between the Cupola's competing air systems and the structure's main ventilation, and the air will change — either better or worse, but different. The sound will change. The flies' subthreshold whisper will be replaced by the structure's deeper mechanical voice, and you will suddenly be aware that you had been hearing the flies all along, because now that you can't hear them, the silence they leave is a different shape.

The descent will feel longer than the ascent. You will be tired, and the infrastructure you're climbing through will seem more hostile than it did on the way up, because you've spent hours inside a space that was, despite everything, *alive* — and the cable chases and maintenance corridors are not alive, they are just infrastructure, and the contrast is stark.

When you emerge — into whatever passes for ground level in this structure, into whatever light or weather or society exists at the base of something that reaches high enough to forget its own summit — you will feel a specific and nameable grief. Not for anything lost, but for the ongoing: right now, this moment, a duct is reversing, and the thermal map is shifting, and ten thousand copper-green bodies are lifting from a wall and resettling on the opposite one, and a puddle is trembling, and a panel is opening or closing, and no one is watching.

No one needs to be watching. That is the miracle and the grief in one.

Go home. Clean your boots. Wash your clothes — the particulate will be in everything. Check your skin for any marks or irritation from contact with industrial surfaces or fly residue. Drink water. Eat. Sleep.

And if you find yourself, in the days that follow, watching the way pigeons redistribute on a building ledge when the sun shifts, or the way ants reroute around a new crack in the sidewalk, or the way condensation forms on the outside of a cold glass and traces a path dictated by invisible imperfections in the surface — if you find yourself seeing, everywhere, the Cupola's logic of small lives optimizing endlessly around systems they did not build and cannot control — then the visit worked. Then you brought something back after all, and it was the only thing you were ever meant to take: a way of seeing that was not yours before, and is not yours now, but that you are borrowing from ten thousand flies who never knew they had it.
