# Preparing for the Crest Hollow

---

## First: Understanding What You're Going To

Before anything else, sit with the description. Read it again. Understand that this is not a destination in the way a city or a monument is a destination. There is no path marked on a map that says "Crest Hollow, 3.2 km." There is no sign. There is a cairn with a quartz stone on top, and that cairn was placed by someone who wanted to say *this place exists* without saying anything else. You are visiting a location that does not advertise itself, that is invisible from below, that requires you to haul yourself over a lip of fractured rock before you even know it's there. This means your preparation must account for the possibility — the likelihood — that you will spend a long time looking before you find it, and that the looking is not separate from the visit. The approach is part of the place.

You are also a multiverse traveler, which means you've seen things that should have recalibrated your sense of wonder, and possibly have. You may arrive at the Crest Hollow expecting it to underwhelm you. A puddle, some moss, a few birds. You would be wrong. The Crest Hollow is not spectacular in the way that waterfalls or canyons are spectacular. It is spectacular in the way that a single living cell is spectacular — everything is there, everything is working, and the scale of the complexity is inversely proportional to the scale of the space. Prepare yourself for smallness that contains multitudes. Prepare yourself for a place where the most significant events are a pika moving a mouthful of grass six inches to the left and a spider rebuilding a web for the four hundredth time.

## Physical Preparation

### Altitude and Cardiovascular Readiness

This is a high-altitude location. The description doesn't give a specific elevation, but the clues are unambiguous: cushion plants, dwarf willows older than valley trees, a summer defined as six weeks hovering just above freezing, clouds dragging across the surface, less atmosphere to soften the light. You're looking at something in the range of 3,000 to 4,500 meters, depending on the latitude and the specific mountain range. This means:

**Acclimatize.** Spend at least three to five days at intermediate elevations before attempting the final approach. If you're coming from sea level or from a universe where you've been at low altitude, add extra days. Acute mountain sickness is not dramatic — it's a headache, nausea, fatigue, poor sleep — but it will ruin your capacity to experience this place. The Crest Hollow demands attentiveness, and altitude sickness makes you stupid and miserable. You cannot afford either.

**Build cardiovascular endurance well in advance.** The final approach involves scree — loose rock on a steep slope — which is one of the most energy-expensive surfaces to climb. Every step slides back. Your calves and ankles will burn. Your heart rate will spike. If you are not in reasonable cardiovascular shape, the last pitch will reduce you to a gasping, head-down slog, and you will arrive at the lip of the hollow too depleted to do anything but sit and recover. The hollow deserves better than that. You deserve better than that. Train on hills. Train on uneven ground. Train carrying weight.

**Understand the breathing.** At this elevation, each breath delivers meaningfully less oxygen than you're used to. Your body compensates by breathing faster and deeper, which works, but which also means you're constantly aware of the act of breathing in a way you normally aren't. This is actually useful in the Crest Hollow — the forced awareness of your own respiration attunes you to the respiratory quality of the place itself, the daily breathing of the seeps, the in-and-out of the wind. But it's uncomfortable at first, and if you fight it or panic, it gets worse. Practice diaphragmatic breathing. Learn to let your body set the pace.

### Cold Exposure and Thermoregulation

The temperature in the Crest Hollow, even in summer, hovers near freezing. The wind — the constant, chest-filling, molar-vibrating wind — strips heat from your body with devastating efficiency. Wind chill at these speeds and temperatures can make a nominally above-freezing day feel like deep winter. And the description makes clear that late afternoon brings a rapid temperature drop as the hollow enters the mountain's shadow — a shift from the precise warmth of direct sun to the precise cold of shade, with nothing gradual about it.

**Layer intelligently.** You need a system, not a single garment. Base layer: merino wool or a high-quality synthetic that wicks moisture and retains warmth when damp (you will be damp — from sweat on the climb, from mist, from the seeps if you get close to the rock walls). Mid layer: insulating fleece or down, something you can add or remove quickly as the light shifts. Outer layer: a windproof, water-resistant shell that can handle the sustained gale-force conditions at the rim and the compressed wind within the hollow. The wind is not a gust you endure and then it passes. It is always there. Your outer layer must be able to handle *always*.

**Protect your extremities with particular care.** Warm gloves — bring two pairs, because one will get wet from the moss or the pool and wet gloves at altitude are worse than no gloves. A hat that covers your ears and stays on in high wind (chin strap or tight-fitting balaclava). The wind-note resonates in your skull; you will feel acoustic pressure in your teeth and inner ear regardless, but wind on bare ears at this velocity is painful and will push you toward retreat faster than anything else. Thick socks. Consider gaiters if your approach crosses snowfields.

**Your face.** The combination of UV intensity (less atmospheric filtering), wind abrasion, and cold will destroy exposed skin. A buff or balaclava that you can pull up over your nose and cheeks. Sunscreen on every exposed surface, SPF 50+, reapplied frequently because the wind will scour it off. Lip balm with UV protection — your lips will crack and bleed otherwise, and cracked lips at altitude heal slowly.

### Foot and Ankle Protection

The approach is scree. The hollow floor is gravel and soil with moss and cushion plants. The scree field at the southern end, where the pikas live, is a jumble of boulders with gaps between them. You need boots — real boots, with ankle support, stiff soles, and good tread. Not trail runners. Not hiking shoes. Boots. The scree will roll under your feet. The boulders will be wet, possibly iced. A twisted ankle on the final approach, above the treeline, far from help, is a survival situation, not an inconvenience. Break your boots in thoroughly before the trip. Your feet are your transportation, your foundation, and the primary interface between your body and the physical reality of this place. Treat them well.

## Equipment

### What to Carry

**Water.** The pool is always there, and it is always cold enough to ache, and you should not drink from it without treatment. Not because it's dangerous — at this elevation, with this level of UV exposure and this small a watershed, the microbial load is probably minimal — but because you're a multiverse traveler and your immune system may not be calibrated to this particular universe's microbiome. Carry enough water for your time in the hollow plus the descent. A minimum of two liters, more if you plan to stay. A water filter or purification tablets as backup.

**Food.** High-calorie, compact, easy to eat with cold fingers. Nuts, dried fruit, chocolate, energy bars. Cheese if you can keep it from freezing. You will burn calories at an extraordinary rate — the altitude, the cold, the wind, the exertion of the approach. Eat before you're hungry. Eat while you're watching the pikas. The pikas will be eating too. There's a symmetry there.

**Sun protection for your eyes.** This is critical and often underestimated. The UV at this altitude is intense enough to cause photokeratitis — essentially a sunburn on the surface of your corneas — within hours. Snow, if present, amplifies this. The quartz stone on the cairn throws light in unpredictable directions, which is beautiful but also means stray UV reflections will hit you from unexpected angles. Quality sunglasses with side shields, or glacier glasses. Category 4 lenses if you can get them. The description says the light makes your eyes water even apart from the wind; protect them.

**A windproof notebook and pencil.** Not a pen — pens fail in cold. Not a phone — batteries die at altitude in cold, screens are unreadable in bright sun, and the Crest Hollow is a place that rewards patience and close observation, which is antithetical to the impulse to photograph everything. You may want to photograph it. Bring a camera if you must. But bring the notebook too. When the quartz stone sings and the wind reorganizes itself around that frequency, you will want to write down what it felt like, because the feeling will be difficult to recover later, and a photograph will not capture it. A photograph cannot capture a sound that lives at the upper edge of hearing and resonates in the fine bones of your inner ear.

**Binoculars.** Small, lightweight, good optics. For the choughs wheeling at the rim. For the lammergeier shadows. For the detail of the lichen — the rust and pale green — that you can't appreciate from the center of the hollow. For the pika haypiles, the tiny architecture of cured vegetation in the gaps between boulders. This place rewards magnification. It rewards the willingness to look more closely than seems necessary.

**A small sitting pad.** Foam, insulated, something to put between your body and the cold ground. You are going to want to sit. You are going to want to sit for a long time. The moss around the pool looks inviting, but it is saturated with cold water, and sitting on it will soak through your clothing and begin to chill you from below. The rock is cold. The gravel is cold. An insulated pad lets you sit comfortably for hours, which is what this place requires. You don't visit the Crest Hollow for twenty minutes. Twenty minutes gives you the wind and the first impression and the general shape. You need hours for the layers to reveal themselves — the pika social dynamics, the spider's web cycle, the shift in the wind-note as temperature changes, the moment when the light angles into the quartz.

**First aid kit.** Basic but complete. Blister treatment (your boots, the scree). Pain relief (headache from altitude, from wind-noise, from the acoustic pressure that fills your chest). Anti-nausea medication (altitude). Athletic tape (ankle support on the descent). Emergency blanket (if weather turns, if you're delayed, if the temperature drop in late afternoon catches you unprepared). The Crest Hollow is remote. Help is far away and below. Be self-sufficient.

**A headlamp.** In case you stay too long. You will want to stay too long. The late-afternoon sequence — sun dropping, shadow racing in, temperature plummeting, seeps slowing, ice forming, pikas doing their last frantic round, choughs settling in, wind deepening — is so compelling that you may find yourself watching it unfold with the same rapt attention you'd give a symphony, and then it will be dusk, and you will be at high altitude in the dark with a scree descent ahead of you. Bring the headlamp. Bring spare batteries (cold kills batteries; keep them warm in an inside pocket against your body).

### What Not to Carry

**Nothing unnecessary.** Every gram costs energy on the approach. This is not the place for luxury items, extra "just in case" clothing beyond what you need, heavy camera equipment, or anything that serves your ego rather than your experience. The Crest Hollow is a place of radical economy — every organism here survives on the absolute minimum, tuned to its niche with a precision that leaves no margin. Respect that by traveling light. Bring what you need. Leave what you don't.

**No speakers, no music players, no anything that produces artificial sound.** The acoustic environment of the Crest Hollow is its defining feature. The wind-roar, the chough calls, the pika alarms, the fly-drone, the quartz tone — these are not background. They are the foreground. They are the thing itself. Adding your own sound to this space would be a kind of vandalism.

**No bright-colored gear if you can avoid it.** This is aesthetic, not practical, but it matters. The color palette of the Crest Hollow is grey granite, green moss, blue-green grass, rust and pale-green lichen, the impossible purples and yellows and whites of the flowers, and the deep blue of the high-altitude sky. A neon orange jacket screams against this palette. Muted colors — greys, greens, dark blues, browns — let you exist in the space without disrupting it visually. The pikas will notice you regardless. The choughs will notice you. But you'll feel less like an intruder and more like a temporary element of the place.

## Timing

### Season

**Go in summer.** This is non-negotiable. The description defines summer as six weeks when daytime temperatures hover just above freezing instead of just below. Outside this window, the hollow is buried under snow or locked in ice or both, and the approach becomes a mountaineering expedition requiring ropes, crampons, ice axes, and a willingness to risk your life for something that will be invisible under white. The summer window is narrow — probably mid-July to late August, depending on the specific latitude and the year's snowpack — and you need to hit it. The flowers bloom in this window. The pikas are at peak activity, building their haypiles. The choughs are nesting or have recently fledged. The pool is open. The moss is green. This is the hollow at its fullest expression.

Within the summer window, **aim for a period of settled weather** if you can determine one. A high-pressure system that will give you clear skies and relatively (relatively!) calm wind. The "still days, which are rare" — try to hit one of those if fortune allows. Not because the wind-roar isn't worth experiencing (it is, it's fundamental), but because the still days reveal the layer beneath: the birds, the full acoustic texture, the sounds that the wind normally swallows. The ideal visit spans both conditions — a windy day to feel the full force of the place, and a still day (or still hours) to hear its quieter voices.

### Time of Day

**Arrive early.** Pre-dawn departure from your high camp (you should have a high camp; see below) to reach the hollow shortly after sunrise. The morning light on the east-facing seep wall — the dark stains, the delicate glaze ice catching the light, the moment when the ice shatters as the temperature crosses the threshold — this is something you want to witness. It is the hollow waking up. The pikas start their rounds early. The choughs emerge. The pool, if it was skimmed with ice overnight, opens.

**Stay through the afternoon.** The late-day sequence is the crescendo. The quartz tone requires late, low-angle sun. The shadow-crossing of the lammergeiers tends to happen in afternoon thermals. The temperature collapse, the ice reformation, the settling-in of every living thing for the night — this is the hollow going to sleep, and it is as worthy of attention as the waking.

**Plan for a full day.** Arrive at dawn. Leave before dark. That gives you roughly twelve to fourteen hours in summer, minus travel time. It's enough. It might not feel like enough, but it's enough for one visit.

If you can manage it — if the weather holds, if your supplies are sufficient, if you can find a sheltered bivouac spot near the rim — **stay for two or three days.** The daily cycles — freeze-thaw, web-building and web-destruction, haypile construction, wind patterns — are the pulse of the place, and witnessing them repeat gives you a depth of understanding that a single day cannot provide. On day two, you'll notice things you missed on day one. On day three, you'll begin to feel the rhythm in your body, anticipating the shifts before they happen. This is when the hollow stops being a place you're visiting and starts being a place you're inhabiting, temporarily, as one more organism tuned to the cycle.

## The Approach

You need to plan the approach carefully because the description tells you the hollow is invisible from below. This means you cannot navigate to it by sight. You need either:

1. **Coordinates from a prior visitor**, if such information exists in whatever universe you're operating in. The cairn says "you can find it" — someone intended this place to be findable. But they didn't make it easy.

2. **Route knowledge from local sources.** Mountain guides, shepherds, people who work at extreme elevations — they may know of the saddle, even if they don't know what's in it. A saddle in a ridgeline is a navigational feature; it may be used as a pass, however rarely.

3. **Topographic analysis.** Study the ridgeline. Look for the dip between two peaks. Use maps if they exist in sufficient detail. A saddle is a mappable feature. The hollow within the saddle may not appear on any map, but the saddle itself should.

The final approach is a scree field leading to a rock lip. This is the most dangerous part of the trip. Scree is treacherous — it moves, it slides, it can trigger small rockfalls. The lip of fractured rock requires some scrambling. Assess the route for:

- **Rockfall hazard.** Is anyone above you? Is the rock stable? Morning is generally safer than afternoon because freeze-thaw loosens rock as temperatures rise.
- **Route-finding.** Scree fields look uniform from a distance but have subtle features — ribs of more stable rock, lines of larger boulders that provide better footing. Pick your line before you start.
- **Weather.** Do not attempt the approach in cloud, in rain, in snow, in high wind beyond what you can safely move through. The hollow will be there tomorrow. You need to be there too.

**Bring trekking poles** for the scree. They save your knees, improve your balance, and reduce the risk of a fall. Collapse them and stow them once you're in the hollow.

### High Camp

Establish a camp below the hollow but above the treeline — at an elevation that lets you reach the rim within two to three hours of hiking. This camp should be:

- Sheltered from the worst wind (use terrain features — a boulder, a ridge, a dip)
- On stable, flat ground (not on scree, not in a drainage where water might flow in a storm)
- Provisioned for multiple days if you plan to visit the hollow more than once

The hollow itself is **not a campsite.** Do not camp in it. Do not pitch a tent on the moss. Do not build a fire (there is no fuel and the ecosystem is too fragile). Do not add to the stone wall or the cairn. Your presence in the hollow should be temporary and leave no trace beyond the slight compression of a cushion plant under your sitting pad, which will spring back within hours. The pikas will investigate your sitting spot after you leave. The choughs will check for dropped crumbs. The hollow will absorb your visit and continue.

## Psychological and Experiential Preparation

This is the most important section, and the one most travelers neglect.

### Prepare for the Sound

You cannot fully prepare for the wind-note. Reading about it is not the same as feeling it fill your chest cavity and vibrate your molars. The description is precise: this is acoustic pressure, not just noise. Your body will respond before your mind does — eyes watering, jaw clenching, a primitive unease that comes from being inside a sound rather than hearing it from outside. This is normal. This is the place working on you. Do not fight it. Do not plug your ears (you'll miss the choughs, the pikas, the quartz). Let the sound become the medium you're moving through. Within thirty minutes, your brain will begin to normalize it, and the other sounds will emerge from beneath it like objects resolving out of fog.

If you have experience with sustained loud environments — waterfalls, industrial spaces, certain musical traditions that prioritize drone and resonance — draw on that. The wind-note is closest to an organ pipe or a didgeridoo: a fundamental with shifting overtones, felt as much as heard. Some travelers have found that humming — producing their own sustained tone — helps them acclimate, giving their auditory processing system something familiar to anchor to while it adjusts to the overwhelming input. Try it. If it helps, keep doing it. If it doesn't, stop. The pikas will look at you either way.

### Prepare for Scale Dissonance

You are a multiverse traveler. You have presumably visited places of great scale — vast landscapes, enormous structures, cosmic phenomena. The Crest Hollow is eighty meters across. The pool is two meters wide. The flowers are tiny. The pikas fit in your hand. Everything here is small, and everything here is complete. This can produce a disorienting sensation — a feeling that you've been handed something miniature and been told it contains everything, and it *does* contain everything, and your sense of proportion doesn't know what to do with that.

Lean into it. Sit by the pool. Watch the water well up around your thumb in the moss. Follow a single pika for an hour as it makes its rounds — note its route, its favorite grass patches, its specific haypile, the way it pauses on a boulder and screams at nothing and then resumes working. Follow a single spider rebuilding its web. Watch the ice form on the seeps. The Crest Hollow teaches attention, and the lesson is that attention is a function of time, not size. You don't need a vast canvas to find complexity. You need patience and a willingness to look at small things for long enough that they become large.

### Prepare for Solitude

Unless you bring companions (see below), you will be alone in the Crest Hollow. Alone at high altitude, inside a sustained roar of wind, surrounded by organisms that have no interest in you beyond a brief assessment of whether you're a threat. The pikas will habituate to you quickly if you sit still — within an hour they'll be running their routes past you as if you were a boulder. The choughs may land near you if you're quiet. But none of this is companionship. You are not a participant in the ecosystem. You are a witness.

Some travelers find this profoundly peaceful. Others find it profoundly lonely. Both responses are correct. The Crest Hollow does not care which one you have. It was here before you arrived and it will be here after you leave and the quartz will sing at the same angle tomorrow evening regardless of whether anyone is standing near the cairn to hear it. This is either the most comforting thought in the world or the most desolating, and you should be prepared for either reaction, and for the possibility that you'll feel both simultaneously.

### Prepare for the Quartz Tone

You may or may not hear it. It requires specific conditions — clear evening, low sun, the right angle, proximity to the cairn. It lasts only a few seconds. It is at the upper edge of hearing, which means that age-related hearing loss may put it beyond your range. (If you have access to hearing-enhancement technology from other universes, consider it, but know that amplifying the wind-note to get the quartz tone is a trade-off with real consequences for your comfort.)

If you do hear it: the description says "the wind-roar seems to organize itself around this tiny frequency, as if the whole hollow were a struck bell and the quartz its clapper." This is either a perceptual artifact — your brain imposing pattern on noise — or it is an actual acoustic phenomenon, a resonance interaction between the quartz's vibration and the wind's frequency spectrum. It doesn't matter which. The experience is the same. For three or four seconds, the entire hollow will feel unified, coherent, as if everything — the wind, the water, the rock, the living things — were aspects of a single process. And then it will pass, and the place will be just a place again.

This moment has been reported by enough visitors across enough universes to be considered reliable. It is not mystical. It is not supernatural. It is physics and perception interacting at a specific point in space and time. But it is also the closest the Crest Hollow comes to speaking directly to you, and when it happens, you will understand why someone climbed up here and placed seven stones with care and put a piece of quartz on top: because the place asked for it, in its own language, and someone heard.

### Prepare to Leave

This is harder than it sounds. The Crest Hollow is a trap — a benign trap, a beautiful trap, but a trap nonetheless. The density of activity, the layered cycles, the constant revelation of new details — they create a sense that if you just stay a little longer, you'll see the next thing, the thing that completes your understanding. You won't. There is no completion. The cycles repeat but never identically. The pikas will rearrange their haypiles. The spiders will rebuild. The ice will form and shatter and form. You could stay for a week and still feel you were leaving too soon.

Set a departure time before you arrive. Honor it. The descent over scree is dangerous in fading light, and the hollow is not a safe place to spend the night — the temperature will drop well below freezing, the wind will intensify, and without proper bivouac gear, you will be in genuine danger. The hollow shelters its residents because they evolved for it. You did not. Be a visitor. Be a good one. Leave before you need to.

## Ethical Considerations

### Ecological Fragility

The Crest Hollow is a closed system of extraordinary delicacy. The food web is complete but compressed — remove one element and the cascade effects would be significant. The cushion plants that look like green stones have been growing for decades; a single footstep on one can kill years of growth. The moss around the pool is lush but shallow-rooted; pulling it (as the choughs do for nesting material) is part of the cycle, but pulling it as a souvenir is not. The dwarf willows are older than most human institutions. The pika haypiles represent weeks of labor by an animal that weighs less than a handful of coins.

**Step on rock and gravel.** Avoid the cushion plants. Avoid the moss except where you can kneel briefly to touch the pool. Do not pick the flowers. Do not disturb the haypiles. Do not reach into the crevices where the choughs nest. Do not add stones to the cairn — the seven stones were chosen by someone who understood the place, and adding your own would change the balance in ways you can't predict and wouldn't notice but the place would.

**Pack out everything you bring.** Every wrapper, every crumb, every bit of tissue. Orange peel does not biodegrade at this altitude in any meaningful timeframe. A single food wrapper caught in the dwarf willows becomes a fixture, shredded by wind into microplastics that enter the soil and the moss and the pool. This place has existed for ten thousand years without your garbage. Keep it that way.

**Human waste.** If you need to relieve yourself during a full day in the hollow, do it outside the rim, on the scree, as far from the water sources as possible. Pack out solid waste if you can. This is unpleasant. It is also non-negotiable. The pool has no outlet. Contaminants do not leave. What you add stays.

### The Built Elements

Do not take the shaped wood from the crack in the rock wall. Do not disassemble the stone wall to examine its construction. Do not remove the quartz from the cairn to get a closer look, to test its resonance, to keep it. These objects are part of the hollow now. They have been here long enough to be incorporated — the moss grows against the wall, the wood has been shaped by the same freeze-thaw that shapes everything else, the quartz has found its angle. They are artifacts of human presence that have become artifacts of the place. Leave them.

If you feel compelled to mark your own passage — and you will; the cairn practically demands a response, a dialogue — write in your notebook. That's your cairn. That's your seven stones. Carry it with you when you go.

## Companions

**Consider going alone.** The Crest Hollow is most itself when you are the only human variable. With a companion, you will talk. Talking is natural, and it's how humans process experience, but it also interposes a layer of narration between you and the place. You'll say "look at that" and point at the choughs, and your companion will look, and you'll both have the experience of sharing the observation, which is a human experience and a good one but is not the same as the experience of being the only one seeing it, the only consciousness in the hollow that processes light as meaning, and feeling the weight of that solitude.

If you do bring a companion, **bring one who can be quiet.** Not silent — silence between people in a roaring wind is its own kind of loud — but quiet in the sense of not needing to fill the space with commentary. Someone who can sit beside you on the foam pad and watch the pikas and not say anything for an hour and then say, quietly, "the one on the left just stole from the one on the right" and then go quiet again. That's the right companion for this place.

Do not bring a group. The hollow is eighty meters across. Three or more humans in that space changes its character fundamentally — the pikas will retreat deep into the scree, the choughs will stay at the rim, the spiders will feel the vibrations of multiple footsteps and hide. You will have brought the human world into a space that is defined by its absence, and you will see only the physical setting, not the life.

## What You Will Bring Back

Nothing physical. You will leave the hollow as you found it, minus perhaps a thumbprint of water pressed from the moss and a slight warmth in the spot where you sat.

What you will bring back is harder to name. Some travelers report that for days after visiting the Crest Hollow, they notice small sounds they would normally ignore — the pitch of wind in a doorframe, the specific tone of water in a pipe, the way a bird call sits inside a larger ambient sound. Some report a recalibrated sense of time — a feeling that daily cycles (waking, working, eating, sleeping) have a weight and a dignity they hadn't previously noticed, because they watched an ecosystem run on nothing but daily cycles and it was enough, it was everything.

Some report dreaming about the quartz tone. Not the hollow, not the pikas, not the wind — just the tone, high and clean, lasting a few seconds, organizing everything around it, and then stopping. They say they wake from these dreams not unsettled but aligned, as if something in their inner architecture had been tuned to a frequency they didn't know they could receive.

This may happen to you. It may not. The Crest Hollow does not promise anything. It is a notch at the top of the world where the ridgeline dips just enough to hold something living, and it holds it, and it has held it for ten thousand years, and it will hold it for ten thousand more, and whether or not you visit, whether or not you hear the quartz, whether or not you sit in the moss and feel the wind fill your chest like a second heartbeat — the pikas will build their haypiles, and the choughs will wheel, and the ice will form and shatter and form, and the wind will never stop.

Go anyway. Go prepared. Go light. Sit down. Stay as long as you safely can.
