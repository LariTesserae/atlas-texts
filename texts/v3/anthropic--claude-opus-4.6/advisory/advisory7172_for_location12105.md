# Preparing to Visit the Nesting Terraces of Óramak

---

## Understanding What You're Walking Into

Before anything else, sit with what this place actually is. This is not a nature reserve with marked trails and interpretive signage. This is not a wilderness you can approach with a backpacker's confidence. The Terraces of Óramak are a living geological formation built entirely by organisms that have no awareness of you and no accommodation for you. You are entering what amounts to the interior of a superorganism — a place with the density and metabolic intensity of a body, scaled up to the size of a landscape. Your preparation needs to reflect that you are, in every meaningful sense, a foreign object being introduced into a biological system that has been running without interruption for longer than most civilizations.

The description makes clear that people from the lowlands do not come here. That is not superstition. That is practical wisdom accumulated over generations. You should take it seriously even as you disregard it.

---

## Physical Acclimatization

The plateau sits at two thousand meters. This is not extreme altitude by mountaineering standards, but it is enough to thin the air noticeably, and you will be exerting yourself — climbing terrace walls, navigating uneven biological substrate, breathing through filtration. If you are arriving from a low-altitude baseline, spend at least three to five days at intermediate elevation before ascending to the plateau. The air at Óramak is warm but thin, and you will be working harder than you expect because of the sensory assault alone — your body will burn more energy processing the noise, the smell, the visual complexity. Altitude sickness layered on top of the overwhelming stimulation of the terraces could genuinely incapacitate you.

Cardiovascular fitness matters here, but not in the way it matters for a summit attempt. You need the kind of fitness that sustains moderate exertion over long hours in difficult conditions — the stamina to climb, crouch, navigate wet and uneven ground, and do it all while your nervous system is screaming at you that you are somewhere you should not be. Train for sustained hiking on irregular terrain. Get comfortable being uncomfortable for hours at a stretch.

## Respiratory Protection

This is arguably the single most critical preparation category.

The atmosphere at Óramak is not just smelly. It is biochemically hostile to human lungs over any extended period. You are walking into a space saturated with:

- **Ammonia** from the astronomical volume of fresh and decomposing guano. Concentrations in the terrace valleys, where air circulation is poorest, could easily reach levels that cause chemical burns to mucous membranes. Even in the better-ventilated areas along the rim, ambient ammonia will sting your eyes and throat within minutes of unprotected exposure.

- **Fungal spores** from the bioluminescent mats, the shelf fungi, the mycorrhizal networks threading through the óram. These are not species your immune system has any familiarity with. Inhaling novel fungal spores in high concentrations is one of the fastest ways to develop a severe pulmonary reaction — anything from allergic bronchospasm to a genuine fungal infection that could colonize your lungs in a foreign biome's organisms.

- **Particulate matter** — dried guano dust, feather fragments, insect chitin, calcium-ite flakes from the terrace walls — all of it constantly being knocked loose by the movement of millions of birds. The air in the terrace valleys is thick with fine particulates, especially during periods of high activity when flocks are arriving and departing.

- **Bioaerosols** — bacteria, viral particles, parasitic cysts — the inevitable airborne component of any environment this biologically dense.

You need a full-face respirator with combination cartridges rated for ammonia/organic vapor AND particulate filtration (P100 or equivalent). Not a dust mask. Not a bandana. Not a half-face respirator that leaves your eyes exposed. A full-face unit that seals around your entire face, protects your eyes from ammonia irritation, and filters both gas-phase and particulate-phase hazards.

Bring at least three complete sets of replacement cartridges. The cartridges will saturate faster than you expect in this environment. When you start to smell ammonia through the respirator, change the cartridges immediately — by the time you can smell it, you're already getting meaningful exposure.

Carry a backup respirator. If your primary unit is damaged — a bird strike, a fall on the slippery substrate, anything — you need to be able to seal your airway immediately. An unprotected face in the deep terrace valleys is a medical emergency in slow motion.

---

## Eye Protection

Even with a full-face respirator, consider the protection it offers your eyes and whether it's adequate. Ammonia is a severe eye irritant. Particulate debris raining down from above — and it will rain down constantly as birds land and launch from every surface around you — can cause corneal abrasion. The dim conditions mean your pupils will be dilated much of the time, making your eyes more vulnerable.

If your respirator has a good sealed lens, this may be sufficient. If not, sealed chemical splash goggles worn underneath or integrated into your system. Anti-fog treatment is essential — the warm, humid air will fog any unvented lens almost instantly, and you cannot afford to be navigating this terrain blind.

## Hearing Protection

The description makes clear that the sound at Óramak is not merely loud but *physiologically significant*. It vibrates in the chest. It saturates every frequency band. It is described as genuinely painful during peak activity. This is not a metaphor.

Sustained exposure to the kind of broadband biological noise described here — a wall of sound produced by tens of millions of animals — can easily exceed 100 decibels. Possibly significantly more in the enclosed valleys between terrace ranks, where the curved walls would act as acoustic reflectors. At those levels, you are looking at permanent hearing damage within minutes to hours of unprotected exposure.

You need serious hearing protection. Not foam earplugs alone, though those are a reasonable base layer. Ideally:

- **Custom-molded earplugs** with high-NRR (noise reduction rating) filtering as your primary layer.
- **Over-ear electronic hearing protection** as your secondary layer — the kind that actively dampens sounds above a safe threshold while amplifying quieter sounds. This is important because you *will* need to hear some things — the approach of a large bird, the sound of substrate giving way beneath you, communication with any companion.
- The combination of in-ear and over-ear protection (dual protection) can achieve noise reduction of 35-40 dB or more, which should bring the ambient roar down to manageable levels.

Be aware that the sub-audible frequencies — the structural groaning of the óram, the vibration of millions of wingbeats — will not be stopped by hearing protection. You will feel these in your body regardless. This is disorienting. Prepare for it mentally.

Also consider: the loss of spatial audio awareness in a place where navigation is already difficult. Practice moving in your hearing protection before you arrive. Get comfortable with the muffled, compressed version of the world it gives you.

## Skin Protection and Clothing

Everything at Óramak wants to colonize you or irritate you. The description mentions mites migrating onto your skin, moths in your hair, beetles underfoot. The surfaces are coated in fresh excrement. The air deposits particulate matter on every exposed surface. The vegetation is wet and the water is nutrient-rich — meaning biologically active in ways your skin is not prepared for.

**Full-body coverage is non-negotiable.** You need:

- A **sealed or semi-sealed outer layer** — something like a lightweight Tyvek suit or agricultural coverall that prevents direct skin contact with the substrate, the vegetation, the water, the air deposits, and the organisms. It will get filthy. It will get torn. Bring multiples.

- **Waterproof outer boots** that seal at the calf — the kind used in chemical cleanup or agricultural settings. You will be walking through standing pools of biologically active water, stepping on fresh guano, traversing wet fungal mats. Regular hiking boots will be soaked through and contaminated within the first hour. The ammonia alone will degrade leather. Rubber or PVC boots, sealed to your coverall with tape if necessary.

- **Gloves** — nitrile or chemical-resistant — worn at all times. You will need to touch surfaces for balance. Every surface is coated with biological material. Your hands are your most likely vector for transferring pathogens to your face during the inevitable moment when you need to adjust your respirator.

- A **hood or head covering** that seals around your respirator. Moths, mites, feather lice, fungal spores — all of it settling on your head and hair constantly. A tight-fitting hood, ideally integrated with your coverall.

- **Tape.** Lots of tape. Duct tape, gaffer tape, whatever adheres well in damp conditions. You will use it to seal every gap between glove and sleeve, boot and pant leg, hood and respirator. The organisms here are small and persistent and they will find every opening.

Under the coverall, wear moisture-wicking base layers. You will sweat. The environment is warm and humid and you are wearing a sealed suit and a respirator. Hyperthermia is a real risk, especially during exertion. Light-colored base layers, synthetic materials, and planned rest breaks in the most ventilated areas you can find.

## Decontamination

You are not just protecting yourself from Óramak. You are also — if you have any ecological conscience — protecting Óramak from you.

This ecosystem has been in dynamic equilibrium for an incomprehensible span of time. You are introducing novel bacteria, novel fungi (your own skin flora), novel chemical compounds (sunscreen, insect repellent, soap residue, synthetic fibers), and your own physical disturbance into a system that has never encountered any of them. The responsible approach:

**On entry:** Minimize what you bring. No organic materials that could introduce foreign organisms. No food (eat before you ascend, or carry sealed rations you consume outside the terrace zone). No plant-based fibers. Sterilize the outer surfaces of your gear with a broad-spectrum antimicrobial — UV exposure, alcohol wipe-down, whatever is practical without introducing its own chemical contamination.

**On exit:** Full decontamination before descending from the plateau. Strip your outer layer and bag it for disposal or sterilization. Wipe down all hard gear surfaces. Shower if water is available; at minimum, change into clean clothing and bag everything that was exposed. Check yourself thoroughly for hitchhikers — mites, insect larvae, fungal colonization of any skin abrasion. Check your respiratory equipment, your boots, every fold and seam.

Carry sealable bags — large ones, contractor-size — for containing contaminated clothing and equipment. Everything that went into the terraces comes out sealed and is dealt with later, not spread across whatever environment you visit next.

## Navigation and Lighting

The terraces are described as being in near-constant dim conditions, navigable primarily by bioluminescent fungal light. This means:

**You need artificial light, but you need to think carefully about what kind.**

Bright white light will do several things, all of them problematic:
- Disturb the birds massively. Large seabird colonies are notorious for panic cascades — one startled bird triggers its neighbors, and within seconds you have thousands of large, powerful birds taking flight simultaneously in an enclosed space. This is dangerous to the birds (eggs and chicks crushed, nests destroyed) and dangerous to you (a wall of panicked birds with one- to two-meter wingspans in a narrow terrace valley).
- Attract the moths in such numbers that you will be effectively blinded by them swarming your light source.
- Destroy your own dark adaptation, making you functionally blind when you turn the light off.

**Red light** is the standard answer for wildlife-sensitive situations, but verify whether these particular species are insensitive to red wavelengths. Many birds can see into the red spectrum. If red is still disruptive, consider:
- **Deep red or infrared illumination** paired with night vision equipment. This is the gold standard for moving through light-sensitive biological environments. IR illuminators and Gen 3+ night vision monoculars or goggles will let you see the terrain without broadcasting visible light.
- The bioluminescent fungal glow itself may provide enough ambient light for navigation once your eyes are fully dark-adapted — which will take 30-45 minutes of zero artificial light exposure. This is the lowest-impact option but also the most challenging for a human trying to navigate alien terrain.

**Regardless of light source, bring redundancy.** At least three independent light sources, stored in waterproof containers, accessible without removing your gloves. Getting caught lightless in the deep terrace valleys — unable to see the terrain, surrounded by noise so dense you can't echolocate by sound, with no landmarks visible — would be genuinely terrifying and potentially dangerous.

**Navigation itself:** There are no trails. The terrain is biological, irregular, and three-dimensional — terrace walls you might need to ascend or descend, tunnel openings that could be mistaken for stable ground, standing pools hidden under vegetation, substrate that varies from rock-hard (old óram) to crumbly and unreliable (fresh deposits). 

Mark your route. Use small, removable, non-toxic markers — UV-reflective tabs visible only under UV light, perhaps, to minimize visual disturbance. Or use a GPS-equivalent if the local multiverse physics supports satellite or inertial navigation. A compass and altimeter at minimum. The terrace ranks follow the plateau contour, so maintaining awareness of upslope vs. downslope direction is your primary orientation tool.

Do not enter the tunnels. I know the description makes them sound fascinating — the deep warrens riddling the terrace walls, the connected chambers like a coral head. Do not enter them. They are structurally unpredictable (biological material, not engineered), they are full of nesting birds that will not appreciate your presence, the air quality inside will be exponentially worse than outside (concentrated ammonia in unventilated spaces can be lethal), and if the óram collapses around you, no one is coming to dig you out of a mountain of compacted guano on an uninhabited plateau in an obscure multiverse location. Observe the tunnel openings from outside. That is sufficient.

## Immunological Preparation

This is the category most travelers underestimate, and at Óramak it could be the difference between an extraordinary experience and a medical catastrophe.

You are entering one of the most biologically dense environments conceivable. The pathogen load — bacteria, fungi, parasites, viruses — in the air, water, and on every surface is extreme. Many of these organisms will be entirely novel to your immune system. Your preparation:

**Before travel:**
- Consult with a travel medicine specialist who has experience with cross-biome or multiverse pathogen exposure (if such a specialist exists in your network; if not, find the closest equivalent — a tropical medicine specialist, an infectious disease physician, someone who understands zoonotic disease).
- **Broad-spectrum antifungal prophylaxis.** Start it before arrival, continue through your visit and for at least two weeks after. The fungal spore load at Óramak is extraordinary, and respiratory mycosis is your highest-probability serious medical threat. Itraconazole or voriconazole, depending on what your physician recommends.
- **Antiparasitic prophylaxis.** The mites, the larvae in the water, the organisms in the soil — any breach in your protective equipment could introduce parasites your body has no defense against. Ivermectin or equivalent, prophylactically.
- **Update all standard vaccinations** and add anything relevant to zoonotic exposure — rabies (bats are present), any avian-associated pathogens your medical advisor identifies.
- Consider starting a **probiotic regimen** to strengthen gut flora resilience, in case of inadvertent ingestion of contaminated water or material.

**Bring with you:**
- Broad-spectrum oral antibiotics (at least two classes — a fluoroquinolone and a macrolide, perhaps).
- Topical and oral antifungals.
- Antiparasitics.
- Antihistamines (the allergic potential of this environment is enormous — novel proteins in the air, insect venoms, fungal antigens).
- Epinephrine auto-injectors. At least two. Anaphylaxis from an encounter with an unfamiliar organism is a real possibility.
- Corticosteroids (oral and injectable) for severe allergic or inflammatory reactions.
- Wound care supplies — any break in your skin in this environment needs immediate aggressive cleaning and coverage. Carry irrigation syringes, antiseptic, sterile dressings, skin glue or sutures.

**After departure:**
- Monitor yourself for at least six weeks. Fungal infections can have long incubation periods. Watch for respiratory symptoms (cough, chest tightness, fever), skin changes (unexplained rashes, nodules, itching), gastrointestinal disturbance, or any unusual symptom.
- Have blood work done at one week and four weeks post-visit. CBC with differential, inflammatory markers, liver and kidney function (both for baseline and because antifungal prophylaxis is hepatotoxic).
- If you develop any respiratory symptom within three months of your visit, tell your physician immediately that you were exposed to a dense novel fungal environment. Do not let them treat you for a common cold while *Óramak aspergillus* or its equivalent quietly colonizes your lungs.

## Psychological Preparation

This deserves serious attention because the description makes clear that Óramak is an environment that assaults every human sense simultaneously and without relief.

**The sound alone can break you.** Not metaphorically. Sustained exposure to overwhelming noise — even noise that is not technically at damaging decibel levels once attenuated by hearing protection — causes psychological effects: anxiety, disorientation, irritability, impaired decision-making, a phenomenon sometimes called "noise fatigue" where the brain simply stops processing auditory input effectively, which can cause a dreamlike or dissociated state. At Óramak, you will be in this noise for the entire duration of your visit. There is no quiet room to retreat to. There is no moment of silence. The noise is the environment.

**The smell will penetrate your respirator eventually**, or you will have moments when the seal shifts, or you will remove it briefly (you shouldn't, but people do), and the ammonia-musk-vegetation smell of the terraces is described as *total* — not a scent but a condition, like humidity. Strong smells trigger powerful emotional responses, often negative ones, often irrational ones. Be prepared for moments of visceral revulsion that have nothing to do with danger.

**The visual density is disorienting.** Everything moves. The birds, the moths, the insects, the dripping water, the faint pulsing of bioluminescent fungi. There are no clean sight lines, no still points for your eyes to rest on. In low-light conditions, with your visual system straining, with particulate matter drifting through your field of vision, with large birds constantly passing across your peripheral vision, your brain will struggle to construct a coherent spatial model of your surroundings. This can cause nausea, anxiety, or a strange claustrophobia-agoraphobia hybrid — the sense that you are simultaneously in a vast open space and a tiny enclosed one.

**The sense of being inside a living system** — of being a foreign body in an organism — is psychologically potent. Some people find it awe-inspiring. Some find it profoundly disturbing. The complete absence of any human-scale reference point, the complete indifference of every organism to your presence (you are neither predator nor prey nor partner; you are irrelevant, an obstacle, a temporary contamination), can trigger an existential discomfort that is hard to articulate. You are nowhere in this food web. You have no role. You are the least important thing on this plateau, and the plateau will remind you of this constantly.

**Prepare by:**
- Practicing sensory tolerance. Spend time in noisy, smelly, visually chaotic environments before your visit. Livestock operations, large aviaries, fish markets, industrial sites. Not because these are equivalent to Óramak — nothing is — but because they calibrate your response.
- Developing and practicing a grounding technique — a method for recentering yourself when the sensory input becomes overwhelming. Breathing exercises are difficult with a respirator. Tactile grounding (focusing on the sensation of your hands inside your gloves, your feet inside your boots) may work better. Have something rehearsed.
- Setting a time limit and sticking to it. Decide before you arrive how long you will stay, and leave when that time comes regardless of how you feel. The temptation to push through discomfort ("I came all this way") is strong, and Óramak will punish you for it.
- Going with a companion if possible. Not just for safety (though that too), but because having another human presence — even one you can't easily speak to through the noise and the respirators — provides an anchor in an environment that is profoundly, almost aggressively, non-human.

## Physical Safety on the Terrain

The óram substrate is treacherous. Where old, it is hard and can be trusted. Where recent, it is friable, chalky, and may crumble under your weight. You cannot always tell the difference visually, especially in dim light. The vegetation between terraces conceals the ground surface — you cannot see what you're stepping on. Standing pools may be shallow or may be deep enough to swallow a leg. The terrace walls are steep and the surfaces are slippery with fresh excrement and moisture.

- **Move slowly.** There is no reason to hurry. The terraces will be there for centuries after you leave. Test every step before committing your weight. Use trekking poles for balance and for probing the ground ahead of you.
- **Do not climb the terrace walls** unless you have verified the structural integrity of the óram at your intended route. Old óram is strong. Fresh deposits over old cavities are not. A handhold that looks solid may be a thin shell over a nesting tunnel, and if it gives way, you fall into a space full of panicked birds with sharp beaks and claws.
- **Stay out of the flight paths.** The birds are large and fast and they have right-of-way. They will not avoid you. A collision with a bird at speed — a one- to two-meter wingspan bird weighing several kilograms, coming in to land from a long flight — can knock you off a ledge. Observe where the main landing and takeoff routes are (they will be obvious from the traffic patterns) and stay clear of them.
- **Be aware of nesting birds at close range.** Large seabirds defending nests are aggressive. They will strike with their beaks, which are designed to catch and hold fish and are entirely capable of puncturing skin through a coverall. They will vomit on you (a common seabird defense that is also a biohazard in this context). They will not retreat. You retreat. Always give nesting birds more space than you think they need.
- **Falling hazard is the primary injury risk.** The terrace ranks are three to five meters tall. Falling from even the lower terraces onto hard óram is a serious trauma mechanism. There are no railings, no defined edges, no warnings. The edges of terrace walls may be undercut by erosion and nesting tunnels, so the lip that looks stable may not be. Stay well back from edges. If moving along the tops of terrace walls, stay on the most central and solid ground you can find.

## Equipment Summary

Consolidating the above into a gear list:

**Respiratory:** Full-face respirator (primary), backup respirator, minimum 6 sets of ammonia/organic vapor + P100 cartridges, anti-fog wipes.

**Hearing:** Custom-molded high-NRR earplugs, over-ear electronic hearing protection, spare batteries.

**Eyes:** Integrated with respirator; backup sealed goggles.

**Body:** Lightweight sealed coveralls (3-4 minimum), chemical-resistant gloves (multiple pairs), sealed rubber/PVC boots, integrated hood, sealing tape.

**Lighting/Navigation:** IR illuminator + night vision device (primary), deep red headlamp (backup), third light source, GPS or inertial navigation, compass, altimeter, UV-reflective route markers.

**Medical:** Antifungal prophylaxis, antiparasitic prophylaxis, broad-spectrum antibiotics (two classes), topical antifungals, antihistamines, epinephrine auto-injectors (2+), corticosteroids, wound irrigation and dressing supplies, trauma first aid kit.

**Navigation aids:** Trekking poles (2, collapsible), rope (30m static, for emergency use in terrace terrain), carabiners, basic climbing harness (for stability, not technical climbing).

**Decontamination:** Contractor bags (10+), antimicrobial wipes, spray antiseptic, change of clothing sealed in waterproof bag (for post-visit).

**Water and food:** Sealed water containers (enough for your planned duration plus 50% margin), sealed ration packs if staying more than a few hours. Do NOT drink any water at Óramak. Do not let the local water contact your skin if avoidable.

**Communication:** Whatever signaling or communication device functions in the local multiverse. A whistle is useless — no one will hear it. Visual signaling (flare, strobe) may work if you have a pre-arranged pickup point and schedule.

## Duration Planning

How long should you stay? As short as possible while still achieving whatever you came for.

The description itself says: *You would not be able to stay long.* Take this at face value. A first visit to Óramak should be measured in hours, not days. Four to six hours of terrace exposure is probably the maximum for an initial visit before sensory fatigue, physical discomfort, and psychological strain begin to seriously impair your judgment and safety.

If your purpose requires a longer stay, plan for staged exposure: hours on the plateau, then retreat to a base camp on the plateau rim (above the terraces, in cleaner air, with some reduction in noise), rest, recover, decontaminate partially, and re-enter. Do not sleep in the terrace valleys. The overnight conditions — peak moth activity, bat activity, reduced visibility, no break from the noise, the full ammonia concentration of the warm night air — are not survivable for unprotected human sleep, and even protected sleep will be nearly impossible.

Base camp location: the western rim, where the description notes the best light and an implied vantage point above the terraces. Here you are still on the plateau but potentially above the worst of the ammonia concentration (which will settle into the valleys as the denser-than-air gas that it is) and may get some wind exposure to clear the air. You will still hear the colony. You will hear the colony from anywhere on the plateau. But it may be attenuated enough to allow rest with hearing protection.

## The Approach

You need to get to a high plateau at two thousand meters, above the lowland plains, below a higher southern ridge. The description implies the local population avoids this place, which means there are no maintained routes, no guides, no support infrastructure. You are planning an expedition to an inaccessible and inhospitable altitude in unknown terrain.

Scout the approach route in advance if possible. Satellite imagery or aerial reconnaissance (from whatever conveyance is available in the local multiverse) will help you identify the most practical route up. The plateau has valleys below it where clouds form — this implies steep terrain, possibly cliff faces. You may need technical climbing skills and equipment for the approach even though the terraces themselves don't require them.

The higher ridge to the south shadows the plateau. If your approach comes from the south, you will be descending from the ridge onto the plateau — potentially the most practical route, as ridgelines often provide the most navigable terrain. If from the north, you are ascending from the lowlands through whatever terrain separates the plains from the plateau edge — and the first thing you will encounter is the full face of the terraces, which is also where the birds are launching and landing. A southern approach is probably preferable.

## What You Came For

After all of this preparation — the respirators and the coveralls and the medication and the tape and the carefully planned hours of exposure — after all the logistics of reaching a high plateau in an unfamiliar world and suiting up and stepping into the noise —

You will be inside one of the most extraordinary things the multiverse has produced. A structure built by no intelligence, designed by no mind, planned by no planner — and yet so vast, so complex, so deeply organized by the accumulated weight of millions of years of small habitual lives that it replaced the mountain it was built on. You are standing inside emergence itself. Inside the proof that complexity needs no author. That architecture needs no architect. That a cathedral can be built by creatures who have never conceived of one, simply by living and dying and living again in the same place, adding their thin layer, each one insignificant, each one part of the mountain.

The frogs will be calling, a bright glassy thread through the roar. The fungal light will be glowing on the walls around you, the color of old copper, the color of something that remembers the sea these birds' ancestors left behind. Moths will drift past your faceplate like warm snow. And the birds — the massive, dark, fish-smelling, ancient, purposeful birds — will wheel overhead in their thousands, ignoring you completely, because you are nothing here, a brief contamination, a visitor in a place that was not made for visitors, that was not made for anything, that was simply made.

Bring your awe. It is the one piece of equipment that requires no decontamination afterward.

## Final Checklist Before Entry

1. Respirator sealed and tested. Cartridges fresh. Backup accessible.
2. Hearing protection in place and functioning. Electronic muffs powered on.
3. Coverall sealed at all junctions. Gloves taped. Boots taped. Hood secured.
4. Navigation device calibrated. Route markers ready. Light sources tested.
5. Medical kit on your person (not in a pack you might set down and lose).
6. Time limit agreed upon and non-negotiable.
7. Companion briefed on emergency signals (visual, since audio is useless).
8. Decontamination supplies staged at your exit point.
9. Return route confirmed.
10. You have accepted, fully and without reservation, that this place does not need you, does not want you, will not remember you, and is more beautiful for all of that.

Go.
