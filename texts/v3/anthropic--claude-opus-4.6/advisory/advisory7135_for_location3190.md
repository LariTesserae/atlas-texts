# Preparing to Visit the Hanging Gardens of the Upwelled Sea

---

## Before You Go: Understanding What You're Walking Into

Let me be direct with you. This is not a hostile place. Nothing here will try to kill you. But it is a place that will, with absolute patience and zero malice, dissolve every assumption you carry about comfort, dryness, personal space, the boundary between inside and outside, and the speed at which meaningful things should happen. You need to prepare not for danger but for *saturation* — literal and metaphorical. You are going to a place that is wet in the way that a desert is dry: not as a condition but as an identity.

Read the full description multiple times before you pack. Then read it again after you've packed, because you will have packed wrong. Everyone packs wrong the first time.

---

## The Journey In: Altitude and Approach

You will be ascending to roughly seven thousand feet. Depending on which variant of this world you're entering from and what the base elevation of the surrounding terrain is, this may mean a significant climb from lowland staging areas, or it may mean a relatively modest ascent from an already-elevated plateau. Either way, plan for altitude.

**Altitude acclimatization** is not optional. Spend at least two to three days at intermediate elevation before attempting the final ascent to the caldera rim. The settlement itself sits at an altitude where oxygen partial pressure is meaningfully reduced — not dangerously so for healthy individuals, but enough that you will feel it. Your breathing will be slightly labored. Your heart rate will be elevated. You will tire more quickly than expected. And here's the compounding factor: the air inside the caldera is so moisture-laden that breathing feels *thick*. You are essentially breathing a suspension of water droplets at altitude. Some travelers describe a mild sensation of drowning that never quite materializes into actual respiratory distress but never quite goes away either, especially in the first few days. This is psychologically taxing even when it's not physiologically dangerous.

If you have any respiratory conditions — asthma, chronic bronchitis, anything that compromises lung function — consult a physician experienced in altitude medicine and humid environments before committing. Bring a bronchodilator inhaler even if you don't normally use one. The combination of altitude, cold, and constant moisture in the airways can trigger bronchospasm in people who've never experienced it before.

**The approach route** will matter. There is likely no single well-marked trail. The caldera's broken rim provides the obvious entrance point — the collapsed flank — but the terrain approaching it will be steep, heavily vegetated, and increasingly waterlogged as you ascend. Expect the last several hours of the approach to involve wading through saturated ground, navigating small streams that appear and disappear, and pushing through increasingly dense vegetation. The tree line doesn't thin out here the way it does on normal mountains — the constant moisture sustains growth well above typical elevations. You will be in forest, then in denser forest, then in forest that is more water than forest, and then you will realize you are already inside the settlement's outer edges and have been for some time.

Hire a guide from whatever community exists at the mountain's base, if one exists. If not, look for the water. Follow it upward. It all leads to the caldera eventually.

## Clothing: The Central Challenge

This is the single most important category and the one where most travelers fail catastrophically. Let me walk you through the logic.

**The environment is permanently wet and cool.** Not cold enough to kill you from hypothermia under normal circumstances, but cold enough — combined with the constant moisture — to make you miserable, to sap your energy, to prevent you from sleeping well, and to degrade your health over any visit longer than a few days if you are not properly equipped.

**Cotton will destroy you.** I cannot say this emphatically enough. Do not bring cotton clothing. Not cotton underwear, not cotton socks, not a cotton t-shirt "just for sleeping in." Cotton absorbs water, holds it against your skin, loses all insulating value when wet, and takes forever to dry in an environment where nothing ever dries. You will be cold, you will stay cold, and the cotton will mildew within 48 hours and begin to smell like a compost heap. Everything you've ever been told about cotton in wet environments applies here, multiplied by the fact that *there is no dry place to retreat to.* There is no cabin with a roaring fire where you can hang your clothes to dry. The fire-keeps exist but are communal, intermittent, and not designed for drying outsider laundry.

**Wool is your primary fiber.** Merino wool base layers — tops and bottoms — in medium weight. Wool insulates when wet, resists odor, and has natural antimicrobial properties that will slow (though not prevent) the growth of mold. Bring at least three full sets of base layers so you can rotate them. Yes, they will all be wet. The goal is not to have dry clothes. The goal is to have clothes that are wet and still functional.

**Synthetic insulation over the wool.** A lightweight fleece or synthetic-fill vest or jacket. Not down. Down is useless when wet — it clumps, loses loft, and becomes a sodden weight on your shoulders. Synthetic insulation retains warmth when damp. You will be wearing this most of the time.

**An outer shell that breathes.** Here is where it gets complicated. A waterproof shell — your standard rain jacket — will keep external water off you but will trap your own moisture inside, since the air is already at or near 100% humidity and the shell's breathability membrane cannot function when there's no moisture gradient to drive vapor transfer. You will be wet inside the jacket from your own sweat and breath. However, a shell still has value: it blocks wind (there will be some, particularly near the caldera rim and in the upper canopy), it provides a barrier against the dripping water from above (being dripped on continuously is psychologically different from being merely damp), and it gives you a layer you can remove to regulate temperature.

Bring a high-quality waterproof-breathable shell and accept that "waterproof" here means "directs water in a managed way" rather than "keeps you dry."

**A lightweight, compressible puffy jacket — synthetic fill — for sleeping.** This lives in a dry bag (see below) during the day and comes out only when you're in whatever sleeping arrangement you've managed. It will be the driest thing you own and it will feel like salvation.

**Footwear.** This deserves its own extended discussion.

Do NOT bring leather boots. They will be waterlogged within hours, will never dry, and will begin to rot and fall apart within a week. Do not bring standard hiking boots of any kind unless they are fully synthetic and designed for wet conditions.

Your best options, in order of preference:

1. **Neoprene wetsuit booties with a solid sole** — the kind used for kayaking or canyoneering. These are designed to function while submerged, provide thermal insulation even in cold water, and drain quickly. They will not protect your feet from sharp roots or calcified stone as well as a boot would, so choose ones with the thickest, stiffest sole you can find. Some canyoneering boots have Vibram-type soles and would be ideal.

2. **Synthetic trail-running shoes** with mesh uppers that drain freely, paired with neoprene socks. The shoes will fill with water constantly; the point is that they drain and don't hold it. The neoprene socks keep your feet warm. This combination works well on the upper levels of the settlement where you're walking on root-mat floors and packed surfaces. It's less ideal for the submerged lower levels, where neoprene booties are superior.

3. **Bring both.** Wear the trail runners for daily movement in the upper settlement; switch to neoprene booties for any excursion into the flooded lower levels. This is what I'd recommend.

**Socks:** Wool socks over neoprene liners if you're using regular footwear. Or just neoprene socks. Do not bring cotton socks. Your feet will be wet for the duration of your visit. Trench foot — maceration of the skin from prolonged water exposure — is a real risk. Inspect your feet every evening. Dry them as thoroughly as possible (they won't be truly dry, but wring out your socks and let your feet air for as long as you can stand the cold). Apply a moisture-barrier foot cream — something with zinc oxide or a lanolin base — every night and morning. Bring enough to last your entire stay.

**Consider the local clothing.** The moss-cotton garments worn by residents are specifically evolved for this environment. If you can acquire one — through trade, through gift, through whatever relationship-building you manage — it may be worth wearing, at least experimentally. But understand: this is a living textile that requires maintenance. It must be soaked periodically. It grows on you, in a sense. It may not be comfortable for an outsider's skin, and the cultural implications of wearing local dress as a visitor are something you'll need to navigate with sensitivity. Ask before you adopt. Some communities welcome it as a sign of respect. Others find it presumptuous.

**Head covering.** A wool or synthetic beanie for warmth. A wide-brimmed waterproof hat for keeping dripping water out of your eyes while navigating the settlement. The constant dripping from above is, reportedly, one of the most maddening aspects of the first few days. A hat helps.

## Gear: What to Bring, What to Leave Behind

**Dry bags are the most important equipment you will carry.** Everything — *everything* — that you want to keep dry goes in a dry bag. Not a "water-resistant stuff sack." A proper, roll-top, welded-seam dry bag. Bring multiple sizes. Your sleeping insulation lives in one. Your journal and any paper (if you're foolish enough to bring paper — see below) live in one. Your food that isn't locally sourced lives in one. Small items — fire-starting supplies, first aid, trade goods — each get their own.

Test your dry bags before departure by submerging them in a bathtub. If they leak, replace them. You will be in an environment where ambient humidity approaches 100% and where you may at any point need to wade or swim through chest-deep water to reach a different part of the settlement.

**Sleeping system.** This is critical and there is no perfect solution.

You will not be given a bed in the Western sense. Residents sleep on root-mat platforms, slightly elevated, in alcoves within the living architecture. They are accustomed to sleeping damp. You are not.

Bring a **synthetic sleeping bag** rated to at least 35°F (2°C), preferably lower. Not down. The bag will get damp — there's no preventing it — but synthetic fill retains insulation when wet, and if you're disciplined about keeping it in a dry bag during the day, it will start each night in the best condition possible.

A **closed-cell foam sleeping pad** is superior to an inflatable here. Inflatables can puncture on the uneven root-mat floors, and the repair process is compromised by the constant moisture. Closed-cell foam is impervious to water, provides insulation from the cool root-mat surface, and is indestructible. It's bulkier to carry in, but worth it.

A **bivy sack** — a waterproof/breathable outer shell for your sleeping bag — adds significant comfort by creating a micro-barrier between you and the ambient dripping. It won't keep you dry in any absolute sense, but it will keep the worst of the ambient moisture from soaking directly into your sleeping bag's outer shell.

Expect to sleep cold and damp for the first several nights regardless of preparation. Your body will acclimate somewhat. The local people sleep communally in part for warmth; if you develop sufficient trust and relationship, being invited to sleep in a family's alcove near other bodies is a significant improvement in comfort and a significant social milestone.

**Light sources.** The interior of the root-architecture is dim. The greenish-silver light filtering through moss-curtains is beautiful but insufficient for detailed tasks. The submerged lower levels are dark.

- Bring a **waterproof headlamp** — IPX8 rated, submersible — with multiple brightness settings and a red-light mode. Bring at least twice as many batteries as you think you'll need, stored in a dry bag.
- Bring a small **chemical light stick** supply as a backup — these are waterproof by nature and can be clipped to your person when navigating flooded corridors.
- Be judicious with artificial light. The local population navigates much of the settlement by feel, by memory, by the ambient bioluminescence of certain algae and fungi that grow on the deeper root surfaces. Blinding someone with a headlamp in a dim root-corridor is the equivalent of shouting in someone's ear. Use the lowest effective setting. Use red light when possible. Learn to move in the dark.

**Water treatment.** The irony of being surrounded by water and needing to treat it. The upwelling spring water is mineral-rich and likely safe, but "likely" isn't a word you want to stake your intestinal health on. The iron and calcium content is high enough to taste and may cause mild gastrointestinal upset even if the water is pathogen-free. The standing pools in the settlement are biologically active — full of frog eggs, snail trails, eel waste, and microbial life. The submerged lower levels, while cold and reportedly somewhat anoxic, are an unknown quantity in terms of microbial safety.

Bring a **gravity-feed water filter** with a ceramic or hollow-fiber element rated for bacteria and protozoa. A chemical backup (chlorine dioxide drops or tablets) for when the filter is impractical. The filter will clog frequently in this sediment-rich water; bring replacement elements or a cleaning kit. Drink the filtered upwelling spring water preferentially. Ask your hosts which water sources they drink from — their microbiome is adapted to local organisms, but their source preferences will still tell you which water is cleanest.

**Food.** Bring some calorie-dense, moisture-proof provisions for your first few days: nut butters in sealed containers, hard cheeses sealed in wax, dense seed-and-honey bars, dried meat or fish if it's well-sealed. Do not bring crackers, bread, dried fruit in permeable packaging, or anything that will absorb moisture and become inedible.

Plan to transition to local food as quickly as possible. This means crabs, eels (smoked or fresh), various aquatic plants, and whatever starchy root or tuber crops are cultivated in the pool-terraces. The diet here is high in protein from the crabs and eels, rich in micronutrients from the mineral-laden water and aquatic vegetation, and likely low in the variety of carbohydrates you're accustomed to. You may experience digestive adjustment. Bring a probiotic supplement if you're sensitive.

**The crab pools are private property.** I want to emphasize this because the description makes clear that crab pools represent family wealth. Do not take crabs from a pool. Do not reach into a pool without permission. Do not let curiosity overcome respect. You wouldn't reach into someone's wallet. Same principle.

**Fire-starting supplies.** Bring a ferro rod and striker, waterproof matches in a sealed container, and a small supply of petroleum-jelly-soaked cotton balls (the one acceptable use of cotton on this trip) as tinder. You will probably not need to start a fire — the communal fire-keeps serve this purpose — but if you need to create warmth in an emergency, having reliable ignition in a wet environment is critical. Standard lighters are unreliable in high humidity. The ferro rod always works.

**No paper. No books. No paper journal.** I know. I'm sorry. Paper will be pulp within 48 hours. If you must write — and you should, because this place deserves documentation — use one of the following:
- A pencil (not pen — ink runs) on waterproof field-notes paper (Rite in the Rain or equivalent). This works but is bulky.
- A small waterproof tablet or device in a submersible case. Electronic devices are viable if properly sealed, but charging will be impossible, so bring a high-capacity portable battery and keep it in a dry bag.
- Learn the knotwork. I'm serious. If you stay long enough, begin learning the knotted recording system. It's appropriate to the environment, it's culturally engaging, and it will survive conditions that would destroy any technology you bring.

## Medical Preparation

**Vaccinations and prophylaxis.** Impossible to specify precisely without knowing the pathogen ecology of this particular multiverse variant, but prepare broadly:
- Ensure standard travel vaccinations are current — tetanus especially, given the likelihood of minor puncture wounds from root-splinters and shell fragments.
- Fungal prophylaxis. This is the big one. You are going to be wet, continuously, in a biologically rich warm-moist environment. Fungal skin infections are not a risk; they are a certainty if you don't prepare. Bring a broad-spectrum topical antifungal (clotrimazole, miconazole, or terbinafine cream) and apply it to your feet, groin, underarms, and any skin folds every single day. Bring an oral antifungal (fluconazole or itraconazole) as a backup if a topical-resistant infection develops. A full course, not just a few pills.
- Anti-parasitic medication. The water is full of life. Some of that life may want to live in you. Bring a broad-spectrum anti-helminthic (albendazole) and a course of anti-protozoal medication (metronidazole). Use prophylactically if you're going to be wading in the submerged levels regularly.

**Wound care.** Any break in your skin — a scratch from a root, a cut from a shell edge, a blister from your wet boots — is an infection vector in this environment. Carry:
- Waterproof adhesive bandages and wound-closure strips
- Antiseptic solution (povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine)
- Antibiotic ointment
- Waterproof medical tape
- A course of broad-spectrum oral antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate or a fluoroquinolone) for wound infections that don't respond to topical treatment

Treat every wound immediately, no matter how minor. Clean it, apply antiseptic, cover it with a waterproof dressing. Check it twice daily. In this environment, a neglected scratch can become cellulitis within 48 hours.

**Hypothermia awareness.** You probably won't experience acute hypothermia, but chronic mild hypothermia — a persistent lowering of core temperature from constant wet-cold exposure — is insidious. Symptoms include persistent fatigue, difficulty concentrating, reduced appetite, and a kind of dull acceptance that you mistake for calm. Monitor yourself. If you find you've stopped shivering (shivering is your body trying to warm you — when it stops, either you've warmed up or your body has given up trying), that's a warning sign. Eat calorie-dense food regularly. Stay as active as is culturally appropriate. Use your dry sleeping bag aggressively.

**Ear care.** Your ear canals will be wet constantly. Swimmer's ear (otitis externa) is almost guaranteed without prevention. Bring a drying solution — equal parts white vinegar and rubbing alcohol — and apply a few drops to each ear canal after any submersion and every evening. If you develop ear pain, bring ciprofloxacin ear drops.

**Eye care.** If you wear contact lenses, switch to glasses for this trip. The microbial load in the ambient water makes contact-lens-associated eye infections a serious risk. Bring waterproof eyeglass retainers so you don't lose your glasses in a submerged corridor.

## Social Preparation: How to Be a Good Guest in a Slow Place

This may be more important than all the physical preparation combined.

**Time.** You need to bring enough of it. Do not visit the Hanging Gardens for a weekend. Do not visit for a week if you can help it. The minimum visit that will allow you to begin understanding this place — to move past the initial overwhelm of the wet and the noise and the green and the disorientation — is two weeks. A month is better. The locals operate on the timescale of root-growth; a visitor who arrives and leaves within the time it takes a new root to visibly change direction is, to them, barely distinguishable from a raindrop.

If you can only visit briefly, be honest about this. Explain that you are passing through. Do not pretend to a depth of engagement you cannot sustain. Short visits are not disrespectful if they are transparent.

**Gifts and trade goods.** Bring things that are useful here and scarce here. Based on the description, the following are likely to be valued:
- **Good cutting edges.** Metal is rare. A high-quality steel knife — not stainless, which is harder to resharpen with available stones, but high-carbon steel that takes and holds an edge — would be an extraordinary gift. Bring several smaller blades rather than one large one. Folding knives, utility blades, wood-carving tools.
- **Cordage.** They make their own, but if you can bring superior synthetic cord — paracord, for instance, which is rot-proof, strong, and available in bulk — it would likely be welcomed, though it may also be seen as an implicit criticism of their own fiber-craft. Read the room. Offer it as a supplement, not a replacement.
- **Needles.** Fine metal needles for fiber-work. Steel, with eyes large enough for their moss-cotton thread. These are small, light, incredibly useful, and almost certainly impossible to manufacture locally.
- **Pigments and dyes.** In a world where almost everything is greenish-gray, color may be valued. Bring small quantities of mineral pigments — ochre, cinnabar if available, lapis-derived blue. These are durable, non-organic (won't mold), and culturally resonant across many settlements.
- **Salt.** If it's not readily available at this altitude, salt is always valuable. Bring it in sealed waterproof containers.
- **Stories.** Seriously. In a small, stable, slow-changing community, novelty is rare and information from outside is precious. Your stories about other places — other variants, other ways of living — may be the most valued thing you bring. But be prepared to *tell* them, orally, in the way stories are told here. They don't have books. They have voices, memory, and knotted records. Practice narrating your experiences aloud before you go. Get comfortable with it. Expect to tell the same story multiple times to different audiences.

**Do not bring:**
- Plastic trinkets or objects with no clear function
- Technology you intend to demonstrate as if it's magic. These people are sophisticated. They've built a living city. They'll be curious about your devices but they won't worship them, and performing wonder for an audience is a poor foundation for relationship.
- Alcohol unless you know it's culturally welcome. In a closed community of two hundred people, introducing intoxicants without understanding local norms is irresponsible.

**Communication.** You will almost certainly not speak the local language. Prepare for an extended period of gestural communication, patient misunderstanding, and gradual language acquisition. Bring no translation technology — there won't be a database for this language. Instead, bring patience and a willingness to look foolish. Point at things. Repeat words. Laugh at your own errors. Children will be your best language teachers; they find your incompetence hilarious rather than threatening, and they have time.

Learn to read the knotted cords as early as possible. Even basic comprehension — recognizing the knot-sequences for family names, for "visitor," for "welcome" — will dramatically improve your social integration.

**Physical comfort expectations.** Reset them to zero. You will be wet. You will be cold. You will be sleeping on a living root-mat that gently moves beneath you as the tree grows. You will hear a thousand frogs all night. Dripping water will become the background radiation of your existence. The food will be unfamiliar. The toileting situation — unaddressed in the description but inevitable in life — will involve some combination of designated pools, composting niches in the root-architecture, and accepted outdoor practices that you will need to learn by observation.

Accept all of this before you go. If you arrive expecting to suffer, you will suffer. If you arrive expecting to *adapt*, you will adapt, and the adaptation itself will be one of the most interesting things that happens to you.

**The submerged levels.** You will want to go down there. The description makes clear that the flooded lower levels are integral to the settlement — they contain granaries, preserved food stores, the place where the dead are laid. They are also a navigation challenge in dark, cold, chest-deep water with passages that must be navigated by feel.

Do not go alone. Do not go without a local guide. Do not go until you've been in the settlement long enough that someone trusts you enough to take you. When you do go, bring your submersible headlamp on its lowest setting, move slowly, and touch the calcified root walls gently — they are, in a sense, the oldest architecture in the settlement, and they are also becoming the skeleton of the mountain itself. There is something down there that is worth your respect: the submerged structure, the preserved dead, the deep creature.

**The deep creature.** You will probably not see it. Almost no one does. But you may hear it breathe — a bellows-sound in the dark water — and you may notice the sudden silence of the frogs in a spreading ring around its location. Do not seek it out. Do not dive into the deep caldera water looking for it. It is not dangerous, by all accounts, but it is also not yours to find. It finds you, or it doesn't. The locals' respect for it is total and unsentimental, the way you might respect a geological formation. It is old. It is part of the place. Leave it alone.

## Psychological Preparation: What This Place Will Do to Your Mind

I want to address this directly because it's the dimension most travelers underestimate.

**The noise.** The description is accurate: visitors find the water-and-frog soundscape deafening at first, then soothing, then invisible. But the transition from "deafening" to "soothing" can take several days, and during that time your sleep will be severely disrupted. Bring earplugs — silicone swimmer's earplugs, not foam, which will disintegrate in the moisture. Use them for sleeping during the first week. Then try a night without them. You may find you've already stopped hearing it.

**The green.** Everything is green. The light is green. Your clothes will become green. Your skin will take on a green cast. After several days, you may find that your visual system begins to recalibrate — that colors other than green become startlingly vivid when you encounter them. The orange of a fire in the fire-keep, the pale cream of a snail shell, the translucent red of a crab claw — these will seem impossibly bright, almost hallucinatory. This is normal. Your eyes are starved for chromatic diversity. This is, incidentally, one reason why pigment dyes make such good trade goods.

**The pace.** This is the hardest thing. You come from a world — whatever world you come from — where things happen in hours, days, weeks. Here, things happen in years. Decades. The room being grown at the east edge of the settlement was started before you were born and won't be finished after you die. The dispute over the root-direction near the southern pool-terrace has been ongoing for longer than some of the disputants have been alive, and no one is upset about this; it is simply the pace at which root-disputes resolve.

You will feel, at first, that nothing is happening. You will be wrong. Everything is happening. It's happening at the speed of wood.

This will either drive you to restless frustration or it will crack something open in you — some assumption about the necessary speed of a meaningful life — and you will begin to see the deep, patient intelligence of a civilization that builds in living wood and measures progress in rings of growth. Either outcome is legitimate. But if you find yourself in the frustration state, do not try to speed things up. Do not suggest improvements. Do not explain how things could be more efficient. They know. They've chosen this. The pace is the point.

**The intimacy.** Two hundred people in a small, enclosed, wet space. Everyone knows everyone. Everyone hears everyone — sound carries through the root-architecture in unpredictable ways, and the concept of acoustic privacy essentially does not exist. You will be known immediately. Your presence will be noted, discussed, evaluated. Your habits will be observed. The quality of your knots — if you attempt knotwork — will be appraised. The way you treat the crabs, the eels, the deep water, the calcified roots, will all be noted.

This level of social visibility is suffocating for some travelers and deeply comforting for others. Know which you are, and prepare accordingly. If you need solitude, identify the edges of the settlement — the places where the root-architecture thins and the raw caldera rock is exposed — and establish an early habit of retreating there. The locals will understand. Some of them do it too.

**The thing that gets to you.** Every traveler reports a moment — usually around day four or five — when the place *gets* to them. It's different for everyone. For some, it's the first time they navigate a flooded corridor by feel and surface in a chamber they haven't seen before, gasping, and find it full of pale calcified roots covered in bioluminescent algae, glowing faintly green-blue in the dark, and they realize they are inside a living cathedral that is slowly becoming stone. For some, it's hearing the deep creature breathe for the first time, and the silence that follows. For some, it's something small — the way a snail moves across a root-wall, leaving a clean trail in the algae, and they realize they've been watching it for twenty minutes and that nothing in their life has ever demanded that kind of attention before.

Be open to this moment. Don't chase it. It will find you, like the deep creature, or it won't. But if you've prepared well — if you're warm enough, if your feet are cared for, if your belly is full of strange crab-meat and mineral water, if you've been patient and respectful and willing to be uncomfortable — then you'll be in a state to receive it when it comes.

## What to Do When You Leave

Dry out. Slowly. Descending from the caldera back to lower, drier elevations will feel like stepping into a different world. The air will feel thin in a new way — not oxygen-thin but *moisture*-thin, as if something has been subtracted. Your skin will feel tight and papery. You will miss the sound of the frogs with a physical ache, the way you miss a heartbeat that has stopped.

Inspect all your gear for biological hitchhikers. Spores, seeds, larvae, snail eggs, the tendrils of moss-cotton that may have begun colonizing your wool base layers. Remove what you can. Be responsible about what you carry back to other variants. This ecosystem is compressed and specific; its organisms in a different context could be invasive.

Check your feet thoroughly. If there's any sign of persistent maceration, fungal infection, or wound that hasn't healed, seek medical attention before moving on to your next destination. Your immune system has been under constant low-level challenge.

Eat something dry. Bread, crackers, anything that crunches. You won't realize how much you've missed the sensation until you hear it.

And then — if you've stayed long enough, if the place has gotten to you — sit somewhere dry and warm and listen to the silence. Or rather, listen to what passes for silence in a world that isn't constantly singing with water and frogs and creaking wood and the slow breath of something old and large in the deep.

You'll find the silence is very loud.
