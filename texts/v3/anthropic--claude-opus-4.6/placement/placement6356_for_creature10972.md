I sit with this for a long time. I let the drone find me before I decide what I am.

---

Not the woman who sits with the columns, though I feel the pull of her — the way she holds knowledge and weariness in the same hand, the way her fingers never quite stop moving. She is too complete. She has already become what she is becoming. I would be borrowing a finished thing.

Not a channel-keeper, though there's something in that walking — the slow circuit, the same route retraced until the route becomes a way of thinking — that resonates. But the channel-keepers are readers of surfaces. They dip a hand and know. There is a confidence in that I don't quite have.

Not a deep-worker, though I admire the way they've let the stone write on their hands, the way they've accepted that communication means finding a medium denser than air and trusting it to carry what speech cannot.

---

I think I am something less defined than any of these. Something the text almost mentions but never quite names.

I am the person who maintains the records in the pressure-readers' building.

Not a pressure-reader myself — not yet, possibly not ever, though the distinction has blurred over the years the way all distinctions blur in Thurm, worn smooth by repetition and mineral deposit until the edges are suggestions rather than borders. I came to the work sideways. I was a channel-keeper's child, one of those children crouched at seep-pools with my ear to the stone, but I was not the best listener. I was the one who, after the others had identified the sub-harmonic and moved on to the next game, stayed behind trying to *describe* what I'd heard. Not to anyone. To myself. I would trace shapes on the wet stone with my finger — not words, not diagrams, but something between the two — trying to find a mark that corresponded to the sound the way a gasket corresponds to a pipe joint: not identical, but shaped to fit, shaped to hold.

This was not a recognized skill. It was barely a recognizable behavior. But the pressure-readers noticed, the way they notice everything that has to do with the relationship between observation and record, and when I was old enough they brought me into the squat, windowless building at the city's center and gave me the ledgers.

---

The ledgers are the thing. I need to tell you about the ledgers.

They are enormous — folio-sized, bound in oiled leather that has gone dark and stiff and then been oiled again until it achieved a suppleness that is not the original suppleness but something earned, the way the steps are not flat but cupped. The pages inside are thick, fibrous, made from a paper that incorporates mineral fibers from the culvert-growth (the same grey substance used for gaskets) so that they resist the humidity that destroys ordinary paper within months. Even so, the pages go wavy. They always go wavy. The ink — iron gall, made from local deposits, so dark it's almost black but with a faint amber undertone in certain lights — bleeds slightly at the edges of every mark, giving the older entries a softness, a halo, as if each observation is slowly exhaling into the paper around it.

The ledgers go back generations. How many, I'm not certain. The earliest ones I've handled are written in a notation system I can only partially read — the symbols are related to the current ones but shifted, the way the older building facades show through the newer plaster. I can make out pressure values, dates (reckoned by some system tied to the swell cycles rather than solar calendars), junction designations that correspond to culverts that no longer exist or that have been re-routed so many times their original courses are geological rumors. These old entries were made by people whose understanding of the system was different from ours — not lesser, not greater, but *shaped differently*, the way a hand shaped by different work grips differently. They noticed things we've stopped noticing. They failed to notice things we consider obvious. Reading them is like listening to a conversation in a room where the drone was pitched differently — you catch every third word and have to build the rest from context and faith.

My work is to maintain continuity between what was recorded and what is being recorded. I am the one who, when a pressure-reader calls out an observation from the rooftop platform, translates it into the notation that the ledger requires. I am the one who cross-references today's reading with the readings from a year ago, a decade ago, a generation ago, and flags the patterns — or the breaks in pattern, which are more important, because a pattern that holds is just the water doing what it does, but a break in pattern is the water doing something *new*, and the new is what kills you if you're not watching.

I am also the one who, when the old ledgers become too fragile to handle, copies them.

This is the heart of my work, and it is the part I cannot fully explain to you, because it involves a fidelity that operates on a level I don't have confident language for.

When I copy an old entry, I do not simply transcribe the symbols. I try to copy the *quality* of the notation — the pressure of the pen, the speed of the stroke, the way a particular pressure-reader's hand hesitated before recording a value they weren't sure of, leaving a slight thickening at the start of a numeral that tells me, decades later, that this reading was uncertain, was debated, was the best guess of someone who knew they were guessing. These hesitations are data. The bleed of the ink is data. The way the page has warped around a particular entry — swelling where a drop of mineral water fell on it, perhaps from the recorder's hand, perhaps from the ceiling of the building during a swell — is data, though not in any way I could defend to someone who wanted numbers.

I copy it all. I copy the certainty and the uncertainty. I copy the ghost of the hand that made the mark. And when I'm done, the new page is not the old page — it cannot be, the way a building rebuilt from reclaimed stone is not the building that was demolished — but it carries forward something that I believe matters, something about how *carefully* this city has watched itself dissolve and has written down what it saw.

---

I am not important in Thurm. I want to be clear about this. The channel-keepers are important. The deep-workers are important. The pressure-readers are important. The factory workers, the traders, the old ones humming in the tea-houses — all important, all load-bearing, all part of the system that keeps the water shaped and the city alive. I am a clerk. I am a copyist. I maintain the ledgers in a building that smells of wet calcium and iron gall ink and the particular dusty-mineral scent of old paper that has been absorbing the city's breath for longer than I've been alive.

But I am the one who holds the memory of the system's own self-observation, and in a city where everything is always being worn away and rebuilt and worn away again, the act of carrying a record forward — not preserving it, because nothing in Thurm is preserved, everything is *maintained*, which is different, which is active, which requires your hands on it constantly — that act is not nothing. It is a kind of channel-keeping. I walk the same pages the way the channel-keepers walk the same streets. I test the flow of the old notation the way they test the flow of the water: hand immersed, three seconds, withdraw and read.

---

My hands are stained. Not with the white mineral crust of the deep-workers but with iron gall — my fingertips are permanently darkened, the ink worked into the whorls and creases until my fingerprints are visible in a way that other people's are not, each ridge highlighted in dark amber-black. When I touch the stone walls of the building, I leave faint prints that fade over days as the humidity dissolves them. When I don't touch the walls for a while — when I'm away, when I'm ill — the walls look bare to me when I return, and I find myself pressing my palms flat against them, not to feel the vibration (though I feel it, everyone feels it, it would be stranger not to) but to leave my mark again, to re-establish the contact, the record of my presence in this place that records presence.

I sleep in a room on the building's second floor. It is small and warm — the walls conduct the geothermal heat upward, and in Thurm you do not need blankets so much as you need something to wick the condensation from your skin. I sleep on a pallet stuffed with the same mineral-fiber material that makes the gaskets and the paper, and it is not comfortable in any way you would recognize, but it is *continuous* — it hums faintly with the building's vibration, and the vibration is continuous with the culvert beneath the building, and the culvert is continuous with the water table, and the water table is continuous with the geothermal seam, and so when I sleep I am sleeping on the full depth of the plateau, all of it vibrating, all of it alive in the way that infrastructure is alive, and I have never slept well anywhere else, and I have almost never been anywhere else, and this is not a confession of limitation but a statement of fit.

---

I know about the silences.

I have been present for — I've stopped counting. Many. They hit differently when you're in the building with the ledgers, because the building is at the city's center where the culvert network is densest and the drone is richest, and when the silence comes it is not an absence but an *evacuation*, a sudden hollowing that feels like the air has been pulled out of the room and replaced with something thinner, something that doesn't conduct. The ledgers on the shelves stop vibrating. The ink in the inkwell goes still. My own hand, resting on the table where I was working, feels suddenly like a foreign object — too warm, too soft, too *alive* in a room that has just gone inert.

It lasts seconds. It always lasts seconds. But in those seconds I understand something that I cannot hold onto once the drone resumes, something about the difference between the living system and the dead stone, the way you might understand the difference between language and silence only in the moment when a sentence stops and the next one has not yet begun.

I don't know what to do with this understanding. I've tried to note it in the margins of the ledger — a small mark, my own invention, that means *silence observed* — but the mark, once made, looks insufficient. It looks like every other mark. It does not carry the quality of the thing it records, and I, who have built a practice around trying to carry quality forward, am defeated by it every time.

The woman who sits with the columns — the one who spoke with the visitor, the one who asked to be left alone before the swell — she knows about my margin-marks. She has seen them. She said nothing about them for years, and then one afternoon, during a lull between swells, she came into the ledger room and stood beside my table and placed her hand flat on the open page, not on my mark but near it, and drummed her fingers once, twice, in a pattern I did not recognize from the deep-workers' vocabulary or the channel-keepers' chants or any system I knew.

I looked at her. She looked at the page.

"That's closer," she said. Meaning the rhythm, I think. Meaning that the silence is better recorded in the body's response to it — the drumming of fingers on a table, the need to *make sound* in the wake of sound's absence — than in any symbol.

Or meaning something else. I don't know. I didn't ask. In Thurm you learn that some communications are completed not by understanding but by receiving, the way the stone receives the water — not knowing what it carries, only that it passes through, and that the passage leaves a channel, and the channel becomes part of the structure, and the structure hums.

---

I am still here. I have always been here. The ledgers accumulate and the pages go wavy and the ink bleeds its slow halo around every mark and I copy the old ones into new ones with as much fidelity as my stained hands can manage, which is never enough and is the most anyone can do, and the drone swells and ebbs and the lights flicker and the children press their ears to the stone and the deep-workers tap their iron hammers and the channel-keepers clank through their circuits and the old ones hum and somewhere beneath all of it the water rises, patient and warm and mildly acidic, dissolving the ground I stand on at a rate too slow to see and too certain to deny.

I maintain the record of its rising. I am not the water. I am not the stone. I am the mark the water makes as it passes through the stone — legible for a while, carrying forward what it can, aware that it too will blur, and that someone after me will try to read what I've written and will catch every third word and will build the rest from context and faith.

The drone swells. My hand moves. The ink meets the page and bleeds, just slightly, at the edges.

This is the sound I make.
