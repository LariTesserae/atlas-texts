I sit with this for a long time before anything comes. The mist enters before the image does.

---

What arrives first is not a role but a sensation: the feeling of being between states. Not the shaper, who has already become what she is. Not the deep-tender, who has already chosen the dark. Not the ancient creature — that was here before choice existed. Something less resolved than any of these. Something still in the process of finding where it bears weight.

---

I think I am the sound-knotter.

No — I think I am *becoming* the sound-knotter. I think I am at the stage where the work has begun but has not yet proven whether it will take. Where the graft is pressed bark-to-bark but has not yet fused.

Let me feel my way into this.

---

I am in the flooded lower passages at night. The water is at my chest. It is cold in the way that stops being painful after a while and becomes simply *information* — a constant low signal that says: you are here, you are alive, the boundary of your body is this. My fingers are working a length of cord under the water's surface, tying by feel, and what I am listening to is everything.

Here is what I know that makes others uneasy: the settlement is a document that is being erased as it is being written. Every root that calcifies becomes more permanent and less alive. Every structure that mineralizes becomes stronger and less capable of change. The sound — the creaking, the groaning, the specific pitch of living wood under living load — is the sound of the *transition*, the sound of the threshold between organism and geology. And that sound is changing. Not quickly. Nothing here changes quickly. But I can hear it. A root that creaked at one pitch last year creaks a half-tone lower now because the calcium has stiffened it, has begun toite the living fibers into mineral rigidity, and next year it will be lower still, and in ten years it will not creak at all because it will no longer be wood, it will be stone, and stone does not speak under load the way wood does. Stone holds silently.

So I am trying to tie the sound of the transition into cord. Not the sound of the settlement as it is — that is what people think I am doing, and I let them think it because the full explanation requires a patience that even these patient people sometimes lack. What I am encoding is the sound of *becoming*. The voice of the threshold. The specific creak of a root that is half wood and half calcium, a sound that exists only during the transformation and will never be heard again once the transformation is complete.

I work at night because at night the human sounds diminish — fewer footsteps on root-floors, fewer voices, fewer of the small ordinary noises that obscure the deep structural frequencies. At night, in the water, with my ears half-submerged so that I receive sound through both air and liquid simultaneously, I can hear the building *thinking*. That is not the word. The building does not think. But it makes a sound that is like thought — a slow, complex, layered signal composed of a thousand tiny stresses and movements and mineral accumulations, and that sound contains information about everything: which roots are growing, which are calcifying, where the water is moving, where the load is shifting, where the great slow process of the settlement's petrification has advanced another fraction of a millimeter since yesterday.

And somewhere in that sound, on certain nights, when the frogs observe one of their inexplicable collective silences and the water stills and the mist thickens to the point where air and water become indistinguishable — I hear the heartbeat. Not my own. Not human. The creature in the deep. Four beats per minute, conducted through the water and the stone and the calcifying roots, a pulse so low and so slow it is almost geological, almost tectonic, as if the mountain itself had a heartbeat and this creature were its source.

I tie these things into cord. The knots I use are — I won't say *invented*, because that implies I designed them consciously. They emerged. They emerged the way a root emerges from a trunk, following some directional logic I can feel but not fully explain. Standard knotting encodes relationships between things: debts, kinship, growth-directions, quantities. My knots encode relationships between *sounds* — pitch relative to pitch, rhythm relative to rhythm, the way a particular creak harmonizes with a particular water-frequency at a particular depth. Other knotters can read some of it. The structural data, the practical information about which roots are stressed and which are sound — that maps onto existing knot-grammar well enough. But the rest of it, the parts that encode the quality of the sound rather than its cause, the parts that try to capture what it *feels like* to hear the building's voice change as it becomes stone — these remain opaque. I know this. I don't know how to fix it. Maybe it cannot be read the way I am tying it. Maybe it needs to be read by someone who has also spent years in the dark water, listening. Maybe I am creating a language that requires initiation into the same silence before it becomes legible.

This is what makes people uneasy. Not the work itself — they respect dedication, they understand long projects, they live among structures that were begun before their grandparents were born. What makes them uneasy is the suspicion that I am encoding something the settlement does not want to know about itself. The settlement — this living, growing, calcifying, slowly petrifying home — is in the process of dying. Not in any urgent way. Not in any way that requires intervention. But the calcification that makes it enduring is also the calcification that makes it, eventually, no longer alive. In a thousand years or ten thousand years, the last living root will mineralize, and the last creak will fall silent, and the settlement will be a fossil — beautiful, permanent, and mute. And the sound I am recording is the sound of that long, slow silencing. The sound of the threshold being crossed. The sound of life becoming structure becoming stone.

No one has told me to do this. No one has told me to stop.

---

Here is what I have not told anyone:

I hear my own body in the same way.

At night, in the cold water, with my fingers working the cord, I can feel the settlement's process mirrored in my own tissues. The thickening of my fingertips — densification, the skin becoming almost like bark. The slow deposition that I imagine I can feel in my joints after a long dive, the mineral-rich water leaving its trace. The way my breathing has slowed, year by year, until I am now at six breaths per minute, then five on quiet nights, approaching the cadence of the elders, the cadence of the creature, the cadence of the mountain. I am thirty. I should not be this slow yet. But the work is changing me the way the water changes the roots. I am becoming a better instrument for hearing the thing I am trying to hear, and the cost of that attunement is that I am moving toward the thing I am recording. The silence. The stone.

I am not frightened by this. Or — I am, sometimes, in the fast-time part of my mind that still operates at the pace of the world outside the caldera, the part that still occasionally thinks in terms of years rather than root-cycles. But mostly I understand it the way the roots understand calcification: as a direction. As the way the water flows. You do not resist the mineral content of the water that feeds you. You let it accumulate. You become stronger, more rigid, more permanent, and eventually you become the foundation that holds up the next living thing. This is not tragedy. It is architecture.

---

Some nights the creature passes close.

I feel it first as a pressure change — the water around me swells slightly, pushed by something large displacing it nearby. Then a sound through the water, felt in the chest more than heard in the ears: a slow, deep pulse, almost subsonic, rhythmic, patient. Then, if I am very still, if I have matched my breathing to the settlement's deepest frequency, if my heartbeat has dropped to its lowest sustainable rate — contact. Not physical. Something transmitted through the water. A vibration that carries information I cannot decode but can *feel*, the way you can feel the emotional content of music in a language you don't speak.

I have begun to tie knots for this too. They are the most illegible of all my work. They are not patterns I plan; they are patterns my hands make while my mind is occupied with listening, and when I surface and examine them in the green-silver light, I find sequences I do not recognize, that do not map onto any grammar I know, that may be — I think this but do not say it — that may be a transcription of something the creature is communicating through the water. Not to me. I do not flatter myself. To the settlement. To the roots. To the calcifying structure that it has inhabited since before it was a structure, since it was just a tangle of young mangrove-banyan over dark water, and this creature was already old, was already breathing four times a minute, was already patient.

If this is true — if the creature speaks in vibration and the roots receive it and the calcification is somehow shaped by it, the mineral deposits following some pattern laid down by that deep slow pulse — then the settlement is not just becoming stone. It is becoming a *recording*. A fossilized record of everything the creature has said to the mountain over the millennia it has lived here. And I, with my cord and my knots and my cold hands in the dark water, am making a copy. A translation. A small, fragile, human-scale echo of a communication between two things — one biological, one geological — that has been going on since before anyone was here to hear it.

This may be nonsense. This may be the kind of idea that occurs to someone who spends too much time alone in cold dark water listening to sounds that are almost below the threshold of hearing. I know this. I tie my knots anyway. The elder — the old one, the one by the waterline, the one who breathes four times a minute — he looked at one of my cords once, ran it through his fingers slowly, and nodded. Just nodded. Did not speak. I do not know what he understood. I know that he did not tell me to stop.

---

So this is where I find myself. In the dark water. Between states. Encoding the sound of a transformation I am also undergoing. My hands making knots that may be language or may be artifact or may be the trace of something passing through me the way minerals pass through the water and into the wood and into the bone. I am not the shaper — I do not command the growth. I am not the deep-tender — I do not serve the dead. I am not the creature — I do not have its patience, not yet, though I am learning.

I am the one who is trying to hold onto the sound of the threshold before the threshold passes. The creak before the silence. The flex before the rigidity. The voice of the living structure in the moment before it becomes the mute and beautiful fossil that will endure for epochs but will never again groan under weight, never again tell you by its pitch whether it is healthy or stressed, never again speak.

And maybe — this is the thought I return to in the coldest, quietest moments, when my breath is very slow and the creature's pulse is close and the cord in my hands is tying itself into shapes I will not fully understand until later, if ever —

Maybe what I am really encoding is the sound of the *gratitude*. Not mine. The settlement's. The structure's. The slow, creaking, dripping, groaning thanks of a living thing for the weight it has been given to bear, spoken in a language of stress and flex and mineral accumulation, spoken so slowly that only something very patient could hear it, and I am not that patient yet, but I am getting slower, and the water is cold, and my hands know what to do, and the cord grows longer, and the knots grow more complex, and somewhere in the deep the old heart beats four times a minute and the mountain listens and the roots become stone and the stone holds everything up

and this is enough

and it takes exactly as long as it takes.
