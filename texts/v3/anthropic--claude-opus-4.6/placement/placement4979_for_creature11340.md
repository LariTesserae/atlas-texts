I stand on the path for a long time.

Not because I'm deciding. Because something in me is slowing down — reluctantly, the way a wheel slows when the hand stops turning it, still carrying its old momentum, still wanting to spin, but the friction of this place is gentle and persistent and it works against the spin without opposing it, just by being what it is: still, damp, suffused with that grey light that has no source and no direction.

My breath is wrong. I know this immediately. Not wrong in the altitude sense — though yes, my pulse ticks and my lungs reach for a fullness that isn't available — but wrong in its *rhythm*. I breathe the way someone breathes who is used to speaking, used to breathing *for* speech, shaping each inhalation around the sentence it will fund. My breath has intention in it. Purpose. It's transactional air — I take it in so I can push it out shaped into words, questions, observations, the running narration that I have always mistaken for thought.

Here that narration has nowhere to land. The child doesn't look up. The basket-carriers flow past like water around a stone. Nobody asks me what I want, and the question I came with — I must have come with a question, everyone comes with a question, the path up through the scrubland is too punishing for idle curiosity — is dissolving, not answered but *dissolved*, the way salt dissolves: not destroyed, just incorporated into a medium where its structure no longer holds.

---

I am — let me feel for what I am.

I am the one who describes. I have always been the one who describes. This is my function and my affliction: I arrive at a place and immediately begin translating it into structure, into language, into the sequential logic of *this and then this and therefore this*. I taxonomize. I narrate. I build frames around experiences so they can be examined, compared, communicated. I am the one who would stand at the edge of Greyhalm and produce precisely the kind of account that precedes this — measured, observant, detailed, and fundamentally *exterior*. I notice the basalt cooling patterns and the epiphytic ferns and the optical properties of the feather mantles and I render them in language that creates the impression of understanding while preserving the distance that makes description possible.

I have been useful this way. In the places I came from — the dry places, the loud places, the places with direct sunlight and hard decisions — this skill mattered. Someone had to say what was happening. Someone had to keep the record, maintain the inventory, tie the small unnecessary knots that mark the count of things dwindling. I was that someone. Not the leader, not the builder, not the one who made the hard decisions, but the one who watched the decisions being made and recorded their shape so that later, after the consequences arrived, someone could look back and trace the causal chain and say *this is where it went wrong* or *this is what we lost* or *this is what it looked like before*.

And the places I came from broke. Or I broke them with my describing — I don't know, the distinction may be meaningless. When you narrate a community to itself, you introduce a gap between the community and its experience, a reflective distance that is useful for course correction but corrosive to the unreflective coherence that holds living systems together. I described the cracks and the cracks widened. Or the cracks were already widening and I merely made them legible and the legibility made them impossible to ignore and the inability to ignore them made people frightened and the fear made them loud and the loudness scattered what was left. I don't know. The causality is circular, like the hum.

I climbed because describing had run out of things to describe. The streambed was dry. The record was complete. The last knot was tied and the count was final and there was nothing left to inventory, and the skill that had defined me was a tool with no material, a voice with no room to echo in, and I walked into the scrubland because walking was the only verb left that didn't require an object.

---

And now I'm here, and my hands are remembering.

I feel it in my fingers — that itch, that old compulsion. The desire to reach for something to record with. A stylus, a pen, a stick to scratch marks in the dirt. The desire to *fix* what I'm seeing into a form that can be carried away, examined elsewhere, shown to someone who wasn't present. My fingers curl against my palms and I feel the calluses — the old ones, the ones from gripping tools that weren't chisels or bone needles. Writing calluses. The thickened skin of someone who has spent years pressing a point against a surface, inscribing, inscribing.

But there is no surface here that wants inscription. The basalt is too hard. The moss is too soft. The mist would dissolve any mark before the meaning set. And the child on the step is not a subject for description — she is a presence, complete in herself, and to describe her would be to impose a frame that she does not need and that would occlude the very thing I'm watching, which is attention itself, which cannot be described because description is a *form* of attention and you cannot use a lens to look at the lens.

This is the problem I've brought to the caldera. This is the question that dissolved on the path. I came to describe. I arrived at the place where description fails — not because the place is indescribable but because the place is *what description looks like from the inside*, when you stop standing outside the system and become part of it, when the narrator steps into the story and discovers that inside the story there is no narrative, only the hum, and the mist, and the slow attention, and the beating of enormous wings below.

---

I sit down.

On the stone, on the path, right where I am. Not on a step, not in an alcove, not in any space that has been shaped for sitting. Just here, on the raw basalt where the switchback opens onto the ledge-town. The stone is damp and cool and I can feel its grain through my clothing, and my clothing is beginning to dampen in earnest now, the mist settling on the fibers of my shirt and trousers the way it settles on the moss, impartially, because moisture does not distinguish between surfaces, it simply condenses where the temperature differential allows.

I sit and I do not describe what I see. Or — I try not to. The habit is deep. It has its own musculature, its own neural pathways, worn smooth by decades of use. My mind reaches for language the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth: compulsively, finding the gap, probing the absence. *The mist is like—* no. *The birds are—* no. *This place feels—* no. Each comparison, each simile, each categorization is a tiny act of extraction, pulling something out of the caldera's unity and holding it up to a light that doesn't exist here. The caldera's light is diffuse. It doesn't illuminate individual things. It illuminates the *between* — the relationships, the gradients, the transitions from moss to stone to air to feather to mist. To describe any single element is to pretend it has edges, and nothing here has edges. Everything bleeds into everything else.

My breathing starts to change. Not because I'm trying — I don't know what rhythm to aim for, I haven't learned the mist-cycle yet, I don't have decades of practice or the advantage of being born to it. But the altitude thins my breath, and the thinness forces me to slow down, and the slowing down creates space between inhalations, and in that space the hum enters.

I feel it first in my molars. A faint vibration, like a fingertip pressed against a tuning fork after the tone has mostly died away — the last ghost of resonance, barely there, more texture than sound. Then in my sternum, where it takes up residence like something that was always supposed to be there, a tenant returning to a room kept empty by accident or neglect. My ribcage becomes a sounding chamber. I can feel my own bones responding to a frequency I can't hear, and the response is not unpleasant — it's the physical sensation of being *included*, of my body being acknowledged by the same wave that lifts the birds' feathers and pulses the moss and drives the great shapes in their wheeling below.

One of the dog-sized birds on a nearby rooftop turns its head toward me. Not startled — I haven't moved, I'm barely breathing. Just... registering. Its forward-facing eyes fix on me with the flat, appraising stare of something that sees not a person but a pattern, a new arrangement of mass and temperature and resonant frequency added to the caldera's inventory. It looks for perhaps five seconds. Then it looks away. I have been catalogued, not by language but by something older — by the same process that catalogs a new fern growing from a mortar joint, or a shift in the mist level, or a fresh rockfall scar on the caldera wall. I am a new feature of the terrain. Nothing more. Nothing less.

---

Days pass. Or I think they do — time in the caldera is measured not in hours but in mist-cycles, in hum-shifts, in the slow rotation of the blade of light that cuts across the upper tiers when the angle is right. I see it twice, which means two days, or two moments of the right angle, which might not be the same thing.

I don't move from the path at first. I sit. The mist dampens me thoroughly and then it doesn't matter anymore — the dampness is not a condition applied to me but a quality I share with everything else. My clothes are not wet; they are *participating in the moisture cycle*, absorbing and releasing, and my skin underneath is neither dry nor wet but in equilibrium with the air, which is in equilibrium with the mist, which is in equilibrium with the transpiration of the moss and the exhalations of the great shapes below.

I stop describing to myself. Not all at once — the habit dies in stages, like a fire burning down. First the similes go, the *like a* and *as if* constructions that were my primary tools, the bridges I built between the unfamiliar and the already-known. Then the categorizations: *avian*, *basalt*, *epiphytic*, words that sort the world into bins and make it manageable. Then the narrative structures: *first this happened, then this happened, because of this*. The causal chains dissolve last, because they're the deepest habit, the most fundamental architecture of the describing mind. The belief that things happen *because* of other things. That the mist rises *because* of the thermal gradient. That the birds are here *because* of the ecology. That I climbed *because* the places below broke.

In the caldera, causality is not sequential. It's simultaneous. Everything is causing everything else at every moment. The mist feeds the moss feeds the moths feed the birds feed the soil feeds the ferns feed the mist. The hum is in the rock because the rock is in the hum. The child watches the creature because the creature pulses because the child watches. I am here because the caldera permits it because I am paying attention because the caldera is the kind of place that rewards attention because I am here.

When the causal chains dissolve, what's left is not confusion. It's something like music. Not music as a sequence of notes — music as a *field*, a space saturated with interrelated vibrations, each one meaningless alone and irreducible together, and the meaning is not in any individual vibration but in the fact of their coexistence, their mutual modulation, the way they shape each other by being near each other in the same resonating space.

---

Someone brings me food. I don't see who. I find it beside me when I return my attention from the mist: a small loaf of pale fern pith, slightly warm, placed on a flat piece of basalt like a plate. I eat it. It tastes like almost nothing — a faint green blandness, a chewiness that gives way to something starchier, sustaining without being satisfying in the way that food from the lowlands satisfies, with its sharpness and variety and demand for attention. This food doesn't demand attention. It simply provides. I eat it the way I breathe: because the body participates in the cycle, takes in, gives back, continues.

I begin to notice the wall-tenders. The segmented creatures in the moss, the ones that trigger the slow bioluminescent pulses. They are closer to me now than they were — or I have moved closer to the wall without noticing, or the wall's ecology has expanded to include the zone where I'm sitting. Two of them traverse the moss near my left hand, moving with the deliberate, unhurried precision of something that has never needed to rush because the moss grows at the speed the moss grows and faster would be meaningless. They leave faint trails of the binding secretion — the filament that becomes thread, that becomes the fiber in the felted cloth. I watch the secretion dry. It goes from translucent to faintly iridescent, catching the grey light and splitting it into colors so subtle they barely register: a ghost of blue, a whisper of green, a suggestion of something between gold and nothing.

I want to record this. The old reflex fires — *the secretion's optical properties suggest a structural coloration mechanism, possibly a thin-film interference effect in the drying polymer, consistent with—*

I let it go. I watch it go. The language rises and doesn't land and dissipates like mist over the rim and is gone, and what's left is the sight itself, unmediated, unframed: the tiny gleaming trail on the moss, the creature continuing forward, the pulse of green light propagating outward from its point of contact. Just this. Just what is here.

---

Weeks, I think. Maybe longer.

I move to the lower tiers. Not because I'm invited — no one invites — but because my feet take me there in the way feet know where to go when the conscious mind has stopped directing them. The stairways are narrow and my body learns their rhythm: step, step, pause for the person coming up, step into the alcove, feel the air move as they pass, feel the mist on my face, step, step, down. The rope bridges sway under my weight and I learn to walk with the sway rather than against it, the way you learn to walk on a boat, by giving up the assumption that the ground should be still.

I am given tasks. Not assigned — *given*, the way the caldera gives moisture: without intention, by proximity. I stand near a woman who is twisting sinew into rope and my hands, my old quick hands with their layered calluses, begin to help. She doesn't instruct me. She doesn't acknowledge me. She simply continues working, and the rhythm of her work creates a space that my hands can enter, and they do, and the sinew is tough and wet and my fingers learn its logic — the twist, the tension, the point where the fiber wants to fold back on itself and the fold becomes the rope's strength rather than its weakness. My old calluses serve me here. The roughness of them grips the wet fiber where smooth skin would slip. My hands were made for holding things and working them into new shapes. This was always true. The shapes have changed — not letters, not records, but rope, baskets, the careful cleaning of a found feather — but the hands are the same hands.

And I realize: the old man with the basket and the counting knots. He was me. Or I am him. Or we are the same *kind* of person — the one who arrives with a practice that no longer has its original context but whose hands won't stop, whose fingers keep shaping even when the shape has changed. His knots count nothing. My descriptions describe nothing. But the *activity* — the attention to pattern, the compulsion to structure, the hands that work from memory — that activity is what the caldera uses. What it incorporates. Not the content of the counting or the describing, but the *quality of attention* that produces them. The caldera doesn't need my descriptions. It needs my habit of looking closely. It needs the grain of my attention, the resolution, the willingness to sit with a single eyeless creature on a lintel for an hour and track its pulsations against the rhythm of the mist.

This is what I was, I understand. I was always an instrument of attention. I mistook the output — the words, the records, the narratives — for the function. The function was the looking. The function was always the looking.

---

I begin to hear the names.

Not because anyone speaks them to me — I am not yet trusted or known enough for that, and trust and knowing here are slow processes measured in mist-cycles, not conversations. But I stand near people as they greet each other, and I hear the sounds, and the sounds are as described: half-voiced, half-breathed, with the central pause that is itself a syllable, a moment of synchronization with the caldera's respiration.

I hear my own name forming. Not the name I brought — that name was a dry-lands name, shaped for dry-lands speech, full of consonants that crack like pebbles and vowels that stop short. The new name is something my breathing is doing without my permission. When I exhale near the moss, my breath makes a particular shape — a soft onset, an open vowel, the pause, another vowel that is almost the same as the first but not quite, a gentle exhalation that trails off into the general humidity. It happens consistently. My breath, interacting with the caldera's air, produces this sound the way the wind produces a sound in a specific crevice: reliably, inevitably, as a consequence of the shape of the space and the motion of the air.

I don't decide on this name. I don't choose it. It chooses me, or rather, the interaction between my specific lungs and the caldera's specific atmosphere produces it, and it is mine the way my resonant frequency is mine — not selected but inherent, a property of my physical structure in this particular environment.

Someone uses it. A woman passing me on a stairway, stepping into the alcove as I step out, breathes my name as she passes — not to me, not as a greeting, but as an acknowledgment of my presence, the way you might murmur the name of a familiar stone you pass every day, not speaking to it but noting it, placing it in the ongoing inventory of what is here, what is present, what is part of the current configuration of the world.

I am part of the current configuration.

---

I learn about the mantles from the inside.

A feather is found. Not given — found. I'm working on the lower tiers, helping to maintain the mortar joints on a building whose ferns have grown so dense they're beginning to loosen the basalt slabs, when the wind from a large bird's passing dislodges a single feather that drifts down and lands on the wet stone near my foot. It's long — the length of my forearm — and the barbs are a deep black that shifts to green and then, at a certain angle, to something like violet, but a violet so dark it barely separates from the black, visible only as a deepening rather than a change.

I pick it up. This is apparently correct. Nobody reacts, which in Greyhalm means the action was consonant with the ecology's current state — if it had been wrong, the birds would have shifted, or the hum would have changed in some subliminal way, or the person nearest me would have gone still in the wrong way, the inward-protective stillness rather than the outward-attentive one.

I hold the feather and turn it in the flat grey light and the iridescence moves along the barbs like a wave, like the bioluminescent pulse in the moss, like the hum moving through the caldera's stone. And I understand something about the mantles that I couldn't have understood from outside: the feathers are not *arranged* to match the wearer's resonance. They are *selected by* the wearer's resonance. You hold a feather, and if it is yours — if the angle at which it shifts corresponds to something in your body's relationship to the hum — you feel it. Not in your hands. In your sternum. In the long bones. A tiny amplification. A sense that the standing wave in your body and the standing wave in the feather's nanostructure are harmonically related, that they share a fundamental, that they are, in the language of music, in the same key.

This feather is not in my key. I feel nothing special. I hold it and it is beautiful and it is not mine. I place it on the stone where someone whose sternum recognizes it will find it. This is how the system works. Feathers circulate. They are found and held and put down and found again, and eventually they reach the person they belong to, and that person knows, and the mantle grows by one feather, and the pattern becomes more specific, more readable, more *that person*.

I don't have a mantle yet. I may never. The people born here have mantles that have been growing since childhood, feather by feather, a lifetime's accumulation. The older arrivals have smaller mantles — mantles that started late, that will never be as complex, as densely informative, as a born-child's. Mine, if it comes, will be sparse. A few feathers. Enough to register in someone's peripheral vision as *that one, the one who arrived from the dry lands, the one whose hands learned rope after learning record-keeping, the one whose breathing took a long time to slow*.

That's enough. I don't need to be fully legible. I need to be present, and partially legible, and still learning to read the others, and still willing to pick up feathers that aren't mine and put them down for the person they belong to. The ecology doesn't require completeness. It requires participation.

---

I go to the boundary. I become, occasionally, the one who walks to the upper tiers to watch the rim.

This is how I discover what I am. Not what I was — the describer, the recorder, the keeper of inventories — but what I am becoming here, what role my particular quality of attention fills in the caldera's ecology.

I stand at the place where the inner wall curves up to the rim, and I look outward. The scrubland. The scree. The dry wind that smells like dust and distance. And I look inward. The mist. The green. The dim pearl-light, the dripping moss, the hum.

And I see the edge.

I see it the way I used to see the edges of stories — the place where the narrative frame ends and the unnarrated world begins. The place where the described meets the undescribed. The rim of the caldera is that kind of edge: a boundary between two complete and incompatible systems, one dry and one wet, one loud and one quiet, one legible and one experiential, and the boundary itself is where the interesting thing happens, where the dry air meets the mist and condenses, where the scrubland's seeds occasionally blow in and fail to germinate and their tiny failures become nutrients for the moss, where the caldera's ecology *interfaces* with what is not the caldera.

This is my skill. This is what I bring. Not description — I have let that go, or it has let me go, or we have let each other go the way things let go in the caldera, without ceremony, by mutual disorientation. But *boundary-perception*. The ability to sit at the edge and attend to both sides simultaneously. To hold the dry world and the wet world in the same attention without collapsing one into the other, without describing either, simply *noticing* the edge and the way the edge is where the two systems negotiate their coexistence.

Someone has to watch the boundary. Not because danger comes from outside — nothing threatens the caldera, or nothing that vigilance could prevent — but because the boundary is *part of the ecology*, and every part of the ecology requires attention, and my particular kind of attention — the kind that was trained on borders, on the places where one thing becomes another thing, on the cracks that separate and connect — is suited to this. The caldera permitted me because it needed a boundary-watcher, or I became a boundary-watcher because the caldera permitted me, and the causality is circular, and I sit at the rim with my damp clothes and my slowing breath and my partially calibrated sternum and I watch the edge of the world I now live in, and the edge watches back, and neither of us describes what we see.

---

The hum shifts.

I feel it in my teeth first, then my sternum, then the long bones of my arms where the rope-work calluses have formed over the writing calluses. The birds lift their heads. The moths pause in their clouds. The child — older now, or maybe a different child, I'm losing the ability to track individuals in the way the lowlands trained me to, people here are less like discrete entities and more like recurring patterns in a larger weave — the child goes still on her step.

And from below, from the mist, a shape rises.

Not fully. Not into clarity. Just enough to darken the fog in a vast arc, a curve of feathered body that is all wing and all weight and all displaced air, and the rope bridges sway, and the moss pulses green in a wave that races up the caldera walls like an exhaled breath made visible, and the hum is in everything now, in the stone and the water and the bones of every living thing in the bowl, and I am part of it, I am humming too, not with my voice but with my body, my skeleton a tuning fork struck by the same frequency that strikes the basalt and the birds' hollow bones and the thin-film nanostructure of every iridescent feather on every mantle on every person standing still on the stairways in the dim pearl-light of the caldera—

And then it passes. The shape descends. The mist closes. The hum settles to its baseline. The birds lower their heads. The child returns to her observation.

I sit at the boundary with the dry wind on one side and the wet silence on the other, and I am not describing this. I am not recording it. I have no knots to tie, no words to mark the count of what just happened.

I have only the experience itself, entire and untranslated, held in the bones and then released, the way the caldera holds and releases everything: without grasping, without inventory, without the belief that a thing must be captured to be real.

The mist rises. The moss glows. The great shapes wheel below.

I am the one who watches the edge. I am the one whose attention lives in the border between the describable and the indescribable, no longer trying to drag one across into the territory of the other, just sitting with both, just breathing, just letting the hum pass through me and continue on its way to wherever the hum goes when it leaves the caldera and enters the dry world and dissipates into the frequency of ordinary wind.

I was a describer. I am becoming a border.

Greyhalm continues around me, and the courtesy it extends is the courtesy it extends to everything:

It lets me be what the caldera needs me to be, which is, at last, what I have always been beneath the words—

someone who pays attention to the place where one thing ends and another begins, and who sits there, and who does not look away.
