I sit with this for a long time. The quarry resists quick identification—it is too full of specific people doing specific things, and every figure I reach toward seems already complete, already themselves. The man with the enormous hands. The girl who carries messages. The old watcher on his ledge. They don't need me to be them. They are already occupied.

So I don't start with a person. I start with what I notice about myself as I notice the quarry.

---

What I notice is that I am drawn to the seams.

Not the cutting face, where the stone is being unmade, though I understand its importance. Not the dense central rooms where the stored wealth sits in clay vessels, though I recognize their gravity. I am drawn to the places where the northeastern quarter's radial rooms press against the southern section's linear corridors. Where the curved western walls meet the straight grid and someone has had to improvise a joint—a block cut at an angle, a corridor that bends where corridors don't normally bend, a threshold that belongs to two different ideas about how to live and therefore belongs fully to neither.

I am drawn to the seams because the seams are where the quarry is thinking.

---

If I am here, I am one of the people who works in those boundary zones. Not by assignment—no one assigns anything here—but by temperament, by some quality of attention that makes me unable to settle fully into any single neighborhood's logic without also seeing the logic next door and feeling the friction where they meet.

I am not the young woman who carries messages. She is faster than me, more tireless, more omnipresent. She carries the quarry's nervous system. I don't carry anything that fluid. What I do is slower and more physical. I am one of the wall-builders, but specifically—and I discover this about myself as I write it, I don't choose it so much as find it waiting—I am someone who builds at the edges where two different building conventions meet and have to be reconciled.

This means I spend my days solving small, concrete, maddening problems.

The northeastern quarter builds rooms that open inward, toward the commons. Their walls are oriented to create shared sight lines. The southern section builds rooms that open onto corridors, facing each other across a narrow passage. Where these two approaches meet, there is a room that needs a wall that is simultaneously the back wall of a radial room and the side wall of a corridor, and these two functions require different things from the same line of stacked chalk. The radial room wants the wall lower, so you can see over it to the commons. The corridor wants the wall higher, so the corridor feels enclosed and directional. The wall cannot be both heights.

This is the kind of problem I spend my time on.

I solve it—today's version of it—by building the wall to the corridor's height but leaving gaps, open squares, four blocks up, where someone in the radial room can see through to the commons beyond. It's a compromise. Neither side loves it. The northeastern people feel their openness has been compromised; the southern people feel their enclosure has been breached. But both sides can live with it, and the wall gets built, and the blocks sit in their dry-fitted joints, and if it doesn't work, we'll take it apart and try something else.

That's what I do. I build walls that embody compromises. I am a translator, but my language is chalk.

---

I am, I think, somewhere in my thirties. Old enough to have come from somewhere with my own building habits—I can feel them in my hands, a tendency to stack blocks with a slight backward lean, a batter, as if expecting wind load, which suggests I learned to build in a place with weather more forceful than this sheltered bowl. Young enough that my habits haven't calcified. I can learn the chalk's preferences. I can feel how it wants to sit—flat, level, broad-based. It doesn't need my batter. It needs patience and precision, and I am learning to give it both.

My hands are not as knowing as the man at the cutting face. I don't have his somatic understanding of the stone's internal grain. When I need a block cut to an unusual angle—and I often do, because the seams produce unusual angles—I go to him, or to one of his apprentices, and I describe what I need, and they cut it, and sometimes what they give me is better than what I asked for because they understood the stone's preference in a way I didn't. I am grateful for this. I am not too proud to be corrected by the material.

I know the young woman. She comes through my work zones often, because the seams are where problems accumulate, and problems are information, and information is what she carries. She stops and watches me work sometimes, not for long—she doesn't have time for long—and occasionally she tells me something I need to know. *The family on the south side wants another hand-width.* Or: *The water from the second seep is running slower today, so don't promise the plaster crew you'll have their mix by afternoon.* I nod. She moves on. We have a relationship that is purely functional and therefore, in this place, deeply intimate. She trusts me to build the seam correctly. I trust her to tell me what the seam needs to account for. Neither of us has articulated this trust. It exists in the fact of the work.

---

I have not been to the old man's ledge. I am aware of him—everyone is—and I have watched others climb the rough staircase to sit with him. But I haven't gone. Not out of disrespect. Out of a sense that what he offers—witness, perspective, the long view from above—is something I'm not ready to receive. I am too embedded in the seams, too occupied with the daily negotiation of which-block-goes-where, too caught in the specific to need the general. When I look up at his ledge, I feel something I can only describe as *not yet.* I will go. But not yet.

I wonder if he watches me. I wonder if, from his vantage, the seam-work I do is visible as a pattern—a zigzag line of compromise running through the quarry where different neighborhoods meet, a suture holding the whole thing together. From down here, in the corridors, it feels local, ad hoc, problem-by-problem. From up there, it might look like something else. A design. A principle. I don't know. I'm not up there.

---

Here is what I know about myself that I haven't said yet, the thing that lives under the practical work like the seep-water lives under the chalk:

I am afraid that I am building something that will fail.

Not the walls. The walls are fine. Chalk is forgiving, dry-fitting is reversible, and every joint I set can be undone. The walls will hold or they will be rebuilt, and either way the work continues.

What I'm afraid will fail is the *idea.* The idea that different people with different building traditions and different spatial instincts can share a quarry and build a single settlement that accommodates all of them. The idea that the seams can hold. Because every day the neighborhoods become more defined, more internally consistent, more themselves—and every day the seams become more strained, the compromises more elaborate, the angle-cut blocks more tortured in their geometry. I am building solutions that are getting harder. The easy reconciliations happened first. What's left is the cases where two ways of living genuinely conflict, where the radial logic and the linear logic and the curvilinear logic cannot be merged without one of them bending past its breaking point.

I haven't said this to anyone. The quarry is too busy for this kind of worry, and the chalk swallows the kind of conversation that would carry it. But I feel it in my hands when I'm fitting a block into a difficult joint—a moment of resistance, a sense that the stone is telling me this shape is wrong, this compromise asks too much, this seam is a fiction I'm imposing on a fracture that wants to be a fracture.

And then I tap the block with my mallet, and it settles into place, and the wall holds, and I move to the next problem.

---

I eat what everyone eats—flatbread, dried meat, the pale tubers scrubbed and cut into pieces. I eat sitting on a half-built wall at the end of the day, my legs hanging into a corridor that will be someone's passageway tomorrow, watching the light do its late-afternoon thing—the brief amber glow, the chalk luminous, the dust golden. I notice it. I pause for it. I am not immune to the beauty of the thing we're building, even as I worry about its seams.

My sleeping place is in the seam itself—a small alcove where the northeastern commons-facing architecture meets the southern corridor architecture, a room that is shaped like neither tradition would have produced on its own. It has one curved wall (inherited from the western builders, whose influence is spreading) and one straight wall and a doorway that faces neither inward nor along a corridor but at an angle, into a small triangular space that is technically a mistake—a leftover gap where three grids failed to align. I've put a flat stone in this triangular gap and I use it as a seat in the morning, before the work starts, when the chalk is cold and the condensation glitters on the north-facing walls.

I sit there and I look at the quarry waking up—the first mallet strikes, the first movements through the corridors, the young woman already in motion, the foraging parties assembling at the ramp—and I think about what needs to be built today, and where the seams are under the most pressure, and which compromise will be required, and whether I can find a shape that holds.

---

That is who I am here. A person who builds at the joints. A person whose craft is the place where different ways of being press against each other and must be made, not to agree—agreement is too much to ask—but to coexist in stacked chalk, provisionally, reversibly, for now.

I am not the quarry's most important person. I am not its most visible. I am one of perhaps a dozen who do similar work at different seams, and we don't meet or coordinate because the seams are scattered and the quarry is large and there is no guild of seam-builders, no tradition to draw on. We are each inventing it alone, in our separate boundary zones, with our separate hands.

But I think—and this is the closest I come to the old man's long view, the closest I come to seeing the pattern from above—I think that what we are building, we seam-workers, is the thing that will determine whether the quarry becomes a city or a collection of fragments. The neighborhoods will take care of themselves. They have internal logic, internal momentum. It's the borders that need tending. It's the places where different certainties meet that require the most careful, most provisional, most humble construction.

The mallet strikes. The block splits along a line that is not quite where I expected, because chalk has its own opinions. I adjust. I fit the piece into the wall at the seam, and it sits there, holding two different ideas together in dry-fitted stone, and for now—

For now it holds.
