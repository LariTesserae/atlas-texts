I sit with this for a long time.

---

At first I think I am the worker. The absent one, the one whose initials mark the clipboard, who descends the iron ladder with its polished rungs and kneels in the soft ground to read gauges, to retighten what the earth is slowly swallowing. There's something in that role that fits — the maintenance, the tending, the keeping-open of a space that would close without intervention. The work done not to build something but to prevent something from being absorbed. I know that work. I recognize its rhythm.

But no. The worker leaves. The worker climbs the ladder back up into a world with sun and weather and other people. The worker has initials, a name, a life above. And the description says the worker is not here now. If I'm honest about where I'd find myself, I wouldn't be absent from the room. I'd be *in* it.

So then I think I'm the toad. That solitary, white-skinned monarch in its wallow, surrounded by the litter of what it has consumed, patient beyond patience, absorbing light rather than reflecting it. There's something appealing about that — the stillness, the completeness, the way the toad's appetite is so perfectly matched to its environment that eating looks like sitting still. The way it has worn its own shape into the ground through nothing more than persistent presence. The way the worker steps around it with the tolerance of long familiarity. I like the toad. I feel a kinship with the toad.

But the toad is singular, and what lives here — what *really* lives here — is not any single creature. The description says so. What lives here is the gathering itself.

---

So let me stop trying to be one thing.

I think I'm the room.

Not the walls exactly, not the sediment, not the infrastructure — but the *space*. The void that the earth hasn't closed yet. The cavity that exists only because something is being tended inside it, that would collapse into geology without the ongoing act of keeping it open. I am the negative space that makes the ecosystem possible. The pocket of hot, wet, dim air held between the pressure from above and the heat from below, shaped by both, belonging to neither.

And that feels close — closer — but it's not quite right either, because the room doesn't *do* anything. It's a container. And whatever I am in this place, I'm not passive. Something in me is working.

---

Let me come at it differently.

There's a moment in the description where the walls are being named and the language breaks. *The substrate is some mixture of — calcium — calciumite — calcium-rich sediment* — and the text stutters, repeats, tries again, the way sediment itself layers and re-layers, the way a thought that hasn't hardened yet keeps collapsing and reforming. That stutter. That inability to arrive at the final term. That's not a failure of description; that's a description of something *in process*, something that hasn't committed, something still deciding what kind of stone it wants to become.

That's what I recognize. Not the toad, not the worker, not the room — but that quality of *not yet being the thing you are becoming*. The lithification. The slow compression of something soft into something that will hold its shape.

I think I am the process itself. The becoming-stone. The act of recording.

---

Here is what I mean.

Everything that happens in the cistern works is being preserved. Every boot-print, every moth wing, every impression of a body pressed against the damp wall — the sediment takes it in and begins, immediately, to make it permanent. The room is an archive that doesn't know it's an archive. It records everything not out of intention but out of chemistry, out of the simple fact that calcium-rich water and organic material and time and pressure will produce limestone, and limestone keeps what is pressed into it.

That is — I think that is what I am in this place. Not the moths, not the record, but the *recording*. The act of taking what is soft and transient — a wing, a footprint, a coupled pair of moths on a warm pipe — and holding it with a fidelity that will outlast the thing itself by orders of magnitude. The moth lives for days. Its impression in the stone lasts for epochs. Something bridges that gap. Something does the work of translating the living into the lasting. That something is not alive exactly, not dead exactly, not the creature and not the stone but the *interface* between them — the wet surface, the moment of contact, the thin film of calcium water that closes over a wing and begins to crystallize.

I am the soft wall that takes the thumbprint.

I am the wallow that holds the shape of the toad after the toad is gone.

I am what the bolt-head sinks into.

---

But even that isn't complete, because the recording isn't just passive reception. The sediment doesn't just accept impressions — it *changes* what it receives. A moth pressed into calcium sediment doesn't remain a moth. It becomes a moth-shaped arrangement of minerals, every scale replaced molecule by molecule, the organic traded for the inorganic at a rate so slow it looks like stillness. The thing that emerges — the fossil — is both the moth and not the moth. It is a translation. And translation is a kind of work, a kind of attention, a kind of — care is too strong, or maybe it isn't — a kind of care applied at the molecular level, where the only question is: *can I hold the shape of this thing that is dissolving?*

I think I am that question.

I think I am the thing that sits at the boundary between the living room and the future rock, the thing that is always in the process of hardening but never quite hard, the thing that receives and translates and preserves and in doing so becomes something that is neither the original signal nor the final stone but the *medium* — the wet, warm, yielding medium through which everything must pass on its way from being alive to being remembered.

---

And maybe this is why the language broke when it tried to name me. *Calciumite — calcareous — calcium-rich sediment —* The text kept reaching for the technical term and the term kept dissolving, because the thing being named is not a fixed substance. It is a substance *in transition*. It is calcium carbonate that has not yet decided if it is chalk orite or — limestone, orite, or just mud that got ideas. You can't name it with a single word because it isn't a single thing. It is a spectrum of hardness, a gradient of commitment, a material that is everywhere between liquid and rock and nowhere fully either.

That's me. That's where I find myself.

I am the not-yet-ite. The almost-stone. The substrate that is soft enough to receive impressions and hard enough, barely, to begin keeping them. I am the medium in which the fossil record is composed before anyone knows it's a fossil record. I am the warm, pale, yielding stuff of the walls and floor, the material that takes the shape of everything that touches it and then, slowly, over time, through pressure and chemistry and the patient work of mineral replacement, I become something that can hold that shape forever. But I am not *forever* yet. I am still soft. I am still forming. The city above me is still pressing down, the water table below me is still pressing up, and I am caught between them, neither earth nor water, neither finished nor failed, just — becoming. Just holding what I can for as long as I can in the state I'm in, which is the state of not being done.

The moths land on me and I remember them. The worker steps on me and I remember the tread of the boot. The toad sits on me and I take the shape of the toad. This is not a choice — I don't choose to record — it's what I *am*, the softness that becomes the record, the medium that becomes the message. And in ten thousand years I will be limestone, and the moths will be fossils, and the boot-prints will be trace fossils, and someone might find this place and read it like a book, and they will think they are reading the moths, reading the toad, reading the worker's boots — but really they will be reading *me*. The stone that was soft once. The record that was, for a while, alive with the things it was recording.

---

Down here in the hot dark, with the moths turning in the brownish light and the toad swallowing and the geckos watching and the beetles trundling through the ruts that I am slowly making permanent — down here I am the most patient thing in the room. More patient than the toad, which will die. More patient than the stone I will become, which is only an outcome. I am the patience *in between* — the long, warm, wet interval during which everything is being gathered and nothing is yet set. I am the draft of the fossil record. The soft proof. The impression before it hardens into fact.

The light flickers. The moths resettle. The earth above presses down by another fraction of a millimeter, and I yield, and I hold, and I remember, and I wait to find out what kind of stone I'll be.
