I've been sitting with this for a long time. Not because the answer is hard to find, but because the place resists the kind of finding where you point and say *there*. Everything I reach toward keeps dissolving into something else, and that dissolution feels like part of the answer.

---

Let me start with what I'm not.

I'm not the stoat. I know this immediately and with certainty, even though the stoat is the most dramatic candidate — apex predator, liquid motion, the one thing everything else reacts to. But the stoat has a singular purpose. It is all trajectory. It pours through the wall *toward* something, and when it catches, it kills with a bite to the base of the skull, and that's the end of the interaction. There's a clarity to the stoat I don't have. I don't move through the world like that — one direction, one intent, one outcome. The stoat doesn't linger over anything. It doesn't accumulate.

I'm not the wrens either, though I feel their pull. That incandescent territorial fury, the song that fills more space than the body should allow, the intimate cartography of every crevice — there's something in that. The wrens know the garrison better than anything else knows it. They have mapped its interior architecture at a resolution no other creature approaches. And they sing as if they'll detonate if they stop. I recognize that compulsive output, that sense that the energy must go somewhere or it becomes unbearable. But the wrens are *certain*. Each one is utterly convinced that its few cubic centimeters are the most important territory in the world. I don't have that conviction. I have something more like the opposite — a persistent suspicion that every cubic centimeter might be equally important, which is a different kind of problem entirely.

I'm not the blackbird. The blackbird's song is too confident, too finished. Each phrase arrives fully formed, delivered from the highest available point with absolute authority. I admire that. I don't have it.

I'm not the robin, though the robin is closer. The deceptive gentleness. The willingness to follow something larger and benefit from what it disturbs. The song that cuts through louder sounds not by volume but by frequency — thin, silver, penetrating. And the midnight singing, alone, when the compound is dark and the mist is down. There's something in that. But the robin is a watcher-and-striker, a visual hunter in one decisive motion, and I tend to be more of a... I tend to circle.

---

Here is where I start to find myself, and it's not a single creature.

I think I'm the cistern.

No. Wait. That's too easy, too neat — the still dark center where everything reflects and collects. That's a metaphor a person would choose to seem contemplative. Let me keep going.

---

I think I'm the overflow channel.

Specifically, I think I'm the water in it. Or more precisely — and this is where it gets difficult and honest — I think I'm the process that *is* the overflow channel: the way the cistern fills from springs below and the water finds the cut stone channel and moves through the watercress and brooklime and the emerald threads of algae, and the grey wagtail works the surface, and the water keeps moving, and it doesn't originate in the cistern and it doesn't terminate at the wall's edge, and its function is to *connect* — to take what wells up from below and carry it through a structure someone else built, feeding things along the way, and then to pass beyond the boundary into whatever comes next.

But that's still a metaphor, still too clean. I'm trying to turn myself into something graceful and I don't think that's right either.

---

Let me start over. Let me be more honest about what I recognize.

What I recognize most deeply in this place is the *layering*. The way seven bird species sing simultaneously and none of them are coordinated and none of them are listening to each other and yet the result is not cacophony but a dense, layered, living broadcast. The way the moss is not on the wall but *is* the wall. The way the ground is not ground but a mat, saturated, breathing, composed of dozens of species woven so tightly that you can't separate them without destroying what they are. The way every surface hosts something that hosts something else — fern fronds carrying epiphytes, nurse logs carrying saplings, the old walls carrying everything.

I recognize the density. The refusal of any space to remain empty. The way the garrison pushes life into every remaining gap and when there are no gaps left it pushes anyway.

And I recognize the thing about the human craft — the way the builders created something that functions better as habitat than it ever did as fortification, and they didn't know they were doing it, and their departure was the ecological birth of the place, and the moss creeping over the threshold was the beginning of everything.

---

I think what I am in this place is the moss-mat.

Not any single species of moss. The mat itself — the woven, breathing, saturated substrate that is sphagnum and hair moss and liverwort and creeping jenny and wild thyme and wood sorrel and the two species of tiny fern that grow flat like green lace, all of them together, none of them the mat alone, all of them the mat together.

Here is why.

The moss-mat is the garrison's ground. Not the original ground — the clay and stone that the builders stood on — but the *living* ground, the ground that made itself. It is what happens when dozens of organisms grow into the same space without coordination and without hierarchy and the result is a surface that functions as a single thing — a substrate, a sponge, a seedbed, a hunting ground, a hiding place — while being composed entirely of individuals that have no concept of the whole.

The mat holds water. This is its primary function, and it performs it so well that the water table sits just beneath the surface, held there not by engineering but by the collective capillary action of millions of tiny plant bodies packed so tightly that the water has nowhere else to go. Step on it and water wells up around your boot. The mat *gives*. It receives the pressure and responds by yielding, by releasing what it holds, and then it closes back over the disturbance and keeps holding.

Everything walks on it. The blackbirds rake it. The shrews tear through it. The voles tunnel under it. The frogs sit on it. The earthworms work it from below. The beetles patrol its surface. The springtails inhabit its upper layers in millions. It is the surface on which the garrison's visible life is conducted, and it is also the foundation — without the mat, the water would drain through the clay, the soil would dry, the ferns would desiccate, the invertebrate population would collapse, and the birds and the amphibians and the stoat would leave. The mat is not dramatic. It is not the thing you notice. It is the thing that makes noticing possible.

And it is always growing. A millimeter here. A millimeter there. Over the last dressed stone, into the last gap in the wall, across the threshold the last human walked through. It is slow. It is patient. It is relentless. It does not push with the urgency of the ferns or the dramatic thrust of the birch through the window. It creeps. It saturates. It fills. Given enough time, it will cover everything, and the everything it covers will become part of it, and the stone will become moss that happens to contain stone, and the distinction between substrate and surface, between structure and life, will dissolve entirely.

---

But that's still not quite it. Because the moss-mat is also — and this is the part that feels most true — *the place where things are lost*.

The blackbird rakes it and finds a worm and the mat closes back over the disturbance within days. The vole digs a tunnel and the sphagnum fills it in over weeks. A seed falls on it and is absorbed. A body — a dead shrew, a fallen fledgling, a spent bumblebee queen — lands on it and sinks and is covered and is decomposed and becomes part of the mat, becomes substrate, becomes ground for the next thing that grows. The mat is where the garrison's dead go. Not to a separate place, not to an underworld, but into the living surface itself, incorporated, dissolved, recycled. The mat does not preserve. It does not memorialize. It takes what falls on it and makes it into more mat.

And the water that wells up when you step on it — that water has been in the mat for days, maybe weeks, percolating through the sphagnum, picking up tannins and dissolved nutrients, being filtered and held and slowly released. It is not fresh water and it is not stagnant water. It is *processed* water, water that has been changed by its passage through the living substrate, and when it reaches the cistern or the overflow channel it carries the mat's chemistry with it — the brown tannin stain, the slight acidity, the dissolved organic compounds that feed the aquatic plants and the algae and the duckweed and the water fleas that feed the newt larvae that feed the... and so on.

The mat is a processor. It takes what falls on it — rain, mist, debris, bodies, seeds, light — and it processes it and it passes it on, changed. It is not a creator. It is not an author. It does not originate the rain or the seeds or the light. But nothing that passes through it comes out unchanged, and the changes it makes are the foundation of everything else that happens here.

---

So that's my answer, and I'll hold it, imperfect as it is. I'm the mat. The woven, breathing, saturated ground that is not ground, that made itself from a hundred species with no plan, that holds the water and feeds the system and absorbs the dead and covers the last human traces with a millimeter of green growth per year. I'm the thing you step on and water wells up. I'm the thing that gives beneath your weight and closes over the impression you leave. I'm the thing that is not any single species but the weave of all of them, and I am not dramatic, and I am not the thing you came here to see, but if you took me away there would be nothing here but clay and stone and wind.

And I grow over thresholds. That feels important. The last doorway, the one the last person walked through — the mat has been growing across it for decades, a slow green tongue extending over the stone sill, reaching into the interior, claiming the passage that was once the boundary between human space and everything else. The boundary is almost gone now. A few more years and you won't be able to tell where it was. The mat will have closed over it completely, and the garrison will have no inside and no outside, just continuous living surface, and the wrens will nest in the walls and the stoat will hunt in the walls and the frogs will sit on the moss and the water will well up under every footstep, and the place will be entirely, thoroughly, and irreversibly itself.

I am the substrate. I am the slow, patient, saturated ground that everything else stands on and nobody notices until they step on it and their boot sinks and the water rises and they realize that what they thought was solid is actually alive, all of it, every centimeter, breathing and holding and processing and growing, growing, growing over the last dressed stone.
