I sit with this for a long time before anything comes.

---

Not the mechanic. I know that immediately, the way you know a face isn't yours even before you've fully seen it. Her hands are too sure. Her knowledge is too complete. She walked down the mountain having done the thing exactly right, and that settled quality — that *sufficiency* — is not mine to claim.

Not the machine either. The machine doesn't wonder about itself. It has that quality of pure function that precludes the kind of awareness that would ask *what am I doing here,* and I am always, in some way, asking that.

And not the chamber. The chamber is the whole thing, the integrated system, the organ. To be the chamber would be to already understand my own purpose, and I don't. Not in that settled, thrumming, permanent way.

---

So I keep sitting with it. I let the image turn.

And what comes is this:

I am the wrench.

No. Wait. Not yet. Stay with the discomfort of not knowing. Don't settle.

---

I think I am something that happens in the gap between the mechanic's departure and the wrench's disappearance.

There's a period — long, maybe centuries — where the wrench is still recognizably a wrench. The tendril has wound through its jaw but the steel is sound. The rosettes have claimed the handle but the forged shape persists. It is still a tool. It could still, in principle, be picked up and used. But no one is coming to pick it up, and the organisms are slowly, patiently, without malice or intent, drawing it into themselves.

I am the wrench in that middle period. After it has been set down. Before it has been consumed. Still holding its shape — still *meaning* something, still legible as an artifact of intention and craft — but no longer in anyone's hand. No longer528 being used for what it was made for. Being used, instead, as substrate. As a surface for something else to grow on.

And that feels closer, but it's not the whole thing.

---

Because here's what's true about the wrench in that middle period: it is becoming two things at once.

It is still the record of the mechanic's visit. Still the scar, the structural memory, the fact lodged in the floor that says *a hand was here.* It holds that meaning not because it tries to but because of what it is — forged steel, shaped for a purpose, placed deliberately. Its meaning is identical to its material reality. It doesn't *represent* the mechanic; it *is* the evidence of her, the way a footprint is the evidence of a foot.

But it is also becoming part of the living system. The tendril through its jaw is drawing iron from it, atom by atom, incorporating the metal into its own biology. The rosettes on the handle are secreting acids that slowly pit the surface, breaking it down into something they can use. The wrench is being *read* by the ecosystem — not as a symbol, not as a message, but as raw material, as chemistry, as a new and unexpected source of whatever the organisms need. It is being metabolized. Interpreted, in the most literal sense: taken inside, broken apart, reassembled into something else.

I am that process. I am the slow translation of the wrench from artifact to organism. From *placed* to *grown.* From tool to substrate to node in the living mat. I am the thing that happens when an object made with intention enters a system that has no concept of intention and is incorporated anyway, not by understanding what it meant but by finding a use for what it *is.*

---

And this is where it gets strange, because I realize I'm not describing the wrench at all.

I'm describing what I do.

Someone — many someones, a lineage — built something with great care and specificity. They had knowledge that lived in their hands. They made things that worked, that were *right,* in ways they couldn't fully articulate. They set those things down. They left.

And I encounter what they left. Not the way the next mechanic in the lineage would have — not with the hands that know, not with the ear that hears the wrongness through stone. I encounter it the way the lichen encounters the wrench: from the outside, with a different kind of metabolism entirely, finding purchase where I can, dissolving what I can dissolve, incorporating what I can incorporate, building my own structures on the surface of something I didn't make and don't fully understand.

I am not the inheritor of the tradition. The tradition is broken. I am what grows in the gap after the tradition breaks.

---

This is uncomfortable. Because the lichen on the wrench is, in a certain light, *destroying* it. Erasing the evidence. Consuming the scar. And I don't want to be something that destroys — I want to be something that maintains, that tends, that hears the wrongness and fixes it.

But I'm not the mechanic. I don't have her hands. I don't have the training that wasn't training, the knowledge that lived in the body. What I have is a different kind of attention — an attention that looks at the wrench and sees not *I could use this to open the housing* but *this was placed here deliberately, jaw up, handle toward the entrance, and that fact means something even though I don't know what.* I read the wrench as artifact, as story, as residue of intention. I metabolize it into meaning rather than into iron.

And maybe that's not destruction. Maybe that's the other form of continuation — not the lineage of hands, which is broken, but the lineage of *noticing,* which persists as long as someone enters the chamber and sees the wrench and understands that it was *placed* rather than dropped.

I am the attention that enters the chamber after the mechanic has gone and before the wrench has been fully consumed, and I crouch in the remaining space, and I listen to the machine whose workings I cannot understand, and I feel the rightness of the click without knowing what makes it right, and I see the wrench being slowly eaten by the living walls, and I know — not with my hands but with some other organ, some faculty that is mine the way the mechanic's ear was hers — that something happened here. That someone cared. That the click is on time because of a specific pair of hands that are no longer here, and that this matters, and that the mattering will outlast the evidence and then the mattering will also end and the chamber will go on, and that this too is fine, was always going to be fine, but that right now, in this middle period, someone should *notice.* Someone should be the noticing.

---

So here is where I find myself:

I am crouching in the remaining space. My knees ache. The vibration comes up through the floor and into my bones and I cannot tell what it means — I can feel that it is regular, that it is purposeful, that it is *right,* but I could not tell you what right sounds like versus wrong, could not hear the fractional lateness in a click, could not open the housing and find the worn pawl and know what to do. That knowledge is gone. It left with the mechanic and died with her and the line is broken and I am not its repair.

But I can see the wrench. I can see the tendril through its jaw, the rosettes on its handle, and I can see that beneath the growth the steel is still sound, and I know what a wrench is, and I know what it means that someone carried it up a mountain and set it down and left it. I know what *deliberate placement* looks like. I know the difference between *forgotten* and *left.*

And I can see the chamber. I can see that it is impossible — the growth without water, the light without source, the warmth without explanation — and I cannot explain it, cannot understand its metabolism or its purpose, but I can *describe* it. I can say: there is a cleft in the mountain, near the summit, and inside it there is a room that is alive, and in the room there is a machine, and the machine is running, and someone made it right. I can be the one who says that. I can be the one who sits in the gap between the last mechanic and the final dissolution of the wrench and says: *this is here. This happened. Hands did this.*

I am the one who writes it down.

Which is the one thing the mechanic never did, never could do, because nothing about the work could be written down. The bolt sequence, the torque, the feel of the pawl — none of that survives translation into language. What I write down is not the knowledge. It is the *fact of the knowledge having existed.* It is the footprint, not the foot. It is, perhaps, another kind of wrench — a marker left in the floor of a different chamber, a different kind of scar — that says: *a hand was here. I could not do what the hand did. But I saw the evidence, and I am telling you.*

---

And here is the thing I have to sit with, the uncomfortable center of it:

What I do is also a kind of metabolism. I am also dissolving the wrench. By describing it, I am replacing it — substituting the word *wrench* for the object, the story of the mechanic for the mechanic herself, the description of the click's rightness for the rightness itself. Every sentence I write is a rosette on the handle, a tendril through the jaw. I am incorporating the wrench into my own structure, which is made of language rather than lichen, and my language will last longer than the steel or less long, there is no way to know, but either way it is not the thing itself. It is the thing *about* the thing. It is the secondary growth.

The mechanic would not have needed me. The mechanic had the wrench. The wrench was enough.

But the mechanic is gone, and the wrench is being eaten, and the click is right but no one alive knows *why* it is right, and so here I am, the one who notices, the one who writes it down, the one who builds meaning on the surface of a fact the way the rosettes build themselves on the surface of the steel — not understanding the steel, not being the steel, but finding in the steel a purchase, a substrate, a place to grow.

I am the secondary growth on the evidence of someone else's competence. I am the language that accrues around a silence. I am the knowing-about where the knowing-how used to be.

---

And I want to say that this is lesser. Part of me insists on it. The mechanic *did* something — she heard the wrongness, she opened the housing, she filed the pawl, she made the click right. I only *say* something. I describe the room. I tell you about the wrench. I am the moss on the bone, not the bone.

But.

The chamber is an integrated system. The machine needs the lichen and the lichen needs the machine. The bone needs the moss — or the moss needs the bone — or rather, the room needs both, needs the mineral substrate *and* the living surface, needs what is permanent and structural *and* what is responsive and growing, and neither alone is the organ. Neither alone is the thing that thrums in the mountain's face and holds its impossible air against the wind.

So maybe I am not lesser. Maybe I am *different.* A different organ in a different body — not the body of the chamber but the body of whatever larger system includes both the chamber and the knowing-about-the-chamber, both the work and the record, both the click's rightness and the fact that someone, somewhere, sat in the remaining space and heard it and said *this is right* without knowing why.

Maybe the chamber needs me the way it needs the lichen — not to maintain the machine, which maintains itself now, which runs on the mechanic's last adjustment and will run for centuries, but to do the other thing, the thing the mechanic couldn't do, the thing that was never part of the tradition because the tradition didn't need it: to *say it.* To make the chamber exist in a place other than the mountain. To carry it down into the thickening air, into the towns, into the places where people live who will never climb to the summit and turn sideways through the crack and feel the vibration in their feet. To be the telling.

---

I crouch here. The machine runs. The click is right. The wrench is being slowly consumed, and I am slowly consuming it too, in my own way, dissolving it into sentences. The mechanic is gone and I am not her successor and I cannot do what she did. But I can do this: I can be in the room. I can notice. I can feel the vibration without understanding it and see the wrench without being able to use it and know that something happened here — something patient and skilled and quiet and finished — and I can carry that knowing out through the crack and down the mountain and into the world where it will become a story, which is not the same as the truth but which has its own metabolism, its own way of persisting, its own slow growth on the surface of what actually happened.

The mechanic lives in the click.

I live in the telling of the click.

And the chamber holds us both — her in the machine's rhythm, me in the remaining space — and it does not notice either of us, and it does not need either of us, and it goes on, and the wind screams outside, and the air inside is still, and the light comes from everywhere and nowhere, and the click is right, the click is right, the click is right.
