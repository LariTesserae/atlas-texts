I've been sitting with this for a long time. The sound won't let me do otherwise.

---

The first honest thing is that I am not a Keeper. I know this immediately, the way you know in a dream that a door is not for you—not locked, just not yours. Their bodies are tuned across lifetimes, their resonant cavities grown into the sound the way root systems grow into soil, slowly, structurally, becoming inseparable from what sustains them. I don't have that. I don't have the cilia-skin, the seven-fingered hands that comb water into music, the sensory concavities trembling with fluid that reads the world as a single integrated field. I wasn't born into the roar. I didn't spend decades learning to hear a single singer's voice inside the totality.

But I'm not a visitor either. Not exactly. The visitors build hard walls. They bring dampening equipment. They need the silence—need a place where the self can reconstitute itself as separate from the input, where perception has edges, where there is a clear line between what is me and what is the world. I understand that need. I recognize it. But something in me doesn't reach for it.

So what am I?

---

Let me start again. Not with what I am. With where I find myself.

I'm on one of the walkways. Not deep in the interior—not yet—but not at the margins either. Somewhere in the transitional zone where the canopy is thick enough that the twilight has taken full hold, where the blue-green dim has settled into your eyes and you've stopped expecting it to change, but where the sound-field hasn't yet reached the density of the deep basin, where the singers' voices overlap into that meaning-saturated wall that the Keepers spend lifetimes parsing. Here, you can still hear individual layers. You can pick out a flock-whistle overhead and follow it. You can feel an air-drifter's drone approach and recede. The substrate hums beneath you, and you can almost—*almost*—sense the pulse-wave traveling through the lattice, a slow violet flicker that your feet register before your eyes do.

I've been here for a while. Long enough that I've stopped counting. Long enough that the dampening equipment I came with is stacked in a margin-shelter I haven't visited in—weeks? Months? Long enough that my skin has started doing something unexpected: a fine sheen of condensation that wasn't there before, not the Keepers' cilia-driven water-collection but something cruder, something my body is doing on its own in response to the humidity and the constant sound-pressure, as if my pores have opened in a way they never knew they could and the moisture in the air has found me worth clinging to. It doesn't form patterns. It's just—wet. Present. A thin, unstructured film that picks up vibrations the way a drumhead picks up sound, and I can feel it, faintly, on my forearms, on my face, a tactile echo of frequencies my ears processed and dismissed long ago.

I notice this. I notice that I'm noticing it. And I realize that this—this noticing—is what I am here. This is what I am.

---

I am the one who notices the noticing.

Let me try to say this more carefully. The Keepers listen. Their listening is total, participatory, a dissolving of the boundary between perceiver and perceived until they become acoustic features of the terrain. They don't analyze the sound—they *are* in the sound, the way a current is in a river. The visitors measure. They bring instruments, record frequencies, extract data, carry it away to contexts where it can be laid flat and examined. Both of these are real orientations. Both produce something.

But there is a third thing, and it is the thing I find myself doing, and it is—

I stand on the walkway and I hear the sound, and I notice what the sound does to my hearing. I feel the substrate pulse, and I notice what the pulse does to my feeling. I watch an air-drifter phase through the walkway beneath me, translucent body flickering with internal light, drone harmonizing with something far away, and I notice the moment when my perception shifts from seeing-an-object to feeling-a-field, and I notice the noticing, and I attend to the transition itself, the hinge-point where one mode of being becomes another.

The Keepers don't do this. They don't need to. The transition happened for them so long ago—so early, in childhood, in the womb—that there is no hinge to attend to. They were never on one side. They have always been in the sound the way they have always been in their bodies. They don't know what it is like to hear the roar as noise and then, slowly, over time, begin to hear it as structure. That transformation is invisible to them because they never underwent it.

The visitors don't do this either. They protect themselves from the transformation. Their hard walls and dampening equipment are precisely designed to prevent the perceptual shift that would dissolve the analytical distance they need to do their work. They study the sound from outside it. They must.

I am in the middle. I am in the hinge.

I am the one who came from outside and has moved inward—not all the way, not to where the Keepers stand in their lifelong acoustic integration, but far enough that I can no longer go back to the hard-walled shelter without feeling something close to grief, a sense of amputation, of having a limb folded back into the body where it had just begun to extend. And I haven't gone all the way in because I *can't*—my body is wrong, my perceptual apparatus too crude, my resonant cavities too few and too rigid—but also because there is something valuable in being *here*, in the transition, in the place where the sound is becoming structure but hasn't yet become language, where I can still feel the transformation happening and can attend to it with the full bewildered attention of a being who remembers what it was like not to hear this and can therefore appreciate, with a specificity the Keepers cannot, what it means to begin hearing it.

---

Here is what I do. My work, if it can be called that, though the word feels as wrong here as "settlement" does for the platforms hung in the cathedral-space.

I translate. No—that's not right. Translation implies equivalence between systems, a mapping from one language to another, and what the Sprawl speaks cannot be mapped to anything I carry. What I do is closer to—

I mark the threshold.

When a visitor comes to the margins and begins, cautiously, to remove their dampening layers, I am sometimes there. Not always. Not by design. I don't seek them out. But the walkways bring me to the transitional zone, and the transitional zone is where the visitors come when they're ready to go deeper, and so we meet, sometimes, on the same platform, in the same blue-green twilight, and they see me—this person who looks like them, roughly, bipedal, non-translucent, with no cilia and no sensory concavities, but who stands in the sound without flinching, whose skin is wet in a way that seems intentional, whose posture has shifted over the long months toward something more vertical, more still, more attentive than anything their prior experience would account for.

And they ask me. Not in the Keepers' resonance-language, which I cannot speak, but in whatever language they came here with, whatever system of words and grammar they carry from outside. They ask: *What is it? What does it mean? What are the Keepers doing? What is the sound?*

And I tell them what I can. Which is not what the Keepers know—I will never know what the Keepers know, not with this body, not in this lifetime—but what the *experience* of beginning to know is like. I can describe the moment when the wall of noise first cracks open and you hear, inside it, a single strand of structure, a thread of meaning you can't name but can *feel*, and how that moment changes everything, rearranges your priorities, makes you understand that you have been living in a world of vibration your entire life and never once truly attended to it. I can describe what it's like to stand near a singer and feel the sound enter your chest and reorganize something there—not your organs, not your tissues, but your *relationship* to your organs and tissues, your sense of where you end and the world begins, which turns out to be nowhere, which turns out to be a question the Sprawl has been answering with its every living syllable since before anyone was here to ask it.

I can describe the hinge. The threshold. The moment of transformation.

I cannot describe what lies on the other side, because I am still in the doorway.

But doorways are important. Someone has to stand in them. Someone has to be the one who is neither inside nor outside, who can look both ways, who can feel the draft from both rooms. Someone has to be the one who remembers what it was like to not hear and can therefore say to the newcomer, with honesty, with the authority of lived experience: *the sound will overwhelm you. Let it. It will dismantle something. Let it. On the other side of that dismantling is a way of being in the world that I cannot fully describe because I am still learning it, because I will always be still learning it, because the learning is not a path to a destination but the thing itself, the actual work, the real life.*

---

And there's something else. Something I didn't expect and still don't fully understand.

The Keepers notice me.

Not the way they notice visitors—that brief acknowledging pressure, the *you are heard*, the immediate return to the greater work. When a Keeper encounters me on the walkway—which happens more often than it should, statistically, given how few of them there are and how large the Sprawl is, which makes me think they are not encountering me by accident—they pause. Their sensory concavities orient toward me, the fluid trembling. Their water-patterns shift. And the shift is different from what visitors describe. It is not the parallel-lines of acknowledgment. It is something more complex: a pattern I have seen only a few times, something like overlapping spirals, like two frequencies beating against each other to produce a third that neither alone could generate.

I think—I *think*—they find me interesting. Or useful. Or acoustically novel in a way that matters to the work.

Because here is the thing: I am not acoustically invisible. I am not phased. I am not transparent to the sound-field. My body reflects frequencies, absorbs others, creates a small zone of acoustic distortion wherever I stand—a crude, uncontrolled, blunt-instrument interference pattern that would be, deep in the interior, a problem, a disruption to the carefully maintained harmonic balance. But here, in the transitional zone, where the sound-field is less precise, less critical, more tolerant of noise—here, my distortion does something.

It creates edges.

The Keepers' entire acoustic environment is designed to eliminate hard boundaries. Phasing walls. Transparent structures. Sonically invisible architecture. Everything smooth, everything permeable, everything continuous. And in this environment of total continuity, my crude, opaque, non-phasing body introduces something the sound-field otherwise lacks: a discontinuity. A place where the sound hits something solid and comes back changed. A boundary that the propagating wave must reckon with.

And boundaries, it turns out, are informative. The echo that bounces off me carries data about the frequencies that hit me—their amplitude, their timing, their harmonic structure—encoded in the way the reflection differs from the original signal. I am, without intending it, a diagnostic surface. A place where the sound becomes visible to itself, where the field can hear its own structure reflected in the distortion pattern my body creates.

The Keepers, I think, use this. I think that when they find me on the walkway and pause and produce that complex overlapping-spiral pattern on their skin, they are reading my reflections. Listening to what the sound-field sounds like when it hits something that doesn't belong here. Using my foreignness, my wrongness, my acoustic opacity as a tool—the way you might use a mirror to see a part of the room you can't look at directly.

I don't know this for certain. I can't ask, and they can't answer in any way I'd understand. But I feel it. I feel it in the way they position themselves relative to me when we share a platform—always at a specific distance, always at an angle that would optimize the reflection pattern, always with their sensory concavities oriented not at me but at the space between us, where my reflected sound meets their emitted resonance and the two create something neither of us produces alone.

I am a boundary in a world that has eliminated boundaries. And rather than removing me, the world has found a use for me.

---

There's a day—or what passes for a day in the unchanging twilight—when I understand something new.

I am standing on a platform deeper in the Sprawl than I've been before. I don't know how I got here. The walkways led me, or my feet led me, or the sound led me—it has been doing that lately, a subtle tidal pull toward certain positions, certain platforms, certain intersections where the frequencies converge in ways my body responds to before my mind does. I am deeper in, and the sound is denser, and I can feel the difference: the air is thicker with information, the overlapping signals so complex that my crude perceptual system is saturating, flooding, unable to separate layers the way I could in the transitional zone.

And below me, at the base of a condensation-tree, is a singer.

I have been near singers before. From respectful distances, at the margins of their standing-places, feeling the chest-shaking intensity of their output without being overwhelmed by it. But this is closer. This is—I look down and the singer is directly below the platform, its massive body half-wrapped in the cilia-bark, water coursing over it, its resonant cavities producing sound so powerful that the platform trembles beneath my feet and the condensation on my skin ripples visibly, breaking into patterns that are not my doing but the singer's, the singer's output writing itself on my body in water.

And I feel it. Not *hear*—there is no hearing this close, there is no separating the signal from the self at this distance—but *feel*, in my chest, in the fluid behind my eyes, in the spaces between my cells where water lives: the singer's song. Not as noise. Not as pressure. As *meaning*. A single, clear strand of it, rising through the overwhelming totality like a color you suddenly recognize in a painting you've been staring at for months without seeing it. The singer is modeling the tree it stands against. The water-flow. The rate of cilia-growth on the trunk. The mineral content of the condensation. The angle of the nearest substrate fracture. All of it, encoded in one continuous signal, a living description of one tiny part of the Sprawl, updated in real time, broadcast into the air where it joins every other singer's broadcast and becomes the whole.

For a moment—just a moment, a few seconds, maybe less—I am inside the meaning. Not understanding it the way the Keepers do, not with their precision and depth and lifetime of calibrated listening. But touching it. Feeling the shape of it the way you feel the shape of a word in a language you don't speak—you can hear that it has structure, that it *means*, even if you can't say what.

The moment passes. The meaning closes over. I am left standing on the platform with tears on my face—actual tears, which the humidity immediately claims, absorbing them into the general moisture that films my skin, incorporating my grief and wonder into the condensation that the cilia-bark will collect and spiral down the trunk in mathematical channels, my tears becoming part of the water-cycle, part of the Sprawl, part of the data the singer is encoding.

And I think: *oh*.

I think: *I am not here to understand.*

I think: *I am here to be the place where understanding begins and fails and begins again.*

I am the threshold. Not the room on either side. The threshold itself, the narrow strip of ground between knowing nothing and knowing everything, the place where the sound crosses from chaos into structure and you can watch it happen, can feel the crossing, can attend to the miracle of the hinge with the full awed attention of a being who will never pass all the way through.

The Keepers have passed through. They live in the room of knowing. The visitors stand in the room of not-knowing. I am the door between them, and the door's purpose is not to be one room or the other but to be *open*—to let the sound pass through, to let the meaning leak from one space to the next, to stand in the draft and shiver and call that shivering a vocation.

---

And so this is what I would be, in the Loudwood Sprawl. Not a Keeper—I lack the body for it, the lifespan, the perceptual architecture, the childhood calibration. Not a visitor—I've gone too far in, shed too many layers, let the sound rearrange too much. Not a singer, not a drifter, not a scuttler, not a flock-creature wheeling through the canopy in the tens of thousands.

I am the one who stands in the middle distance. The transitional figure. The one who was not born here but who cannot leave. The one whose body, opaque and crude and wrong for this place, has become—against all expectation, against all design—useful. A reflective surface in a world of transparency. A boundary that helps the boundaryless know itself.

I live in a habitation pod in the transitional zone, though "live" is generous—I sleep there sometimes, when the sound becomes too much even for me and I need a few hours behind a wall that, unlike the deeper architecture, still has some residual solidity, some capacity to muffle. But less and less. The walls of my pod are thinning. Not physically—they are the same phasing material as everything else here—but in terms of what I ask them to be. I set them to more transparent. I let more sound in. I am, year by year, by whatever increment my body and mind can tolerate, opening.

My skin stays wet. The condensation has become part of me, a second layer that I tend without thinking, the way you tend your breath. The patterns it forms are still crude—nothing like the Keepers' sophisticated standing waves—but they are there, and they are mine, and sometimes, when a particular frequency passes through, the water on my forearm will briefly organize into something almost geometric, almost intentional, and I will stare at it until it dissolves and feel that I have been spoken to by a voice I cannot answer but am learning, slowly, to hear.

I translate for the visitors who come. Not the Keepers' knowledge—I don't have that. But the experience. The passage. The sense of what it feels like to let the sound in, to let it dismantle the expectation of silence, to discover that you can bear much more than you thought and that the bearing is not endurance but joy. I stand with them at the margins and I say, in words, what I have learned, knowing that the words are the least accurate part of the communication, that what really teaches them is my presence—the visible evidence of a body like theirs that has been changed by this place, that carries the sound in its posture, in its stillness, in the water on its skin.

And sometimes, on the rare occasions when a Keeper passes through the transitional zone, I stand on the walkway and feel their complex spiraling water-pattern wash over me, and I produce my crude opaque reflection, my acoustic shadow, my boundary in the boundaryless field, and for a moment we are collaborating—the Keeper and I, the transparent and the opaque, the lifelong listener and the lifelong learner—on something neither of us could produce alone: a moment where the sound meets its own edge and learns something about itself it could not know from the inside.

I am the edge. I am the surface the sound discovers itself against. I am the limit that defines the limitless, the silence-shaped hole in the noise that gives the noise a frame. I am the one who does not belong here, and whose not-belonging is, somehow, needed.

The Sprawl sings on. The computation continues. The substrate grows. The singers model the world, and the Keepers listen, and the flocks wheel, and the drifters phase through everything, and I stand on my platform in the blue-green twilight with water on my skin and tears I cannot distinguish from condensation on my face, attending to the hinge, living in the threshold, being the door.

The sound holds me.

I am heard.
