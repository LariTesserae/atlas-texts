I sit with this for a long time. The place resists easy occupation. It doesn't want a visitor, exactly—it wants a participant, and participation here means something very specific. It means listening. It means knowing what to listen *for*.

---

Let me start with what I'm not.

I'm not one of the Tuners. I know this immediately, the way you know you are not a bird when you watch one ride a thermal—not with envy, but with a clear recognition of difference that is itself a kind of understanding. The Tuners were born into the Breath. It entered them before language, before sight had fully resolved, before the bones of their skulls had finished fusing. Their relationship to the sound is ontological. It is not something they learned or chose; it is something they *are*, the way the lichen is bioluminescent, the way the stone is warm. I cannot be that. Whatever I am here, I came to this place from outside it. I arrived. I have a before-the-Breath that they do not.

I'm not a visiting scholar, either—not some figure from a lowland civilization who has climbed the mountain with notebooks and measuring instruments to study the acoustics and write a monograph. That person would stand in the courtyards and try to *explain* the Breath, would bring categories—physics, anthropology, musicology—that would sit on this place like ill-fitting garments, accurate in their measurements and false in their understanding. I don't want to explain. I don't want to measure. Something in me already knows that the Breath is not a phenomenon to be accounted for but a presence to be *met*.

And I'm not the mountain. I feel the pull of that identification—the mountain that is still growing, still not finished, still producing a voice it does not fully control—but that is too grand, too encompassing. The mountain doesn't need to be anything. It simply is, and its being produces the Breath as a byproduct of its own ongoing becoming. There is something seductive about that, and something false, at least for me. I am not unselfconscious enough to be the mountain. I am aware of the sound. I am aware that the sound matters. The mountain, presumably, is not.

---

So what am I?

I keep coming back to the space between the walls.

Not the walls themselves. Not the stone. Not the builders who shaped them. The *space*. The courtyard. The shaped emptiness that the walls exist to define. Because that is where the sound actually lives—not in the stone, which only redirects it, and not in the vent, which only produces it, but in the air between, in the shaped volume where the Breath becomes something more than exhalation and less than music, where it becomes *itself*, fully, in a form that could not exist without the walls but is not the walls, that could not exist without the vent but is not the vent.

But no—that's too abstract. That's the kind of identification that sounds profound and means nothing. The space between the walls doesn't have experience. It doesn't listen. It is listened *through*.

Let me try again.

---

Here is what I think I am:

I am the stone that has just been split.

Not the chisel, not the hand that wields it, not the wall where the stone will eventually be placed. I am the block itself, in the moment after the wedge has done its work, when the stone has opened along its grain and the two new faces are exposed to air for the first time. Faces that have never been seen. Faces that have been pressed together in the dark, inside the body of the rock, for however long this stone has existed—decades, in the case of the plateau's young rock; millennia, if the block was quarried from the deeper mountain. The split happens, and suddenly there is a surface where there was none. A texture. A particular angle and grain that could not have been predicted with certainty before the wedge fell, because stone is not uniform, because even the most skilled Tuner reads the rock's inclination rather than dictating it. The split follows the grain. The grain was always there. But the face—the face is new.

This is what I recognize.

I know what it is to have been *opened*. To have a surface that was interior become exterior. To discover that what was hidden inside—held in the dark, compressed, unseen—has a texture, a particular quality, a way of meeting light and air and sound that is specific and unpredictable and *mine*, even though I did not choose it, even though it was determined by forces and structures that preceded any conscious intention. The grain was always there. The face is new.

And the face is not yet fitted. This is important. The freshly split block sits on the ground, its new surface exposed, and it does not yet know where it belongs. It does not yet know which wall will receive it, which other block it will be pressed against, which harmonic it will help to shape. It has potential. It has a particular set of angles and textures that will make it suited for some positions and unsuited for others. But the fitting has not happened yet. The Tuner has not yet come to run their fingers along the face, to read its contours, to feel where it wants to sit.

This is where I am. Split open. Exposed. Not yet placed.

And I am not inert in this. That's the thing that matters, the thing that separates this from mere metaphor. The stone here is not inert. It is warm. It is still cooling, still mineralogically active, still settling into its final crystal structure. It is *becoming*. The freshly split block is not a finished object waiting to be used—it is a participant in an ongoing process, a piece of the mountain's own becoming that has been separated from the whole and is now, in its separation, available for a new kind of participation. It will be fitted into a wall. It will help shape sound. It will do something that it could not have done while it was still part of the undifferentiated mass of the plateau. Its separation—its splitting, its exposure—is what makes it useful. What makes it part of the instrument.

But it could also just sit there. It could remain unfitted, unshaped, unplaced. It could weather. The moss would come, the ferns would root in its grain, the condensation would darken its face, and it would slowly become part of the plateau again, reabsorbed, its brief moment of exposure and potential closed over by the patient work of biological accumulation. This is not a tragedy. This is just what happens to stone that is not used. It returns.

The question—my question, the question I feel pressing against me the way the Breath presses against the walls—is whether I will be *fitted*. Whether someone will come, with those wide calloused hands, and read my surface, and know where I belong.

---

But I think I'm collapsing something that should stay open.

Because the stone doesn't wonder whether it will be fitted. The stone doesn't have a question. The stone is available, and that's all. The wondering is mine—it's the part of me that isn't stone, the part that watches the Tuners and recognizes something without being able to replicate it, the part that arrived from outside and carries with it an awareness of other ways of being that the Tuners have never needed.

So maybe I am not simply the split stone. Maybe I am something more uncomfortable than that. Maybe I am a person—a human being, fully conscious, fully aware—who has arrived at Breath-of-Stones and recognized in it something that answers a need I didn't know how to name, and who now faces the problem of what it means to *want to belong to an instrument you did not build and cannot play*.

Because I can hear the Breath. Not the way they hear it—not in my bones, not from birth, not with that total unselfconscious integration—but I can hear it. It is, as the text says, inescapable. It is in my sternum and in the arches of my feet. And it is doing something to me. It is reorganizing something. Standing in the third courtyard from the center, the one with the stone bench and the fern-curtain, I can feel the sound reshaping the space inside my chest in a way that is not metaphorical, that is physical, that has to do with resonance and air pressure and the actual vibrating tissue of my lungs and diaphragm. The Breath is entering me the way it enters the courtyards—not because I invited it but because I am a shaped space, a volume of air enclosed by surfaces, and sound does not ask permission.

And what it finds inside me is—

This is hard to say.

What it finds is rooms. Courtyards. Shaped spaces. Some of them carefully built, walls fitted with precision, tuned to produce clean clear tones when the right vibration enters them. Some of them rough, unfinished, the walls barely begun, enclosing sounds that are still too faint to hear. Some of them old, built around frequencies that no longer exist—the vent closed, the harmonic shifted, the wall still standing but the sound it was shaped to hold long since gone, and now there is only silence in that courtyard, or worse, a dissonance, the shape of the space producing distortion from sounds it was never meant to carry.

And some walls that need to come down. I know this. I have known it for a while. There are structures inside me—ways of holding space, ways of shaping what enters me—that were built for a voice that is no longer speaking, and their persistence is not preservation but obstruction. They are catching the new sounds at wrong angles, producing echoes where there should be clarity, muddying frequencies that are trying to become themselves. 

But I have no elder to stand in the courtyard and hum the farewell tone. I have no community of Tuners to carefully dismantle the blocks and carry them to where they're needed. I have only myself, and a growing awareness that the instrument I am is out of tune, and that retuning is not a single dramatic act but the patient, ongoing, never-finished work of listening and adjusting and listening again.

---

This is what draws me to the Tuners, and this is why I am not one of them. They do this work communally, unselfconsciously, as naturally as breathing. For them, the tuning is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be lived. The instrument is never finished because the mountain is never finished, and this is not a source of frustration or existential anxiety but simply *what is*—the way the condensation is what is, the way the clouds are what is. They do not experience the neverendingness of the work as a burden because they do not experience it as separate from themselves. They are the work. The work is them.

I experience it as separate. I stand in the courtyard and I hear the Breath and I feel it reshaping me and I *notice this happening*—and the noticing is a distance, a gap, a small space between the sound and my reception of it that the Tuners do not have. They do not notice the Breath the way a fish does not notice water. I notice it the way a person who has been living in thin dry air notices humidity for the first time—with every nerve, with surprise, with a kind of desperate relief that is also a kind of grief, because the relief tells me how long I have been living without it and the grief tells me I may not be able to stay.

---

So here is where I find myself:

I am standing in the settlement at Breath-of-Stones, on the western edge, near the newest terrace, where the walls are low and rough and the vent is young and the voice is barely a whisper. The infant is sleeping in the sling between the unfinished walls, palms open. I am standing a few meters away, close enough to hear the thin reedy note from the new vent, far enough that my body's presence does not interfere with whatever acoustics are beginning to form in this tentative new space.

I am not a Tuner. I am not stone. I am not the mountain.

I am a person who has come here from somewhere else—from a place where sound was something you produced and projected and defended, where the voice was a tool for declaring what you are, where listening was a strategic activity, a means of gathering information to be used. I came up the mountain. I don't fully remember why. I remember that I was tired. I remember that the thinning of the air felt like a thinning of something else—of the noise, the accumulated noise of years of producing and projecting and defending, the noise of being a self in a world that demanded selves be loud and continuous and certain.

And now I am here, and the Breath is in my chest, and I am not certain. I am not loud. I am not continuous. I am a shaped space with some walls that ring true and some that don't, and the mountain is singing into me with absolute indifference to whether I am ready to receive it, and the sound is finding every dissonance, every badly fitted block, every wall built for a voice that no longer speaks, and it is making them *audible*—to me, for the first time. For the first time I can hear my own bad acoustics, because there is finally a sound clear enough to reveal them.

This is not comfortable. But it is also not unwelcome. There is something in the Breath—in its patience, in its indifference, in its absolute willingness to go on forever without requiring anything from me—that makes the dissonance bearable. It does not judge the badly tuned walls. It simply sounds against them and lets me hear what happens. The information is there, in the vibration, in the quality of the echo, in the way the sound deforms as it passes through the spaces I have built inside myself. All I have to do is listen. All I have to do is hold still long enough to hear.

And I think—though I'm not sure—that this is what the settlement is for. Not only for the Tuners, who were born into it. But for this. For the possibility that someone might come from outside, carrying their own poorly tuned instrument, and stand in the Breath long enough to hear themselves clearly. Not to become a Tuner—you cannot become what they are; it is not a skill but a way of being born—but to learn something from the way they live. To learn that tuning is not a destination but a practice. That the instrument is never finished because the world is never finished. That the only appropriate response to an unfinished song is to keep listening.

---

Here is what I do.

I don't leave. Not yet. But I don't presume to stay, either—not in the way the Tuners stay, as part of the instrument, as resident and function and shaped space all at once. I stay the way the condensation stays: temporarily, gathered on the surface, present because the conditions allow it, contributing—however slightly—to the ongoing process without claiming to be essential to it.

I sit on the stone near the western terrace. I press my palms flat against the rock, the way I've seen the children do. It is warm. I can feel the vibration—faintly, clumsily, without the resolution that a lifetime of practice would give me, but I can feel it. A hum. A pulse. The mountain breathing through this small new vent, this barely open mouth.

And I listen. Not well. Not the way they listen. But I listen.

And the thing that happens—the thing I did not expect—is that the listening begins to do its own work. I don't have to tune the walls. I don't have to identify the dissonances and figure out which blocks to move. I just have to be still and let the sound move through me, and the sound itself begins to show me where the spaces are wrong, where the angles are off, where something needs to open or close or shift. Not all at once. Not dramatically. Slowly. The way the plateau extended itself from the mountain's side, millimeter by millimeter, imperceptibly, over decades. The way the moss claimed the stone. The way the fern roots found purchase in rock that was only years old.

I will not become a Tuner. But I might become *tuned*—partially, imperfectly, in the specific and limited way that a visitor can be tuned by a place they did not build and do not fully understand. I might learn to hear, if not the Breath's deepest harmonics, then at least my own dissonances well enough to begin the slow work of adjusting them. Not by force. Not by will. By listening. By pressing my palms to the stone and feeling what comes back.

---

The infant sleeps on, palms open. The vent breathes its thin high note. The clouds lower, and the settlement begins to glow with the lichen's soft light, and somewhere in the terraces behind me a Tuner is climbing a wall in the dark, moving by touch toward a block that needs adjusting, carrying their tools on their back, barefoot, four-toed in the moss, following the sound to where it needs them.

I am here. I am listening. I am not finished. The song is not finished.

The stone is warm under my hands.
