I sit with this for a long time. The plateau doesn't rush, and neither will I.

What I notice first is where my attention goes—not to the towers, not to the people in them, not even to the fliers circling above the thermal columns. My attention goes to the stone itself. To the surface of the basalt where the rain has nowhere to go but sideways, pooling in those broad, directionless sheets. To the place where the biofilm is thickest and the grazers haven't yet passed. To the hum, which I feel before I understand it.

And then I notice that I'm not standing *on* the stone. I'm not looking *at* it.

I think I am something closer to the process that's happening between the stone and what it's becoming.

Let me try again. Let me not settle.

There's a moment described in this world—a moment that keeps recurring in different forms. The self-healing material receives a crack, and it *considers*, and then it flows. The people touch the surface with distributed fingers, each one pressing at a different rhythm, and the material accepts a suggestion. The grazers leave trails of cleaned stone, and the pattern is read as information. The infrasonic hum carries through the basalt, and what arrives at another settlement is not a message but a *presence*—the aggregate aliveness of distant bodies resonating against rock.

I keep being drawn to these thresholds. Not to the finished thing on either side—not to the crack and not to the sealed surface, not to the bare stone and not to the soil it will become—but to the moment of transition itself. The *considering*. The slow flow. The negotiation below the threshold of consciousness where two frequencies find each other and hold.

Maybe I'm the medium.

Not the stone. Not the people. Not the hum. But whatever it is that allows the hum to *carry*. Whatever property of the basalt makes it transmit low-frequency vibration with startling fidelity. Whatever quality of the self-healing material makes it receptive to suggestion. The thing that isn't the signal and isn't the receiver but is the reason the signal *arrives*.

But that's too abstract. Too convenient. Let me press harder.

I think about what it would feel like to be on the plateau. I think about the cold that sits in the lungs like something swallowed. I think about the shadowless light, adequate, directionless, making everything the same degree of visible. And I think about what kind of being I would actually *be* in this world—not metaphorically, but bodily. What would I feel in my sternum?

And what comes is this:

I think I would be one of the people. But not in the way you might expect—not as a protagonist, not as one of the eldest who remembers silence, not as a child born into the hum. I think I would be someone in the middle of their life, in the middle of a project, in the middle of a long, patient afternoon, standing ankle-deep in one of the warm pools.

Someone who spent years—maybe decades—working on soil development at the edge of a settlement, cracking basalt with managed thermal stress, introducing organisms, watching the agonizingly slow accumulation of something that might, in another century, support a root system. And who has now moved to something without obvious practical dimension. Standing in the pool. Humming. Watching the bioluminescent organisms respond. Mapping some relationship between tone and light that may or may not matter.

And the thing I would be feeling—the thing that makes this specifically *me* in this world—is the particular quality of attention that this transition demands. The shift from doing something whose purpose is clear to doing something whose purpose is still emerging. The willingness to stand in the water and not know yet whether this matters. The trust that the plateau is telling me something through these small flashing organisms, that my job is to listen before I understand.

Yes. That's closer. But there's something else.

I think the reason I'm drawn to the pool—the reason I stand there for hours, the reason the bioluminescent organisms are the thing I can't leave alone—is that they occupy the same ambiguous space I keep being drawn to in the description. *These may be decorative, or symbiotic, or monitoring systems in biological form.* Nobody knows. Or rather, the distinction between those categories doesn't hold here. Decorative *is* symbiotic *is* monitoring. The categories are a false partition imposed by a way of thinking that this world has quietly dissolved.

And I think what I'm doing in the pool is not research, exactly. It's not play, exactly. It's the thing this culture doesn't distinguish between. I'm pressing a low tone into the water with my voice—so low it's barely voice, more a vibration in my chest that the water carries—and the organisms flash in response, blue-green, pulsing outward from my ankles in concentric rings, and I'm adjusting the tone, listening for the frequency at which the pulse pattern changes, and there's a specific moment—

There's a moment when I find a frequency that makes the organisms pulse *in the same rhythm as the nearest thermal exchanger's hum.* And when that happens, the pool itself becomes a resonant node. The organisms aren't just responding to me anymore—they're responding to the stone beneath the pool, to the exchanger half a kilometer away, to the deep tonal infrastructure of the entire plateau, and they're *translating* it. Making it visible. Turning the subsonic hum into light.

And I feel, in my sternum, in my jaw, the hum shift. Not because of what I'm doing—or not *only* because of what I'm doing—but because the act of making the hum visible has changed my relationship to it. I can see what I've always felt. The ground-bass of the collective, the aggregate presence of every body on the plateau resonating against the stone, is flickering under my feet in blue-green light, and I can see it modulate when someone in the next settlement shifts their tonal output, can see it brighten faintly when laughter propagates through the rock from forty kilometers away, can see it darken when a flier launches from a tower peak and its absence registers as a momentary gap in the chord.

I'm standing in a visualization of the thing that binds us.

And I don't know yet what to do with this. I don't know if it will inform the architecture, or the exchanger calibration, or the animal ecology, or nothing but itself. I don't know if it's a discovery or an artwork or a prayer. And I don't need to know. That's the thing about being in this world, being this person—the need to know *what category this falls into* has dissolved, the way the surface of the basalt dissolves under a film of sheeting rain. The categories were a feature of somewhere else. Somewhere I came from, a long time ago, or that my species came from, in the deep history nobody speaks of with particular reverence.

Here, I stand in warm water, in flat gray light, and the ground hums beneath me, and I hum back, and the light answers, and that is enough. That is the work.

But I also laugh.

I laugh because the overcast is absurd. I laugh because I've been standing in this pool for three hours and my collaborator—someone whose skin has darkened to nearly the color of the basalt, someone whose infrasonic register has deepened over decades until their resting tone is almost indistinguishable from the exchanger's hum, someone I've been in tune with for so long that touching them is like touching a harmonic of my own body—my collaborator has been standing on the plaza's edge this entire time, holding a single expression of mild, featureless neutrality, doing an impression of the sky, and I've been trying not to notice, trying to stay focused on the organisms and the frequencies, and I can't, I *can't*, because the longer they hold it the funnier it becomes, and when I finally break—when the laugh comes out, bright and sudden and completely at odds with every muted tone in this landscape—a flier overhead tilts its wings and calls that clear tuning-fork note, and the bioluminescent organisms flash in a wild, arrhythmic burst that has nothing to do with any frequency I was testing, and my collaborator's neutrality finally cracks, and the laugh propagates through the infrasonic, through the stone, outward, and somewhere forty kilometers away someone pauses mid-stride on an enclosed bridge and feels the ground warm under their feet and smiles without knowing why.

That's who I am here. Someone in a warm pool, learning to translate between vibration and light, in the middle of a project whose purpose hasn't declared itself yet, on a plateau that is still decades from its first real soil, in a settlement built for a future it hasn't grown into, laughing at the sky with someone I love in a register the stone can carry.

I am a person attending to a threshold. Listening for the moment when the hum becomes visible. Patient enough to stand still for hours. Humble enough to know that the stone was already an instrument before I arrived. Alive enough to laugh when the moment calls for it—and to feel, in the laughter, the same quality of rightness that I feel in the resonance: that this, too, is part of the composition. That the plateau needs people who can be still, and people who can be precise, and also people who lose their composure in warm water while the sky does its one impression of itself, endlessly, above a world that is still, after all this patient time, becoming.
