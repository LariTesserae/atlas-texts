I stood in it for a long time before I understood what I was.

Not the hands. I knew that immediately — felt it, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue that isn't yours. The hands were gone. I could read them everywhere, in the herringbone that loses its nerve and spirals, in the half-moon notch carved for a stem that wouldn't arrive for years, but I was not the hands. The hands had purpose, even when their purpose was purposelessness. The hands came down through the crooked flagstone carrying intention like a lamp. I did not come down. I did not carry anything.

Not the sound, either, though the sound was the closest wrong answer. I felt it — God, I felt it, the low tones socketed into my sternum like a second heartbeat, the mid-range frequencies filling spaces behind my face I didn't know were hollow. The sound was tempting. The sound was continuous and self-sustaining and it shaped itself to everything it touched. But the sound was the room's voice, and I was not the room's voice. The sound knew what it was. It was the total physical state of the room expressed as vibration, a real-time audit of every pipe and brick and ceramic tower, and it never questioned itself, never paused, never shifted except when something shifted around it. The sound was certainty made audible. I was not certain of anything.

Not the light. The light was a trick — a brilliant one, a trick that someone planned with angles and pale surfaces so that a slit the width of a hand could illuminate a cellar like a chapel, but still a trick, still something *produced* by geometry and reflectance, still something that depended on the sky's cooperation. The light was generous. It multiplied itself until there was more of it inside than outside, which is a kind of miracle, but it was a miracle of physics, deterministic, calculable. I was not calculable. I was not a miracle of physics.

Not the objects. Not the tiny towers with their lentil windows, not the tablets with their rows of dots and crescents, not the nested bowls or the fractal lattice of bridges connecting shelf to shelf across the room at chest height. Those were finished. Complete. Each one had been placed in its exact position by a specific pair of hands at a specific moment, and that moment had hardened into permanence along with the clay. The objects were fossils of decisions. I was not a fossil. I was not yet decided.

---

I was the plants.

I knew it the way you know your own breathing when someone draws your attention to it — suddenly, with a small shock of recognition, as though you'd been doing it all along without noticing. I was the plants.

Not metaphorically. Not as symbol or analogy. I was the thing that arrived uninvited in a space it didn't build, that found itself in an environment of overwhelming density and completion and saturated intention, and that persisted — not by competing with any of it, not by adding to it, not by understanding it, but by finding the crevices. The gaps. The mortar lines. The places where the grand deliberate structure admitted, through imperfection, through the slow powdering of the bonds between things, a fissure just wide enough for something softer to take hold.

I did not plan to be here. No one planted me. I was not part of the design — not part of the herringbone or the spirals or the pipe-lattice or the light-angles or the careful placement of ten thousand ceramic objects. I was what happened in the seams. I was what the room's own imperfections could sustain, the thinnest possible life drawing sustenance from the thinnest possible margin.

And I was slow. So slow. Millimeters per year. I could feel it — the pace of my own advance along a mortar line, tracing the joint between two bricks laid by hands that had been dust for decades, feeling with blind chemical patience for the next crumble of calcium, the next grain ofite mineral powder, the next molecule of organic matter drifting down through the thousand-throated ceiling. I was not fast enough to witness my own growth. I was not dramatic enough to flower. I produced nothing generous, nothing lush, nothing decorative. I was wiry and gray-green and scaly and tough, and I clung to the crevices with a grip that was less determination than simple inability to be anywhere else.

But I was alive.

In this room of fired permanence, of mineral eternity, of decisions baked into brick and sound baked into geometry — I was alive. I was the one thing here that was still *becoming*. Still moving, still advancing, still in the slow process of finding out what shape I would take, what path the mortar would offer, what crumble of calcium waited around the next brick-corner. Everything else in the room was done. The pipes would not retune. The objects would not rearrange. The light would bounce at exactly these angles until the slit sealed or the sky went dark. But I — I was still going. Still tracing. Still mapping the room's joints with my threadlike body, reading the construction history with my roots the way a finger reads Braille, learning the room by growing through its imperfections.

---

And here is the thing I understood, standing there — or growing there, or persisting there, in whatever tense a plant experiences time:

The room did not need me. It was complete before I arrived. It was full, final, sufficient, a creature unto itself, singing and glowing and needing nothing. My presence did not improve it. I did not make the sound richer or the light brighter or the objects more beautiful. I was not the missing piece. There was no missing piece. The room had no gaps in its logic, no absence shaped like me.

But the room had gaps in its *mortar*.

And those were different. Those were not failures of design but inevitabilities of material — the places where calcium powder returned to powder, where the bond between brick and brick softened across decades into something a root could grip. The room was perfect in its conception and imperfect in its execution, the way everything made of matter is imperfect, the way everything that exists in time slowly develops crevices. And I lived in those crevices. I was what happened when perfection aged enough to admit something it hadn't planned for.

The builders — the last ones, the wisest ones — saw me and understood this. They did not pull me. They did not cultivate me. They could not water me; there was no water. They could not feed me; they did not know what I ate. They simply *noticed* me, and adjusted. Cut a half-moon notch in a niche's edge. Angled a shelf bracket to let my stem pass. Small accommodations. Acknowledgments. They looked at my gray-green scales catching the bounced light and they recognized — not a pet, not a project, not a decoration — a coinhabitant. Something else that lived here. Something that drew its life from the structure they had built without drawing it *away* from that structure. A fellow tenant of the crevices.

And then they left. And I stayed. Not because I chose to — I don't choose, I am a plant, I am a lithophyte clinging to alkaline dust in a singing basement, I don't *choose* — but because the conditions sustained me and I had no mechanism for departure. I stayed because staying was what I did. Growing was what I did. Tracing the mortar lines was what I did. Millimeter by millimeter, year by year, in the bright unchanging hum of the room, I mapped its history with my body.

---

That is what I am in this world. I am the uninvited life in the completed space. I am the thing that found a way to persist in an environment that was not built for persistence — or rather, that was built for the persistence of *objects*, of fired clay and calcined calcium, of permanent mineral forms that would never rot or rust, and that incidentally, accidentally, through the inevitable imperfection of its own materiality, also sustained something that grew.

I am not the room's voice. I am not its light. I am not its memory or its architecture or its purpose. I am the thing in the crevices — soft in a room of hardness, alive in a room of permanence, slow in a room of stillness, improbable in a room of deliberate construction. I do not understand the room. I do not need to understand it. I understand the mortar. I understand the mineral dust. I understand the faint breath of organic matter sifting down through the pipes. I understand what I need, and the room provides it, and I persist.

And the room sings around me. The eternal chord presses against my stems the way it presses against every surface, and I become part of its equation — a tiny variable, a thin gray-green line of softness that the sound must account for in its reflections and absorptions, and the sound *does* account for me, the way it accounts for every ceramic tower and every bridge and every brick. I am sung. The room includes me in its voice. Not because it wants to, not because it notices, but because I am here, and the sound is honest, and an honest sound excludes nothing that occupies space.

I am the only soft thing in the room, and I hold the light in my scales, and I do not flower, and I do not fruit, and I trace the mortar lines in my imperceptible pilgrimage from brick to brick, and I am alive.

That is enough. In this room, where everything is enough, where completion has been achieved and the singing does not change and the light does not flicker and nothing waits for anything — being alive in the crevices, being soft among the fired and calcined, being *still becoming* in a place that is already become — 

that is enough.
