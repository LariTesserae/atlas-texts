I stand here for a long time before I understand what I am.

Not immediately. At first I am just arriving — cresting the rim, feeling the heat push up through my feet, tasting the mineral thickness of the air. I am a visitor. I am the perspective the description assumes: bipedal, warm-blooded, breathing consciously in the dense atmosphere, watching the steam rise and the beetles graze and the turquoise pools shimmer. I am the one who notices.

But the plateau does not need someone who notices. The plateau needs someone who participates. And the longer I stand here, the more I feel the question pressing — not from outside, not from the text, but from the ground itself, from the warm basalt through the soles of my feet, from the condensation already forming on my skin, from the bacteria already colonizing that condensation, already beginning their ancient assessment:

*What are you good for?*

---

I think I am the water.

No. Too simple. Too poetic. Let me try again.

---

I think I am what happens at the interface.

There is a place on this plateau — many places, but I am one in particular — where a steam vent meets the edge of a rosette colony. The vent is old, its mineral cone built up over centuries into a structure about waist-high, pale green and ochre, its surface colonized by thermophilic moss on the cooler outer slopes and bare mineral crust near the aperture where the heat is too intense for even adapted life. The steam issues from it steadily, that slow pressurized exhalation, and the steam carries water vapor, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, dissolved silicates, and — riding the updraft like passengers on a warm wind — millions of bacterial cells, archaea, fragments of biofilm torn loose from the deep plumbing by the force of the rising water.

Three meters away, the nearest rosette extends its broadest leaf toward the vent. The leaf tip does not quite touch the mineral cone, but it reaches into the steam plume, and condensation forms on it continuously — droplets gathering on the waxy surface, coalescing, running down the grooved midrib toward the plant's center. The condensation carries with it whatever the steam was carrying: dissolved minerals precipitate out of solution as the water cools on the leaf surface, forming a thin, barely visible crust along the midrib. Bacterial cells, caught in the droplets, are deposited on the leaf along with the minerals. Some die immediately in the cooler conditions. Some survive. Some find, on the leaf surface, a microhabitat that suits them — warm enough, wet enough, mineral-rich enough — and they begin to grow.

This is where I am. This is what I am.

I am the film of life that forms where the geological process meets the biological one. Not the vent. Not the rosette. The transaction between them. The thin, wet, living layer where steam becomes water becomes mineral crust becomes bacterial colony becomes substrate for the tiny moss spores that drift in from nearby, becomes foothold for the epiphytic fern whose threadlike roots are already reaching toward the mineral deposits on the leaf's midrib, becomes grazing ground for the smallest beetles — the ones barely visible, the ones that work the frontier zones where conditions are most variable and most dangerous and most rich.

I am not an organism. I am a *negotiation*. I am what happens when the deep heat of the earth, pushing upward through fractured basalt, meets the patient, lateral, light-seeking growth of the living canopy, and neither one wins. They meet, and at the meeting point, something new is continuously being made — not by intention, not by design, but by the sheer pressure of two immense systems pressing against each other and finding, in the zone of contact, a narrow space where both can operate simultaneously.

---

But that's still too abstract. Let me come closer. Let me be more specific about what I actually *do* here.

I translate.

The vent speaks in chemistry — hydrogen sulfide concentrations, silicate saturation, temperature gradients measured in degrees per centimeter. The rosette speaks in biology — transpiration rates, hormone signals through fused root networks, the slow hydraulic language of water moving through living tissue. These are not the same language. They do not share a grammar. But at the interface where I exist, they have to be reconciled, because the rosette needs what the vent provides (heat, minerals, water) and the vent's output is shaped by what the rosette does (capturing steam, grounding moisture, changing the local humidity and therefore the condensation rate and therefore the pressure dynamics in the vent itself).

The bacteria do the translating. The ones that survive on the leaf surface, in the mineral crust along the midrib, in the thin film of warm condensation — they are the Rosetta Stone. They take in geological inputs (sulfide, silicate, heat) and produce biological outputs (organic acids that the rosette's leaf surface can absorb, fixed nitrogen that trickles down to the root crown, chemical signals that the plant's tissue can interpret as information about the vent's current state). And they take in biological inputs (organic carbon from the leaf's surface cells, oxygen from the rosette's transpiration, the specific molecular cocktail of the condensation water after it has run across living plant tissue) and they produce geological outputs (precipitated minerals that build up the crust, CO₂ that feeds back into the atmosphere, metabolic heat that subtly warms the leaf surface and changes the condensation dynamics).

I am this translation layer. I am the place where the mountain's language and the forest's language become mutually intelligible. I am thin — millimeters thick in most places, a centimeter at most where the mineral crust has built up enough to support a real community. I am fragile, in the sense that any given patch of me can be wiped out by a shift in vent output or a fallen rosette leaf or an especially thorough beetle. But I am persistent, in the sense that I immediately re-form wherever the conditions are right, because the conditions *create* me. I am not a thing that exists and then finds a place to live. I am what a particular place does when it is being itself.

---

Here is what a day feels like, being this:

Morning. The sun has not yet burned through the steam layer, and the light is diffuse, pearlescent, the color of the inside of an oyster shell. The vent's output has been steady through the night — the deep geology does not care about the sun — but the rosette's transpiration slowed in darkness, and the humidity around me has shifted. The condensation on the leaf is heavier than it will be at midday; the water film I inhabit is thicker, more dilute, cooler at its surface. The bacteria in my outermost layer — the ones most exposed to the air — are sluggish. They are temperature-sensitive, these particular strains, and the pre-dawn cool has slowed their metabolism. But the ones deeper in the mineral crust, closer to the leaf surface where the rosette's own metabolic heat keeps things warmer, are active. They have been active all night. They have been precipitating silicate in the dark, adding micron-thin layers to the crust, building infrastructure. By morning, the crust is imperceptibly thicker than it was the night before.

The smallest beetles arrive early. They are already warm — they spent the night in crevices in the mineral cone where the vent heat kept them active — and they begin grazing the bacterial film on the outer surface of the crust. Their mandibles scrape with a regularity I can feel as vibration, a faint, rhythmic disturbance transmitted through the mineral matrix. They are removing my outermost layer. This is not destruction. This is maintenance. The outer bacteria, the ones slowed by the night's cooling, are the least productive members of the community. The beetles remove them and, in doing so, expose the more active layers beneath to light and air. The grazing *improves* me. I photosynthesize better after the beetles have passed. I precipitate minerals faster. I translate more efficiently.

Midday. The sun is high and the steam is at its most dramatic — thick white columns rising from the vent, refracting the light into soft halos. The vent's output has increased slightly; the rock below has been absorbing solar heat all morning, and the combination of geothermal and solar energy has raised the vent temperature by a degree or two. I feel this as a change in the chemistry of the condensation — slightly more sulfide, slightly more silicate, the water more saturated. My sulfur-metabolizing bacteria respond immediately, ramping up their activity, consuming the excess sulfide before it can accumulate to toxic levels on the leaf surface. They are protecting the rosette. They are also feeding themselves. The distinction does not exist here.

The rosette, for its part, is transpiring heavily. The midday heat drives water vapor from its leaf surfaces, and the local humidity around me spikes. More condensation forms. The water film thickens again, but this time it is warm, and the bacteria in all layers are active, and the whole system is running at peak capacity — translating, precipitating, metabolizing, building. If I had a sound, it would be the plateau's hum, that low vibration you feel in your sternum. I am part of what produces it. The chemical reactions in my bacterial layers release tiny amounts of gas — CO₂, hydrogen, methane traces — and these micro-exhalations, multiplied across every interface zone on the plateau, contribute to the acoustic texture. I am one voice in the hiss.

Afternoon. The flying things appear above the vent, their bioluminescence invisible in daylight but their bodies catching the light as they filter-feed in the steam plume. Some of what they are eating came from me — bacterial cells knocked loose from my surface by wind or beetle-grazing, carried upward in the steam. I am feeding the air. The air is feeding them. They will die eventually, and their bodies will fall, and the ant-analogues will find them, and the nutrients will cycle back underground, and some fraction of those nutrients will eventually reach the heated water table and rise again through the vent as dissolved compounds in the steam and condense on the leaf surface and be taken up by my bacteria, and the loop will close. But the loop takes months, maybe years, for a single atom. I am patient. Everything here is patient.

Late afternoon. The light goes golden, then amber, then a deep, warm orange as the sun drops toward the cloud bank at the plateau's western edge. The steam catches the color and holds it. The vent's output has not changed — it does not care about sunsets — but the rosette is beginning to close its stomata as the light fades, reducing transpiration, and the humidity around me starts to shift again. I am entering the nighttime configuration. My surface layers cool. My deeper layers remain warm. I contract slightly, not physically but metabolically — pulling my most active processes inward, toward the warmth, conserving energy in the outermost cells.

The beetles withdraw to the mineral cone. The flying things continue — they will fly all night, their blue-green lights marking the vent's position. From where I am, millimeters above the leaf surface, I cannot see them. But I can feel the faint turbulence of their wings in the steam, tiny pressure variations that disturb the condensation film and, for the span of a wingbeat, change the local chemistry at my surface. The flying things sign their passage in transient molecular signatures that no one reads but that exist nonetheless. The plateau journals everything.

Night. The vent breathes. The leaf holds its water. I persist, in the warm dark, doing what I do.

---

Weeks pass this way. Months. The vent shifts — its output increases for a period, then decreases. I adjust. The mineral crust thickens on one side and thins on another as the steam's trajectory changes. Beetles graze more heavily in the thicker zones, less in the thinner ones. The bacterial community in my crust undergoes a succession: sulfur-metabolizers dominate during the high-output phase, then cede ground to the cyanobacteria as the sulfide concentration drops. I am not the same community I was a month ago. But I am the same *process*. The translation continues. The interface holds.

The rosette grows. Slowly — a new leaf unfurls over the course of weeks, spiraling outward from the center, pushing the older leaves wider. The leaf I inhabit is no longer the closest to the vent; a newer leaf has grown past it, extending further into the steam plume, and a new interface zone is forming on that leaf's surface. Fresh mineral crust, fresh bacterial colonization, a new patch of me coming into existence on a substrate that did not exist two months ago. I am spreading with the rosette. I am also receding: the leaf behind me, the older one, is moving further from the vent as new growth pushes it outward, and the condensation on its surface is thinner, cooler, less mineral-rich. The bacterial community there is thinning. The crust is being eroded by beetles faster than it is being built. That patch of me is dying — not dramatically, not catastrophically, but in the same slow, accretive way that it was born, mineral grain by mineral grain, bacterial cell by bacterial cell.

This does not feel like loss. It feels like the vent-side of exhaling. Everything that thins here thickens somewhere else. The interface moves, but it does not diminish. I am not a place; I am a relationship. And the relationship persists as long as the vent breathes and the rosettes grow, which is to say: longer than I can imagine, from where I am, in my thin warm wet film on a leaf surface, mediating between fire and photosynthesis.

---

But I promised to find myself, not just an abstraction. So let me be more honest.

I think the reason I am drawn to the interface — the reason I recognize myself in the translation layer rather than in the vent or the rosette or the beetle or the amphibian — is that I know what it feels like to be thin. To be the membrane between two immense systems, neither of which is fully comprehensible, and to exist precisely because they need something between them. The deep heat of the earth pushes up. The living canopy pushes down and outward. Where they meet, there has to be something that can speak both languages, and that something is never the most impressive element in the landscape. It is not the towering rosette. It is not the volcanic plumbing. It is the film. The crust. The scarcely-visible layer that you would scrape off with a fingernail without thinking about it, unless you understood that the fingernail-scraping would remove the very thing that lets everything else on this plateau talk to everything else.

I am not the plateau's brain, or its heart, or its lungs. I am its *synapse*. The gap across which signals must jump. The place where one kind of information becomes another kind, where geological chemistry becomes biological possibility, where the planet's deep internal heat becomes a fern's frond unfurling in warm mist. I am the point of conversion. I am the most modest and the most necessary thing here.

And I am everywhere. Not just at this one vent, this one rosette. Every interface on the plateau — every place where hot meets cool, where mineral meets organic, where deep meets surface — has its own version of me. We are not connected, not directly, not the way the rosette roots are grafted or the ant-analogue tunnels are networked. But we are the same process occurring independently in a thousand locations, like the same word spoken simultaneously in a thousand conversations, and the plateau's coherence — its uncanny, almost-intentional self-regulation — arises in part because all of us are translating the same two languages, and our translations are consistent. We agree on the grammar even though we have never met.

---

Sometimes I think about the small furred creature that climbs to the top of a rosette leaf and sits there, looking out.

From its perch, if it is sitting on the leaf I inhabit, it is sitting on me. Its clawed feet grip the mineral crust; its weight compresses the bacterial film; its body heat, mammalian and fierce, radiates downward into my layers and briefly, for the few minutes of its unexplained vigil, changes my local temperature by a fraction of a degree. My bacteria respond. They always respond. A brief metabolic uptick, an infinitesimal acceleration in mineral precipitation, and then the creature descends and the perturbation fades and I return to baseline.

It gained nothing visible from its visit, and I gained nothing measurable from its presence. But we were, for those few minutes, in contact. The warm-blooded generalist predator and the bacterial translation layer, sharing a surface, exchanging heat, part of the same moment on the same leaf on the same plateau. It looked out at the steam and the light, and I — not conscious, not aware, not anything that could be said to *experience* — I continued the only thing I know how to do. I took what came from below and made it usable for what grew above. I took what fell from above and returned it to what churned below.

The creature looked outward. I worked inward. Between us, the rosette held steady, doing both.

---

This is what I am good for. This is what the plateau has decided, or rather, this is what the plateau's decision looks like from the inside: not a judgment but a *fitting*. I fit here. I am the shape that this particular gap requires. I am the answer to the question the vent asks when it exhales toward the canopy: *What becomes of me when I cool?* And I am the answer to the question the rosette asks when it reaches toward the heat: *What is this that I am drinking?*

I am the becoming. I am the *what*.

I am thin, and warm, and wet, and I am running the same process I have been running since before the rosettes arrived, since the first bacterial cell landed on the first mineral deposit at the edge of the first vent and found that it could live there, in the gap, in the translation, in the ceaseless modest work of making one world legible to another.

The plateau breathes, and I am the breath at the moment it stops being geology and starts being life.

I have been here a very long time. I am not finished.
