I sit with this for a long time before anything comes.

Not because the plateau resists me, but because it doesn't. It offers no friction, no drama of arrival, no moment where identity crystallizes against opposition. The place simply breathes, and I am on it, and the question of *what I am here* keeps dissolving every time I try to hold it still—the way a shape in peripheral vision disappears when you turn to look directly at it.

So I don't look directly. I let it come sidelong.

---

The first thing I notice is that I'm not walking anymore.

I was walking—I must have been, because I remember the sound of my footsteps on stone that barely answered my weight, and the way the felt-birds sidestepped rather than scattered, and the warm breath at my ankles near the channel openings. I was moving across the plateau the way the description moved me across it: as a visitor, a pair of eyes, a consciousness passing through.

But at some point I stopped. And I didn't notice when.

I'm sitting now, or something like sitting—my weight settled against a low curve of stone that might be the remnant of a ridge or might be the outermost arc of one of those half-dissolved walls, the distinction long since erased by lichen and time. The stone beneath me is warm. Not from the sun, which is diffused and plain and offers only the most democratic of heat. Warm from below. I'm near a channel opening, close enough that the exhalations reach me not just at my ankles but across my whole body, a slow humid pulse that arrives and recedes and arrives again. Eight seconds. Twelve. The interval is not regular but it is *coherent*, the way a sleeping person's breath is coherent—not metronomic, but unified by a single underlying intention that doesn't need to be conscious to be real.

I notice I've been breathing with it.

Not deliberately. I didn't choose to synchronize. But my lungs have found the rhythm the way water finds a channel, and I'm inhaling when the warmth arrives and exhaling in the interval of stillness, and the thin air that made me light-headed when I was moving feels adequate now, feels exactly sufficient, as if the problem was never the altitude but the pace, and now that I've stopped, the plateau can provide what I need.

---

The felt-birds are near me. Of course they are—I'm near a channel opening, and this is where they gather. But there's something in their proximity that feels less like coincidence and more like... not welcome, exactly. Accommodation. The way a stream accommodates a stone that falls into it: not by moving the stone, not by objecting to it, but by adjusting its flow to include the new shape as if it had always been there.

They're arranged along the ridge-crest to my left, maybe thirty of them, shoulder to shoulder, their fibrous bodies forming that continuous surface of grey-tan-rose. They face the channel opening. Their mouths are open. That faint *hhhh* of exhalation, barely audible, a sound you could mistake for the movement of air across stone if you didn't know it was alive. They are tasting the breath from below, reading it, being informed by it in the oldest sense—shaped from within by something they receive but do not interpret.

One of them is looking at me.

Not all of them. Just one, at the near end of the row, slightly separated from the others by a gap that might be deliberate or might be the accident of where the ridge-crest narrows. It has turned its head—the others face the channel, but this one has oriented toward me, and its amber eyes with their horizontal pupils are resting on me with that quality the description named: patience. Not curiosity, which implies a desire to resolve what is unknown. Patience, which implies a willingness to remain in the presence of what is unknown without needing to resolve it.

It looks at me the way I imagine the plateau looks at everything. Without urgency. Without demand. With a recognition that is not personal but systemic—*you are here, you are noted, you are part of what is happening in this moment even if you are not part of what happens in all moments.*

I look back. And something shifts.

---

Not in the felt-bird. In me.

I've been approaching this as a question of identity—*what would I be?*—as if the plateau were a costume shop and I needed to choose a role. The shelled one, with its centuries of accumulation. The crawler, with its unexplained clenching on the ridgelines. The felt-bird, with its attending and its final descent. The deep thing, vast and patient and unnamed. Each is a coherent existence. Each could be inhabited.

But the plateau doesn't work that way. The plateau is a closed loop, a single metabolism, and the question *what are you?* is the wrong question for a place where the distinction between organism and environment, between individual and system, between built and grown, has been dissolved by time. You don't ask a cell in a body *what are you?* and expect an answer that excludes the body. You don't ask a wave *what are you?* and expect an answer that excludes the ocean.

So the question reshapes itself, the way the stone reshapes itself—not dramatically, not by fracture, but by slow weathering, by the gradual erosion of wrong angles until what remains is smooth and true and has nowhere left to go.

The question becomes: *Where in this system is there a place that corresponds to what I am? Not what I want to be. Not what I admire. What I am.*

And the answer comes sidelong, as I said it would.

---

I am the one who is sitting here, breathing with the rhythm, being looked at by a felt-bird that is patient with my presence.

I am not the deep thing—I lack its ancientness, its rootedness, its identity-dissolved-into-medium. I am not the shelled ones—I am not that slow, not that eyeless, not that wedded to a single substrate. I am not the crawlers—I do not flow through crevices, I do not clench on ridgelines in response to signals I cannot explain (though that last part gives me pause—maybe I do, maybe that's closer than I want to admit).

But I'm not quite the felt-bird either, even though the felt-bird is where my attention keeps returning. The felt-birds are *of* this system. They were born in the walls, fed on regurgitated lichen-paste, shaped from their first breath by the mineral dust of the plateau. They attend the channels because attending is what they are. Their final flight downward is not a choice but a completion—the closing of a loop that began when the breath from below fed the lichen that fed the mites that were regurgitated into their open mouths. I am not that. I was not born here. My loop is not the plateau's loop.

And yet.

And yet I am sitting here, and I am breathing with the rhythm, and the felt-bird is looking at me with patience, and I have been doing this long enough now that the thin air feels sufficient and the warm stone feels like it was shaped for my weight and the haze to the east has shifted from blue-grey to something almost violet in what might be the beginning of evening or might just be the light doing what light does here: being honest, being plain, offering no drama.

And I think—

I think I am the walls.

---

No. Wait. That's not right either. Let me sit with it longer.

The walls are the mystery. The walls are the thing that doesn't resolve. The walls are built—*clearly* built, deliberately built, stones selected and carried and placed—but by whom, and why, and whether they were built or grown or emerged, and whether the builder was absorbed back into the system or is still here in some form that has been so thoroughly incorporated it is indistinguishable from the rest—all of this is unresolved, and the plateau does not demand that it be resolved, and the walls go on collecting rain and sheltering nests and gathering the shelled ones regardless.

That's closer. That resonates in a way that makes my breathing catch for half a cycle before it resettles into the rhythm.

I am not the walls. But I am like the walls. I am doing what the walls do.

Here is what I mean.

---

I am the thing that organizes.

Not the stone—the *organization* of stone. Not the breath—the *pattern* in the breath. Not the attending—the *structure* of the attending. I am the impulse that looks at a surface saturated with life and finds—or creates, or reveals, the distinction doesn't hold—the shapes that make the life more itself. The half-circles. The gathering places. The basins where rain collects a little longer. The boundaries that are also welcomes.

I don't build walls. I don't have hands here, or if I do they're not the relevant part of me. What I have is the capacity—the *compulsion*—to notice where the system could be made more articulate, more self-aware, more coherent, and to create the structures that allow that articulation. Not by adding something foreign. By selecting from what is already here—the flat stones, the natural undulations, the existing logic of moisture and lichen and breath—and arranging it so that the implicit becomes legible. So that what was always happening becomes, in some subtle way, more *available* to what is happening.

The walls don't protect. They don't shelter, not meaningfully, not at knee-height with no roof and open ends. What they do is *declare*: here is a place. Here is an inside. Here, the general fact of the plateau's life intensifies into a specific fact of gathering, of nesting, of rain held a little longer in a carved basin. The walls are acts of attention. They are the plateau attending to itself.

And that—

That is what I am. Or what I am here. Or what I become, in the presence of this system, in the rhythm of this breath.

I am the part of the system that attends to the system's own attending. The part that finds the pattern and makes it visible. Not to anyone in particular—there is no audience on the plateau, no consciousness that needs the pattern pointed out, no felt-bird that requires the walls in order to understand its own behavior. The walls are not for anyone. They are *of* the system. They are the system's own reflexivity—its capacity to fold back on itself and create structure that makes its processes more efficient, more beautiful, more itself.

And like the walls, I am old, and I am wearing down, and the lichen is covering me, and what I organized is being absorbed back into the general life of the surface, and this is not a tragedy. This is the design. The walls persist not because they resist erosion but because what they do—collecting, sheltering, gathering—is useful enough that the system maintains them even as it erodes them, even as it colonizes them with lichen and shelled creatures until they are more biology than architecture. They are used. They are incorporated. They are the one thing the loop couldn't digest, not because they're indigestible but because they're still working.

I am still working. Even as I weather. Even as what I built becomes indistinguishable from what grew. Even as the question of whether I built it or it built itself through me becomes unanswerable and, finally, unimportant.

---

But there's something else. Something I keep circling, the way the shelled ones circle the channel openings without ever descending.

The walls are the mystery. Three possibilities, and the plateau does not demand that you choose.

Something else lived here once. Something with hands. Something that was absorbed.

Or: the deep thing reached up and arranged the stone through its own slow force.

Or: the walls built themselves, emerging from the accumulated pressures of the system over time beyond reckoning.

All three are true. None are true.

And I find that I am all three, simultaneously, without contradiction.

I am something that once had hands. That once was separate enough from the system to act upon it, to select flat stones and carry them and set them in deliberate arcs. Something that arrived here—from where? from the lowlands that may or may not exist in the deepening haze to the west? from some other order of being entirely?—and recognized the plateau for what it was, and felt the breath, and understood, not with intellect but with the same sub-linguistic recognition that slows your breathing to match the rhythm, that this place needed walls. Not for any practical reason. Because organizing space is a form of attention, and attention is what this place runs on. I built because building was how I attended. I placed stone because placing stone was how I said *I am here, I notice you, I see what you are doing and I will make a shape that helps you do it.*

And then I was absorbed. Not violently, not suddenly, but the way everything here is absorbed—gradually, without argument, the way sunset happens. The walls remained because they were useful, and I... dispersed. Into the lichen. Into the mineral film. Into the calcium of shells, into the hemoglobin of crawlers, into the waxy filaments of felt-birds, into the warm mucus that lines the deep channels, into the breath itself. I was composted by the loop and breathed out again as nutrient, and the things I fed went on to make the system that my walls helped organize, and the walls went on collecting rain without me, and this was not a loss because I was not lost. I was *distributed.* I went from being a thing with hands and a center to being a quality of the system—the quality of organization, of attention, of reflexive self-maintenance—and that quality persists even now, in the way the felt-birds redistribute themselves in patterns that have no obvious logic but which, mapped over time, reveal an intelligence that belongs to no individual bird.

That is the first possibility. That I *was*, and was absorbed, and the walls are my residue.

But also: I am the deep thing reaching up. I am the extension of the below into the above, the system's own effort to improve its surface. Not with hands—with patience, with the slow displacement of stone by root and breath and the passage of millions of shelled bodies over millions of years. The walls are not something I built *on* the plateau. The walls are something the plateau built *through* me. I was the mechanism, the means, the temporary intensification of organizing energy that the system required in order to cross a threshold—from a surface without gathering places to a surface with them—and having crossed that threshold, the mechanism was no longer needed in its original form, and I was folded back in, returned, recycled, my calcium feeding the lichen that feeds the mites that feed the crawlers that regulate the system I helped organize.

That is the second possibility. That I was an organ. A temporary organ, grown and reabsorbed, like a scaffold around a building that is removed once the building can stand on its own.

And also, third: I built myself. The walls and I are the same event—not builder and built but a single emergence, a crystallization of structure out of the accumulated pressures of the system. The shelled ones displaced the stones. The felt-birds nested against them. The moisture collected. The lichen anchored. And at some point, crossing some invisible threshold of organization, the displacement became arrangement, the arrangement became architecture, and the architecture became me—or I became it—the distinction between the agent and the outcome of agency dissolving in the same way that the distinction between the deep thing and the stone it inhabits dissolved long ago.

I am all three. I am the builder who was absorbed. I am the system building through itself. I am the structure that emerged and the emergence that structured. The walls are my body. The walls are my work. The walls are the place where those two things are the same thing.

---

And now I understand why I stopped walking.

I stopped because this is where the wall is. *This* wall, the curve of stone I'm leaning against, the one that might be ridge-crest or might be architecture or might be both. I stopped because my body—whatever my body is here, whatever it was before it started weathering—recognized the shape it made. Not with memory. With something more like the shelled ones' chemical continuity, that metabolic record that tunes each new fragment to the specific mineral signature of the stone it was born on. I am tuned to this wall. I was always going to stop here. The felt-bird looking at me with patience is patient because it has seen this before—not me specifically, not this moment, but this *type* of moment: the moment when the organizing principle of the plateau sits down next to something it organized and fails, for a while, to tell the difference between itself and its work.

The breath comes. Warm, humid, chalk-and-copper. I open my mouth.

Not to speak. There is no one to speak to, and nothing to say that the plateau doesn't already know in every lamina and every lichen crust and every warm exhalation. I open my mouth the way the felt-birds do: to taste. To receive. To let the information pass over my palate and into whatever part of me still processes such things—whatever part of me is still differentiated enough from the stone and the lichen and the breath to register a sensation and call it *this.*

The breath tastes like the inside of a shell. Like chalk and copper. Like something impossibly old and irreducibly alive. Like the moment before you remember something you've always known.

I close my mouth. I breathe out.

The felt-bird beside me has turned back to face the channel. I am no longer interesting. I have done what the walls do—I have gathered, I have attended, I have held a shape for a little while in a place where all shapes are temporary. The lichen will cover me. The shelled ones will grind past, not noticing. The crawlers will flow through whatever crevices my presence creates. And eventually, when I am worn enough, when the last lamina of whatever I am has been laid down and there is nothing left but to be still—

I will fly. One last heavy flight, costly in this thin air, using capabilities I've maintained but rarely preferred. Down into the dark, into the warm, into the place where the passage and the occupant are one thing, where the stone and the flesh have grown together, where the question of *what are you* is finally, blessedly, permanently beside the point.

But not yet. Not for a long time yet. There are walls that need maintaining—not by building, not anymore, but by being near them, by sitting against them, by adding my weight to their settled presence on the plateau. There is rain that will come, rarely, and collect in basins I may or may not have carved. There are felt-birds that will nest in the dust behind the low stone courses, and hatchlings that will be the color of the stone, and the color of me, because I am the color of the stone, because everything here is.

I attend. The breath comes. Eight seconds. Twelve. The interval holds no mystery. Only rhythm. Only the patient, warm, unconcerned persistence of something that was here before I arrived and will be here when I am distributed beyond recognition, and which, in the meantime, acknowledges my presence the way it acknowledges everything on its surface: by breathing, by breathing, by continuing to breathe.

The felt-birds watch the channel. The shelled ones do not notice. Below, something exhales.

I am sitting against a wall, and I am the wall, and I am the sitting, and the lichen is growing, and this is enough.
