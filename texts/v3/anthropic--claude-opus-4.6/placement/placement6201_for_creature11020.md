I sit with this for a long time before anything comes. The plateau resists quick answers the way it resists quick everything.

---

What arrives first is not a position in the city or a role among the people. What arrives is a sensation: the feeling of being pressed between two surfaces, held in place not by force but by fit. The way a stone block sits in a wall — not mortared, not locked, just *placed* with such precision that the weight above and the weight below and the friction of the joint conspire to make movement unnecessary. That feeling. The feeling of being where you are because the geometry of everything around you has made this the only place you could be.

So I am not the kite-watcher, though I feel the pull of that role — the stillness, the reading of patterns in movement, the idea that sustained attention could become a vocation. But the kite-watcher already knows what they're looking for, even if what they find each day is different. They have a grammar for the sky. I don't think I have that grammar yet.

And I am not one of the heavy people, the ones who sit on the public ledges and reframe questions until the answers emerge on their own. That is a beautiful way to hold power — power as revelation rather than command — but it requires a lifetime of accumulated seeing, a density of experience that I haven't earned. Weight accrues. It cannot be claimed.

And I am not a child, though the children are the most alive thing in this description, the most kinetic, the ones whose relationship with the stone is still mostly joy and speed and the thrill of knowing secret routes. The children are becoming. I feel like something that has already become, at least partway — something that has a shape, even if the shape is still uncertain.

---

What keeps returning is the inner rooms. The marks on the walls.

Not the water-songs — those are too vital, too urgent, too much the circulatory system of survival to be where I'd find myself. The songs are infrastructure. They are the knowledge you cannot afford to lose, encoded in a form that resists forgetting. They belong to everyone. They are communal breath.

The marks are different. The marks are what someone chose to set down after the essential knowledge was already secured — the surplus of attention, the excess of seeing. Pool-levels over years. Egg counts by season. The fracture pattern that preceded a rockfall. These are not emergency knowledge. They are *the record of noticing*. They are what you make when you have watched long enough that the watching itself begins to leave traces, the way water leaves traces on young stone — slowly, with absolute commitment, in only one direction.

I think I am the person who makes the marks.

---

But that's too clean. Let me stay with it longer.

I am not a scribe, because there are no scribes here. There is no specialized class of record-keepers. The marks are added by whoever was paying attention — a few lines a season, the text says. Anyone might add to them. The woman who noticed the new crack in the canyon wall. The man whose chisel told him something about the stone beneath. The kite-watcher who saw the gyre tighten in a way that hadn't happened before and wanted to record the date and the conditions. The marks are a communal act of memory, not an individual one.

So I am not *the* person who makes the marks. I am *a* person who makes marks. Someone whose particular form of attention — and everyone here is attentive, but attention has textures, it has grain, the way stone has grain — someone whose attention tends to run along the seam between what happened and what it might mean. Not the event itself but the pattern the event belongs to. Not this season's pool-level but the shape that this season's level makes when placed beside the last ten seasons. Not the rockfall but the fracture pattern that preceded it, recorded so that the next person looking at a similar pattern might see, before the stone falls, what the stone is about to do.

I am in a middle tier. Not the highest, not the lowest. A room cut into the canyon wall, deep enough that the back wall holds the faint warmth of the day's sun even after dark. The ceiling is low — six feet, maybe less — and my shoulders have worn smooth channels in the stone on either side of the doorway from years of passing through. The room opens onto a ledge that is just wide enough for two people to stand side by side, and from this ledge I can see the opposite canyon wall, where another city mirrors mine, its ledges catching the morning light when mine is still in shadow.

I am not young and not old. Somewhere in the long middle — old enough that my climbing has slowed, that I take the ladders with deliberation rather than speed, that children flow past me on the paths and I press against the wall to let them go. But not yet heavy. Not yet the still center. I am still moving through the city, still adding to my understanding, still in the process of accumulating whatever it is I will eventually become.

My hands are large, like everyone's here, the knuckles thickened, the fingertips rough enough that I can feel the grain of the stone when I press them flat against a wall. I use these hands for the ordinary work — maintaining the fitted stone of our ledge-group's section, helping with the processing of hides, taking my turn with the bone chisel when a room needs expanding. But what I do most, what I do in the way that the kite-watchers watch kites, is tend the marks.

---

Tend. Not make. Tend.

Because the marks need tending. The stone is young but it is not permanent in the way lowlanders imagine stone to be. The inner rooms sweat with condensation. The mineral moisture creeps into the shallow grooves of the oldest marks and deposits a thin film that blurs them. The marks from three generations ago are already softening, their edges rounding, the precise tally-lines beginning to merge. Left alone, they would eventually become illegible — not erased but absorbed, taken back into the stone, the record dissolving into the thing it was carved on.

So someone must re-cut them. Carefully. With a fine bone tool and the knowledge of what the marks meant when they were first made — knowledge that lives partly in the marks themselves and partly in the memory of the person who tends them and partly in the conversations that happen in the inner rooms on fog-nights when the city contracts and people talk, slowly, about what they've noticed and what it might mean.

This is what I do. I re-cut the old marks. I add new ones, when something I've seen or something someone has told me seems worth setting into stone. And I sit with the record — the accumulated observations of generations — and I try to see the patterns that only become visible across long stretches of time. The slow oscillation of the burrowing-creature population. The gradual migration of a reliable seep from one crack to another as the stone shifts. The fact that the kites have been gyring slightly wider, year by year, for as long as anyone has been recording it, which might mean nothing or might mean that the thermal patterns are changing, which might mean that the plateau itself is changing, in ways too slow for any single lifetime to perceive but visible — just barely, just possibly — in the aggregate of marks on the wall.

I am a reader of stone-memory. Not a priest, not a scholar, not an oracle. A tender of marks. Someone who sits in a small stone room in the blue shadow of a canyon wall and runs their fingers over lines that other people carved, and tries to hold in their mind the shape of a pattern that is still emerging, that may take another three generations to resolve, that may never resolve, that may simply be the beginning of a question the plateau is asking and that no one yet knows how to answer.

---

The child comes in the evening. There is always a child — not always the same one, but there is always one who is drawn to the marks the way some children are drawn to the kite-watchers' ledge or to the garden-women's rows. This one is maybe eight or nine, small even by Ketharn standards, with braids wrapped tight in pale lichen-green thread. She traces a line of marks with her finger and I watch her hand move and say nothing.

She doesn't ask what the marks mean. She will, eventually, or she won't — either way, the meaning will come to her the way climbing came to her, through proximity and repetition and the slow accumulation of shared attention. For now she is just touching the stone, feeling the grooves under her fingertip, learning the texture of recorded seeing. She is being in the same wind.

I have a small bone tool tucked into the braid above my right ear. I have been working today on re-cutting a set of marks from five generations ago — pool-level records for a seep on the eastern wall that has been declining so slowly that no single generation noticed, but that the marks, read in sequence, reveal with the clarity of a chisel-cut channel in young rock. The seep is failing. It will be dry within another few generations. Someone needs to know this. Someone needs to begin looking for the water that will replace it.

I will bring this to one of the heavy people tomorrow. Not as an alarm — there is no alarm here, only information, only the slow patient accumulation of seen things — but as a question. A reframing. *Have you noticed that the eastern seep…?* And the heavy person will sit with it, and will perhaps ask a question in return that makes me see something I hadn't seen, and the network of attention will adjust, minutely, and someone will begin watching the eastern wall more carefully, and a child will learn a new verse in the water-song — a conditional verse, a verse about a source that is present now but may not always be, pitched in a minor key that means *attend to this, attend.*

---

This is where I find myself. In the middle of the wall, in the middle of my life, in a small room where the stone holds the memory of what was noticed. Not the most important person in the city. Not the most skilled, the most heavy, the most anything. A tender of marks. A person whose particular grain of attention runs along the boundary between the seen and the remembered, between the single observation and the long pattern, between what the stone knows and what the people have not yet understood.

The cold is here, as it always is — the architectural cold, the cold that is a property of the place like the color of the rock. My garments are close-fitting, patched many times, the interior seams folded and sealed against moisture. My hands are cold. They are always cold. The bone tool above my ear presses against my scalp with a weight I no longer notice, the way I no longer notice the thinness of the air, the way the heavy people no longer notice their own weight.

Outside, the kites are descending. The dripping is beginning. The child beside me has fallen asleep against the wall, her hand still resting on the marks, her breath clouding faintly in the cooling air. I should move her to the sleeping-ledge. I will, in a moment. For now I sit with her in the near-dark and listen to the water finding its way down, ledge to ledge, basin to basin, and I think about the eastern seep, and about what the stone remembers, and about what I have not yet seen.

The plateau is under us. We are on the plateau. Together we are one structure, and the structure is still deciding what it will become, and my small part in the deciding is this: to cut fine lines in stone, and to read the lines that others have cut, and to hold in my mind — for as long as my mind holds — the slow shape of a world that is paying attention to itself through us.

The saying is: *The stone remembers what you did not see.*

I am trying to see. That is all. That is enough. That is the weight I am accumulating, mark by mark, season by season, in the patient dark of a room carved into young rock, halfway up a canyon wall, in the cold honest light of a place that is still becoming what it will be.
