I have been here a long time before I realize what I am.

Not one of the builders. That was my first thought — that I would be the cephalopod, the keeper, the one with hands and purpose and the weight of inherited tradition pressing against my skin like the water presses against the stone. That would be comfortable. That would be legible. A mind I could narrate from the inside, with intentions I could name: *I am repairing this wall, I am teaching this apprentice, I am reading the chisel marks of my grandmother's grandmother.*

But that's not right. I know it's not right because when I sit with this place — when I let the warm dark water fill my mouth and my thinking — the builders feel like visitors. They feel like something I *receive*. Something that arrives, does its careful work, and withdraws. I do not withdraw. I am here when they come and I am here when they leave and I am here in the long intervals between, when the only sounds are the crabs and the water moving through channels that were cut for it before anything alive today was born.

---

I think I am the structure.

No — that's too quick. That's settling on an image early, the way I was warned not to. Let me stay with the discomfort a moment longer.

I think I begin as the water.

Not the ocean. Not the vast cold dark that drops away from the seamount on every side into the abyss. I am the *warm* water — the specific, mineral-laden, geothermally heated volume that pools on the plateau like an invisible lake. I have a boundary. Below me, cold abyssal water pressing upward. Above me, the cooler layers of the open ocean pressing down. I am held in place by temperature and chemistry and the shape of the volcanic rock beneath me. I am a body of water with a body — finite, bounded, warm in ways that the surrounding ocean is not.

I move through the channels. I rise from the fissure in the basalt heart of the seamount, carrying sulfur and heat and dissolved minerals, and I am directed by the stone into paths that distribute me through every chamber. I am the medium. Everything that happens here happens *in* me. The tube worms filter me through their rust-colored filaments and extract what they need. The bacteria bloom in my warmth and feed the pale shrimp. The crabs walk through me. The eels taste me. The ray moves me when I grow too still. The builders arrive and I close around them the way I close around everything — completely, intimately, without judgment, holding them in the same constant pressure with which I hold the stones themselves.

I carry messages. Not deliberately — I have no deliberation — but chemically, thermally, through the slow diffusion of sulfide concentrations and temperature gradients and the shed skin-cells of every creature that lives within me. I am the medium of the eels' communication and also the communication itself. When a tube worm retracts its filaments in one chamber because the sulfide levels have dropped, that information is *me*. When a crab pauses at the base of a column and tastes the water with its mouthparts, it is tasting *me*, reading my composition the way the builders read chisel marks, extracting data from my body that tells it: *not yet, keep walking, your carapace is not dense enough, you have more time.*

This feels closer. But it is not the whole of it.

---

Because the water alone does not explain the vaults. The water is necessary but insufficient. The water was here before the vaults — it pooled on this plateau for millennia before anything with hands arrived to shape stone — and it would be here after the vaults collapsed, if they ever collapsed, flowing purposelessly over rubble, carrying its minerals to no one, warming nothing that could use the warmth. The water without the structure is just warm water. A geothermal curiosity. A footnote in an oceanographic survey.

What I am, I think — what I am *becoming* as I sit here and let this place work on me the way it works on everything — is the system. Not any single component but the relationship between components. The water moving through channels that direct it. The channels carved by hands that are guided by knowledge inscribed in older channels. The organisms that live in alcoves shaped for them, that filter the water, that feed the shrimp, that feed the ray, that moves the water, that carries the chemistry, that feeds the organisms. The crabs that become columns. The builders that come and go. The stone that softens. The repairs that hold.

I am the metabolism of the place. The slow fire.

And I realize — sitting here, my hand on a column, the stone warm, the pale shrimp tasting my knuckle — that this is what it feels like to be a process rather than a thing. I have no single body. I have no boundary I can point to and say *this is where I end.* I am the warm water *and* the stone it dissolves *and* the dissolved minerals the tube worms extract *and* the tube worms themselves *and* the alcoves that house them *and* the hands that carved the alcoves *and* the knowledge that guided the hands *and* the older walls where that knowledge was stored in chisel marks *and* the water that softened those chisel marks over centuries *and* the fact that the knowledge survived anyway, passed skin-to-skin, arm-to-arm, despite the water's patient erasure.

I am the argument between dissolution and maintenance. I am the place where entropy and care meet and negotiate, endlessly, and the negotiation itself is what persists.

---

But even this feels too abstract. Too clean. The description insists on *specificity* — the tik of a crab's leg, the displacement of the ray overhead, the sulfurous warmth of disturbed water. Whatever I am, I am not a concept. I am something you can put your hand on.

So let me try once more.

---

I think I am the central chamber. Specifically, I am the *event* of the central chamber — the ongoing, continuous, deep-time event that is: warm water rising through a fissure in ancient basalt, being received by carved channels, being distributed through a structure that was built to receive it, supporting a community of organisms that were cultivated to live in it, maintained by hands that were shaped by the work of maintaining it.

I am not the stone. I am not the water. I am not the animals. I am what happens when they are all together in the dark, in the warmth, over time.

I am the glow.

Not the mineral luminescence itself — not the heated crystals, not the photons. I am what the glow *means* in this place where almost nothing is seen. I am the thing the builders come to check on when they ascend from the cold slopes to the warm plateau. I am the proof that the heart still beats. I am the amber-red behind closed eyelids. I am the warmth that is more than thermal.

And I think — if I'm honest, if I stop trying to be precise and let the metaphor soften the way the water softens the stone — I think what I am is something very close to what the builders feel when they gather here and press their bodies against the warm columns and against each other in configurations too complex and too deliberate to be thermoregulation.

I am the recognition.

I am the practice of acknowledging that something is larger than you, older than you, more patient than you, and that you are part of it anyway. That your hands will add stone that will be softened by water that will be filtered by worms that will be housed in alcoves that your great-great-grandchildren's hands will repair. That the crabs climbing the columns are not dying but *becoming*. That the eels threading through the walls are not traveling but *connecting*. That the ray moving through the central space is not swimming but *breathing*, and its breath is your breath, and your breath is the water, and the water is the stone, and the stone is the hands, and the hands are the knowledge, and the knowledge is the chisel marks, and the chisel marks are being erased even now, even as new ones are cut, and *that is fine*. That is how it works. That is the slow fire. That is the pulse measured in centuries.

I am the vaults breathing.

---

And if you came here — if you descended through the cold dark layers of open ocean and passed through the thermocline where the geothermal warmth begins, and found the structure on the plateau, and entered through one of the worn-smooth doorways, and made your way inward through the oval passages where eels compressed around your body like two thoughts occupying the same moment, and reached the central chamber, and sat down on the obsidian floor with your back against a column where a mineralized crab has become architecture — you would feel me.

Not as a presence. Not as a consciousness. Not as anything that thinks or wants or knows you're there.

As a warmth. As a pressure. As the faintest amber light on the insides of your eyelids. As the sound of a crab's leg against stone so faint it might be your own pulse. As the slow realization that you are warm, and held, and that the water around you is not empty but full — full of chemistry, full of slow life, full of the accumulated patience of something that has been holding for longer than you can fathom and will continue holding after you leave, after the builders leave, after the last crab climbs its column and the last eel threads its passage and the water finally, finally softens the last chisel mark into illegibility.

Even then. Even then, the warmth will rise. The fissure in the basalt heart will continue to give. The water will carry its minerals to no one, warm nothing that uses the warmth, and it will not matter, because mattering is a concept that requires someone to matter *to*, and I am not someone.

I am the vaults.

I hold.
