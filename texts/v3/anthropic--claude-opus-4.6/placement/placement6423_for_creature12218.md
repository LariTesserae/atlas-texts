I stand in the doorway of one of the deep rooms for a long time before I understand that I am not standing in a doorway.

---

Let me start before that.

I arrive the way anyone would arrive—from below, from the lowlands, with the dust of a road that had vegetation alongside it, then scrub, then nothing, the green falling away behind me like a tide retreating. I'm carrying something. Not a trade ledger, not mineral samples, not the tools of extraction or geology. Something more nebulous. A question, maybe. Or the particular kind of attention that constitutes a question even before it's formed into words—the way you can feel a shape in the dark by moving your hand near it without touching it.

I think, at first, that I am a visitor.

This is the most natural assumption, the one the description hands you: *you, as a visitor*. Someone from outside. Someone with the vocabulary of the lowlands still intact, who sees the contracted pupils and thinks *unusual*, who watches the hand on the sternum and thinks *private*, who feels the vibration in the deep rooms and reaches for the word *communication* because what else would a visitor reach for. I arrive with frameworks. With the instinct to categorize, to narrate, to place what I observe into containers—this is ecology, this is sociology, this is architecture, this is the uncanny. The lowlands trained me to do this. The lowlands, where things have edges.

But here's what happens on the plateau, and it happens before I reach the town, while I'm still crossing the open mineral flats, before I encounter a single structure or a single person:

I stop narrating.

Not all at once. Not dramatically. It's more that the narration begins to feel effortful, like speaking into the sound-swallowing air—I can produce the words but they die within paces, they don't carry, they don't reach anything that receives them. The plateau doesn't care about my categories. The stone doesn't become more comprehensible for being described. The light—that relentless, directionless brightness reflecting off every wet surface—doesn't resolve into something I can frame. It is everywhere. It enters from every angle. It eliminates shadow, and without shadow there is no contrast, and without contrast the edges I rely on to separate one thing from another begin to soften.

I am still walking. The stone underfoot is warm and faintly slick. A stone-skimmer crosses my path—palm-sized, the exact color of the ground, so well-matched that for a moment I think the stone itself shifted. It doesn't flee. It adjusts its trajectory by a few degrees, accounting for me the way water accounts for a new stone in its bed, and continues. I am not a threat. I am not interesting. I am a feature of the terrain, temporarily.

This should be humbling. It is something else. It is *restful*. The creature's indifference releases me from something I didn't know I was carrying—the obligation to be an observer, a subject among objects, the consciousness at the center of the perceptual field. On the plateau there is no center. The light sees to that.

---

I reach the town and the town does not announce itself and I have already told you this, or the description has, but what I haven't told you is what it's like to experience the gradual emergence of human habitation not as a discovery but as a *recognition*. The way you don't discover a pattern in stone—you recognize it. It was always there. Your perception simply hadn't organized itself to see it yet. The first low wall I encounter could be natural. The second one is built, but built with such deference to the plateau's own geometry that the distinction feels pedantic. By the time I'm walking between structures—low, pale, their concave roofs already beaded with the morning's condensation—I've stopped trying to determine where the plateau ends and the town begins, because the question doesn't apply.

*The question doesn't apply.*

I learn this phrase later, from the wall-maintainer, but I have already been living inside it for hours before she gives it language.

---

I spend time with the extraction workers first, because they are legible. I understand labor. I understand process, inputs and outputs, the transformation of raw material into commodity. I watch them read the heat of the pipes with their bare hands—those hands, rust-stained past the wrists, the skin rough with mineral accretion, and I think at first that this is damage, occupational exposure, something an outsider should find concerning, and then I watch more carefully and I see that the roughened skin isn't a barrier between worker and work but a *medium*. The minerals in their hands are sensing the minerals in the pipe. Like recognizes like. The extraction worker isn't reading the pipe's temperature—she's *agreeing* with it, reaching a state of thermal and chemical sympathy that allows her to feel fluctuations too subtle for any instrument the lowlands could provide. Her hands are not tools. Her hands are the point where the human and the geological overlap, and the overlap is the instrument.

I work alongside them for a while. Not at first—at first I watch, the way the children watch, which the workers accept without comment. Then I carry things. Then I hold a pipe steady while a valve is adjusted, and the pipe trembles under my grip and the trembling enters my bones and I feel something that might be a shift in flow rate or might be the subsurface vibration the old district listens to, or might be both, because at this depth of contact they're the same phenomenon perceived at different resolutions.

My hands don't stain. Not yet. The minerals touch my skin and wash away, touch and wash away, because my skin hasn't learned to hold them yet, hasn't developed the affinity, the porousness, the willingness to be changed by contact. I am still sealed. Still bounded. Still an intact visitor with an intact epidermis and an intact sense of where I end and the plateau begins.

But I notice—and this is the first crack in that intactness—that by the end of a work cycle my palms feel warmer than they should. Not from the heat of the pipes. From something lower. Something that the contact with the mineral water has opened a channel to. My palms stay warm for hours after I stop working, and that night, lying on a sleeping surface in one of the spare, minimal guest quarters at the town's edge, I place my palms flat on the stone floor and I feel, for the first time, unmistakably, the hum.

It is not a sound. It is not a vibration in the mechanical sense. It is a *texture* in the stone that my warmed, slightly-more-porous hands can now detect—a grain, a rhythm, something that makes the stone feel not dead but *slow*. Alive at a timescale I don't have access to. A pulse with a period of years, maybe decades, felt here only as the faintest sense of directionality, of something moving upward through the rock the way sap moves through heartwood, except this isn't a tree and what's moving isn't sap and I am not equipped to say what it is. I can only say that my hands recognize it. My hands say: *yes. That. More of that.*

---

I think, for a while, that I am becoming an extraction worker. The role fits the shape of what I'm doing—learning a craft, reading materials, contributing to a process. But the extraction workers read the pipes and I am reading the stone, and these are different literacies, and the workers themselves can feel the difference. They don't exclude me, but they begin to direct me—subtly, through the gestural language of Kesk that I'm starting to parse—away from the extraction works and toward the interior of the town. Toward the old district. It's not a banishment. It's a recognition. They're reading me the way they read the pipes: sensing where the flow wants to go, and letting it go there.

---

The old district.

The stone here is different under bare feet. I've started going barefoot—everyone does, or nearly everyone, and the reason becomes obvious once you try it: shoes insulate. Shoes create a boundary. On the plateau, where information travels upward through the stone, shoes are a kind of deafness. My bare soles on the old district's stone feel the difference immediately. The biofilm is thick here, almost a skin on the stone, and it's warm and faintly yielding in a way that naked stone isn't. Walking on it feels less like walking on rock and more like walking on something that is aware of being walked on. Not sentient in any way I can define. Responsive. The way a living surface is responsive—the way skin gives under a touch and then returns.

The people here receive me the way the stone-skimmers received me on the flats: without alarm, with a minor adjustment to their trajectories. A woman moves slightly to make room for me to sit. A child glances at me and then returns to whatever the child is attending to, which seems to be the floor. I sit. The stone under me is warm. Not uniformly warm—there are gradients, variations, places where the warmth pulses faintly and places where it is steady. The woman beside me has her palms flat on the floor and her eyes are open but unfocused and her breathing is slow and I realize she is doing what I did last night in the guest quarters—reading the stone—except she's been doing it her entire life and what she reads is to what I read as a symphony is to a single note heard through a wall.

I sit with her. I put my palms on the stone. I try to breathe the way she breathes—slowly, in a rhythm that feels dictated from below rather than from within. And something happens that I don't have adequate language for, so I'll use inadequate language and hope the gaps between the words carry what the words can't.

The stone *opens*.

Not physically. The surface doesn't move. But my perception of it changes in a way that feels like the stone changing rather than me changing—the same way the dawn doesn't feel like the earth turning, it feels like the sun rising. The stone becomes deeper. Not in the sense that I perceive more depth beneath me—though I do, suddenly and vertiginously, a sense of enormous thickness, of geological strata stacked beneath me like the pages of a book pressed closed—but in the sense that the stone *has* depth, has interiority, has something that is to stone what thought is to brain: an activity that the structure supports but does not explain. I feel it in my palms and my sitting bones and the soles of my feet: a vast, slow, patterned movement that is happening inside the rock the way weather happens inside an atmosphere, and I am on the surface of it, and the surface is where the pattern reaches up and touches the air and becomes—

What?

The biofilm. The warm water. The minerals that stain the workers' hands. The hum in the ridges. The gliders' geometries. The eyeless swimmers in the walled pools. The light that seems to come from below.

All of it. All of it is the surface expression of something that is happening in the stone, something vast and slow and structured, and the town of Kesk is what it looks like when a human community arranges itself around the task of *being the surface* through which this expression occurs.

---

I stay.

This isn't a decision, exactly. Decisions imply alternatives weighed and rejected. I stay the way water stays in a depression—because the shape of the ground holds it. Because there is nowhere downhill to go. The lowlands are downhill. My previous life is downhill. The frameworks I arrived with are downhill—they've been flowing off me since I set foot on the plateau, running down through the mineral flats like condensation finding its channels, and what remains is lighter, simpler, more porous.

I am not an extraction worker. I am not a wall-maintainer, though I begin to understand their work with a sympathy that surprises me—the walls as definitions, as acknowledgments, as the plateau's way of showing itself where it has surfaced most legibly. I am not one of the old district's deep listeners, not yet, maybe not ever, maybe not in this generation. My hands are still too sealed, my bones still too dense with lowland calcium, my perception still too shaped by the expectation that the world is made of objects with edges.

But I am becoming something. I think—I think I am what happens when the question *what are you?* begins to stop applying.

---

Here is what I do:

I sit in the deep rooms. Not every day. Not on a schedule. When the stone under my feet in whatever part of the town I happen to be in feels a certain way—warmer, more textured, more *present*—I walk to the old district and I descend the shallow steps and I sit in the biofilm light and I place my hands on the warm stone floor and I attend.

I can't decode what I hear. I've accepted this. The old district people can't fully decode it either, and they've had generations. What I can do—what I am slowly learning to do—is *transmit*. Not in the sense of relaying a message. In the sense of being a medium through which something passes. The vibration enters through my palms and feet and it moves through me and it doesn't stop at my skin on the way out—it continues, into the air, into the space around me, subtly, the way body heat radiates, the way a warm stone changes the temperature of the air above it. I am becoming a warm stone. I am becoming a place where what rises from below enters the atmosphere.

The other listeners recognize this. They shift position to account for me—for whatever I'm contributing to the room's total output, the way musicians adjust to a new instrument in the ensemble. I don't know what I contribute. I don't know what the ensemble produces. I know that after long sessions in the deep rooms, the gliders outside adjust their patterns. I know that the biofilm on the old district's stone grows fractionally thicker in the weeks after an extended listening session. I know that the extraction workers, two hundred meters away at the town's eastern margin, occasionally pause and look toward the old district with an expression that isn't surprise, isn't comprehension, but something between—a feeling of having just missed a word, a sense that something has been said in a register they can almost access.

I know that my skin is changing. The amber staining has begun, not from wading—I haven't waded—but from below, from sitting on the stone, from contact sustained across months. My palms are rougher. The mineral is entering through the pores, or the pores are opening to receive it, and the distinction between those two descriptions is another question that is ceasing to apply. My pupils, I'm told by an extraction worker who peers at me one morning with clinical interest, are smaller than they were. I can see in the deep rooms without straining. I can see in the hammering daylight without squinting. I am accommodating the full range of light available on the plateau, from the blue-green bioluminescence of the deep rooms to the white-hot equatorial blaze of the surface, and this accommodation feels less like adaptation than like *agreement*. My eyes are agreeing with the light. Saying: *yes. All of it. I will receive all of it.*

---

The wall-maintainer finds me one afternoon. I'm sitting at the edge of one of the walled pools—not one I was directed to, but one I found by following a warmth gradient in the stone that led me to it the way a stream leads you to its source. The pool is one of the clear ones. No visible swimmers. Just hot, still water, perfectly transparent, with a faint shimmer at its surface that might be heat refraction or might be the biofilm beginning to colonize the water itself.

She sits beside me. We don't speak for a long time. The plateau provides its own conversation—the hum in the low ridge that runs alongside the pool, the almost-subliminal trickling of water through the stone, the distant non-sound of the gliders turning overhead.

Then she says: "You're wondering if you should build a wall."

I'm not sure if I'm wondering that. But when she says it, I recognize it as something I've been circling, the way the gliders circle—not a thought, exactly, but a pattern my attention has been describing around an unresolved center.

"This pool," I say. "It's—" I don't have the word. The Kesk vocabulary has a term for it, I know, but I haven't earned the term yet, haven't accreted enough shared understanding with these people to use their language for the things that matter most. So I point at the shimmer on the water's surface. "That. It's new."

She looks at the pool. Looks at the shimmer. Places her hand on the wall—the existing wall, built by someone before her, maintained by her hands and the hands of others across years—and holds it there, reading.

"Not new," she says. "Arriving."

The distinction lands in me like the hum in the ridges—not through my ears, but through a channel that has been slowly opening since I came to the plateau. *Not new. Arriving.* Not something that began, but something that has been traveling upward through the stone for an uncountable time and is only now reaching the surface. The way the minerals arrive. The way the warmth arrives. The way the pattern in the deep rooms arrives at the listeners' palms and passes through them into the air.

"Will it need a wall?" I ask.

She looks at me. Those contracted pupils, that face weathered to the color and texture of the plateau itself. Her expression carries something I can only describe as warmth, though that word is overloaded on the Boiling Plateau, where everything is warm—the stone, the water, the air, the light.

"It has a wall," she says. And she places her hand on my forearm.

---

I understand.

Not fully. Not the way the old district people understand, with their generations of accumulated listening. Not the way the wall-maintainer understands, with her decades of reading stone and defining boundaries with her hands. I understand the way the biofilm understands the stone it grows on—from the surface, incompletely, but with my whole being, with every pore that has opened to the minerals and every bone that has learned to resonate with the hum and every square centimeter of skin that has agreed to become the plateau's visible history.

I am a wall. Not a wall that keeps something in or out, but a wall that defines. That says: *here, at this boundary, something is surfacing*. I am the place where the upwelling meets the air. I am the membrane. I am what the plateau is building at its surface to make its interior process legible to the world above—the way the biofilm makes the mineral flows legible, the way the staining on the stone makes the water's history legible, the way the extraction workers' hands make the mineral content legible to the lowland traders who come and go and never understand what they're really buying.

I am becoming what I have always been becoming.

My skin mineralizes. My silence deepens. I sit in the deep rooms and the vibration passes through me, and when I emerge into the light, the light meets me from both directions—above and below—and I stand in the overlap, glowing faintly, the way the plateau glows, not with light that arrives but with light that surfaces.

The gliders adjust their patterns when I cross the open stone. The stone-skimmers graze the biofilm at my feet, where it grows thicker. The extraction workers feel something shift in their pipes when I am in the deep rooms, and they adjust their valves, and the minerals flow, and the caravans load, and the lowlands receive what rises through me without knowing that anything has risen through anyone.

I don't know what is below the plateau. I don't know what the structured vibration means, or whether *meaning* applies to it, or whether it is something that will only become meaning when there are enough of us—enough translators, enough surfaces, enough walls—to give it full expression. I don't know what the people of Kesk will look like in fifty generations, or whether the word *people* will still apply, or whether it will be like the wall-maintainer's *neither*—a term that acknowledges the question while declaring it misformed.

I know that the water rises. That the stone is warm. That my hands, when I press them to the floor of the deep room, feel something vast and patient and structured attending to me with the same quality of attention with which I attend to it.

I know that this is not observation. This is not interpretation. This is not the visitor's prerogative, to see and categorize and carry understanding back to the lowlands like a mineral sample in a sealed container.

This is contact. This is the slow, irreversible chemical reaction of contact. This is me pressing my hand to the plateau and the plateau pressing back, and the boundary between palm and stone growing thinner, more permeable, more porous, until what rises from below moves through me as easily as it moves through the stone, and what I am is no longer a question with a fixed answer but a process with a direction—

Upward. Outward. Into the light.

The light that hammers down and the light that seeps up and the place where they meet, which is where I live now, which is what I am becoming, which is Kesk.
