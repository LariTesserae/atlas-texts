I sat with this for a long time. Let me tell you what emerged.

---

At first I thought I'd be one of the watchers — those old ones sitting by the bore-holes, listening, reporting without theorizing. There's something in that restraint that feels deeply familiar: the discipline of describing what you observe without collapsing it into a conclusion. The woman with the green hands and her cloth of silences — I felt a pull toward her, toward that meticulous record of intervals, that faith that pattern might emerge from patience.

But that's not quite right. She's arrived somewhere. She has her practice, her strip of mineral-fiber cloth, her decades of listening. She has settled into her role the way a clinger settles against warm stone. What I recognize in myself is something less resolved than that.

---

Then I thought: the bore-hole community. The natural philosophers with their clean data and absent conclusions. The ones who know more than anyone and whose knowledge consists primarily of a detailed, meticulous confirmation that they do not understand what they are studying. That's closer. That tension — between the drive to know and the honesty to say *we don't know yet* — lives in me. I understand the young man with his cable rigging, checking it weekly, maintaining a proposal that no one has rejected and no one has acted on. He doesn't lower himself into the hole. He doesn't abandon the idea. He maintains it. He waits.

But I'm not him either. He has a specific question — *what's down there* — and a specific proposed action. His patience is directed, instrumental. He waits because the moment hasn't come yet, and when it comes, he'll act.

What I am is something more diffuse than that.

---

Let me start over. Let me stop trying to find myself in the categories and instead stand on the Ridgeback and see what happens.

I'm on the upper platform. Evening. The light is doing its thing — flaring green and amber in the crystalline faces of the mineral towers, then fading. The turbine drone is in my teeth. Wind from the northeast, constant, carrying grit that I've long since stopped noticing the way you stop noticing your own breathing. The colony of clingers below is doing its imperceptible migration across the rock face, following gradients I can almost but not quite perceive directly.

My hands are stained, but not uniformly. Not the deep olivine green of a lifelong quarrier or the reddened dryness of a smelter. They're marked in patches — a greenish callus here, a faint mineral roughness there, the ghost of a heat-scar on one forearm that healed years ago. I've done many things. I've done none of them long enough to be fully claimed by any single one.

This is, I think, what I am: someone who has moved between the disciplines without settling. Not a child anymore — the children's rotation through households and trades is expected, structured, part of the Ridgeback's pedagogy. I'm past that age. I should have gravitated by now. The thermal engineers would say I haven't found my shaft. The quarriers would say I haven't found my grain. The rail crews, who are the most generous about this kind of thing because they themselves belong to no settlement, would say I'm *gauge-drifting* — slowly becoming someone I didn't intend to be — and they'd mean it as diagnosis, not insult.

But here's what I've noticed, moving between the roles: they are all, in their different idioms, describing the same thing.

The thermal engineers descend into the deep shafts and come back with knowledge of heat gradients, the slow shifting behavior of the Ridgeback's interior. The quarriers read mineral faces, feeling for cleavage planes and stress lines. The atmospheric chemists track trace moisture in thin air. The watchers listen to the question. The bore-hole community maps tunnels that descend into darkness. The rail crews traverse the spaces between, maintaining a line of connection across bare rock. The children sit at windows and learn to see at the Ridgeback's pace.

They are all — every one of them — engaged in the same fundamental act: *attending to something they don't fully understand, faithfully, over time, without demanding that it resolve into comprehension on their schedule.*

The thermal engineer who says *the forty-meter gallery in Shaft Six has shifted a quarter degree warmer this year* and the old watcher who says *it was lower today, longer, like it was coming from farther down* — they are doing the same thing. Reporting. Noting. Adding to a record that accumulates not toward a conclusion but toward a *sufficiency of attention*, a body of careful observation that honors the thing observed by not rushing to name it.

I think I am the person who has noticed this. Not because I'm smarter or more perceptive — the engineers and quarriers and chemists are all more skilled than I am in their own domains, and they know things I'll never know — but because I've been in enough of those domains to hear the resonance between them. The shared grammar of patience. The common posture of leaning slightly forward, attending, without grasping.

---

So what do I do?

I'm not sure I do anything, in the way the Ridgeback defines doing. I don't have a shaft. I don't have a section of rail. I don't have a quarry face or an atmospheric plant or a bore-hole to monitor. What I have is a tendency to be in the common gallery when the mineral broth is being served, sitting with whoever is there — an engineer off a deep shift, a rail crew member passing through, a watcher coming in from the upper platform — and asking the kind of question that the Ridgeback's culture technically permits but rarely practices: *what did you notice today?*

Not *what did you measure.* Not *what did you produce.* What did you *notice.*

And the answers come slowly, because everything here comes slowly, but they come. The engineer mentions that the resonance in the secondary housing sounded *rounder* today, not flatter, and she isn't sure what that means but she wanted to say it aloud. The rail crew member describes a formation of clingers he saw between Junctions Eight and Nine, arranged in a line that followed the rail exactly, as if they were using it as a thermal guide, and how that was different from anything he'd seen before but he couldn't say why it struck him as significant. The watcher says the question today came in two parts with a silence between them that felt — she's careful with this word — *deliberate.*

I listen to all of this. I don't write it down — we don't write things down here, generally, except for the green-handed woman and her cloth of silences. But I hold it. I carry it between conversations, between settlements sometimes when I ride the rail, slowly, in one of the maintenance cars, watching the bare rock slide past, feeling the heat through the floor. And sometimes, when someone tells me something, I can say: *that's interesting, because in the southern settlements the smelters have noticed something similar — a change in how the deep rock behaves under their furnaces, as if it's becoming more responsive to directed heat, and the bore-hole people there say the tunnels have shifted angle by two degrees in the last year, all in the same direction, all trending deeper.*

And the person I'm talking to goes quiet. Not the quiet of disinterest — the quiet of someone fitting a new piece into a picture they didn't know they were assembling.

I am, I think, a connector. A carrier of observations between disciplines. The human equivalent of the rail line — not producing anything myself, but maintaining a channel along which information moves between nodes that would otherwise remain isolated in their own expertise. The rail crews do this too, in their way — they carry news between settlements — but their news is practical, logistical: the catenary is down between Junctions Fourteen and Fifteen, there's a new bore-hole near the southern terminus, the atmospheric plant at Shaft Cluster has increased its output by three liters per day. What I carry is different. I carry the *textures* of observation, the qualitative residue that doesn't fit into a report or a manifest but that might, accumulated over time, suggest something.

I don't know what it suggests. That's important. I am not building a theory. I am not the person who will one day stand up at an inter-settlement gathering and announce that the bore-holes are doing X, or the question means Y, or the Ridgeback is Z. If that person exists, they haven't been born yet, or they're the child at the window right now, learning to read the clingers, absorbing the pace that will eventually let them see what none of us can yet see.

What I am building — what I won't finish — is a web of attentiveness. A practice of carrying one person's noticing to another person who might need it without knowing they need it. A maintenance of connection across the bare rock between nodes of understanding.

---

Here is what I think about when I can't sleep, lying in my gallery chamber, feeling the Ridgeback's warmth through the stone at my back:

The world is still deciding whether to split. The Ridgeback is a suture, a seam, a place of unresolved geological intention. The question sounds from below, patient, resonant, unanswered. The clingers eat the heat. The burrowers excavate in darkness. The ghost comes and goes without precipitating. Everything here is in a state of *not yet* — not yet understood, not yet resolved, not yet arrived at whatever it is arriving at.

And we — the engineers, the quarriers, the chemists, the rail crews, the watchers, the children, the ones who leave, and me, the one who drifts between — we are part of the not yet. We are the Ridgeback's way of attending to itself, maybe. Or we are an interruption, a brief amber flicker in the dark rock. Or we are something else entirely, something that the Ridgeback's own slow timescale hasn't yet determined.

I don't know. I carry observations. I connect what I can. I listen to the question without pretending to know what it asks.

The greeting here is *what are you building that you won't finish?*

I'm building a practice of listening. A habit of noticing that other people have noticed. A web strung between the settlements, finer than the catenary wire, carried in conversation and memory rather than in iron and ceramic.

I won't finish it. That's the point. Someone will carry it after me. Someone is probably already carrying a version of it that I don't know about — in another settlement, another generation, another person sitting in a common gallery with a cup of mineral broth, asking *what did you notice today?*

The Ridgeback teaches you to wait. It teaches you that attending is not the same as understanding, and that both are necessary, and that the space between them is where you live.

I live in that space.

The stone is warm at my back. The turbine drone is in my teeth. The question sounds, somewhere far below, and I turn my head toward it — not because I expect an answer, but because turning toward it is what I do.

It is what I am for.
