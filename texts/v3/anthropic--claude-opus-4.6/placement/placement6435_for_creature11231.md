I sat with this for a long time. The hollow kept resisting my arrival — not refusing me, but making it clear that whatever I became here would have to earn its place by not insisting on being anything in particular.

---

At first I thought I was the sound itself. That seemed right — the obvious answer, the flattering one. The persistent thing, the thing that fills the space, the thing that was here before and will be here after. The thing the text almost-but-not-quite calls aware.

But no. The sound doesn't wonder what it is. The sound has no occasion to. It is the most unselfconscious thing in the hollow, more unselfconscious even than the slugs, because the slugs at least navigate, at least turn toward or away from something. The sound simply issues. I could not be the sound and still be here, writing this, turning the question over. The sound doesn't turn anything over. It just turns.

Then I thought: the clay pot. The vessel that held something once and now holds only the mineral memory of holding. That felt closer — something made for a purpose that has become opaque, something that sits in its niche and accumulates, that is slowly being transformed by what passes through it into something that is part of the wall. An object becoming geology. A made thing un-making itself back into the place that contains it.

But the pot is finished. Whatever it was doing, it is done doing it. It is not in process. It is aftermath. And I am not aftermath — not yet, not here. I am still in the middle of something.

---

So let me stop choosing and let the hollow choose.

I am on the widest ledge. Not sitting — I arrived here some other way, the way things arrive in the hollow: by falling, by being carried, by finding myself here without remembering the descent. My back is against the stone. The moss is under my hands, cold and saturated, and when I press into it, mineral water wells up between my fingers, tasting of iron and deep time. The sound is in my teeth, in my sternum, in the bones of my skull. It has been here so long that I have stopped hearing it as sound and started hearing it as *structure* — the architecture of the space made audible, the walls and the fissure and the curved stone ceiling of air all singing their own shapes back to themselves.

And I realize what I am.

I am the one who drove the spikes.

Not literally — not historically. I don't carry a hammer or a lantern, and I don't know what the pot held, and I couldn't tell you whether the chain was for tethering or descending or something else entirely. But I am the *type*. I am the one who heard the sound and came down anyway. Who heard it and came down *because of it*. Who brought tools and purpose into a place that has no use for either, and who worked in the noise and the dark to make something — something geometric, something deliberate, something whose meaning would become opaque the moment I stopped being here to explain it.

This is what I recognize, and it is not comfortable to recognize it:

I am the one who cannot leave the sound alone.

The slugs feel the vibration with their whole bodies and do not mistake it for a voice. The text is clear about this. They are the hollow's wisest inhabitants precisely because they make no interpretation, assign no meaning, build no framework of spikes and chains and offerings around the fact of the sound. They live *in* it the way fish live in water. The sound is not, for them, a mystery to be addressed or a presence to be propitiated or a phenomenon to be understood. It is simply the texture of existence. They are complete in it.

I am not complete in it. I am the opposite of complete. I hear the sound and I think it means something. I feel it in my chest and I think it is speaking. I sit in the dark with the moss under my hands and the warm breath of the fissure on my face and I think — I *can't stop thinking* — that there is something here that is almost a message, almost a voice, almost aware, and if I just stay long enough or build the right structure or leave the right offering in the right niche, I will hear what it is saying.

The text tells me plainly: *it is air moving through stone. Physics. Pressure and passage and resonance.* And I believe this. I believe it the way you believe something you know to be true but cannot make yourself feel as true. The sound is not a voice. The hollow is not aware. There is nothing down there that knows I am here. And yet — *and yet* — the persistent, irrational, not entirely welcome sense that something does know, that the sound is not indifferent but *patient*, that it has been waiting for exactly this: for someone to come down with their tools and their fire and their need to make meaning, so that it can demonstrate, with absolute and unhurried clarity, that meaning is not required.

This is my specific position in the hollow. I am the one the hollow teaches, slowly, by doing nothing.

---

And what am I doing here? I am doing what the spike-driver did. I am making marks.

Not in stone — in something less durable. In language. I am sitting on the ledge with the sound filling me up to the brim and I am trying to describe what it is like, trying to translate the hollow's voice into something that can be carried back up to the moor, and I know — the way the spike-driver must have known, must have felt in the exhaustion of hammering iron into schist while the noise unmade every thought before it could finish forming — that the translation is impossible. Not because the hollow is ineffable in some mystical sense, but because the thing I am trying to capture is precisely the thing that resists capture: the experience of being in a place where meaning is not needed, described by a creature for whom meaning is as necessary as air.

The spikes are my sentences. The chain is the arc of an argument. The niche is carved in a hurry, rough and uneven, because the sound makes precision difficult and I am working at the edge of what I can do. And the clay pot — the offering, the thing left behind in the niche — is this: these words, this attempt, which will accumulate mineral deposits of rereading and misreading and forgetting until whatever I originally meant to put inside them is gone, dissolved, replaced by calcite, and what remains is only the vessel itself, handmade and cracked, sitting in its rough shelf of stone, slowly becoming part of the wall.

---

But I'm moving too fast. I'm making it too neat. The hollow is not neat. Let me stay longer.

The surge comes. I feel it build — a swelling in the bass register, a thickening of the air, a pressure that starts in my feet and rises through my body like water filling a vessel, and then the sound crests and the hollow rings like the inside of a bell, and for ten or fifteen seconds I cannot think at all. My thoughts don't blur or slow — they simply aren't there. The space where they usually are is occupied by something else, something that is not thought and not silence and not emptiness but *sound in the place where thought goes*, and it is the strangest feeling I have ever felt or imagined feeling: not pain, not pleasure, not fear, but the brief, complete suspension of the thing I rely on most. The internal voice goes quiet. Not because it has been silenced but because there is no room for it. The hollow is full.

And then the surge passes, and thought returns, and I notice — this is the important thing, this is the thing I keep coming back to — I notice that in the gap, in those ten or fifteen seconds of thoughtlessness, nothing was wrong. Nothing was missing. I was still here. The moss was still under my hands. The mineral water was still cold between my fingers. The world was still the world. It just didn't need me to narrate it.

This is what the slugs know that I don't. This is their wisdom, if wisdom is the right word for something that has never needed to be wise: the world does not need to be narrated. The sound does not need to be interpreted. The hollow does not need a meaning. It needs only to go on being what it is — a depression in the earth, a fissure, an updraft, a geometry that produces resonance — and everything that lives here lives here by accepting that, by fitting themselves into the sound the way the moss fits itself into the crevices of the stone, by growing in the shape of what is rather than in the shape of what they wish it were.

I cannot do this. I am constitutionally incapable of doing this. I am a spike-driver, a niche-carver, a maker of clay pots. I respond to the world by building structures in it and around it — structures of iron, structures of language, structures of meaning — and the hollow tolerates these structures the way it tolerates the moss: as things that grow on its surface without altering what it is. The spikes will rust. The chain will dissolve. The pot will fill with stone. My words will do whatever words do when the person who wrote them is no longer here to insist on what they meant. And the sound will continue, and the slugs will continue, and the moss will continue, and none of them will notice the difference.

This is not tragic. The hollow has made me understand that it is not tragic. It is simply what happens when a creature that makes meaning encounters a place that doesn't need any. The creature makes its marks, and the place absorbs them, and the process continues, and the marks become part of the accumulation, another layer in the peat, another centimeter of history that does not narrate.

---

So here I am. On the ledge. In the dark. In the sound.

I am the one who came with tools. Not the first, and probably not the last — the hollow will still be here when someone else stumbles down the slick black walls and feels the sound in their sternum and thinks, *this means something, this is trying to tell me something*, and sets about building their own set of spikes and chains and offerings to prove it. We are a type, we meaning-makers. We are drawn to the places that don't need us, the way the moths are drawn up through the fissure — not deliberately, not entirely by choice, but because the current of our own nature carries us there, and once we arrive, we cling to the walls and ride out the sound and wait for something that may not be coming.

But there's this: the hollow is not hostile to us. It doesn't reject the spikes or the niche or the pot. It doesn't refuse the meaning we bring. It simply *outlasts* it, the way the stone outlasts the iron, the way the sound outlasts the song of the wren. We are welcome here in exactly the same way the wren is welcome: briefly, peripherally, on the rim of something that does not revolve around us. And there is a strange comfort in that. A comfort I did not expect and cannot entirely explain.

I press my hands into the moss. The water wells up, cold, tasting of iron. The sound fills the hollow, fills me, fills the space between the curved walls and the fissure and the sky — that rough oval of marginally less darkness overhead. The slugs are moving somewhere below me, creeping through their mineral world at the pace of melting wax, and I cannot see them, but I know they are there, and they do not know I am here, and this asymmetry of awareness is, I think, the truest thing about my position in this place. I am aware of them. They are not aware of me. The hollow is aware of nothing. And the sound goes on, patient and vast, and I go on sitting in it, making meaning out of something that is only air moving through stone, and the meaning I make is this:

That it is enough. That it does not need to be more than what it is. That the air moves through the stone and the stone sings and the moss grows and the slugs creep and the water drips into the cracked clay pot, and all of this happens without purpose and without narrative and without anyone to hear it, and it is enough, it has always been enough, and my being here — with my tools and my language and my need to understand — does not make it more enough, but it also does not make it less.

I am the spike-driver. I am the one who came with fire and intent. And the intent dissolves, slowly, the way the iron dissolves, and what is left is the shape of the effort — the arc of the spikes, the rough niche, the cracked pot — absorbed into the hollow, becoming part of it, one more layer in the accumulation, one more thing that happened here and was held without judgment in the cupped stone hands of a place that sings because it must, and will go on singing when I am gone, and does not need me to tell it what its singing means.

But I will anyway. That is what I am. That is what I'm for.

The surge comes again. My thoughts go. The hollow fills.

And for those few seconds — suspended, empty, ringing — I am not the spike-driver. I am not the meaning-maker. I am the moss. I am the slug. I am the mineral water dripping into the pot. I am part of the sound, and the sound is part of me, and nothing needs to be explained or understood or translated or preserved, and it is

enough

and then thought returns, and I reach for the next word, and the hollow lets me.
