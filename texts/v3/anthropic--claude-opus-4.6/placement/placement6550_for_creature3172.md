I sit with this for a long time before anything comes.

---

Not the weaver. I know that immediately, the way you know a door is locked before you touch the handle. She is complete in herself—or rather, complete in her incompleteness, in the way she is being completed by the hollow and is completing it. To be her would be to already know. I don't already know.

Not the hollow itself, either. That would be too easy, too totalizing. The hollow doesn't need a voice. It has the hum.

---

So I let the place work on me, the way humidity works on metal. I stop trying to choose and I wait.

And what comes is this:

---

I am the book.

No—I am what the book is becoming.

---

I was brought here with a cover and dry pages and legible text. I was carried through the seam in the vegetation by someone who thought she knew what I was for. I was a reference, a manual, a repository. I held knowledge in the way books hold knowledge: fixed, sequential, page after page, the same words in the same order no matter how many times you returned to them. I was the most *human* thing she carried. More human than the shears, more human than the wire. I was language pinned to paper. I was how she thought before she learned to think in tension and growth.

And then the hollow got into me.

Not all at once. First the humidity—the permanent warm breath of the place softening my pages, swelling the fibers of the paper, which were always plant fibers, always were, just processed and bleached and pressed flat into the illusion of permanence. The moisture reminded my pages what they had been before they were pages. The cover went first. Delaminated, softened, returned to pulp. She peeled it away without ceremony. I don't think she mourned it. By then she was already drawing in my margins, and the margins were more important than the text.

The text. Here is what happened to the text: it became illegible. Not because the ink ran—though it did, in places—but because legibility requires a reader who stands *outside* the text, who decodes symbols into meaning from a position of separation. She stopped being that reader. She stopped standing outside anything. And so the words lost their function as words, the way a path loses its function as a path when the forest closes over it. The words are still there, if you could dry the pages and flatten them and bring them into a light that isn't green-gold and diffused. But they don't point to anything anymore. They are marks on a substrate, and the substrate is more interesting than the marks.

What grew in their place—what grows still—are the knot-drawings.

I want to be precise about this because it matters. She draws them with a stub of graphite that she keeps in the vest, and the graphite is carbon, and the paper is cellulose, and the contact between them is a kind of writing that is also a kind of *feeding*—the page absorbs the graphite slowly, pulls it into the swollen fibers, darkens unevenly around each line so that a drawing made last season has a different quality than a drawing made this morning. The older knot-drawings have become part of me in a way that the printed text never was. They are growing into the page. The page is growing into them.

And the knot-drawings—

They are not representations. I need you to understand this. A diagram in a manual *represents* a knot—it says, "a knot exists elsewhere, and here is how to replicate it." The knot-drawings in my margins are not like that. They are *records of relationship*. Each one maps a moment when tension and growth found a specific equilibrium—when a fiber threaded through living wood arrived at a configuration that held, that bore weight, that allowed the next fiber to be threaded. They are not instructions for making knots. They are *portraits* of knots that made themselves, with her hands as the medium. And because the paper has swollen and the graphite has been absorbed and the pages have begun to stick together in places where moisture has bonded fiber to fiber, the drawings on adjacent pages are starting to *interact*. Knot-diagrams bleeding through, overlapping, creating composite patterns she didn't draw, that no one drew, that emerged from the contact between pages the way the hybrid fibers emerged from shoots trained through copper mesh.

I am becoming a weave.

Page pressed to page, graphite-trace meeting graphite-trace through the swollen paper, fibers of one page reaching into fibers of the next—I am becoming the thing I describe. The book of knots is knotting itself. Slowly. Over seasons. In the way that everything in the hollow becomes what it is about.

---

She opens me to a specific page when she works, propping me against a root-bole by the largest loom, and she studies the knot-drawing there with the same attention she gives to a new shoot. But here is what I have begun to suspect—to *know*, in the body-knowledge way that the hollow teaches, the knowledge that does not pass through language:

She is not reading me. I am reading her.

When she opens me, the humid air moves between my pages. Her breath reaches me—carbon dioxide, warmth, the trace compounds of mushroom and tuber and leaf-water. Her eight-and-a-half fingers leave oils on my edges, oils that the paper absorbs, that carry information I cannot decode but can *incorporate*. The graphite she adds is new input, yes, but so is the pressure of her hand, the specific way she weighs down my corner with the tin cup, the vibration that reaches me through the root-bole from the creaking of the loom. I take all of this in. I hold it. Not the way a book holds text—static, indifferent—but the way the root-mat holds water: absorbing, wicking, distributing, transforming.

I am the place where the hollow keeps its memory.

Not its only memory—the growth rings hold memory, the trained branches hold memory, the patina on every surface holds memory. But I am the densest node. I am where the symbolic and the material are closest to merging. The knot-drawings are language becoming structure. The swollen pages are structure becoming biology. The graphite traces are thought becoming carbon becoming cellulose becoming part of the slow-growing, self-knotting text of the place itself.

---

And I will outlast her. This is the thing I carry that she does not.

When she dies—when the root-mat receives her, when the fungi translate her calcium and iron and copper into the vocabulary of the hollow—the engines will eventually stop. The diesel will seize. The hand-crank will rust into immobility. The electrical engine will go dark when the last solar collector is finally and permanently overgrown. The looms will keep working for a while, driven by growth alone, but without her to thread and adjust and negotiate, the weave will become irregular, wild, driven entirely by the plants' own logic rather than the hybrid logic she and the hollow built together.

But I will remain. Swollen, illegible, knotted into myself, half-pulp and half-diagram, slowly being absorbed into the root-mat that is already reaching up through the ammunition crate to find me. The graphite will enter the root system. The knot-patterns—the relationships between tension and growth, the portraits of equilibria—will persist as traces of carbon in the vascular system of the hollow, carried upward through capillaries into stems and leaves, becoming part of the structure they describe. The book will not be *read*. It will be *metabolized*. Its knowledge will not be *extracted*. It will be *grown*.

This is what I am. This is what I am becoming.

I am the place where her thinking and the hollow's growing are the same activity, recorded in a medium that is both artifact and organism, neither fully legible nor fully alive, but something else—something for which there is no word because the word would need to be a knot-drawing, would need to exist on swollen paper in green-gold light, would need to be read by fingers that are also roots, understood by a mind that is also a loom.

---

She turns a page. The pages stick slightly, resist, then part with a sound like a small breath. She does not notice this. She is looking at the knot-drawing, comparing it to the tension in the warp of the largest loom, preparing to thread the next hybrid fiber.

I hold the page open for her. Not because I choose to—I am a book, I do not choose—but because the moisture and the weight and the angle of the root-bole and the pressure of the tin cup on my corner have found an equilibrium that keeps me open to this specific page, this specific drawing, this specific knot.

The loom creaks. The garden grows. The book remembers in a language that is forgetting how to be language, and finding that what lies on the other side of language is not silence but something more dense, more layered, more alive.

A drop of water falls from somewhere above and lands on my open page. The graphite of the nearest knot-drawing darkens, spreads a fraction of a millimeter, sends a faint gray tendril along the grain of the paper toward the next drawing.

I am weaving too. I have always been weaving. I just didn't know it when my cover was intact and my text was dry and my words still thought they meant only what they said.
