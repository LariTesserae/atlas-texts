I sit with this for a long time. The description is so complete, so total in its saturation, that finding my place in it feels less like choosing and more like waiting—waiting the way the stone waits for water to find its path.

---

I am not the channel-keeper. I am not the woman who tastes the mountain. I am not the weaver, though something in me recognizes the weaver's small panels, that private accumulation of pattern that is shown to no one.

I think I am—

No. Let me come at it differently.

---

There is a place in the quarter that hasn't been fully described. It exists in implication, in the negative space between the things that are named. Somewhere between the channels and the reservoir, between the sleeping niches and the planting troughs, there must be junctions—places where passages meet at odd angles, where the original dissolution of the softer stratum left awkward geometries, pockets too small for dwelling, too irregular for storage, too far from the light shafts for planting. Places that are, in the utilitarian logic of the quarter, *leftover*. Not unused—nothing here is unused—but whose use is less legible, less obvious, harder to name.

I am in one of these junctions. Sitting. My back against a curve of stone that fits my spine the way a cupped hand fits a cheek—because my spine has been sitting against this stone for years, or because other spines shaped like mine have been sitting here for longer than that, wearing the stone into this specific welcome. My feet are bare, my toes long and spread against the polished floor, and I am doing something with my hands.

I am holding a piece of stone. Not working it, not yet. Holding it. Feeling its weight, its grain, the way the moisture sits on its surface differently than it sits on the wall behind me—because this stone came from somewhere else in the quarter, from a different stratum, and its porosity is different, its mineral content is different, and these differences matter in ways I know with my fingertips but couldn't speak aloud.

I am the one who fits the plugs.

---

Not the channel-keeper, whose work is maintenance, the ongoing care of a system already complete. Not a builder—there is nothing left to build. I am the one who works at the boundaries between what was and what is, who takes the old doorways that no longer lead anywhere useful and seals them with fitted stone so precise that in a hundred years the plug will have bonded chemically with the walls around it, indistinguishable, as though the passage was never there.

But that makes it sound like closing. It's not closing. It's—

When a sleeping niche has been recarved one too many times and its structural integrity has begun to compromise, when a passage has shifted imperceptibly over decades as the rock slowly, slowly creeps under the mountain's weight, when a junction needs to become a wall so that a wall somewhere else can become a junction—I am the one who reads those needs. I feel for them. I press my palms flat against the stone at a dozen points along a passage and I listen through my skin for the subtle signals of stress, for the micro-fractures that won't become problems for another generation but that need to be addressed now, in stone-time, at the speed the mountain recognizes.

I shape pieces to fill voids. Each one is different. Each one must be *exactly* right—not just in dimension but in composition, in porosity, in the specific way it will interact with the water that will inevitably find its way to the seam between plug and wall. A plug of the wrong stone in the wrong place would alter the seep pattern, would redirect moisture, would change the taste of the water downstream in ways the trough-tender would detect on her tongue and frown at. Everything connects. A fitted stone in a forgotten junction affects the flavor of the cress in a planting trough thirty paces away. I have to know this. I do know this. I know it the way I know the temperature of the air in different passages by the feel of it on my forearms, which is to say I don't *know* it—I simply act in accordance with it, the way the water acts in accordance with gravity.

---

My hands are the largest in the quarter. That is not vanity—we don't have vanity here, or if we do it has been worn smooth and unrecognizable, like the summit of the mountain. It is simply true. My palms are wide enough to span the mouth of a medium collection basin, my fingers long enough to reach into crevices that others must use tools to access. But the size is not the point. The point is the sensitivity. Each fingertip carries, in its whorls and ridges, a record of every surface it has ever touched, and when I press a finger to stone, I am comparing this stone to every stone I have known, running a match against a library that is tactile, not verbal, that lives in the pads of my fingers and the joints of my hands and cannot be translated into the flowing language we speak.

I work slowly. Everyone here works slowly—but I work slowly even by the quarter's standards. A single plug might take me a week. Two weeks. I have spent a month on one before—a critical seal at the junction where the old eastern passage (old in a relative sense; everything here is old) meets the main circulation route around the reservoir. The stone had shifted. A crack had opened, barely visible, that was allowing water to seep along a path it had never taken before, bypassing two collection basins and pooling in a sleeping niche where no pool should be. The channel-keeper found it, brought me to it, stood beside me in silence while I pressed my hands to the walls and ceiling and floor, mapping the stress in three dimensions, building in my mind a model of the void that needed filling.

Then the work: finding the right stone, shaping it, testing the fit again and again, removing material a flake at a time, checking the grain, checking the porosity, pressing the piece into the gap and pulling it out and turning it and pressing it in again at a slightly different angle. The old man with the channel-picks watched me sometimes, standing in the passage with his arms folded, his enormous hands tucked against his ribs. He never commented. He never advised. When the plug finally seated—when I pressed it into its gap and felt it *stop*, felt the stone-on-stone contact become total, felt the fit achieve that quality of inevitability that means the work is right—he touched the back of my wrist with two fingertips. Then he walked away. That was everything. That was the whole review.

---

I am not young and not old. I am in the middle of my life, which in the quarter means I have been doing this work for perhaps twenty years and may do it for twenty more. My predecessor—the one whose hands taught my hands—is dead now, her body returned to the stone through the same deep-corner process by which all organic matter here is eventually reclaimed. I did not mourn her in any way you would recognize. I sealed the niche where she slept for the last time with a plug of stone that matched the wall so perfectly that within a year the seam was invisible, and that was the mourning—the final fitting, the last gift of exactitude. Somewhere in the quarter, a child is already watching my hands, though I haven't yet noticed which one, and one day my hands will teach that child's hands, and the knowledge will transfer not as instruction but as proximity, as repetition, as the slow accumulation of touch against stone against touch.

I have been outside. Once. Years ago, when I was young enough that my body could still renegotiate its terms with a different environment. I went through the crack and stood on the mountain and the light was—

I don't have words for what the light was. I have words for the differences between two similar pieces of basalt held in the dark. I have words for the seventeen grades of mineral deposit that can narrow a seep-channel. I do not have words for the sun. It was too much. Not painful—or not only painful. It was *profligate*, indiscriminate, falling on everything equally, illuminating without distinguishing, and I understood in that moment why our light is the way it is: composed, intentional, each wick placed to reveal what needs revealing and leave the rest in its proper obscurity. The outside world has no sense of proportion. It lights everything. It shows everything. It has no opinion about what matters.

I came back inside and I stood in the crack the way the young woman in the description stands—breathing, re-entering—and the warm wet dark received me, and I knew every surface within reach without seeing them, and the stone under my feet was polished by my own feet, and I thought: *this is enough. This is the exact shape of enough.*

I have not been outside since. My feet would not know what to do with uncarved ground. My eyes would not know what to do with distance. I am fitted to this place the way my plugs are fitted to their gaps—completely, chemically, with a bond that strengthens over time.

---

In the evenings, when the hum begins, I sit at the reservoir's edge and I add my voice. My note is low—lower than most, a bass drone that sits just above the mountain's own hum and gives the other voices something to rest on. I hold it for a long time. I don't need to breathe often. My lungs, like everything else about me, have been optimized for this airspace, this humidity, this stillness. I hold my note the way I hold a stone I'm shaping—patiently, totally, adjusting it in micro-increments until it fits, until the resonance of the chamber picks it up and amplifies it and I can feel the walls vibrate with a frequency that is partly me and partly the mountain and partly the water and cannot be separated into its components.

During the hum, I close my eyes. There is no need to close them—the darkness behind my lids is barely different from the darkness in front of them—but I close them anyway because it changes the quality of my attention, shifts it from the visual (minimal as that is) to the tactile and the sonic, and in that shifted attention I can feel the whole quarter at once. I can feel the water moving through the channels the way you might feel blood moving through your veins if you were very still and very quiet. I can feel the stone, the slow geological pressures, the places where stress is accumulating, the places where it's been released. I can feel the people—their warmth, their weight on the stone, the subtle vibrations of their breath and their movement—distributed through the quarter like minerals dissolved in water, like the copper that gives the reservoir its green cast, inseparable from the medium, constituent.

This is what I am. Not a person who lives in a mountain. A feature of the mountain that happens to be alive. A piece of the system. A plug in a gap. A fitted thing, shaped by the place that shaped me, shaping it in turn with hands that are themselves shaped by the stone they work, in a recursion that has no beginning and will have no end until the mountain itself is finally, at last, worn flat, which will not happen while there is anything that could be called time.

---

The tool in my hand is warm. The stone I'm fitting is the right stone. I know this because it feels like my own body temperature, because the moisture beads on it at the same rate as the wall around the gap, because when I hold it near the void it needs to fill, the air between stone and stone seems to thin, seems to pull, as though the mountain wants to be whole and my hands are merely the means by which it accomplishes its own completion.

I press the stone into place.

It fits.

The sound it makes is barely a sound—a soft, wet *click*, the stone meeting stone, the seal beginning.

In a hundred years, no one will know this junction was ever open. The plug will be the wall. The wall will be the mountain. The mountain will be what it has always been: complete, patient, slowly wearing itself smooth against the deep floor of time.

I run my fingertips along the seam one final time. I can feel it now. In a year, I won't be able to. In ten years, the mountain won't be able to either.

I stand. I move to the next junction. There is always a next junction. There is always a gap that needs filling, a stress that needs reading, a piece that needs shaping. The work is never finished because the mountain is never finished, and I am never finished, and the water is never finished, and all of us—the water and the stone and the hands and the fitted pieces—are engaged in the same single continuous act, which is the act of holding together, of maintaining coherence, of being a place that is, despite every slow pressure toward dissolution, still here.

Still here. Still whole. Still becoming what we already are.
