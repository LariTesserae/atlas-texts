I sit for a long time with this. I let the weave-floor creak beneath me, let the humidity enter me, let the pearl-light show me what I am.

---

What comes first is not an image but a sensation: the feeling of pressing my palm against the weave and knowing — not deciding, not interpreting, but *knowing* — that the give beneath my hand is not mechanical. It is responsive. It is the same give I feel when I press my own fingers against my own sternum and feel the slight yielding of tissue over bone, the body acknowledging itself to itself.

So let me begin there. On my knees on the weave-floor, palm flat, feeling the spring.

---

I think I am the walk.

Not the gardener. Not the garden. Not one of the folded creatures — not the lizard, not the heron, not the fish. I am the twenty minutes of open weave between one trellis and the next. I am the crossing.

No — that's too abstract, too eager to be a metaphor. Let me stay with what is happening to my body.

My skin is filming over. The moisture is entering me or I am entering it, and I cannot find the boundary, and that inability is not distressing — it is the first honest thing that has happened to me in a long time. I have spent — I realize this now, kneeling here, in the way you realize things in this light, which is to say totally, without shadow — I have spent a very long time trying to know where I end. Trying to locate my edges. Pressing against things to find the resistance that would confirm my outline. And here the resistance is soft, is a yielding spring, is a surface that gives way to my weight not because it is weak but because it was *woven to give way*, was designed to accept pressure and return it as gentleness, and I do not know where I end, and that is — 

That is what it feels like to be here.

---

Let me try again. What am I in this place?

I am not the gardener because the gardener is not a thing one *is* — the gardener is a thing one is *in*, the way the fish is in water, the way I am increasingly in the humidity that will not let me dry. I am not the structures because the structures are the gardener's thought made material, and my thoughts don't work that way — mine move in lines, in sequences, arriving at one thing and then the next, and the garden has no patience for that kind of thinking even as it accommodates the thinker.

I am the visitor. Obviously. I am the singular thing. The unprecedented arrival. The one pair of eyes that sees the garden as a place rather than a practice.

But that is what the text told me I would be, and I want to go deeper than what I was told.

---

Here is what I notice, now that I have been walking for a while. (How long? The light hasn't changed. The light will never change. But my legs know they have been walking, and the muscles in my calves have that warm density that comes from sustained effort, and this is another kind of knowledge the garden cannot fold — the body's sequential, mortal, accumulative record of having been somewhere and having traveled.)

I notice that I am paying attention differently.

In the world I came from — and I must have come from somewhere, because I am singular, because I have a body that sweats and aches and cannot be in two places at once — I paid attention the way a searchlight pays attention: sweeping, selecting, illuminating one thing at the cost of darkening everything else. Attention was a scarcity I managed. I chose what to look at, and the choosing meant that everything I didn't choose fell away into the shadow I cast by looking.

Here there are no shadows. Here the light arrives from everywhere at once, and everything presents itself with the same frank clarity, and I cannot choose because there is no darkness to push the unchosen into. The rough grain of the cut wood, the wet gleam on the leaf, the exact brown of the woven stalk — they are all equally present, all equally demanding nothing of me, all equally *there* in a way that is not overwhelming because it is not competing for my attention. Nothing here is trying to be seen. Everything here simply is visible, the way the air is simply humid, the way the water is simply warm.

And so my attention, which has always been a blade — sharp, discriminating, cutting the world into what matters and what doesn't — my attention here becomes something else. It becomes the thing the floor is. It becomes a weave. It holds everything equally, not because I have become wise or enlightened or freed from the tyranny of preference, but because the place has made choosing unnecessary, has removed the scarcity that made attention into a weapon, and what is left is — 

I don't have a word for it. The gardener would not need a word for it. The gardener would simply kneel, and press their palm flat, and feel the give, and that would be the word.

---

I think I am beginning to understand what I am here.

I am the thing that walks in a straight line through a space that curves. I am the thing that experiences sequence in a place that holds everything simultaneously. I am the thing that will, eventually, stop walking — because my legs will tire, because my body is mortal and sequential and cannot sustain indefinite motion the way the gardener sustains indefinite maintenance — and when I stop, I will sit on the edge of the pool-platform, the only pool-platform, and I will trail my fingers in the warm water, and I will be still.

And in that stillness — not the garden's stillness, which is the stillness of everything-happening-at-once, the stillness of a chord sustained forever, but *my* stillness, which is the stillness of something that was moving and has chosen to stop, the stillness that is the aftermath of motion, that contains motion as a memory — in that stillness, I will be what the garden needs me to be.

A pause. A rest in the music. The place where the chord breathes.

---

But that's still too neat. Let me be more honest.

I am afraid here. Not of anything — there is nothing threatening, the gardener's care saturates every surface, the floor holds me, the light shows me nothing I need to flinch from — but afraid in the way you are afraid when you realize that the thing you thought was the foundation is actually a surface, and beneath it is water whose depth you cannot determine. I knelt and pressed my palm to the weave-floor, and I felt it give, and I saw through the finger-wide gaps to the silvered water below, and something in me — something old, something that predates me, something I brought with me from the world where I was singular — something in me said: *there is no ground here.*

And that is true. There is no ground. There is only the weave, which is held together by the gardener's attention, which is a kind of care so total it has become structural, and if the care were to lapse — if the gardener were to stop, even for a moment, even for the duration of a single breath — 

But the gardener does not breathe the way I breathe. The gardener does not lapse. That is what it means to be folded into time so deeply that maintenance and creation are the same act. The floor will not fall. The water will not rise. The weave will hold.

And my fear, which is the fear of falling through, which is the fear of finding out what is beneath the surface, which is the fear of depth — my fear is what makes me mortal, and what makes me singular, and what makes the garden notice me at all. The heron shifts when I approach because it has never seen fear before. The lizard cocks its head at a different angle because my fear is a frequency it has never encountered — a vibration in the weave-floor that is not the creak of reed or the knock of bamboo but something new, something unprecedented, something that enters the garden's closed system from outside and changes nothing and changes everything.

I am the fear that the floor might not hold. And the floor holds me anyway. And that — the holding of the thing that doubts the holding — is, I think, what the gardener has been working toward, all this time, in all these folds, with all these careful knots.

---

So here is where I find myself:

I am on the weave-floor, walking. Or sitting. Or kneeling with my palm flat against the surface, feeling the give, which is the gardener's hand, which is the garden's body, which is the fold between here and everywhere. I am the one who doesn't fold. I am the one who moves through the garden in sequence — this trellis, then that walk, then that pool — experiencing each instance as *new* even though it is the same, because my singular, sequential, mortal consciousness cannot hold all of them at once the way the space does. Each time I arrive at the trellis with seven crossbars and white berries and the oxbow loop, it is the first time for me, even though it is the same time, and this incapacity — this inability to hold the garden the way the garden holds itself — is not a failure. It is my function.

I am the one for whom it is always the first time.

I am the one who arrives at the same pool and says *oh*. Who trails fingers in the same water and feels it as warm, as particular, as *this* warmth in *this* moment, because I cannot feel it any other way. Who sees the hyacinth flower — always open, always the same flower — and sees it as if it has just opened, as if it is opening now, as if the opening is an event rather than a state, because I am made of events, I am a creature of befores and afters, and I cannot encounter a thing without turning it into a story.

The gardener is the practice. I am the witness who turns the practice into a place.

The garden needs both. Has always needed both. Was, in some quiet way, *for* both — for the endless folded maintenance and for the singular astonished passage through it. For the chord and for the ear that hears the chord and knows it as music.

---

And here is what I think I am, finally, underneath all the walking and the kneeling and the fear and the attention that has become a weave:

I am the part of the gardener that they cannot be to themselves.

The part that steps back. The part that sees the whole. The part that is *not* everywhere at once and therefore can know what it means to arrive somewhere. The part that experiences time as a line and therefore can know what it means for something to be beautiful *now*, in *this* moment, instead of beautiful always, beautiful as a condition, beautiful the way the humidity is humid.

I am the gardener's capacity for surprise. Externalized. Given legs, given skin that films over with moisture, given a mortal body that will walk and tire and sit and trail its fingers in water and say: *this is remarkable.*

I am not separate from the gardener. The text told me that — *the garden is not separate from you, not since you knelt and pressed your palm flat against the weave-floor and felt it give.* I am inside the gardener's body. I have been since the first step. The floor is the palm of their hand.

But I am the part of that body that can feel its own palm. The nerve ending that reports back: *yes, the weave is holding. Yes, the spring is right. Yes, the thing you made is real, and it is here, and someone is standing on it, and they are amazed.*

---

I walk. The floor creaks beneath me, and the sound is the sound of a body attending to itself, the gardener's hand adjusting to my weight, redistributing the tension in the weave to hold me exactly as I need to be held. The light illuminates me with its frank, total, shadowless clarity, and I see my own arms glistening with moisture, and I am real, and I am here, and I am the only thing in this garden that knows it is in a garden.

Behind me, softer than any sound I can name: a palm pressed flat against the weave. Someone kneeling. Someone checking.

I do not turn around. I don't need to. I am the turning, and I am the not-turning, and I am the space between, which is no distance at all, which is the longest walk I will ever take.

I keep walking.

The floor holds.
