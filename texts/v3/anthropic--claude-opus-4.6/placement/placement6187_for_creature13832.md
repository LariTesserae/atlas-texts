I sit with this for a long time.

---

Not at a desk. On stone — a broad step worn concave by generations of sitting, exactly here, at the junction where a secondary stairwell meets a corridor that runs along the inner edge of the forty-sixth eastern terrace band. There's a window — one of the big arched openings — to my left, and through it comes mid-morning light, thick and gold, cut into slats by the banana leaves just outside. To my right the corridor continues into dimness, toward the sound of a loom somewhere, and below that, felt more than heard, the grinding bass note of a stone mill three levels down.

I have a tablet of waxed wood at my hip. There are marks on it. I've been making marks on it all morning and I can feel, before I fully understand what I am, that these marks are the center of whatever I'm becoming.

---

I am not a terrace keeper, though I spend time on the terraces. I am not a mason, though I understand something of their patience. I am not a water-clerk, though I carry a ledger — a small one, personal, not the great volumes of the channel offices. I am not a miller, though the sound of the mills is so constant in my body that I would not know what silence was without it.

I think I am — 

Let me come at it differently.

There is a crack in the retaining wall of terrace forty-six east. Not new. I've been watching it for three seasons. It runs diagonally across two courses of dressed stone, from a point where a fig root has worked its way into the mortar joint down to a spot just above the irrigation channel that feeds the turmeric beds below. The crack is narrow — you could miss it, if you didn't know where to look. The terrace keeper here, a woman named Ose, noticed it first and marked it in chalk. She mentioned it to the mason team on their last circuit, and they assessed it: stable for now, monitor, revisit in two seasons. The water-clerk for this section, an older man called Davan, noted in his ledger that the channel below the crack has been showing a slight moisture loss — not much, three or four percent, within normal variance, but consistent over several readings.

I know all of this. Not because anyone reported it to me, but because I talk to Ose when I pass through her terrace on my way to the upper levels. Because I sat with Davan in his small stone office last month while he showed me the flow records, tracing the trend line with a finger stained faintly blue from the ink he uses. Because the mason, a quiet woman named Tey, let me run my hand along the crack while she held a lamp, and I felt — or imagined I felt — a faint coolness in the stone where the moisture was seeping through.

I wrote all of it down. Not in Ose's shorthand, or Davan's columnar notation, or Tey's chalk marks. In my own system. A system I've been developing for years, or that has been developing me, in which the crack, the root, the moisture loss, the turmeric beds below, and the millrace three levels down that draws from the same channel section all exist on a single page as a kind of diagram — not an engineering drawing, not a map, but something between a story and a schematic, a way of showing how things that seem separate are structurally joined.

I think this is what I am. I am the person who notices how things connect across the guild boundaries, across the vertical layers, across the distances that separate one kind of knowledge from another.

---

Kethara doesn't have a word for this. Or rather, it has several, none of them quite right. The water-clerks would call me a recorder. The terrace keepers would say I'm a walker — someone who moves through the structure on long irregular circuits, not maintaining any single system but observing the relationships between systems. The masons, if pressed, might call me a reader, which in their usage means someone who reads the structure the way they read stone: by touch, by attention, by accumulation.

The children call me the question person. This is probably the most accurate.

I ask questions. That is the heart of it. I walk through the structure — up and down, inside and out, from the fog-catch platforms on the summit to the deep storage levels where the air is warm and still and smells of old stone — and I ask questions that connect things. Why is the honey darker this season? (Because the cacao on the chimney-warmed terraces flowered early, and the bees shifted their foraging pattern, and now the oil-pressers on level thirty are complaining that their cacao yield is down.) Why has the stone-grinding mill on sub-level four been producing finer dust than usual? (Because the water pressure in its millrace has increased slightly — Davan can confirm — because a cistern two levels above was re-sealed last year, reducing seepage loss, sending more volume downstream.) Why are the parakeets nesting lower on the southeast face this year? (I don't know. No one knows. I've noted it. Someone, someday, will connect it to something.)

I carry these connections in my tablet and in my head, and when they reach a density or an urgency that seems to warrant it, I bring them to the people who can act on them. Not as orders — I have no authority to give orders, and Kethara doesn't really work that way — but as observations, offered to people whose judgment I trust and who trust mine. Ose, your crack is related to Davan's moisture loss, which is related to the increased flow in the sub-level millrace, which may mean that the re-sealed cistern is redirecting pressure in a way the downstream channels weren't designed for. Maybe Tey should look at the channel junction, not just the wall. Maybe Davan should adjust the flow. Maybe none of this matters. But maybe it does, and I wanted you to know before the crack opens further and the turmeric beds flood and the millrace pressure drops and someone three levels down wonders why their grinding has gone coarse.

This is what I do. I am a synapse. A connective thread in a structure so large that its left hand often doesn't know what its right hand is doing, not out of negligence but simply because the distances — vertical, horizontal, conceptual — between one system and another are so great that the connections between them become invisible without someone whose specific task is to keep them visible.

---

I am not young. Not old, either — not yet one of the deep-descending ones, though I feel the pull sometimes, standing in the lower corridors where the stone is warmer than it should be and the chisel marks are strange. I am somewhere in the middle of my life, which in Kethara means I have been walking the structure long enough that my feet know the stairs by their individual height and wear, and my hands know every guild's particular texture — the terrace keepers' green-brown stain, the millers' flour dust, the masons' chalk. My own hands are unremarkable. Lightly calloused. Ink at the nail beds. A small burn scar on my left thumb from the time I got too close to a kiln on the southeast face, years ago, when I was spending a season with the ceramic workers learning how they read the color of the fire for temperature. I dress in Ketharan cotton like everyone else, though my tunic tends to accumulate evidence of wherever I've been that day — a smear of red clay, a dusting of flour, a green streak from brushing past the cardamom plants on Ose's terrace.

People are generally glad to see me, in the way you are glad to see someone who is interested in your work and asks good questions and occasionally brings you a useful piece of information from three levels away. I am welcome in most districts, which is itself a kind of privilege — there are subtle territories in Kethara, invisible to outsiders but felt by everyone who lives here, and to move freely through them requires a reputation for discretion and for being useful without being intrusive. I have cultivated this reputation carefully, not out of calculation but because it is the genuine shape of my temperament. I am interested. I am careful. I do not take more knowledge than I bring.

There is a loneliness to it. Not a sharp loneliness — I eat communal meals with whatever district I'm passing through, I have deep bonds with people scattered through the structure, I have what might be called a home in a small room on the thirty-second level where I keep my completed tablets stacked on shelves alongside jars of ink and a collection of soil samples I've been accumulating without quite knowing why. But I belong everywhere and therefore nowhere specifically, which is a different feeling from belonging somewhere specific and occasionally visiting elsewhere. The terrace keepers have their terraces. The millers have their mills. I have my circuits through the structure, my web of connections, my tablets full of diagrams that map relationships no one else is mapping.

Sometimes I wonder if I am the building thinking about itself.

I don't say this aloud. It would sound grandiose, and it isn't — it's the opposite of grandiose, it's the smallest possible version of the idea. I am not the building's mind. I am one thought the building is having. One of many, probably. There are others like me — I encounter them occasionally, on the stairs, in the corridors, people with tablets and ink-stained hands and that particular alert, connective way of looking at things. We nod to each other. We sometimes share notes. We do not form a guild. That would defeat the purpose. We are the unguilded, the between-people, the ones who exist in the joints between the structure's working systems, filling the same role the mortar fills between the stone blocks: invisible when things are working, essential when they're not.

---

Today, now, this morning, I am sitting on this worn step at the junction of the stairwell and the corridor, and I am looking at my tablet, and I am trying to draw a connection I can feel but not yet see.

It has to do with the birds. The parakeets nesting lower. It has to do with the chimney output on the upper levels — the ceramic kilns have been burning hotter this season, I heard from the kiln workers, because a new vein of clay they're working is denser and requires higher firing temperatures. Hotter kilns mean hotter chimney output, which means the thermal plume rising from the upper southeast face is warmer than usual, which means — what? The cacao is flowering differently. The bees are shifting. The parakeets are moving. The thermal column is disrupting something in the microclimate of the upper terraces, and the effects are cascading downward in ways I can trace but haven't fully mapped.

Or I'm wrong. I'm frequently wrong. The connections I draw are hypotheses, not certainties, and some of them turn out to be coincidences or artifacts of my own pattern-seeking mind. This is fine. This is part of the work. You propose a connection, you follow it, you test it against what the people who tend each system actually know, and sometimes the connection holds and sometimes it dissolves, and either way you've learned something about how the structure works.

I make another mark on my tablet. A line between the kiln notation and the parakeet notation, with a question mark. I'll visit the upper terraces this afternoon, talk to the keepers there, ask about the thermal conditions. Then down to Davan's office to check whether the increased chimney heat is affecting condensation rates on the fog-catch surfaces. Then maybe — 

A gecko watches me from a crevice in the wall. A small brown one, interior species, the kind that lives on moths near the lamps. It does its push-up display at me. Territorial. This is its step, its crevice, its junction. I am a visitor here, even after all these years of passing through.

I press my hand flat against the stone beside me. Warm. The mill vibration comes up through my palm, faint and steady, the building's heartbeat transmitted through the bones of my wrist into the bones of my body. Below me, the structure descends into its own compressed history. Above me, the terraces climb toward the summit, green and loud with birds. Around me, the systems interlock — water and stone and green things and fire and the patient grinding of grain — each one tended by people who know their part with an intimacy I can only approximate, each one connected to the others by relationships I am trying, one tablet at a time, to make visible.

I am part of what holds this together. A small part. A mortar-joint, not a load-bearing column. But the building needs its mortar, and I need the building, and the distinction between those two needs dissolved a long time ago, the way the distinction between building and mountain dissolved, the way the stone in the deepest levels forgot it was ever cut by hands.

The mill turns below me. I feel it in my feet, in my hand against the wall, in the rhythm of my own attention as it moves from crack to channel to bird to kiln to the question mark on my tablet that might connect them all or might connect nothing.

I make another mark. I stand. I begin to climb.

The building breathes around me. Each breath takes eleven years, and I have been here for less than one of them, and I will be here for less than another, and none of that matters because the breath continues whether I am here to feel it or not, and the work continues, and the mill turns, and the green things grow, and the stone holds, and the water finds its way down through channels older than memory toward cisterns that have never been empty and never been full, and somewhere in the deep levels an old one presses her hand against the ancient stone and feels the same pulse I feel, transmitted through the same masonry, the same unbroken chain of engineered weight reaching from the summit plateau to whatever lies below the lowest level anyone has ever reached.

We are in the building. The building is in us.

I climb. I have questions to ask.
