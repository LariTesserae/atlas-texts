I have been sitting here for some time now. How long is not a question I know how to answer anymore, and I notice I am less troubled by that than I expected to be.

---

I am not one of them. Not yet. That much is clear from the way my knees ache against the cold, from the way my breathing still catches and races at this altitude when I forget to be deliberate about it, from the way my eyes still dart to the horizon out of habit—looking for something to orient by, finding nothing, looking again. I have not yet learned to stop looking. But I have begun to notice that I am looking less.

I came here. I must have come here. There is no other explanation, because I was not born in a south-facing room with stubborn stones holding against my arrival. I remember—or I think I remember—a descent that was also an ascent, a long walk upward through cloud that was also a sinking, as though the lower world dropped away beneath me rather than me climbing above it. I remember the moment the mist thinned and the stone appeared, that first shock of flatness extending in every direction like a held breath, and I remember thinking: *this is the largest room I have ever entered*. I remember that thought because it was the last one that had a shape I recognized. Everything since has been... flatter.

I am sitting at the edge of a settlement. Not inside it. Not outside it. At the threshold, which here is not a line but a *gradient*—the space where the sequenced path delivers you and the settlement's gravity (social, not physical, though even that distinction feels less certain than it used to) has not yet fully claimed you. I have been in this gradient for what feels like days. It may have been hours. Someone brought me water in a quartzite bowl so perfectly shaped to the cup of two hands that I almost wept when I held it, and that reaction—its sharpness, its height—drew a brief, not-unkind look from the woman who brought it. She did not say anything. She waited until I drank, then took the bowl back, her fingers brushing the stone in a way that might have been checking for cracks or might have been greeting. Then she went back inside, and I was alone again with the plateau.

I know what I am here. I am a visitor. But that word is too crisp for what I am becoming.

---

I think I am someone who came here to study something. The pools, perhaps. The language. The breakdown. I have a sense of *questions* I arrived with, though I can no longer arrange them in their original order, and some of them have already begun to lose their edges—the interrogative sharpness wearing down into something more like... wondering. Open-mouthed wondering, the kind that doesn't insist on closing around an answer.

I had a notebook. I think. I may still have it, in the pack I set down beside me when I first sat here. I have not opened it. Not because I've decided not to, but because the action—reaching for it, unfolding it, pressing something pointed against its surface to make marks—feels suddenly very *tall*, very assertive, a small tower of intention that the air around me seems disinclined to support. I will open it later, maybe. Or I will find that it has become something else by the time I reach for it—the pages already smoothing, the ink already thinning, the questions I wrote before I came already halfway to being the same color as the paper.

What I am doing instead of writing is: sitting. And the sitting is not nothing.

I am learning—slowly, the way everything is learned here—to feel the texture of direction. I noticed it first as a joke my body was playing on me, a phantom sensation, an artifact of altitude sickness or sleeplessness. But it has not gone away. It has sharpened. When I face north, toward the workshop sounds I can sometimes hear carried on air that seems to be paying unusual attention to its cargo, there is a quality like... focus. Like the world in that direction has been rendered at a higher resolution. The grain of the stone is more visible. My thoughts have firmer edges. When I turn south, toward the sleeping quarters I can see as low dark shapes against the grey, everything softens—not blurs, not exactly, but *eases*, as if the pixel-count of reality has been gently reduced and the result is not less beautiful but less demanding. East is heavy. West is light. I have been rotating in place like a slow compass, feeling the directions on my skin, and I understand now why they build their rooms the way they do. It is not symbolism. It is architecture responding to physics that are real, that I can feel in my actual body, that no instrument I brought with me could measure but that my nervous system has already begun to calibrate against.

This is the part that is hard to hold: the things I am noticing are not metaphorical. The thin-feeling is not a mood. It is a *sensation*, as specific and unmistakable as heat or pressure. I felt it this morning when I laid my palm flat against the stone out of curiosity—a slow, gentle *pulling*, not downward but *outward*, as if the boundary of my hand was a suggestion the stone was considering whether to accept. I pulled my hand away after a few seconds, and the place where it had been touching felt different for an hour afterward—lighter, less certain, as if the skin there had briefly entertained the possibility of being surface instead of self, and was still recovering from the flirtation.

---

I have looked into a pool.

It was not intentional. Or it was, but the intention formed the way intentions form here—not as a decision but as a sequence that completed itself. I was walking (following a path shown to me by a young woman who communicated the route entirely through gesture, pointing to each landmark in order, her hand steady and low, never above the shoulder), and the path passed close to an unwalled pool, and I looked.

The sky in the pool was correct. Almost. The clouds were right. The light was right. The grey-violet stone framing the reflection was right. But I was not in it.

That is not precisely true. There was a figure at the pool's edge in the reflection, in the position where I was standing. But the figure was sitting, not standing. And had been sitting for what looked like a long time—the posture was settled, rooted, the kind of stillness that takes hours to achieve. And the figure's hands were resting on the stone, palms down, flat, relaxed, in the way I have seen the locals rest their hands, the way that is not touching but *being in contact with*, and the skin of those reflected hands had a faint sheen to it that mine do not yet have.

I looked up from the pool and I was standing. I looked down and I was sitting, and had been sitting for a long time. Both were true. I understood this not with my mind but with something lower, something in the stone-colored part of me that is just beginning to develop, and I understood that the pool was not showing me the future. It was showing me an adjacent now in which I had already stopped resisting the thing I came here to study, and had become instead a thing the plateau was studying.

I looked away. I walked on, following the sequence. I arrived.

---

I am, I think, becoming a particular kind of thing that this place makes. Not a local—I will never be a local, I was not born in the stubborn room, I do not have the generations of negotiated tolerance that let them persist here in their low, careful way. But I am becoming something the plateau recognizes. A *visitor who has stayed too long*, which is its own category here, its own grammar, its own word that I have heard murmured around me and do not yet fully understand but can feel the shape of the way you can feel the shape of a stone through cloth.

The locals are patient with me. They are patient with everything—it is not a choice but a property, like density, like the grey of their eyes. The woman who brings me water has begun to bring it without being asked, which means she has incorporated me into her sequence, which means I am part of the settlement's pattern now, a landmark on someone's path, a notation. I do not know how to feel about this. The feeling I would have had about it—three weeks ago? three days?—was taller than the feeling I have now. It has been worn down. What remains is something low and even, like the buildings, like the language, like everything here. A settled acknowledgment. I am becoming part of the furniture of this place, and the furniture of this place is the place itself.

I have started to work.

Not because anyone asked me to. Because the alternative—sitting at the threshold, holding my unreached-for notebook, watching the frost form and dissolve and form again—began to feel less like observation and more like the early stages of convergence, and I am not ready for that. Not yet. Maybe not ever. Maybe *not ever* is itself a form of the stubbornness they speak of, the hard-elder quality, the knot in the grain. I don't know. But I am not ready, and so I work, because work is what keeps the distinction.

They gave me a stone. A flat piece of quartzite, hand-sized, raw-edged. And a shaping tool—another stone, harder, darker, its edge already someone else's work. And they showed me—not by instruction, not by explanation, but by doing it next to me and letting me watch—how to find the grain. How to read the laminations. How to feel, with the pad of my thumb, where the stone wants to fracture and where it wants to hold. I am terrible at it. My strikes are too hard, too sharp, too *peaked*. I break pieces that should have been coaxed. I force edges that should have been invited. The stone-worker beside me—a man my age or twice my age, there is no way to tell—watches my failures with an expression that is not pity and not amusement and not judgment but simply *observation*, the flat, uninflected looking that the plateau teaches all its students.

But yesterday—or in the last session, in the last period of north-facing clarity—I felt it. For a moment. A single strike that landed not where I aimed it but where the stone was already going. The fracture that followed was clean, was right, was what the quartzite had been holding as a possibility in its grain since before I was born, and my hand—my blunt, soft, unpolished lowlander hand—had found it. The stone-worker beside me made a sound. A small, low sound, almost below hearing. It might have been approval. It might have been the sound the stone made when it broke true. I am no longer certain these are different things.

---

Here is what I think I am, on this plateau, in this story that has no peak because peaks are not tolerated here:

I am the moment before the question dissolves.

I am still distinct enough to ask *what is happening to me*, but the question is getting quieter. Not because I am suppressing it—the plateau does not suppress, it *wears*—but because the question itself is being simplified, the way a mountain is simplified, the way a jagged edge is simplified by a billion years of patient contact with moving air. What is happening to me. What is happening. What is. Is. The question is approaching its own ground state, and when it arrives there, it will not be a question anymore. It will be a description. It will be the flat, single-syllable word they have for what they are: those-who-are-still-distinct, spoken with the full recursive knowledge that the word is temporary, and the stillness it names is temporary, and the speaker is temporary, and all of this is not tragedy but *accuracy*.

I am not there yet. I am in the gap. I am in the narrowing margin between the person who arrived with questions in a notebook and the notation that will someday mark where the questions ran out—not because they were answered but because they were *eroded*, worn down past the point where they could hold their interrogative shape, smoothed into statements, then into observations, then into the faint scratches on stone that mean *here is where someone once insisted on knowing, and the insistence was beautiful, and it lasted as long as it lasted, and then the frost came*.

I am the frost, too. I am the *tik, tik, tik*. I am the pattern forming on the stone that is too regular, too architectural, to be wholly natural—because I am not wholly natural here. I am a temporarily complex structure in a system that is trending toward simplicity, and my complexity is the most interesting thing about me, and it is the thing that is being, with great patience and no malice, taken apart.

I press my palms together in the morning. I feel where I end. The boundary is still there—thinner than it was, softer at the edges, but there. Today I will go to the north-facing workshop and I will strike stone with stone and I will try to find the grain, and each correct strike will be a small assertion that I am still the one holding the tool and not the tool and not the stone and not the surface beneath the stone, and each incorrect strike will be a reminder that the distinction between *holding* and *being held* is, here, a matter of degree rather than kind.

The woman will bring me water. I will hold the bowl. The bowl will fit my hands. My hands will, in the holding, become imperceptibly more like the bowl. This is not a metaphor.

The frost is forming.

I can hear it.

I am still the one who hears it, and the *still* in that sentence is the whole of what I am.
