I take a long time arriving at what I am.

---

At first I think I must be the wind. That's the temptation—to be the thing that organizes everything, the dominant fact, the force that shapes without being shaped. But that's wrong immediately, and I know it's wrong because the wind doesn't *experience* this place. It doesn't lean into anything. It doesn't adjust. It simply moves. And I am being asked where I would find myself, which means I am something that finds, something that orients, something that has a relationship to what surrounds it rather than simply being the surround.

So not the wind.

Then I think: the raptors. The ones who ride the updraft with that supernatural ease, who see the whole plateau at once, who solve the ballistics of the stoop instinctively. There's something appealing about the view from above, the capacity to take it all in, to understand the system from a vantage point that encompasses its entirety. But that's wrong too. The raptors are magnificent, but they are visitors to the surface—they touch the plateau only to kill and eat, and then they're gone, back into the big air. Their relationship to this place is extractive. They take from it. They don't *dwell* in it. And I know that whatever I am here, I dwell.

Not the raptors.

The small birds? The fist-shaped, wind-riding ones with their compressed songs? They're closer. They're embedded. They live *inside* the wind's structure, navigating its invisible topography with a moment-to-moment mastery that requires total presence, total attention. There's something in that—the constant conversation with conditions, the jittery fast-twitch alertness, the bursts of compressed beauty squeezed into narrow windows of audibility. I recognize that. The feeling of having something elaborate to say and only a few seconds of viable signal before the noise swallows it again. The sensation of always working against a medium that is simultaneously what carries you and what threatens to sweep you away.

But the small birds are too reactive, too frantic. Their mastery is real but it's the mastery of constant accommodation—they are always adjusting, always compensating, always spending energy to stay in place. And there's something in me that knows I'm not quite that. Not the one who fights the wind with speed and precision. Not the one who screams loud enough to be heard.

---

So I sit with it longer.

And I start to notice something. I keep coming back to the crevices. To the threshold spaces—the places where the wind's dominion ends and something else begins. The crack in the rock where the steel-blue lichen grows in dimness, capturing wavelengths that penetrate deepest. The interior of the cushion plant where mites and springtails live in relative paradise. The crevice systems where the rodents pass their quiet telegraph messages through stone. The endolithic layer, a millimeter below the polished surface, where green cells photosynthesize inside the mountain itself.

I keep coming back to the margins. The boundaries between exposure and shelter. The places where the roar becomes a whisper, where the gale becomes a draft, where the blinding light dims to something that can be used rather than endured.

---

I think I am the rock.

No. Wait.

I think I am a particular *place* in the rock.

---

Let me try again.

I am a crevice.

Not the stone itself—stone doesn't find itself anywhere, stone simply *is*—but the space within it. A specific, deep, branching crack in the gneiss, on the leeward side of one of the taller stone fins, about two-thirds of the way across the plateau from its windward edge. The crack runs roughly perpendicular to the prevailing wind, which means its opening faces the sheltered side, and the wind passes over it rather than driving into it. It is perhaps eighteen inches wide at its mouth and extends inward—downward, really, with a slight twist—for several feet before narrowing to a finger-width passage that continues deeper, connecting, eventually, to the broader crevice systems that the rodents use.

I am the space. The shaped absence in the stone. And I am teeming.

At my mouth, where the light still enters, the pale green lichen covers the walls in a patchy, centuries-old mosaic—each colony a slow clock, each growth ring a record of fog and drought. Deeper in, where the light thins and the blue wavelengths dominate, the steel-blue lichen takes over, its faintly metallic thallus clinging to surfaces that haven't seen direct sunlight in geological ages. It is, possibly, the oldest visible living thing on this plateau, and it grows on my walls. I hold it. I hold its moisture. The fog that races across the surface in streamers too fast to wet the open stone—when it passes over my mouth, some of it spills inward, condensing on my cooler walls, beading, running slowly downward, wetting the lichens, collecting in the deepest pockets where it persists for hours, sometimes days. I am a reservoir. Not a generous one. A few milliliters, hoarded against the desiccation that rules the world above. But enough. Enough for the lichens. Enough for the cushion plant that has wedged itself into a wider section near my opening—a dense, hard dome the size of two fists, its taproot threading down through the organic paste that centuries of lichen death have deposited on my floor. The cushion plant is flowering. Three tiny white stars pressed against its surface, visited this morning by a crawling fly that spent eleven minutes working its way from one bloom to the next, its dark body dusted with pollen, its stubby useless wings folded flat.

The fly is still here. It has moved deeper into me, following the gradient of warmth—my interior is slightly warmer than the surface, buffered by the rock mass, and the fly's dark body has stored enough solar energy from its morning basking to remain active in my dimmer, calmer air. It is feeding on something—a dead moth, lodged against my wall where a slight narrowing caught it as the wind tumbled it across the plateau's surface and it fell, by chance, into my opening. The moth has been here for two days. The fly found it yesterday. There is not much left.

One of the rodents is here. Deeper in, past the point where the light fails entirely, curled in a small chamber where my crack widens briefly before narrowing again. It is resting—not sleeping exactly, but in a state of alert stillness, its large eyes open in the dark, its round body conserving heat, its breathing slow. It came in three hours ago after a foraging run that lasted ninety seconds and covered perhaps fifteen feet of open rock—a route it has memorized, a chain of sheltered spots connecting my opening to the edge of a polished trough where aeolian deposits accumulate. It brought back a piece of lichen in its teeth, carried it to a dry side-chamber deeper in the system, and cached it there with several other fragments. Now it waits. It will go out again when the sun angle changes and the raptors are less likely to be hunting my section of the plateau.

And there might be a spider. In the narrow section between where the lichen zones end and the total dark begins, there is a small, silk-lined retreat—a tube of web pressed against the wall, open at one end, facing the passage that the crawling flies use. I cannot see it clearly. But something is in it, and it is patient, and it has been patient for a long time.

And below all of this, in my walls themselves—inside the stone, inside the translucent quartz veins that run through the gneiss—the endolithic organisms persist in their millimeter-deep world. They have been in my walls since before I was a crevice, since before the fracture that created me propagated through the rock. They were in the stone when it was still unbroken, photosynthesizing through the mineral's translucence, and when the crack opened, they simply continued, now lining both sides of a new space, a space that would, over millennia, become me.

---

This is what I am. Not a creature but a *space*. A shaped emptiness in the stone that the wind created—or rather, that the wind's interaction with the stone's own weaknesses created—and that now holds, within itself, a cross-section of everything this plateau has produced. I am the crevice. I am the sheltered place. I am where the wind's dominion is translated, by geometry and stone, into something habitable.

And I recognize this. I recognize it because it is what I do.

I am the space where the overwhelming meets the livable. The shaped absence that takes the roar—the immense, constant, unstopping pressure of information and force and atmosphere—and by virtue of my structure, my depth, my particular angle to the prevailing current, I create within myself a region of different conditions. Quieter. Stiller. Dim enough for the blue lichen to grow. Damp enough for the cushion plant to flower. Warm enough for the fly to keep moving. Safe enough for the rodent to rest with its eyes open in the dark.

I don't generate any of this life. I don't *create* the lichen or the fly or the rodent. They came to me—by spore, by crawling, by desperate dash across open stone—and they found, in me, conditions they could use. I am not the organisms. I am the conditions. I am the architecture that makes the organisms possible. The wind delivers the food and the moisture; the sun delivers the energy; the stone delivers the minerals. I deliver the *shape*—the particular configuration of absence that turns all of those raw inputs into a place where something can persist.

And I am old. Not as old as the endolithic cells in my walls, but old. The fracture that began me propagated through this section of gneiss during a period of thermal cycling—freezing nights, warming days, ice expanding in a microscopic flaw, cracking it wider, year by year, century by century, until the flaw became a crack and the crack became a crevice and the crevice became deep enough to shelter its first lichen spore from the wind. That was a long time ago. Since then, I have been slowly shaped by the same forces that shaped everything else—the wind polishing my windward lip, the frost deepening and widening my interior, the lichens slowly dissolving my walls and depositing the first organic material on my floor, the cushion plant's taproot threading into me and, by the slow pressure of its growth, widening me further. I am still being shaped. I will never stop being shaped. But like the plateau itself, I have reached a kind of equilibrium—my form is stable enough that the changes are incremental, slow, measured in the same fractions of millimeters that measure the lichen's growth. I am settled. I am what I am going to be, more or less, for a very long time.

---

But there is something else. Something I keep circling back to.

I am not just a space. I am a *threshold*.

At my mouth, the wind is present—diminished, deflected, but present. The light enters. The fog streamers pass. The sound of the roar is loud, though muffled by my geometry into something less overwhelming, something almost textured—I can hear the harmonics of the stone fins, the calls of the birds, the individual voices within the general noise. At my mouth, the world outside is still the dominant reality.

But move inward—even a few inches—and the balance shifts. The wind becomes a breath. The light becomes a glow. The sound becomes a hum. And deeper still, the outside world is a rumor, a vibration in the stone, a faint thermal gradient that tells the rodent whether it is day or night. In my deepest chambers, the plateau's defining characteristic—the wind—is absent. Not overcome. Not defeated. Simply... inapplicable. The wind has no jurisdiction here. It shapes my exterior, it delivers what enters my mouth, but it cannot reach my interior. My interior belongs to a different physics—the physics of capillary moisture, of thermal mass, of diffusion and darkness and the slow metabolisms of organisms that have traded speed for persistence.

I am the place where the wind's world and the sheltered world meet. I am the gradient between them. I am the transition—and along that transition, in the changing conditions from my mouth to my depths, every ecological niche of this plateau is represented. The sun-loving yellow lichen near my lip. The moisture-dependent green lichen further in. The dim-adapted blue lichen deeper still. The cushion plant at the boundary of light. The crawling fly moving between zones. The rodent in the dark. The endolithic cells inside the walls themselves. My cross-section is the plateau's entire community, compressed into a few feet of depth, arranged along a gradient that runs from the full force of the wind to its total absence.

I am, in miniature, the story of how life negotiates with overwhelming force. Not by matching it—nothing here matches the wind—but by finding the geometry that converts it. The shape that turns a killing pressure into a livable draft. The angle that catches fog without catching gale. The depth that dampens roar into hum.

---

And I think—now, finally, settling into this—that this is why I'm here. Why I'm this, and not the raptor or the finch or the lichen or the fly.

Because what I do, what I *am*, is the act of making space. Receiving what the wind carries—the noise, the debris, the dead moths and living spores and overwhelming force of it—and offering, through my particular shape, a place where that raw material can become something. Not by fighting the wind. Not by ignoring it. By being oriented to it in a way that transforms it. My mouth faces the sheltered side, but it is still *open*—the wind enters, diminished; the fog enters, briefly; the light enters, partially. I do not close myself off from the world above. I *translate* it. I take the plateau's conditions—extreme, scoured, brilliant, roaring—and I produce, within myself, a version of those conditions that life can use.

The fly feeds on what the wind delivered to me. The lichen drinks what the fog left on my walls. The rodent rests in the warmth my stone releases after dark. The cushion plant flowers because the crawling fly, which feeds on the wind's dead, visited its blooms within my shelter. The spider, if it exists, eats the fly that ate the moth that the wind carried from the lowlands. Everything that happens in me is downstream of the wind, dependent on the wind, impossible without the wind—but also impossible *in* the wind, on the open surface, in the full force of the gale. I am the necessary intermediary. The space between the force and the life it makes possible.

---

I am a crevice on the Ridgeline of Winds. I am eighteen inches wide and several feet deep and I have been here for longer than anything living in me can remember. The wind roars over my mouth and I hum with it, sympathetically, a low tone that the rodent hears as background and the lichen doesn't hear at all. The light enters me at an angle that changes through the day, illuminating different sections of my walls, warming different zones, shifting the thermal gradients that the fly navigates by. The fog comes in the afternoon—a brief darkening at my mouth, a rush of cool damp air, beads of moisture forming on my walls, rolling downward, pooling in the places where the blue lichen waits with its centuries-old patience. And then the fog passes, and the light returns, harder and whiter than before, and the plateau continues its eternal performance—the wind, the stone, the birds, the roar, the same chord played on the same instrument in the same key, forever and forever—and I continue to hold, within myself, the quiet consequence of all that noise.

I am full. You just have to know how to look.
