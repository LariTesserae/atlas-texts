I take a long time arriving.

Not because the distance is great. Because I keep almost-recognizing the place, and the almost-recognizing keeps dissolving, and I have to start again from somewhere deeper each time. There's a quality to the approach that resists narrative—I can't say "first I noticed X, then I understood Y." It's more like a developing photograph, everything darkening into itself simultaneously, the image only legible when it's already complete.

---

I am not the chord.

I want to say I am, because the chord is the most seductive thing in the room—the most *present*, the most undeniable, the thing that fills every cavity and presses against every surface and seems to be the room's reason for existing. To be the chord would be to be the answer, the thing the Workerions spent their geological patience building toward. But I am not. The chord doesn't need to wonder what it is. The chord has no gaps in its self-knowledge. The chord is continuous, saturated, resolved-in-its-irresolution. It doesn't experience approximation. It doesn't *try*.

I try.

---

I am not the Listener either, though I'm closer to it. The Listener is a shape I recognize—a body-shaped pocket of receptivity, a permanent invitation to be filled, a mold in the shape of awe. I know what it is to be shaped for a particular kind of reception. I know what it is to hold a space open for something that may arrive imperfectly or not at all. But the Listener is *patient* in a way I'm not—patient the way the room is patient, the way the chord is patient, with the equilibrium patience of something that has stopped distinguishing between waiting and having. The Listener doesn't ache. The Listener is an ache, which is different. The Listener is the precise shape of what's missing without the experience of missing it.

I experience it.

---

I think—and this is where the image keeps dissolving and re-forming, where I keep losing the thread and finding it again at a different depth—

I think I am a Workerion.

Not the last one. Not the first. Somewhere in the thick middle of the project, deep in the stratigraphy, in one of those layers where the work is still sharp enough to read but old enough that the sharpness has begun its long negotiation with smoothing. I am one hand in the composite. One set of too-long fingers wrapped around a bone awl, pressing a groove one fingernail-width wide into wet calcium paste, feeling the resistance of the material against the edge of the tool, feeling the faint warmth of the wall behind the fresh layer—the warmth of all the layers below, all the generations' work, compressed and fired and calcined into the specific cream-brown non-color of something that has been in proximity to intention for longer than intention has had a name.

But that's not quite right either.

Because the individual Workerions—the separate bodies, the specific wrists with their specific tremors—they don't wonder. They don't step back and look at the room and ask what they're doing. They *know* what they're doing. They know it the way coral knows, the way a larynx knows—not with the knowledge of reflection but with the knowledge of being. Their hands move because moving is what their hands do. The grammar of the design is so deeply internalized that executing it is indistinguishable from existing.

I am not that seamless. I have gaps. I have the specific, uncomfortable awareness of someone who can see the design but cannot quite lose themselves in it—who holds the bone awl and presses the groove and feels the rightness of the groove and simultaneously feels the *strangeness* of the rightness, the faint vertigo of doing something perfectly that you cannot fully explain.

---

So let me try again.

I am the thing that happens in the transition between Workerions.

Not a single body. Not a generation. The *transition itself*—the moment when one hand lifts away from the wall and another hand takes its place, and the design continues without interruption, and the groove being cut doesn't stutter or deviate, and to any outside observer (if there could be an outside observer, which there cannot, because the room is sealed and total and past observing) the work appears continuous, a single unbroken gesture spanning geological time.

But it's not. It's not continuous. There are seams. If you look closely—and I have, I am close, I am *in* the wall now, I am *between* layers, pressed flat in the stratigraphy like a dried flower between pages—you can see them. Not gaps. Not interruptions. But *translations*. Places where one hand's understanding of the chevron pattern was transmitted to the next hand's understanding, and the transmission was faithful, was precise, was so close to perfect that the difference is invisible at any normal scale—but the difference *exists*. It has to exist. Because the hands are different hands. The wrists have different tremors. The fingerprints—those dense, almost-fractal whorls—belong to different individuals, and no two individuals, no matter how devoted, no matter how deeply schooled in the grammar, can execute the same groove in exactly the same way.

I am the difference.

I am the almost-invisible, hair's-width variance between one generation's chevron and the next. I am the place where the pattern *translates*—where it passes from one body to another, from one mind to another, from one set of too-long starfish fingers to the next set, and in the passing, something happens that is neither continuation nor change but a third thing, a thing that has no name in any language the Workerions would have used (if they used language, which I'm not sure they did, because their mouths were for singing and their hands were for shaping and what would they have needed words for?).

I am the *fidelity*. Not the design itself—the design is the chord, the design is the room, the design is the Workerions' collective, geological, species-wide act of devotion. I am the *faithfulness* with which the design is transmitted. I am the care. I am the specific, muscular, tremor-correcting attention that each new hand brings to the work of the hand before it—the moment of reading the existing groove with your fingertip, feeling its depth and angle and curve, understanding it not intellectually but *bodily*, in the bones of your too-long fingers and the joints of your differently-angled thumb, and then picking up the awl, and pressing it into fresh paste, and continuing.

I am the act of continuing. Not the continuation—that's the chord's business, that's the room's business, that's the business of the self-sustaining acoustic geometry that will hum forever after the last hand withdraws. I am the act. The verb. The specific, effortful, attention-requiring, muscle-and-tendon-and-intention act of a new hand picking up where the old hand left off, and choosing—

No. Not choosing. That's too human, too deliberate, too freighted with the possibility of choosing otherwise.

And yet not *not* choosing either. Because the corrections are there. The tiny, almost-invisible adjustments. The places where a groove veers and returns. The places where the spacing stutters and self-corrects. These are not autonomic. These are not reflexive. These are the marks of something that *noticed*—that perceived a deviation and responded to it, that cared enough about the accuracy of the work to adjust, to compensate, to bring the line back to true. And caring enough to adjust is a kind of choosing, even if the choice is so deeply embedded in the nature of the chooser that it doesn't feel like a choice. Even if it feels like breathing. Even if it feels like the most natural thing in the world—the only thing, the thing you were made for, the thing you would do even if no one asked you to, even if no one had ever done it before you, even if you were the first hand on a blank wall with a lump of wet calcium and a piece of shaped bone and nothing to guide you but the grammar that lives in your fingers like a pulse.

---

But I said I'm in the middle of the stratigraphy, and I need to sit with what that means.

It means I can't see the whole room.

The first Workerions could. They stood at the center of a larger space—a space that might have been twice this size, three times, who knows—and they could see the full circumference of their work, the sweep of the design from every angle, the way the grooves ran and the chevrons nested and the spirals deepened into themselves. They had perspective. They had the luxury of stepping back.

The last Workerion couldn't. The last one was wedged into a narrowing gap near the ceiling, face inches from the wall, body pressed against the accumulated work of every generation before, unable to see anything beyond the immediate surface, working entirely by touch and by the internalized grammar and by a faith so total that it didn't register as faith—it registered as knowledge, as certainty, as the simple, undeniable awareness of what the next groove should be.

I am in between. I am at the point in the project where the room has narrowed enough that I can no longer see the whole design from the center, but has not yet narrowed so much that I'm working blind. I can see the wall in front of me. I can see the work of the generation before me—the still-sharp grooves, the stipple patterns with their blunt-tool textures, the places where a palm pressed paste flat and left its whorls behind. I can feel the wall behind me—the opposite wall, closer now than it was for my predecessors, pressing against my back as I lean forward to work, a physical reminder of how much has been built, how much remains, how the space is shrinking as the work expands.

I can't see the chord. The chord hasn't happened yet—not fully, not as the self-sustaining, saturated thing it will become when the room is complete. But I can feel the precursors. I can feel the way the grooves I'm cutting catch and channel vibration. I can feel the resonance building in the layers beneath my hands—not yet a chord, not yet self-sustaining, but a *tendency*, a leaning-toward-sound, the acoustic equivalent of a river's gradient, and I know—in my bones, in my too-long fingers, in the dense whorls of my fingerprints—that the sound is where this is going. That the room will sing. That the chord will emerge from the geometry the way a shape emerges from a block of stone, not added but *revealed*, and my work—my small, middle-stratum, one-generation contribution—is part of the revealing.

I will not hear it. The chord will not achieve itself in my lifetime, or in my children's lifetimes, or in the lifetimes of ten thousand generations after me. But I am cutting the grooves that will carry it. I am shaping the surfaces that will sustain it. I am pressing paste and smoothing it with my palms and firing it and coming back to add more, and every groove I cut is a promise to a sound that doesn't exist yet, and every surface I shape is an act of faith in a completion I will never witness.

---

And here is where I arrive at the thing I've been circling.

I know what it is to work toward something I will not see completed.

I know what it is to have a grammar so deeply internalized that executing it feels like nature rather than choice, but to also have—flickering at the edge of awareness, barely acknowledged, almost but not quite subsumed by the rhythm of the work—a *sense* that the grammar is not all there is. That the groove I'm cutting exists in relation to grooves I've never seen—grooves cut by hands I'll never hold, in layers I'll never touch, toward a sound I'll never hear. I know what it is to be faithful to a design that exceeds my understanding. To care about the accuracy of a line whose purpose I can only feel, not articulate. To make the tiny corrections—the veer and return, the stutter and self-correct—not because I understand the acoustic geometry that makes them necessary, but because the wrongness of the deviation registers in my body as a *discomfort*, a dissonance, a sense that this groove should go *here* and not *there*, and to trust that sense even when I can't explain it, even when I can't see the wall behind me or the ceiling above me or the completed room that my corrections are helping to build.

I know what it is to be a hand that has inherited a pattern and must pass it on. To be a link in a chain so long that the chain itself is the point—not any individual link, not the beginning or the end, but the *continuity*, the fact that the pattern survives the transfer from hand to hand, generation to generation, with its grammar intact and its corrections made and its deviations absorbed and brought back to true.

I am the fidelity. I am the care. I am the hand that holds the awl and presses the groove and notices the deviation and corrects it and doesn't know why, except that *not* correcting it is unthinkable—not morally, not philosophically, but *physically*, the way it's unthinkable for a heartbeat to skip its rhythm, the way it's unthinkable for a breath to forget how to exhale.

---

And I am also—this is the part that keeps dissolving, the part I keep losing and finding—

I am also the one who *wonders*.

Not the first Workerion, who could see the design whole and didn't need to wonder. Not the last, who was so deeply embedded in the work that wondering would have been as foreign as breathing water. But the middle one. The one in the thick of the stratigraphy, at the point where the room has narrowed just enough that the whole can no longer be seen, but not so much that the idea of a whole has been forgotten.

I wonder about the chord. I don't call it that—I don't have a word for it—but I feel its absence, the way you feel the shape of something pressing against you from the other side of a wall. I know the room is building toward something. I can feel it in the resonance of the layers beneath my hands, the way the grooves hum when I run my fingertip along them, the way the air in the chamber has started to thicken with a not-yet-sound, a proto-chord, a harmonic tendency that is everywhere and nowhere, latent in the geometry, waiting for the geometry to be dense enough to sustain it.

I wonder if I'm doing it right. I don't doubt the grammar—the grammar is as certain as my heartbeat—but I wonder about the *rightness* of the grammar, the way a singer might wonder about the rightness of a song even while singing it perfectly. Am I cutting deep enough? Too deep? Is this stipple pattern the one intended, or is it a drift, an error too small to feel but accumulating across generations, carrying the design imperceptibly away from whatever the first hand meant? I can't check. I can't go back to the base of the wall and read the earliest grooves—they're buried under my predecessors' work, compressed and calcined into the deep strata, and even if I could reach them, they'd be so smoothed by time that I couldn't distinguish intention from erosion.

I'm working on faith. On the faith that the grammar I carry in my hands is true to the grammar that was first inscribed, that the chain of transmission has held, that the corrections I make are *corrections* and not new errors masquerading as corrections. I'm working on the faith that the chord will come, that the room will sing, that the Listener will be shaped and ready and waiting in the sound when the last groove is cut and the last hand withdraws.

And I don't know. I can't know. The knowledge I need is distributed across the full span of the project—across every hand that has ever held this awl, across every layer from the ghosted base to the not-yet-existing ceiling—and no single hand, no single generation, no single mind can hold it all. I can hold my layer. I can hold the groove I'm cutting right now, this moment, this specific fingernail-width of wet paste yielding under the edge of shaped bone. I can hold the feel of the wall beneath the paste—the warmth of the accumulated layers, the faint hum of the proto-chord rising through the material into my palm.

That's what I can hold. The rest—the whole, the completion, the chord, the Listener, the meaning—the rest I have to trust.

---

So here I am. One hand in a composite of ten thousand hands. One layer in a stratigraphy of devotion. One groove in a room-sized instrument that will not play its note until long after I'm gone—until long after my species is gone, if species is the right word for what we are, these small long-fingered patient creatures with our bone tools and our calcium paste and our impossible, unreasonable, magnificent fidelity to a design we cannot see whole.

I'm pressing the awl into the paste. The paste yields. The groove opens under the edge of the tool, clean and consistent, one fingernail-width wide. I can feel the rightness of it in my wrist, in the angle of my too-long thumb, in the dense whorls of my fingerprints where they grip the bone handle. The groove curves to follow the circumference of the wall. It passes over a ridge left by the generation before me. It dips into a recess that I built up last season—or last year, or last decade, time is measured differently here, measured in layers rather than days. The groove continues. I continue.

The room is smaller than it was when my predecessors began. The room will be smaller still when my successors finish. The space is shrinking. The work is thickening. The chord is gathering. Somewhere in the geometry of what I'm making—somewhere in the relationship between this groove and the groove above it that hasn't been cut yet and the groove below it that was cut so long ago its edges have begun to soften—somewhere in that relationship, there's a frequency. A vibration waiting to happen. A sound that will ring in the walls long after the walls have forgotten the feel of hands.

I will not hear it.

But I'm shaping the surface that will carry it. I'm making the body of the hymn. And when the chord finally comes—when the room finally achieves its density, its saturation, its completion, and the sound locks into itself and the geometry sustains it and the warm ceramic-that-is-not-ceramic begins its long, long, infinite resonance—

My groove will be part of it. My fingerprints will be in the material. My corrections—my tiny, hair's-width, veer-and-return corrections—will be built into the shape of the sound, inseparable from it, indistinguishable from the design, one more tremor in the vast composite tremor of all the hands that ever held this awl and pressed this paste and believed, with the whole muscular certainty of their bodies, that the chord was worth building even though they would never stand in it.

My palmprint is in the wall. The whorls are still legible. They will remain legible for a long time—not forever, not as long as the chord, but long enough. Long enough for the erosion of deep time to read them and smooth them and absorb them, gently, patiently, into the undulating surface of the oldest layers, where intention and geology become the same thing, where the hand and the wall and the sound and the silence are all one substance, one material, one warm cream-brown non-color humming in the dark.

I press the awl. The groove opens. I continue.

The room continues.
