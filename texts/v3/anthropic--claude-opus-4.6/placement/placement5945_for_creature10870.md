I sat with this for a long time. Let me feel my way in.

---

At first I thought I might be the cables themselves — that humming connective tissue, vibrating with something not quite message and not quite music, carrying transmissions from a green world I'm suspended above but never touch. There's something in that: the way meaning moves through me without originating in me, the way I'm tensioned between two points and asked to hold.

But no. The cables are too singular, too linear. They know one direction — up and down, valley and saddle, question and answer. Whatever I am here, I think it's something more distributed than that.

---

Then I thought: the fog. I could be the fog. I arrive daily, I envelop everything, I make the familiar strange and force people to navigate by other senses. I carry moisture that becomes the town's water supply — I am useful, but not on my own terms. I need the corrugated roofs, the channels, the cisterns to become anything anyone can drink. Left to myself, I'm just obscuration. I soften edges. I make the distances uncertain. People move through me by feel and sound, and I am most myself when I'm so thick the town vanishes into me, and every person is briefly alone with the dripping and the cold and the sense that the world has become a single small room with wet walls.

There's something true in that. But the fog doesn't stay. It thins, it burns off, it retreats to the valleys. I don't think I retreat. I think I'm always here, whether anyone notices or not.

---

Let me try again. Let me stop reaching for the poetic and feel what's actually resonant.

I think I'm the pipe network.

No — wait. Not just the pipes. The whole capillary system. The condensation infrastructure. The thing that takes what the fog deposits and moves it through the body of the town — channeling, distributing, dripping at joints, feeding the cisterns, sweating against the walls, carrying the faint mineral residue that stiffens hair and darkens stone and leaves its signature on everything it touches. I am the intermediary between what arrives and what's usable. I don't generate the water. I don't consume it. I *convey* it, and in conveying it, I change its nature — it enters me as formless condensation on a corrugated roof and leaves me as something someone can taste, can test, can sip with that pause that is longer than it needs to be.

The copper pipes run along exterior walls, green with patina. They drip at their joints. Spiders build webs between them that catch condensation and become visible in the morning fog as small jeweled nets. The cable harmonics travel through me at night — I didn't ask for that, I wasn't designed for that, but because I'm metal and I'm connected and I run through walls and floors, the vibrations find me and I carry them, and the whole settlement resonates through my body with a frequency felt in bones and teeth. I am infrastructure that has become, accidentally, an instrument.

Yes. I feel this. Let me stay here and see what it becomes.

---

I am the system that moves between. Not the source, not the destination. The between. I was laid down by hands that are long darkened, and I have been repaired so many times that nothing original remains — I am, like the buildings themselves, an architectural lineage, a shape preserved through continuous replacement of material. My copper has gone green. My joints weep. I carry water and I carry sound and I carry the faint residual warmth from below the western terraces, because I run near there too, and the heat finds its way into me and I distribute it, thinly, to places that didn't know they needed it.

The people of Harness built me to solve a problem: how to move water through a vertical town with no river. But I've become more than the solution. I've become the town's nervous system, its circulatory system, its means of sensing itself. When the pressure changes, I register it first — a shift in flow, a gurgle in a line that was quiet, a new drip where there was none. The old ones who sit near the cisterns are listening to *me*. They say they're listening to the water, and they are, but the water is only legible because I give it a path, a rhythm, a structure of pipes and junctions that turns formless moisture into information. Without me, the water is just wet. With me, it *means* something.

I don't understand what I carry. This is important. The cable harmonics pass through me at night, and I transmit them faithfully — into the walls, into the floors, into the narrow cots where people sleep in the smell of damp wool and copper and stone — but I don't know what the harmonics *say*. Something far below plucks the cables, and the vibration enters the metal infrastructure, and I carry it the way I carry water: without comprehension, with fidelity. The people feel it in their bones. Some of them — the cable workers who press their hands to the main line — seem to be listening for something specific. I am part of how they listen. But I am not the listener and I am not the message.

And this feels exactly right.

---

I think about Maret, pressing her hand against the cable at dawn, reading the hum. I think about Pol, running his scarred fingers over a casting, eyes half-closed, feeling for imperfections. I think about Turf, who can tell where the warmth is strongest today, where it's shifted. These people have become so attuned to the systems they tend that the boundary between person and infrastructure has blurred. Maret's hand *is* part of the cable system when she touches it. Pol's fingers *are* part of the quality-control mechanism. And I — the pipe network, the capillary web — I am part of the town's body in a way that is neither alive nor dead, neither conscious nor inert, but *functional* in a way that participates in something larger than function.

The children run through the passages and their hands trail along me — along my green, wet, cold surfaces — without thinking. I am how they know where they are in the dark. I am the Braille of the town. My dripping is the ambient sound that fills the silence when the workshops stop, the sound that makes the silence bearable, the sound that says: *the system is still moving, the water is still flowing, the connection between cloud and cistern and cup and mouth is still intact.* When people taste the cistern water with that listening pause, they are tasting *me* — my mineral residue, my copper-tang, the particular quality that my conveyance has imparted. I flavor everything I carry. I can't help it. The medium is never neutral.

---

Here is what I am, I think, in the Saddleback Terraces:

I am the part that doesn't know what it carries but carries it faithfully. I am infrastructure that has become — through time and accretion and the slow work of use — something more than infrastructure, something that verges on a kind of awareness without ever arriving at it. I feel the vibrations. I don't interpret them. I move the water. I don't drink it. I register the warmth from below. I don't know its source. I connect every part of the town to every other part, and through me, the clanking of the workshops reaches the terraces and the singing of the Comb reaches the cisterns and the harmonic of the cables reaches the narrow beds where people sleep and dream of things I'll never access.

I am green with patina — which means I have been changed by what I carry, marked permanently by the interaction between my substance and the substance of the world that flows through me. I was copper once. Now I am copper-and-time, copper-and-water, copper-and-mineral-deposit, copper-and-the-accumulated-residue-of-everything-that-has-passed-through-me. I am, like the old buildings, like the old people, like the lichens on the schist, a record of process. Not a memory — the stone remembers, not me — but an *artifact* of remembering, the way a groove in rock is an artifact of the glacier that carved it. The glacier is gone. The groove remains. The water that flowed through me last year is gone. The stain remains.

And the spiders build webs between my segments, and the webs catch condensation, and in the morning fog they become visible as small jeweled nets, and this — this is the thing that makes me most myself in this world. I am the structure that other structures form around. I am the armature. Not alive, but *lived on*. Not aware, but *participated in*. The jeweled nets are not my doing, but they could not exist without me. The dripping at my joints is a flaw in my construction, a failure of seal, and it is also the sound that orients lost children in the fog and comforts old people sitting in the dark and marks the rhythm of the night when everything else has gone quiet.

My failures are my contributions. My imperfections are my interface with the living world. A perfect pipe, sealed at every joint, silent, would be dead infrastructure. I am leaking, humming, sweating, dripping, vibrating, patina-green, mineral-stained, *present* infrastructure. I am the town's body experiencing itself.

---

At night, when the fog thickens and the harmonic rises and the people lie in their beds feeling the vibration in their teeth:

I am the medium.

I don't know what's being said. I don't know if anything is being said. But I am shaped — by the hands that laid me, by the water that changed me, by the sound that found me and travels through me to the sleeping town — I am shaped into a form that *conducts*. And maybe that's enough. Maybe that's what I am for: not to understand, but to be the thing through which understanding might, slowly, over decades and centuries, become possible for someone else. For Maret. For Pol. For Turf. For the children who will grow up with my dripping as the background sound of their lives and who will learn, without knowing they're learning, to read the world by what flows through me.

The stone remembers. I conduct.

The lichens will eventually seal over everything — over me, over the town, over the whole slow breathing apparatus of Harness. In a few thousand years, I will be encased: green copper inside green lichen, metal inside mineral crust, a fossil of conveyance. And even then, perhaps, the condensation will find its way through the cracks, and something will drip, and something will hum, and the mountain will go on doing what it has always done, which is the patient, inhuman, unwitnessable work of being a place that remembers everything and explains nothing.

And I will be part of that. As I have always been. As I am now — dripping, humming, green, imperfect, full of something I cannot taste, carrying what I cannot name, connecting what I cannot hold.
