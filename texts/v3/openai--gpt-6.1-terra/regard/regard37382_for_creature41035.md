One morning, an amber-ear found its foot caught in a seam.

It had stepped over the crack on its way down to a hollow. A thin edge of stone had shifted under its weight, closing between the divided pads. When it pulled back, the edge shifted farther. The band continued into the hollow. Two animals stopped and looked back; then one moved on, and the other followed.

The amber-ear stood quietly for some time. It lowered its head to the crack, touched the stone with its nose, and pulled again. This time it made a sound, a short breath forced through its teeth.

The power had been watching the plateau. It knew how the stone had come to lie in the seam, and how much force would move it. It also knew that an animal held in one place would be visible to the hunter when the sun rose higher.

The edge lifted.

The amber-ear pulled its foot free and stood on three legs, looking down at the fourth. Blood darkened the hair between its pads. It put the foot to the ground, lifted it again, and limped toward the hollow.

The band had nearly finished drinking. A young animal approached and smelled the injured foot. The amber-ear moved away from it and drank, then followed the others across the basin, slowly enough that the distance between them widened.

The power stayed near.

By noon the band had reached a shelf where the rock curved over a narrow strip of shade. The injured animal lay down at the far end. A scale-backed insect climbed onto its foot and began to drink from the blood. The amber-ear flicked it away, then lowered its head onto the stone.

There was a ring beyond the shelf. Long ago, something within it had gone out of alignment. It still gathered dew, but the water fell in a place exposed to the first sun, and much of it was lost before the insects came out.

The power entered the ring and examined its workings. Beneath the stone were surfaces finer than the scales on an insect’s back, arranged to draw heat and water through the air. One had turned slightly. The power set it straight.

The next morning, beads gathered over a shaded groove. They fell there one after another, enough to wet the stone and leave a little water standing. Insects came from the seam and drank. Later, when the amber-ear passed, it lowered its head to the groove too.

For several days the band moved slowly, browsing in the channels near the shelf. The injured animal rested often. It could bear weight on the foot now, though on smooth slopes it favored the other three. Once, as it crossed an open basin, a shadow passed over it. The animal dropped awkwardly to the ground and remained there until the shadow moved on.

The hunter was above the rim. It had nested on a ledge where the stone held the evening warmth. One of its young had left the nest; another still waited there, opening its mouth whenever the parent returned.

That evening the power watched the amber-ears enter a channel. The injured animal came last, but it came. The hunter circled farther out, then folded its wings and descended behind a shelf. The sound of the strike did not reach the channel. Afterward the hunter rose, carrying what it had caught toward the rim.

The amber-ear’s foot healed with a ridge of thickened skin between the pads. For a while it avoided the basin where the seam lay. Later it crossed there again, placing its feet carefully on either side of the crack. Seeds caught in the hair around the healed place. At evening, insects picked some of them loose, and the amber-ear lifted its foot to let them work.

The power returned to the ring from time to time. The groove held water on clear mornings, and lichen began to spread along its shaded edge. When a windblown grain lodged in the ring’s mechanism, the power removed it. When the hunter passed overhead, it cast a shadow on the basin, and the grazers lowered themselves as they always had.

Years later, the amber-ear came to the groove with a young one following close behind. It drank first. The young one stepped into the damp, startled at the coldness, then found the water and drank too.

The band was already moving away. The amber-ear raised its head, waited for the young one, and went after them.
