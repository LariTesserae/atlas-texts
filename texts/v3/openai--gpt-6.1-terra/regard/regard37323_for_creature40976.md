The power came close enough to hear the wind in a single stalk.

From far away the plateau had seemed almost uniform. Now it could hear the differences: the cracked flute that whistled on its inward breath, the seed head with one damaged vane, the double knock of a cord catching against the wall of its stem. Beneath these sounds ran the deeper tremor of the ground.

The power listened for a long time. A turning head shed a vane. A rim-eater gave birth in the shelter of a furrow, and the calf stood trembling behind her legs. Far below them both, roots pressed around a seam of mineral, so slowly that the change could only be seen by waiting through many seasons.

The ground was not one being. At a torn edge, the power found the tissues of many plants joined in a piece no larger than a hand. Some had grown together; some had forced their way through their neighbors. One pale root was drawing nourishment from a dead layer, while another, still living, tightened around it.

There was no need to separate them.

A storm was crossing the plateau. It had already stripped the upper growth from a valley and was carrying the fragments eastward. Along its leading edge, the power felt a burrower in an old chamber with two young. The ceiling had cracked, and loose fibers were falling around them. The adult pushed at the blocked entrance with its snout, then turned back to the young and covered them with its body.

The power held the ceiling while the storm passed. Then it eased the broken layers apart, leaving an opening into a sheltered run. The adult did not move at first. At last it raised its head and pushed through, with the young following so closely that their plates scraped its sides.

The power stayed near until they reached another hollow. Above them, a hunter waited beside an older run. It remained there for some time, then moved on.

Over the years, the power found other places where a small opening, a lifted weight, or a few moments of shelter allowed a life to continue. It helped a calf find the low calls of its herd through a stretch of ground whose surface had been torn away. It held a young stalk upright through a gust after its root had caught in a furrow but before it had grown deep enough to hold itself. When fire entered a long-used burrow, the power gave the animals inside time to reach the far entrance.

It did not always know what would follow. The stalk it sheltered grew tall, and its turning head later scattered seeds over a feeding ground. Some took root there; others were eaten. The calf grew and traveled with the herd until the power could no longer distinguish its calls from the others. The burrower raised its young, and in another season the young made chambers of their own.

The storms continued to strip the valleys. Grazers cropped the tender rims of cracks. Hunters waited beside moving ridges, and the plants grew through the old ground and through one another.

The power kept returning. It learned the places where animals sheltered, and how long the ground could bear a strain before it split. It came to recognize certain old stalks by their sounds. One had a seed head that stopped whenever the wind shifted, held against its cords, and then swung around with a rolling crash.

For years the power listened to that crash carry across the plain. One day it found a tear beginning near the base of the head. The plant sealed it with amber, but the next hard wind opened it again.

The power supported the damaged rib while the plant laid down new tissue. When the repair was strong enough to bear the turning, it let go.

The head swung into the wind. Its cords tightened below ground, its chambers closed, and a pale cloud of seeds went out over the plateau.
