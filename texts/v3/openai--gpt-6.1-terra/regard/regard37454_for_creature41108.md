At first the power makes itself small enough to listen.

It settles into an empty socket at the edge of the platform, where a tree was once intended to grow. The socket is lined with resin, and at its bottom is the print of a thumb. A seam-tender comes to investigate. It walks across the socket, brushes the rim twice, and leaves a little gypsum there.

The power does not yet know whether the enclosure is a refuge, a prison, or simply a place made by someone who had nowhere else to begin. It knows that the shell animals are eaten, that the insects are eaten, and that the trees are eaten slowly enough to go on growing. It knows, too, that the wax in the seams will one day be exhausted unless more is brought. Wisdom does not make these things painless to observe.

Toward evening the caretaker comes along a passage behind the platform, carrying a coil of fiber and a shallow dish of gypsum. The caretaker stops beside a tree and unties a frond that has been pulled too tightly against its trunk. The fiber has worn a pale groove in the leaf. After a while, the caretaker gives the frond a looser tie.

The power notices that the caretaker’s hands are dry and cracked, and that there is green dust on both shoulders.

It speaks softly from the socket. It chooses words the caretaker can understand.

“May I come out?”

The caretaker drops the dish. Gypsum spills over the tiles, and three seam-tenders turn toward it from different directions.

For some time the caretaker stands very still. Then, with the fiber still in one hand, the caretaker asks, “What are you?”

“Someone who found this place.”

“Did you make the shells?”

“No.”

The caretaker looks at the platform, where seven shells have appeared since morning. One has a chip along its rim. Down in the seams, a living animal is slowly growing the lost portion back.

At last the caretaker says, “You can come out, but mind the roots.”

The power does. It takes a form with hands and feet, though it has little need of either, and steps onto a clear tile.

They speak over several days. The caretaker is wary of promises and asks practical questions: whether the visitor can mend a cracked socket, whether it knows why the passages lengthen, whether it has brought wax. The power answers as well as it can. It can mend the socket. It has not brought wax, but can make some. As for the passages, it understands part of their turning and has more to learn.

The caretaker asks for no grand change. There are animals nesting in a wall that would have to be moved before the wall could be repaired. There is a tree whose roots have found their way beneath the platform; the caretaker would rather lift the stones around it than cut them. The pale ceiling has developed a dim patch over the northern beds, and several young trees there are bending toward one another.

Together they attend to these things.

The power makes wax in a shallow basin. The caretaker tests it with a seam-tender before working it into the gypsum, and waits several days to see whether the nearby roots take it up. They do. When the basin is empty, the power makes another, and shows the caretaker how to call for more.

The dim patch in the ceiling is harder. When the power brightens it, the trees turn so quickly that a furred creature slips from its branch. The power catches it before it strikes the tiles, but the creature bites its hand and scrambles away into the fronds. After that, they bring the light back a little at a time.

At the wall, the caretaker points out the nest and asks the power to leave the bricks alone until the young are grown.

It does.

One morning the power finds the caretaker at the platform, sorting shells into small groups. Some have matching chips. Some are almost whole. The caretaker has been trying to learn which markings belong to which animals, though there are more shells than can be followed.

“Would you stop them appearing?” the power asks.

The caretaker considers it. “They don’t seem to hurt. And I like finding them.”

So the shells continue to arrive.

The power also makes a door in the outer wall, in a place where the roots will not be disturbed. It makes the door capable of staying where it is: from either side, a hand laid on the latch will find the same latch again. Beyond it is a sheltered space, with air that moves and ground deep enough to dig. The caretaker may open the door or leave it closed. The animals may pass through if they find it.

For a long while the caretaker goes only as far as the threshold. Once, a grazer follows and stands beside the caretaker, working its teeth. It noses the loose earth, tastes a little, and returns to the tiles.

By evening the caretaker has tied back the leaves around the doorway so they will not be caught when it opens. The power helps gather the cut ends of fiber. Then it leaves the caretaker to the familiar work of making a passage wide enough, but not wider than it needs to be.
