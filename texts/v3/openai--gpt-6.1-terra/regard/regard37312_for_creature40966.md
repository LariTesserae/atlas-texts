The power came in the rain, though it was not the rain. It passed over the plateau slowly, attending to what lived there.

At first it attended to the visible things: a shelfback herd moving toward a ridge, a hunter beneath a bough, the birds scattering ahead of the herd and settling again. Then it listened beneath them, where the water pressed through roots and dark nodules, and the faint currents traveled through the ground.

It listened until the rain had stopped. It listened through the clear days that followed, and through another rain. It learned where the herd crossed the basins and where the hunter waited; it found the tree in whose moss a litter of climbers slept. It followed water downhill until the water left the high country altogether.

There was much it could have changed. It could have held the frost away from the old trees, or filled the hunters when they were hungry, or opened the clouds only over the valleys that needed rain. But the frost made cracks for roots; the hunters fed on lives that had fed on other lives; and the clouds traveled farther than the plateau. For a time it chose only to watch.

Then, during a long wet spell, a calf slipped from the herd’s path into a narrow basin. It was old enough to have made the crossing before, but the ground under its forefeet gave way. It fell onto its side and could not turn; each effort pressed it deeper into the turf.

Its mother came back. She pushed at the calf with her muzzle and braced herself to pull it, but there was little room in the basin, and the bank crumbled under her feet. The rest of the herd waited on the ridge. Before long, several adults began to lift their feet sharply from the wet ground. The mother felt it too. She climbed to the ridge, turned, and called down.

The calf answered. The power felt the current gathering beneath it. It could have lifted the animal out, mother and herd and all, without disturbing a leaf. Instead it loosened a flat stone from the bank and set it under the calf’s feet. The stone sank a little, then held. The calf struggled upright and stood trembling while water ran from its coat.

Its mother returned as far as the edge. The calf found a foothold on a mat of roots, then another against the bank, and climbed to her. They joined the herd on the ridge. Much later, when the rain had passed and the ground was quiet, they came down to feed.

The stone remained in the basin. Water gathered behind it for a while, and a few of the metallic-rooted plants drowned there. In the shallower water above, moss took hold; the following season a ground bird nested on it. After some years the water found a way beneath the stone and carried the soil onward.

The power did not make the plateau safe from loss. It stayed through the seasons, learning the country and helping where it could see how to help. In the crown of an old tree, it held a broken branch in place while roots grew across the break. Farther downhill, it opened a path through a frostfall that had dammed a stream. Most often it left the water and the animals to find their own ways.

And sometimes, on a ridge after rain, it stood among the shelfbacks while the blue light flickered at the edges of the pools below. The animals turned their ears at the unfamiliar presence, then went on feeding.
