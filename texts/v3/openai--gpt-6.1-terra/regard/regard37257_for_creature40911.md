The power arrives during rain, though nothing in the basin marks its arrival. The animals are already in the black-glass shelter, quiet beneath the patter that precedes the drops. A young grazer stands with one foot on the back of a hinged creature. Neither moves it away.

The power attends first to the booming below. It follows each sound downward through layers of warm stone, and waits to feel what happens between one boom and the next.

Then it attends to the pane, the ring, and the shelter: to the work each is doing and the long intervals in which each seems to do nothing at all. It finds that the pane can feel a seam before it opens, and that the ring can make a shelf grow where there is room for one. The shelter has been listening to rain for longer than the basin has had animals.

The power does not know, at first, whether the structures can notice it. It lays a question against the pane, as gently as mist.

For a long while the pane remains clear. Then a cloud passes through its center, though no seam is opening, and the thread of light reaches toward the place where the question came from.

The power answers.

They remain together through several rains. The power learns how much of the basin the pane can steady at once, and how far the ring’s song carries into the stone. It learns, too, that the lip is rising unevenly. This is not in itself dangerous. The basin has always changed. But a deep seam is moving toward the underside of the lip, where the flyers cling in the mist. When it reaches them, a broad piece of stone may break away before the pane can settle it.

There is time. The seam moves slowly, and the power could close it. Instead it shows the pane the shape of the stone beneath the flyers’ resting place. The pane clouds from top to bottom. Across the basin, the ring turns a little farther than it usually does.

For some days afterward the ring sounds more often. New shelves form along the inner side of the lip. They are narrow at first, then broad enough for moss to take hold. The flyers begin to use their undersides, although many still return to the old ledge.

The power helps in a smaller way. On an evening when the mist is thick and the flyers have settled, it sends a warm draft up from below. The flyers let go and drop into the air. They circle, humming unevenly, then find the new shelves. Most remain there through the night.

The next morning the old ledge breaks. Stone falls into the vapor with a sound that reaches the basin after the last piece has disappeared. The pane clouds and holds a thread of light to the broken edge until the movement has passed.

Some flyers had returned to the ledge. The power gathers them from the falling stone and sets them on the new shelf, where they cling for a while before taking flight.

The animals go on feeding. The fronds quiver after the fall and gradually lift again. At a pool near the ring, two grazers call to one another in harsh bursts; one finds water, and the other drives it away.

Before leaving, the power lays another question against the pane. This time the pane answers quickly. Its light reaches past the power, toward a seam opening on the basin floor.

The power turns its attention there. The pane steadies one end of the seam; the power steadies the other. When the stone has settled, water beads along the break, and a small hinged creature comes out from beneath a rock to drink.
