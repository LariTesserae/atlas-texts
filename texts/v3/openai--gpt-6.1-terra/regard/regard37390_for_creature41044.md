The power came at night, when the basins held a little water and the roofs were cold.

It could have filled the cistern. It could have softened the wind, or raised a wall around the bowl to keep the cold away. Instead, it listened for a while: to the breathing in the adjoining rooms, to an animal working through an old wall, to water moving so slowly along a gutter that no one inside the houses could have heard it.

By morning it had found two things that needed attention.

One was a crack beneath the cistern’s stone cover. The man who knew the sound of a flawed slab had not heard it; the cover was too large to lift without several people. Water had entered the crack and frozen there. Another hard night might open it.

The other was a cart, still a day’s walk away. One wheel had broken on a track above a steep hollow. The people with it had food and shelter enough for the night, but not enough wood to mend the axle.

The power went first to the cart. It found the broken end in the grass and held it in place while a woman bound it with the last of their rope. She saw nothing holding it, though twice she let go to fetch another length and twice it stayed where she had set it. When the binding was firm, she tested the wheel. The cart moved.

At the cistern, the power waited until the woman fitting the lintel came down with her water jar. When she set it beside the basin, a voice spoke from under the cover.

“There is a crack in this stone,” it said. “I can show you.”

She stood still. Then she looked up at the roofs and across the square. Her father was in the lower lane, scraping the gutter.

“Who is that?” she called.

“Someone who can help,” said the voice. “May I?”

She went and fetched her father. They listened together. The voice spoke again, and her father put down his scraper. Neither knew quite what to say, but after a time the woman asked it to show them the crack.

A thin line of frost appeared on the edge of the cover, where the stone was shaded. It ran inward and stopped. The day was already warming, but the line stayed white long enough for them to find its beginning with their fingers.

They brought the man who knew slabs, and he brought two others. Together they raised the cover onto blocks. The crack went farther than the frost had shown, nearly to the middle. They laid a sound slab over the opening for the time being, though it did not fit as well, and set the damaged one where they could work on it.

The woman who had heard the voice returned to the square that evening.

“Are you still there?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Can you mend it?”

“Yes.”

She considered this. “Will you?”

“If you wish.”

She looked at the slab on its blocks. The man who knew stone had already marked a line across it. He thought there might be two useful pieces left once the cracked part was cut away.

“Not yet,” she said. “We have another cover. And he wants to see what he can do.”

“Then I will leave it to him.”

She took a little water from the basin and watched it settle again. “The cart should have been back yesterday.”

“It is coming,” the power said. “A wheel broke. They have bound it, but they will need help on the last rise.”

She asked if anyone was hurt, and the power told her what it knew. Before dark, three people went out over the rim with tools and a flask of hot broth. The cart reached the town the following afternoon, its wheel still turning, and people came to their doors to hear the story.

For some days the voice answered when it was called. It told them where it had found the cart and what it had done there. It said it had no house and needed none. It could not tell the man with the slab how best to cut it; it had never held a hammer. He showed it his line, and after he had finished, he showed it the two pieces he had saved.

Then one morning the voice was gone. No one had asked it to stay, though the woman wished afterward that she had asked whether it might come back.

The smaller of the two pieces became a lid for a grain jar. The other was set beside the gutter in the lower lane, where her father could sit while he cleared the last of the grit by hand.

That spring, birds nested above one of the inlets. The children found them when they came to scrape it. There was room enough for water to pass beneath the nest, so they cleaned what they could reach and left the rest until the young had flown.
