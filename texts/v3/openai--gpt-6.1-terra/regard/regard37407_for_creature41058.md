The power first noticed the city through its heat.

From a distance the radiators made a small, steady brightness on the shelf. The warmth spread into the stone and remained there through the long night. Animals gathered at its edges. People came home along the rails and rested against walls that had been warm for generations.

The power attended to these things for a long time. It followed the roads to the depots and down the escarpment. It learned how the animals found their way through charge and how the people spoke when the machinery was running. It watched a worker at a leaking vessel lay an inner arm along the joint, then call another worker to confirm what they felt.

Deep beneath the city, one of the older seams was changing. A fault had opened where the seam narrowed, and pale gas was moving into a chamber that had no outlet. In time the pressure would break the chamber roof. The shock would travel upward through the storage beds and reach the southern foundries.

The workers who inspected the seams had no means of detecting the chamber. They knew the rock above it well and had taken soundings there many times. The readings gave them no cause for concern.

The power held the chamber roof in place. It did not close the fault: the pressure would have found another path, and the people needed the gas from that seam. Instead it gave them time to learn what was happening.

It considered how to tell them. A sudden voice in the foundries might be lost among the machinery; a field strong enough for everyone to feel might frighten the animals sheltering nearby. At last it chose a rail in a quiet inspection passage, where two workers were checking the southern storage vessels.

The rail gave three taps.

One of the workers answered, thinking someone was farther down the passage. No reply came. They waited, then went on with the inspection.

The rail tapped again, more slowly. The workers stopped. The older one set an arm against the metal, and the taps formed a pattern under it: a request to speak.

They looked along the passage. It was empty. The younger worker went to fetch the shift supervisor while the older one stayed beside the rail.

By the time the supervisor arrived, the rail had begun tapping a description of the seam below them. The older worker had marked the pattern on a scrap of plate but had not yet answered it.

The supervisor listened, asked for the description to be repeated, and sent for the seam records.

The exchange lasted through the shift and into the next. Others came to listen. Someone brought a portable instrument and laid it against the rail; someone else checked the passage for hidden machinery. The power answered their questions as plainly as it could. It told them where the chamber lay, what was accumulating inside it, and what it had done to keep the roof from breaking.

“Can you leave it held?” they asked.

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as it is needed.”

They asked whether it had entered the other seams, whether it had altered the gas, whether it intended to stay. The power said that it had examined the seams and had altered nothing else. It would stay if they wanted its help. It could also go on holding the chamber without speaking to them again, if that was what they preferred.

They sent a sounding crew down with the next shift. The power showed them a place where the chamber could be reached without crossing the fault. The crew made a narrow bore and lowered instruments through it. When they brought them up, the casings were cold along one side.

There was much discussion after that. People from the mines came up to the city, and workers from the southern foundries carried the news from household to household. Some wanted the seam abandoned. Others thought the chamber could be relieved through a new bore, with the gas drawn into storage slowly enough to keep the surrounding rock intact. The seam supplied several districts, and closing it would mean long work reopening older ones.

The power could see ways to do either. It described them when asked, including what each would cost in heat, material, and time. It also told them where its own understanding might differ from theirs: it knew the stresses in the rock, but the workers knew how their equipment behaved after years of use, and which replacement parts were available.

They chose to make the new bore.

The power held the chamber while they worked. A crew widened the inspection passage, another prepared a pressure vessel, and a third laid a line to a storage bed farther north. Work at the southern foundries slowed while the line was tested. The radiators there cooled a little, and the animals moved in greater numbers toward the neighboring districts. Workers opened a disused lane between two conveyors so the herds could pass without crowding the street.

When the bore was ready, the seam workers gathered around its valve. The power remained in the rail beside them. They opened the valve a fraction. Gas passed into the line, and the chamber roof settled so slightly that only the instruments showed it.

The release continued for many days. People took turns at the valve and at the listening points cut into the rock. At the end, the power eased its hold and let the chamber bear its own weight again. The roof remained sound.

The workers sealed the bore with a fitting that could be opened if pressure gathered there again. They added the chamber to the seam records and marked its position on a plate at the mouth of the inspection passage. Beside the measurements they recorded that something had held the rock while they worked. They left space beneath for anything they might later need to add.

The power stayed near the city. At first it spoke through rails and walls, wherever someone had prepared a place for it. Later, at their request, it learned to make a field gentle enough to be felt at close range. This took care. A field that was clear to a person could carry much farther among the animals, and the animals sometimes gathered around the place where it spoke. The power learned to stop and wait for them to settle.

People asked it many things. Road workers brought strips from the depots and asked about banks forming beyond the horizon. Mine crews asked whether a dangerous ledge could be made safe. A few asked about the lower country, which the power had seen beneath the veil.

It answered, and sometimes it helped directly. It steadied a ledge while a damaged rail was removed. It warned a traveler that a dust column had shifted across the road ahead. When a young person asked what lay below the escarpment, it described the country first, then showed them, with their permission, a brief impression of it: slopes falling into haze, broad channels cut through dark stone, a moving line of animals too distant to distinguish one from another.

The young person was quiet afterward. They asked for the impression again several days later, and this time brought a companion.

Not everyone wished to speak with the power. Some passed its listening rails without touching them. Some were troubled that anything could know so much about the city and chose their words carefully when it was near. It made no attempt to change their minds. When asked how to tell whether it was present, it agreed to give a small signal at each prepared rail. Away from those places, it attended to the city less closely, except when someone called.

In the years that followed, the people made changes of their own. They built another radiator in the northern district and shaped the ground beneath it into low basins, because the visiting animals had worn the old surface unevenly. They improved the guards around the terminals and found a way to collect dust without drawing in the smallest grazers. The power offered advice on the collectors when asked; the workers tried several designs before settling on one they could maintain with the tools they had.

One evening a seam worker returning from the southern passage found the street nearly full of pale-backs. They were pressed close to the foundry wall, their plates still holding the day’s warmth. The worker waited for a gap, feeling the slow shifts of charge pass from body to body.

A small grazer had climbed onto the rail at the edge of the street. It stood there for a moment, testing the air, then stepped down among the larger animals. Farther on, where the passage met the foundry wall, the listening rail gave its quiet signal.

The worker touched it and told the power that the seam had remained steady through the latest sounding. The power thanked them. After a while the herd opened, and the worker crossed the street and went home.
