The power came at night, when the pumps could be heard from the far side of the town.

It stood for a long time at the edge of an evaporation pond. The water was warm even there, and the crust at the margin broke under its feet. Shrimp moved in the shallows. Farther out, where the water was darker, there were more than could be distinguished as separate bodies.

The power knew each one. It knew, too, the birds sleeping above them, the larvae in the channels, the people lying awake in the heat, and the people asleep beside open windows. By morning a pump would fail, and someone would be sent to repair it with a seal that had already begun to perish in storage.

Before dawn, the power loosened the corroded bolt that would otherwise delay the repair. It drew the worker’s attention to the seal as she opened the cabinet, and she saw the crack in it. She found another, farther back on the shelf.

That was the first thing it did.

Later, it followed the canal upstream. At a bend beyond the flats, the water passed through a district where it was cleaner and cooler, though not abundant. The power considered taking some of it. There were farms there, and towns, and living things in the canal itself. Instead, it went to the old records kept in a room beside the pumping station, and opened a drawer that had stuck for years. The workers found, among the curled plans, a survey of fresh water beneath the eastern flats. The supply was small but renewable; an earlier company had judged it insufficient for the works and left it alone.

The people discussed it for several weeks. They had learned to be cautious about anything described as an easy source of water. Two of them went out with a drill and brought back samples. After more discussion they sank a well, with a meter and a lock that could be opened by anyone in the town. The water was kept for drinking and washing. No pipe was laid from it to the works.

The power helped with this, though it did not tell them what to decide. It made the old drill start after an hour of work rather than a day. When the samples were tested, it let nothing interfere with the result.

It could have swept the salt from the streets in a single night. It could have filled the basin with rain. But the salt would have gone somewhere, and a sudden flood would have washed larvae from the channels and birds from their nests. Even the smallest ponds had their accustomed inhabitants. The power went among them before making changes, learning where their eggs lay dormant in the mud and which waters their young could bear.

It began with the roofs. On the inhabited buildings, cracks closed and gutters straightened. Tanks that had leaked held water again. The power made itself visible then, because the people had noticed and were frightened.

It met them in the yard of the pumping station, in a patch of shade beside the wall. It did not ask to be worshipped. It told them what it had done and what it hoped to do, and listened while they argued about whether to believe it. One worker asked if it could leave the machinery alone. Another asked why, if it had such power, anyone had to work at all.

The power said that some work would no longer be needed. Other work might be needed for a time, and the people should have a say in what became of the place. As for the machinery, it promised not to alter it without telling them. They did not all trust the promise. One person left the yard before the conversation was over.

Over the following months the works changed. The power provided a way to separate the salts with less heat and less water, and showed the workers how to maintain it. It could have kept the new equipment running by itself, but they wanted to understand the gauges and be able to shut it down. Together they marked out a sequence for taking the oldest ponds out of use.

They did not drain them all. The workers set aside water and ground for the birds and the small animals, connecting some ponds and leaving others at different salinities. At first they made the new channels too steep; after rain, beetles and shrimp were stranded on the concrete. The power showed them, and they rebuilt the edges with gentler slopes and sheltered places among the stones. They watched through the seasons to see what returned.

Not every creature could be saved. Birds still fed in the shallows. A dry wind could still leave a line of stranded animals when the water fell. Where it could, the power returned them to water; where it could not, it shortened their suffering. It did the same for a bird with a broken wing that no one could mend, staying beside it in the reeds until it died.

In the town, salt was carried out of the lower streets slowly, in covered loads, and placed where it would not wash into another community’s water. Soil was brought in for a few small gardens near the homes. The first trees were planted in deep beds lined against the saline ground. The children watered them in the morning before school; when one failed in the heat, the people planted another and gave it better shade.

Years passed. Some families chose to leave, and the power helped them go with money, transport, and places to live where they were welcomed. Others stayed. The town was still hot, and there were still wet patches in the streets after rain, but the houses were cooler and the pumps no longer governed every hour of the day.

One evening the power stood again by the pond where it had arrived. A bird walked along the margin, stopping now and then to probe the water. Behind it, flies rose and settled. Across the flats a worker closed a valve, checked it twice, and went home.

The power remained after dark. It could hear the water moving through the new channels, and from the town came the sound of people talking outdoors beneath the young trees.
