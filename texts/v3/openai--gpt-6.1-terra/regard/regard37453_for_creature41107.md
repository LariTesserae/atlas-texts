The power listened before it did anything.

It listened through the limestone and the iron legs, through the water and the bodies moving in it. The pump’s pounding reached every pool, though not in quite the same way. Under one mineral plate, crabs felt a dull, steady tremor. On an exposed walkway leg, the vibration was sharp enough to shake the silt from a snail’s shell.

It listened aboveground too. The pump kept water out of cellars and a road tunnel, and carried it away from fields where people grew food. Simply stopping it would have given the chamber a different trouble, as well as giving trouble to the people above.

For a time, the power did nothing that could be seen. It followed the water from the surface, through cracks and drains and buried pipes, and out again toward the sea. It learned where the warm vents opened, and where fish had laid their eggs beneath the walkway. It watched a worker come down with a wrench and stand in the roar, reading the gauges before he touched anything.

Then, one morning, he found a note in the pump house. It was written in his own hand, though he did not remember writing it:

*The mounting bolts. Look underneath.*

Two bolts had worked loose. The rubber beneath the motor had hardened and split. He made a call, and the next week a crew brought new mounts down the narrow stair.

They worked on a dry platform, with the motor shut down and a temporary pump running aboveground. Even so, the noise and movement sent fish into the deeper pools. A dropped nut landed in the water, and one of the workers climbed down carefully to retrieve it. By the time the permanent pump was running again, the silt had settled over his bootprints.

The new mounts softened its pounding. The pipes still roared, and leaks still struck the flooded floor by the thousands, but there were places beyond the pump house where the water carried smaller sounds: the click of a crab against stone, the scrape of a snail, a fish brushing a curtain of worms.

The power stayed. When the temporary pump was removed, it made certain the flow continued through the old pipes, and that the water reaching the chamber still brought its burden of fine organic matter. It did not seal every leak. The ferns needed the drops that gathered on their fronds, and the moss needed the damp cables, though the cables themselves would soon need attention.

At the next inspection, the worker heard an unfamiliar rattle at a joint. It had probably been there for years, hidden under the motor’s pounding. He marked the place with a strip of cloth and wrote it down in his book.

Below him, shrimp gathered around the cloth where its end dipped in the water. A blind fish came nosing along the pipe and took one. The others scattered, then returned to feed.
