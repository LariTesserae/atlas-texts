The power first noticed the town because of a light that remained after its source was gone.

It followed the glimmers through the wet seams, expecting perhaps a single creature. It found the gray-green film, the lives within it, and the stone on which they depended. It found fine roots and sleeping insects, and below them older cracks, some still widening by a fraction each winter. It found water moving so slowly through the mountain that a human lifetime was scarcely long enough to see its course.

The power did not know whether the seam-light was aware. It could have learned, perhaps, but learning everything about a living thing without its leave seemed a poor way to begin.

It watched instead.

On a dry morning, a girl gathering pans came to one that should have held water. The stone beneath it was damp, but the pan was empty. She lifted it and found a hairline split running from its rim to its base. She tipped the few drops left in it onto a nearby patch of seam-light, then tucked the pan under her arm to take home.

At the works, a mechanic spent an hour listening to a pump. He touched the housing, frowned, and called another mechanic over. Together they shut it down and took the casing apart. A tooth on one of the gears had worn thin. There was a replacement, but fitting it meant stopping the water for several streets.

The cistern keeper went ahead of them, knocking on doors.

By evening the pump was running again. A household at the far end of the line had been missed, though, and an old woman who lived there had no water set aside. Her neighbor brought her a jug. She accepted it with embarrassment, and the neighbor said, truthfully, that he had more than he needed.

The power considered filling every cistern. It could have done so without disturbing a stone. But the town’s pipes and gutters had limits; an overflowing tank on one level could send water into a room on another. And the water would be used, and the dry weeks would return.

So the power went up to the ridge, where the wind station stood. One of its vanes had developed a small crack near the hub. The crack was hard to see unless the vane was stopped in a certain position, and it had not yet made a sound anyone could hear.

The power widened it just enough.

Three days later, during an inspection, a worker found it. The station was stopped while the vane was repaired. With the machinery still, the workers heard water trickling somewhere beneath the ridge. They followed the sound to an old maintenance shaft, then down a stair half filled with grit.

At the bottom was a shallow basin lined in green stone. Water entered it through a narrow seam and escaped through another, falling away into the mountain. There were marks on the walls where a larger pipe had once been fixed, and a rusted bracket beside them. Someone long ago had known about the basin.

The workers brought the cistern keeper to see it. She measured the flow over several days. It was small, and it varied, but in a dry week it would matter. After that came discussions about pipes, labor, and whose streets should receive the water. The discussions were lengthy. The people farthest from the works argued that they should have the first claim; the people nearest the ridge pointed out that the pipe would cross their gardens. Both were right about some things.

The seam-light grew around the basin too, in thin dull streaks that brightened when a lamp was brought near. One worker suggested scrubbing it away before fitting the pipe. Another asked if they could leave it. It was not blocking anything.

They left most of it, clearing only the stone needed for the brackets.

The power stayed through the work. Once, when a dropped tool was about to strike a nesting bird beneath a bridge, it turned the wind. The tool landed harmlessly on a roof, where its owner had to climb out and retrieve it. On another day it held a loosened stone in place until two children had passed, then let it fall where the sound would bring someone to mend the wall.

It did not correct every mistake. It did not make the mountain stop wearing down. The grit in the drains fed the works, and the worn stone made places for roots and water. The people had already learned much about living with it, and there was more they would learn.

When the pipe was finished, the girl with the split pan was sent with a jug to the new tap. She let the water run over her hand first; it was colder than the water in the street cistern. Then she filled the jug carefully and carried it home.

That evening, lamps came on across the shelf. Beneath them, seam-light appeared along the old stairs, a little at a time. The power followed it down to a crack beside a threshold, where a child had set a saucer of water for the birds. The saucer leaked slowly. A narrow dark line led from its base to the crack.

The power considered mending it, then saw that the child had wedged a chip of slate beneath the damaged edge, so the leak ran away from the doorway. Someone had already noticed.

It left the saucer as it was.
