The power came quietly, though quiet was difficult to distinguish there.

For a long time it made no change. It watched water gather into columns and fall apart. It watched a large grazer cross a basin, and the smaller animals follow the edge of the wound it left in the crust. It watched a ring tighten around its pool, pebble by pebble, as the tubes beneath the stones filled and slackened.

In that ring lived an animal whose oldest branches had grown together. It had no single body that could have been lifted out. Some of its tubes passed beneath the stones into the surrounding mud; others lay in the sheltered water inside. It filtered what came to it. It grew where there was shade, and when a gap opened in the ring, it grew toward the gap.

The animal was not keeping a memory of the ring as it had first been. It had no need to. The shape was present in its body: in the tension of one branch against another, in the cool places beneath the stones, in the water that slowed as it passed through. The animal maintained it without ever seeing the whole.

A storm approached from two directions. Its thunder had already crossed the basin once.

The little grazers were withdrawing. A large grazer had fastened itself to the stone, and along its back the openings closed one after another. The ring animal’s tubes swelled in the rising water, drawing the loose stones against their neighbors.

One stone did not move.

It was heavier than the others, a dark slab that had settled across two branches of the animal. In ordinary weather the branches could lift it a little, then let it down. But water was coming up through the seam beneath it, and the slab was beginning to turn. When the water struck from the side, it would roll inward. The ring animal would lose those branches, and the breach would carry away most of the stones around them.

The power knew this. It also saw the soft-bodied animals living beneath the slab, the eggs fastened to its underside, and the pale growth that had spread through the seam. To lift the stone clear and set it elsewhere would save one life at the cost of many smaller ones. To hold it still through the storm would force the water around it, into another part of the ring.

The first rain arrived sideways. In the basin, an extra set of ripples spread from the stone.

The power entered the seam as warmth, no greater than the warmth already rising from below. It loosened a narrow tongue of rock on the far side of the slab. The tongue bent under the pressure of the water and came to rest against the slab’s lower edge.

Then the storm struck.

The slab shifted. Eggs tore free underneath it and were carried into the seam. One branch of the ring animal flattened and split. But the slab caught against the tongue of rock before it could roll, and the current passed over it and through the ring.

For a while the basin was white with spray. Loose stones traveled across its floor. The gray blades behind the wall vanished under the water; when it fell, some were still rooted there.

The power remained until the ring was visible again. The broken branch hung in the current. Small grazers emerged and crossed it, finding particles caught in its torn surface. Beneath the slab, a few of the eggs still held.

The animal began to grow around the obstruction. It sent a new tube along the shaded face of the stone, and in time that tube met another coming from the opposite side. The tongue of rock, softened by the warm flow, dissolved slowly into the basin. The ring was a different shape afterward, wider on one side.

The power could have stayed to guard it. There would always be another storm, another stone, another moment when a life might be spared. It watched the young tube thicken and lift a pebble no farther than the width of a grain. The pebble settled closer to the ring.

The power withdrew its warmth. Water continued to rise through the seam, carrying minerals from below. Along the damaged branch, a dark film had begun to grow.
