The power came upon the plateau in the colder part of the year. It found the herds settled in the lee of the ridges, their coats already silvered with frost. Beneath them, the seams were beginning to close.

It remained long enough to learn the place. It followed the water down through the gravel and watched it freeze from the edges inward. It attended to the dark colonies as they withdrew, to the tiny animals moving ahead of the ice, and to the warmth that persisted in the deepest cracks. It watched predators take their food and watched the scavengers gather. It saw how little of a carcass was lost.

That year the cold arrived unevenly. Rain fell after the herds had settled, then froze before it could pass through the gravel. A clear crust formed over a wide basin, sealing the mouths of the cracks. For some time the colonies below continued to live on the water already there. The small animals found their way to deeper seams, though not all of them could pass through.

One of the old grazers lay near the center of the basin. It had settled late, and its coat had frozen to the ground. When the weather softened a little, it tried to rise. The other animals nearby had begun moving, but it could lift only the front of its body. The effort pulled at its skin. After a while it lowered itself again.

The power warmed the stone beneath it, slowly, from a depth where the change would be difficult to distinguish from the work of the colonies. A narrow space opened between the animal and the ice. Water gathered there, and the many-legged creatures in its coat came down to drink.

The grazer rose on its next attempt. It stood for a long time before following the others. Some hair remained on the ground, and in the damp beneath it a dark film began to spread.

Across the basin, the power opened other passages through the ice. They were small and widely spaced. Air reached the seams, and when rain came again, some of it ran down to the living threads. The power left much of the crust intact. It had seen that the ice held moisture in the gravel through the long cold, and that the colonies knew how to endure beneath it.

Later, as the herds crossed a narrow passage, a predator took a young grazer. The power was present there too. It did not turn the predator aside. When the attack was over, it stayed through the days that followed: while the predator fed and rested, while the first scavengers arrived, and while the mother rejoined the herd.

The power did not make itself known to the animals. There was no need for them to alter their paths or gather around it. It traveled with them for a time, then returned to the basin and followed the water downward once more.

By the season of longer light, the crust had broken apart. Beneath one of its remaining sheets, a small animal moved through a wet seam, feeding on the dark threads along the wall. It reached an opening where the ice had lifted from the stone and continued upward into the gravel. Above it, a grazer stood feeding, its mouth working slowly around the edge of a lichen.
