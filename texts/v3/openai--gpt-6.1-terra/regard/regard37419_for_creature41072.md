The power came at dusk, when the first bats were easing out of the cracks and the birds were still returning to the shelf. It did not come as a light in the sky. No shadow crossed the basin, and the animals did not fall silent.

It listened for a long time.

The sound was not one thing, though from the rim it seemed so. Near a thorn cushion, crickets called beneath the answering rasp of cicadas. On the shelf a young bird cried until a parent settled over it. A bat, momentarily caught at the mouth of a crowded fissure, chattered and pulled free. Below these sounds were others: the faint scuff of a mammal at its burrow, the shifting of grit inside an old wall.

The power could hear farther in. It could hear a worker moving through a gallery with a splinter of leaf held in its jaws. Ahead of it, the passage narrowed where an old ceiling had settled. The termite turned, found another way, and went on.

The power followed the passage downward, through chambers that had been enlarged and divided, through walls made from the remains of older walls. Eggs lay in the warm dark. Workers moved among them, touching them and carrying them where they needed to be. In a chamber deeper still was the queen, attended by workers who had never left her for the open air. She was old even by the measure of the mound, though not by the measure of the basin. The city had existed before her, and, if its work continued, it would exist after her.

The power gave her its attention. It did not ask her to speak. She had workers close around her, and the chamber held the warmth and moisture on which her life depended. There were creatures tending her, and others bearing her young away to the places prepared for them. The power stayed until it had understood what it could of this.

In the upper reaches of the mound, conditions were changing. The season had been dry for longer than the basin’s usual dry seasons. The leaves falling from the trees were smaller; some of the shallow roots had died back. Workers traveled farther along the covered runways for food, and a number did not return. A fissure had opened in the side of one tower, and though it had been sealed, the soil around the repair was friable. The mound still held its warmth, but the deeper chambers were losing moisture.

The power rose through the earth again and considered the basin.

It could have filled it with water. It could have made the trees grow over every bare stretch, or enclosed the city in walls that claws could not break. It could have kept the lizards from the openings, and the bats from the moths, and the long-eared mammals from ever being caught in the open.

Instead, it went out beyond the rim, where thin clouds were passing over the desert. It gathered them slowly, adding moisture to them through the night. Toward morning, as the bats came back and the first birds left the shelf, a light rain began to fall.

At first the drops vanished on the hard ground. Then the shallow channels darkened. Water found its way beneath the trees, soaking through the fallen leaves and reaching the roots. A little gathered in hollows in the rock, where birds came down to drink after the rain had passed.

The shower did not last long. The basin had been dry enough that the water disappeared into it without standing on the flats. Beneath the earth, dampness spread gradually toward the old chambers. Workers found grains they could use and carried them to the fissure in the tower. By the following night the weak place had been thickened from within.

For several days the air near the ground held the smell of wet clay. New shoots appeared among the scrub. A moth laid eggs on leaves that had unfolded since the rain. When the eggs hatched, the leaves fed the caterpillars, and later a bird found some of them. Beneath the mound, the queen continued to lay, and workers carried the eggs away.

The power remained in the basin for a while. It watched a small mammal emerge early from its burrow, drink from a hollow beneath a stone, and retreat when a shadow passed overhead. It watched the termites close a fresh break made by a lizard’s claws. It saw the lizard catch one of them before the opening was sealed.

When it left, the evening chorus was rising again. The rain had brought out insects, and bats turned above the thickets in pursuit of them. On the northern shelf, the birds settled with their young. The power carried the sound with it beyond the rim, until distance separated the calls once more and, at last, they could no longer be heard.
