One night, when cloud had climbed over the southern edge and was moving among the ridges, something attended to the plateau.

It began with the water. Deep below the stations, vapour moved through fractures in the rock, rising where pressure found a way. Some reached the vents and fed the reddish mats. Some was drawn into the boreholes. The power followed both courses, and the older, slower courses below them, where water lay in stone that had not seen daylight for a very long time.

At the easternmost station it found a valve worn thin by grit. The crew had noted it on their last visit. They had meant to replace it, but a part was missing from their supplies, and they had left it running at reduced pressure. By morning a split would open in the valve housing. Hot vapour would scour the ground behind the building, where several lizards had made a shelter under a fallen panel.

The power cooled the metal around the split as it formed. It did not mend the valve. When the engine started at dawn, the pressure gauge shook hard against its stop, then settled near zero. The automatic cut-off closed the borehole, and a red light came on in the little window beside the door.

The lizards stayed under the panel. Later, when the sun reached them, they moved into the shade of the pipe.

Three days passed before the maintenance crew arrived. They found the split and the fused metal around it. One of them crouched to examine the housing and called the others over. They took photographs before replacing the valve. At the next station they checked the same fitting and found it nearly as worn. After that, replacement valves were added to every vehicle’s supplies.

Across the basin the power made a few other changes. Where a condenser discharged hot water onto bare rock, it opened a shallow seam that carried the water farther from the building. There it cooled beneath an overhang. The mineral rim widened, and in time a thin red mat took hold. Beetles found it first; then lizards came to drink.

At another station it left the ground alone. The water there already ran into a fissure beneath the foundations, and a colony of pale, blind creatures lived along its damp walls. The workers had never seen them. The power followed the fissure down and found that the colony extended farther than the warm water reached.

When the long dry period came, as it had come before, the shrubs lost their new leaves and the herds travelled farther between gullies. The power could have brought a storm over the plateau. It watched the clouds build against the mountains, then let them pass in their own time.

Instead, on several nights, it drew a little more moisture from the air onto the shaded stone. The beads joined and ran along the familiar routes. This was enough for some of the smaller animals, and for one calf whose mother found a damp crack in a gully she had not visited since she was young. Other animals died. Their bodies fed the beetles and the birds, and what remained settled into the cracks among old leaves and grit.

At the end of the dry period, rain came without assistance. It struck the hot rock in a hard burst and rose again as steam. Water ran briefly over the tiles, carrying dust and seeds into the hollows. The power attended to the rain as it attended to everything else, following it down into the ground, but did not choose where each drop should go.

In the years that followed, workers sometimes found strange readings in the station logs: a sudden cooling of a pipe under strain, a pressure drop just before a seal failed. They argued about the instruments and replaced several of them. Some of the readings continued. The crews learned to treat them as a reason to look closely before restarting an engine.

One evening a worker stopped beside an iron post to drink from her flask. A lizard waited at its base, its body pressed flat against the stone. The post was still dry, though cloud was gathering below the escarpment. She watched the lizard for a moment, then poured a little water into a shallow cup in the rock and stepped back.

The lizard approached after she had gone. By nightfall, cloud had risen among the ridges, and droplets were gathering on the iron above it.
