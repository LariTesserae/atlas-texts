For a long time the power did nothing that the surveyors could distinguish from weather.

It attended to the bowls. It watched a mouse carry three seeds into a cable trough and drop one between the stones. It watched the crews lay a foundation, and the ground rise over it, and the crews return with jacks and patient faces. It watched the ground receive a stake driven deep into it, and after several hours bring the stake carefully back to the surface.

The power could have held the plain still. It could have given the city a century’s supply of firm, dry land. It waited instead, because it did not yet know what holding the plain still would mean to whatever lived there.

One evening it took the shape of a woman carrying an empty cistern on a handcart. At the advancing edge she found two surveyors packing their instruments.

“Where should I put this?” she asked.

They looked at the cistern. It was a good one, with a fitted lid and a tap near the base.

“Are you with the railway crew?” one asked.

“No.”

They consulted each other and pointed to a place beyond their last markers, where the ground had remained firm for two days. The woman wheeled the cistern there. She filled it with water from a skin, set out a clean bowl beside it, and left the tap closed.

In the morning the bowl was empty. The cistern was full. A marker from twenty paces away lay beside the bowl, its number turned upward.

The woman returned and filled the bowl again. This time she laid a second bowl beside it, empty, and a row of small stones between them.

The next morning there was water in both bowls. The stones had been pressed into the ground.

She brought more stones. Over the following days, the surveyors joined her. They tried different arrangements, recorded what changed, and argued on their walk home. The ground sometimes answered by moving things; sometimes it did nothing for days. A pattern that had seemed clear on Tuesday failed on Thursday. Once, after a hard frost, all the bowls were found tipped over, and the surveyors spent a week trying to discover whether this meant anything at all.

The power did not tell them. It was learning too.

Farther in, the city continued its work. A cable failed during a workshop move, and a street lost heat for half a night. Mice nested in the insulation of the replacement. A child’s chalk marks spread a finger’s width apart by the time she came home from school. The power went with an electrician to move the mice and held the lamp while he repaired the junction.

By late winter, the surveyors had learned to ask one useful question.

They would place a bowl at each end of a proposed rail section, with a line of chalk between them. If both bowls were found upright in the morning, the ground beneath the line usually remained firm through the next day. If one had been moved, the crews laid shorter sections or chose another route. An untouched arrangement was less certain: sometimes it meant the ground was firm, and sometimes it seemed to mean that nothing had been decided.

There were still mistakes. A rail sank, slowly enough that everyone got clear, and took three days to lift. Afterward the crews left a wider strip unbuilt beside the route, and the bowls there remained upright for many mornings.

The cistern stayed at the edge. Others were brought when the work moved east. Each had a lid, and each was filled from the city’s collected rain; the power saw to it that the oldest gutters were mended and that the condensation beneath the great bridges no longer fell uselessly onto stone. It did this in the ordinary way, carrying a tool bag and asking where help was wanted.

In spring, one of the surveyors noticed a line of stakes set out beyond the planned railway. They were old stakes, their numbers worn, gathered from places where they had been lost during the winter. Each stood upright in the gray ground. Between them the surface had formed a shallow, winding ridge.

The crews marked it on their maps. They brought bowls to several points along it and watched for a while before building anywhere nearby. The ridge continued to lengthen, sometimes by only a hand’s breadth in a week.

The power came to see it in the evening. From there it could look back over the roofs and glasshouses, where work lamps were coming on. A workshop was moving between two blocks, its cables lifted high by a person with a pole. Birds walked ahead of its wheels.

Near the first stake, the power put down a bowl and filled it. It sat beside the bowl until the light faded, leaving room around it for whatever might come.
