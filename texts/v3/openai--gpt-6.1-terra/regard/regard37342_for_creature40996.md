The power came first to the roof.

A long crack had begun to open above a shelter where three people were sleeping. Fine grit fell on the stones across the entrance. One of the sleepers woke and put a hand on the shoulder of another; before either could get outside, a slab the length of a broad animal broke loose.

It stopped a handspan above the shelter.

The people came out on their knees. They saw the stone hanging there, dust sifting from its edges, and pulled the third sleeper through the entrance between them. Only when they had reached open ground did the slab descend. It settled slowly enough that the runners escaped from beneath it.

Across the chamber, other stones stopped falling. Some were held until the creatures below had moved away. Some were set down in places where their weight would brace the roof. The thin lid of the chamber grew thick again around the skylights, though the openings remained.

Deep below, the pressure that drove the ridges upward did not cease at once. The power followed it through the soft layers, finding where water was trapped, where stone had folded against stone. It made room for the pressure to pass into the surrounding ground. For many days the floor still shifted, but more slowly. A person could feel a ridge beginning to rise and have time to move a shelter. Animals could leave the troughs before the sides closed in.

The power stayed with the work. It knew that a roof made sound in one place could put a new strain on another, and that a pool left behind a ridge might drain away when the ridge settled. It watched the whole chamber as it changed.

At the pool where the runners pressed shoulder to shoulder, the power opened channels through the rock. Clear water began to flow from several seams, and the channels led it to many shallow basins. There was room at some basins for the broad animals, with gently sloping banks that would bear their weight. Others had narrow entrances the broad animals could not pass. Runners drank there without being pushed underfoot. Higher up, water gathered in cups of stone where the fliers could settle.

When the people first found the new water, they tested it with poles and waited at a distance. Nothing rushed toward them. The animals had already dispersed among the basins.

One person filled a bowl and carried it back to the others. They tasted it, set it down, and listened to the quiet around them. They had grown used to hearing a crowd assemble whenever water appeared. The quiet made them uneasy, but they filled their skins.

The power attended, too, to the hunger that moved through all those bodies. The films on the damp rock could not feed everyone, and there were animals whose mouths and instincts were made to take hold of other animals. The power did not kill the hunters, or take away their movement, or leave them to starve.

It brought warmth and nourishment into the stone from below. In the damp places, the living films spread and thickened. New growths appeared: soft shelves on the walls, pale clusters in the troughs, and long, fine strands that drifted in the pools. As these multiplied, the power changed what the animals could draw from them. It worked gradually, through bodies already living and through the bodies of their young. A runner could find what it needed in a mass of fallen growth. A flier could feed on the drifting strands as readily as it had once taken prey from the floor.

For a long while the old habits remained. Hunters gathered at a wound; runners pressed around a fallen body. When the power could reach them, it eased the injured and drew the hungry toward food nearby. Less often, then, did an animal vanish under the crowd. The broad animals rested without hunters gathered in the folds of their legs, and those with old wounds grew smooth skin over them.

There were still crowds. The runners liked to travel close together, and the fliers still formed turning columns beneath the skylights. But the animals no longer multiplied far beyond the food and space available to them. They lived longer, and fewer young were born into the chamber. Those that did come found water and nourishment waiting.

The people noticed the changes piece by piece. A group returning to a shelter found the same path open on three journeys in succession. Someone set a bowl beneath a dripping seam and found it full the next morning, and again the morning after that. A child lay awake behind fitted stones and heard runners passing outside, but no scraping at the gaps in the wall.

At last the power made itself known to them.

It did not take a shape among the animals or speak from every part of the chamber. It came to a group gathered beneath a skylight, as a voice they could hear without straining. When they were frightened, it waited. When they asked who was speaking, it answered as well as their words allowed. They asked what had been done to the ground, whether the water would last, whether the changes would harm the animals. The power showed them what it could: the deeper courses of water, the braced roof, the food spreading through the chamber. Where an answer would take time to prove, it said so.

It offered them a way to the surface. Together they chose the place, away from the shelters and the basins. The power opened a broad passage through the stone, with landings where people could rest and channels to carry rainwater past the steps rather than down them. At the top was a level plain under a wide sky.

Some people went up the first day. They returned with dust on their wraps and spoke of the distance they could see. Others waited until more journeys had been made. Some preferred the chamber and wanted to remain where they knew the stone and the sounds. The power left the passage open for all of them.

As seasons passed, people built shelters they could expect to find standing when they returned. They still marked their routes with stacks of three stones, though now the markers were more often moved by curious hands than by the rising floor. They learned where the new growths were good to gather, and which basins the broad animals favored. They made room for those animals to pass.

The power watched without requiring that anyone worship or remember it. From time to time it was asked for help, and sometimes it offered help when no one had known how to ask. It remained attentive to the changes it had begun.

One evening, long after the first passage opened, a sleeper woke inside a shelter. Through the entrance came the cool sound of water running into a basin. Runners ticked past the wall, and above them wings rattled briefly as the last light withdrew up the stone.

The sleeper listened. The ground was still.

After a while, they went back to sleep.
