The power came first as a pause in the water.

A keeper had just descended to read the gauges. Rain had fallen all night, and the square was deeper than it had been in months. The fish held close to the grilles. One of the pumps was making a sound like a small stone rolling slowly inside a tin.

The keeper wrote down both readings. The second needle trembled, then settled a little below the first.

In the basin, the reflected building was visible to its foundations. The top room was dark.

The keeper waited. A moth left the lamp housing and flew low over the square. Where its wing touched the basin, a circle spread outward. It reached the reflection of the building and stopped.

The keeper crouched. The circle remained there, a fine bright line around the dark window. After a time it opened into a question mark, written backward from the keeper’s side.

They looked around the square. Water dropped from a pipe onto their sleeve. Nothing else had changed.

The keeper touched the mark. It broke apart under their finger, and the ripples went on to the edge of the basin.

They wrote about it in the ledger. Beside the second gauge reading they made no mark for the light.

***

For three visits the keeper watched the basin. The other two came down as well, bringing a brighter lamp and a board to shade the water from it. They saw the dark window, but no writing.

On the fourth visit, the oldest keeper held a hand over the basin and spoke, though the water took most of the sound away.

“Can you hear me?”

A line appeared in the water. It was uneven, as if made by someone unused to writing with a current.

*Not well.*

The keeper fetched the others.

They asked who was answering. The water remained still long enough that they thought the question had gone unheard. Then the line came:

*I came because the light went out.*

“Can you bring it back?”

*Perhaps. I have not yet asked.*

The keepers looked at one another. The youngest pointed toward the reflection and made their signal for *there*.

The writing returned.

*I can reach the room. I do not know what is wanted there.*

The oldest keeper drew a slow breath. “Ask, then.”

***

They did not see the asking.

The power entered the room without opening the window or disturbing the curtain. There was enough light from the square to make out the table and the things arranged on it: lengths of wire, a small jointed tool, a dish holding screws. The lamp had been taken apart. Its glass lay on a folded cloth.

The figure was at the table, holding a wire against the lamp’s base. When the power spoke, the figure set the wire down.

It had been trying to mend the lamp. A part had worn through, and the material it needed was kept on a lower floor. The stair was difficult in darkness. The figure had made the journey twice and brought back the wrong pieces.

The power offered light.

The figure considered this, then pointed toward the dish. It wanted help with the lamp. After that, there were other things.

The power warmed the small metal fitting between the figure’s hands until it could be shaped again. The figure pressed it into place with the jointed tool. Together they assembled the lamp. When the figure turned it on, light filled the room with its familiar yellow steadiness.

The figure went to the window. Three keepers were bent over the basin below. One of them saw the light and struck the oldest keeper gently on the arm.

The figure raised a hand. All three hands rose in answer.

***

On the next visit, the water in the basin was still enough to hold a line.

*There is a stair inside the building,* the writing said. *It needs a handrail. May I bring it through here?*

The keepers had many questions. The youngest asked whether the figure could come through as well.

*I have not asked it to.*

They discussed the basin, and the animals in it, and the narrowness of the route down from the old street. At last they agreed. They moved the board and the brighter lamp out of the way.

A length of smooth wood appeared in the water, its end resting on the paving. It was old wood, already drilled at intervals. The keepers lifted it clear while fish scattered between their boots. The power asked for screws of a certain size. The middle keeper went up to the pumping station and found a tin of them in a drawer.

One by one, the screws sank through the reflection.

The figure fitted the rail over the following days. Sometimes the keepers could see it working at the edge of the window, carrying the wood away, returning for a tool. They found themselves lingering after the gauges had been read.

The power stayed while the rail was put in place. It steadied one end when asked and held the lamp on the stairs. The figure showed it the lower rooms. In one, rainwater had found a way through a seam and was running down the wall. In another, a window had opened in its frame and could not be closed. There were furnishings under cloths, shelves with things on them, and doors the figure did not open.

The power asked before touching each thing.

By the end of the week, the rainwater had been turned into a basin of its own. The window could be shut. The figure could go down the stairs with one hand on the rail and the other carrying the lamp.

***

The pump was still making its rolling sound.

The power listened to it with the keepers. A bearing had begun to fail; left alone, it would eventually stop turning. The keepers knew where to find a replacement, but the pump had run for so long that they had been reluctant to open it.

Together they made a plan. They checked the second pump, chose a dry day, and lowered the water a little at a time. The youngest keeper moved the snails from the section of wall where they would need to work. The oldest kept a bucket in the shade for anything they found inside the housing.

The repair took most of an afternoon. When the pump started again, its hum was so even that the keepers stood listening to make sure it was running.

Later, the figure asked whether the water in the square would stay at the new depth. The keepers explained that it would rise again, slowly, with the rain. The figure watched the second gauge for a while and seemed satisfied.

The power could have done more. It could have cleared the seams in the masonry and made the pipes bright, or opened a passage between the two buildings wide enough for people to walk through. It knew how much weight the walls could bear. It also knew where the crustaceans sheltered, where the eel fed, and how little anyone yet understood about the water that joined the two places.

So it left the seams alone. When the figure asked about a passage, it said that such a thing might be possible, but that it would take time to learn what it would alter. The keepers and the figure agreed to think about it.

Before leaving, the power made one small change in the basin. When anyone on either side wanted to write, they could lay a hand on the surface and the water beneath it would grow still. It would stay still until the message was finished, then resume its ordinary movement.

***

The oldest keeper began a new section in the ledger. At first the entries concerned the rail, the pump and the repaired window. Soon there were other notes.

*Asked about the moths. Drew one badly.*

*Top-room lamp using less oil since repair.*

*Sent three screws; one returned, too short.*

The figure sent a drawing of the jointed tool. It was not a crustacean, though the resemblance was close enough that the oldest keeper laughed when they saw it.

On a cold morning, condensation gathered over the reflected window. The figure came and cleared a patch. In the square, the youngest keeper put down the gauge book and laid a hand on the basin.

*Is the room warm enough?* they wrote.

The answer took some time. The figure’s hand appeared at the other side, moving slowly as it formed each letter.

*This morning, yes.*

Then the still patch loosened. A drop fell from the overhead pipes, and the window broke into bright pieces. It joined again almost at once.
