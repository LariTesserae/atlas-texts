The power came at a time when the chamber was loud.

A swift had entered the wrong tube and was being driven out again. A shutter knocked above the stair. Someone had begun to descend, and the pots were rattling against one another. Under a shelf, a pale gecko held a moth by one wing.

The power listened until the visitor reached the floor. Then it listened longer.

It could have made the chamber silent. The birds would still have opened their beaks, but their young would not have known where the food was, and a bird at the wrong tube would not have heard the warning. The visitor, stooping to examine a basket, would not have heard the crack that sounded from the vault when the wind rose.

The power attended to that crack. It ran behind a hood of clay, through two older repairs, and into the mortar between the bricks. A hard season of rain might bring the hood down. It would fall across the upper tubes.

The visitor looked up, but from the floor the damage was difficult to see.

The power could have closed the crack at once. Instead, it held the loosened bricks where they were and waited.

The visitor returned the next morning with a second person and a ladder. They climbed as far as they could without disturbing the occupied tubes. From there they saw the split, and the hollow beneath the hood where rain had been getting in. They spent the day lifting tiles from the mound outside. For several days after that, the chamber smelled of damp clay, and the swifts flew out in agitation whenever a shadow passed over the openings.

The repair was awkward. The people argued over how much of the old hood could be kept. One wanted to replace it altogether; the other found that its outer edge was still sound. They kept that part and built around it, leaving the opening broad enough for the birds.

When the work was finished, the power let go of the bricks.

It had found another difficulty. The bowls were shallow, and in a long dry spell the last water vanished before the people came down the stair. The birds ranged far enough to find water elsewhere, though not always easily. The geckos had fewer choices.

The power followed the grooves on the outside of the mound and found that one ended in earth beside a cracked jar. In the next rain, it turned a small part of the flow toward the jar. A person passing afterward noticed the water held there and set a sound pot in its place. Later they fitted a narrow spout through the wall, with a bowl beneath it inside.

The first bowl was too deep. A beetle drowned in it, and the person who came to clean it replaced it with a shallower one, laying a broken tile across one side so that smaller creatures could climb out. Moss spread near the spout. The bowl still went dry when there was no rain for long enough, but not as quickly as the others.

The power went out with the swifts and saw the country over which they fed: fields, ditches, gardens, roofs warm in the sun. It saw places where insects gathered and places where there were fewer than there had once been. Some changes it could make itself. It brought rain to a pond whose banks had dried and held the rain there long enough for the ground to drink. Elsewhere, it helped a stand of flowering weeds take root along a ditch.

People noticed the weeds. One cut them back where they obstructed a path and left the rest. Children later came to watch the insects there, and brought an older person to see them. That person began leaving more of the ditch bank uncut.

In the chamber, the gecko ate the moth. A spider caught another. A young swift died in a tube, and its parents ceased visiting it. The power grieved for it, as it grieved for the moths and for the insects carried through the openings in the birds’ beaks. It did not find a way to keep the life of the chamber as it was while making all its inhabitants safe from one another.

It remained attentive. When the plant’s basket began to split, a strand of power held the weave until the next visitor tested it and tied it up. When a storm tore away a shutter, the power turned the worst of the rain aside until morning. The people made a new shutter, fitting it more securely than the old one. It still knocked a little in a strong wind.

Years passed. The people who had repaired the hood grew old, and others came down the worn stair carrying bowls and brushes. They knew which tubes were occupied and where the tile lay beneath the new spout. They found their own things to mend.

By late afternoon, the light crossed the lower shelves. A gecko drank at the bowl while birds gathered above it, waiting their turn. At the sound of a visitor on the stair, the pots rattled. Several swifts flew up and out; others stayed in their tubes.

The visitor paused beneath the repaired hood, listening. Then they picked up a fallen basket and looked for a place to hang it.
