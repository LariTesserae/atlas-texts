The power first followed the water.

It went through the warm bends and the constrictions behind the machinery, into places no lamp reached. It found the films and the animals feeding on them, and the empty shells beneath the gratings. It went down into the receiving chamber, where water fell a long way before it was pumped up again. Some of what fell survived the passage. Much did not.

It also went among the workers. They had drawings for the basin, and schedules, and instructions about which valves could be closed and for how long. They knew things about the pumps that the drawings did not show. One could tell from the sound of a bearing when it needed attention. Another had learned which stretches of floor would flood first and kept the newer workers away from them.

The power did not stop the press. It did not warm the whole basin or fill it with soil. The works were still being built, and whatever they were being built to do lay partly beyond the power’s sight. There were lives beyond the basin, too, that might depend on them.

At the unfinished edge, it waited for a surge. As the water thinned over the newest plate, three crustaceans caught in a shallow depression near a worker’s boot. The worker saw them moving and crouched to look. When the next surge came, one washed free; the others remained.

There was a length of perforated metal left from another job. The worker set it at a slant between the depression and the channel, wedging its lower end beneath a bolt. Later, when the plate had dried enough for work, the worker fastened it properly.

This was a small thing. The power could have moved each animal itself. Instead it stayed near the worker through the shift, not speaking, but keeping the little ramp from being forgotten. At the break, the worker mentioned it to the others. One said there were shells under the eastern grating, enough to fill a handful. Another wondered aloud whether the pump was throwing water farther than it needed to.

The bearing had to be inspected two days later. While the pump was shut down, the crew lifted a section of grating. They found the shells, grit, and scraps of film dried into the corners. They found several animals still alive in a damp fold of metal and tipped them into the channel.

There was a discussion about the surge. It could not be eliminated: the pump needed the pressure. But a baffle could be fitted at the outlet to spread the force over a wider area. Someone marked the change on a drawing. Someone else checked that the baffle would not make the pipe harder to clean.

The power watched the first test. Water came hard against the baffle and broke around both sides. It still rose over the nearest plates, but it fell back sooner, leaving fewer animals stranded. The crew adjusted the angle and tested it again.

Not every change was made for the small inhabitants. The workers needed safer footing near the unfinished drop, and a guardrail went in along the edge. The power was glad of it. During installation, a bundle of cables had to be shifted, and the crane operator stopped a lift when a hook came too close to a worker guiding the load. No one spoke of the narrow escape afterward. The operator checked the hook twice before the next lift.

Under the staircase, the white growths spread across another loose strand of insulation. The insulation was damaged and needed replacing. A worker scraped it away, including most of the pale mat. The power did not prevent this. The old material was taken out in a sealed bag, and the new section was fitted tightly around the pipe.

Some of the white growth remained on a flake of fiber caught behind a bracket. In time it spread again.

The moths were harder to help. The lamps gave them warmth, but their housings also trapped them. A worker found several inside a reflector and carried them in a cupped glove to a warm wall bracket. Two flew back to the lamp. The others rested with their wings closed until the fog came down.

The power made no promise that they would live. It kept a little warmth in the bracket after the lamp was turned off, enough to carry those resting there through the coldest part of the cycle. When the lamp came on again, one moth opened its wings and flew.

At the receiving chamber, the power worked more directly. There was no worker nearby, and the falling water was too loud for a voice. In a place where the wall had been left rough, it raised a narrow ridge of iron beneath the surface. Grit began to settle behind it. Film caught in the grit. When a folded crustacean came tumbling down with the water, it struck the ridge, opened its limbs, and held on.

The ridge would not save everything that fell. It was low enough that the channel could still be flushed, and the next time a maintenance crew entered the chamber, they would see it. They might decide it had to go. The power left it where it could be seen.

Far above, the crane carried another plate through the mist. Workers guided it down with their poles, calling to one another over the fans. Its corners were sharp, and its casting marks stood clear. When it had been bolted into place, water ran across it for the first time, carrying a few grains of grit and a translucent shred from a warm pipe.

The shred caught briefly against a seam, then washed on. A grain of grit remained.
