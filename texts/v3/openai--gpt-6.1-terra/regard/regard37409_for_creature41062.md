At the cutting face, a worker found a line in the stone that had not been there the shift before.

It ran above the highest cutter, narrow as a root-strand. She marked its ends with chalk and called the others over. While they watched, a fleck of mineral fell from the line and landed on the new film below.

They stopped the cutters. The crew leader sent a cart back for the surveyors and began moving the machines away from the face. One cutter would not start. Its operator climbed down to examine the cable, and a second worker went to help him.

The stone moved before they could finish.

A slab the size of a factory wall eased outward. There was time for the two workers to turn and see it, but not enough to get clear. Then the slab stopped.

It hung a hand’s breadth from the face. Dust fell from its lower edge, pattering onto the roofs of the machines. The cable swayed against the cutter housing. Nothing else moved until the crew leader shouted for the two workers to get out.

They came away at a run. A moment later, more stone shifted above the slab and stopped in the same manner. By then everyone was moving back, taking what tools they could carry. The stalled cutter remained beneath the suspended rock.

The surveyors arrived to find the crew in a wide semicircle around the face. One of them walked forward, despite the warnings, and touched the underside of the slab with the end of her measuring rod. The rod pressed against solid stone. When she withdrew it, the slab remained where it was.

They closed the road and called in the crews from the neighboring cuts. By the dim hours, a crowd had gathered at a distance: cutters, growers, cart drivers, people from the old districts who had come to see. Lamps were brought and trained on the rock. Someone made a tally of everyone who had worked at the face that day, and then another tally, to be certain.

A child asked who was holding the stone.

No one answered until a voice said, “I am.”

It seemed to come from the open ground between the crowd and the face. It was not especially loud. The people nearest it heard it clearly; those farther back heard it too, and afterward disagreed about where it had come from.

“I did not know how else to reach you in time,” the voice said. “The rest of the face is unstable. I can hold it while you decide what to do.”

The surveyors went forward again. This time they carried instruments and a portable lamp. The voice warned them when they approached a place where more rock was likely to fall, and they marked those places on the ground. They found that the fracture extended much farther than the visible line, behind an adjoining section where several cutters were still parked.

During the night, crews laid a route around the closed face. Others fetched braces and lifting gear. The power holding the stone answered questions when it could. It knew where the fractures ran and how much weight each section carried. It did not know which machines could be abandoned, or how long the growers would need to establish a road on a particular patch of film. The workers explained, sometimes at length.

By the following day they had a plan. They would recover the cutters, lower the loose rock in sections, and leave the fractured upper face untouched until it could be examined more fully. The work would take eleven days.

“I can hold it that long,” the voice said.

On the third day, while a cutter was being drawn out, a grazer came along the old road with two young behind her. The road crew had placed barriers across the pavement, but she passed between them and continued toward the face. People called to one another. A driver moved his cart across her path, slowly enough that she could turn away.

She stopped and scraped at the ground beneath the cart. The two young stopped beside her.

The driver waited. When she had finished, she turned back along the road, and the young followed. Only then did the crews resume drawing out the cutter.

For the eleven days, the voice stayed near the face. People grew accustomed to addressing it, though not everyone was comfortable doing so. Some asked why it had come to the city. It said that it had become aware of the place and its inhabitants, and had been paying attention.

Others asked what it had seen.

“Not everything,” it said. “I have seen the animals crossing the squares. I have seen the work at the edge, and the workers returning from it. I saw the stone begin to move.”

One of the surveyors asked whether it could see fractures in the cavern roof.

“Yes.”

That answer caused a longer silence than the first.

The surveyors asked for time to prepare. They brought their maps, and over several shifts the voice helped them mark places that deserved a closer look. There were old cracks already known to them, and others hidden behind mineral shelves or deep inside the rock. Most would require no immediate work. A few lay near lamp cables or occupied buildings. The surveyors went to inspect those first.

When the loose stone at the cutting face had been lowered, the crews thanked the voice. It had one more thing to tell them.

During the work, it had felt movement beneath the beds that released the waxy vapor. The movement was slow and spread over a great depth. It did not appear dangerous yet, but it could alter the flow of vapor into the city.

The growers asked whether it could stop the movement.

“Perhaps,” it said. “I do not know enough about the beds to do it safely.”

So the growers brought their records, and workers opened old access passages under the floor. For many shifts, people went down with lamps and measuring tools. The voice told them where it felt movement; they told it what grew there and what the city drew from it. They found a section of the bed under pressure from a gradual settling of the surrounding stone. The growers believed they could open a second channel for the vapor before the first grew narrow.

It was ordinary work, though the voice helped them choose where to begin.

When the new channel was finished, the growers asked if the power would remain.

“I can come when you call,” it said. “There may be times when I cannot answer immediately. And there will be things I cannot know without your help.”

They chose a place for calling it: a small square near the surveyors’ rooms, with a lamp that could be raised above the roofs. They also kept the maps there, and left space on the walls for new ones.

At the cutting face, work resumed along a different line. The abandoned section stood quiet for a time, its marks and braces visible from the road. Spores settled in the seams between the lowered slabs. A grower came to inspect the first thin films and found a grazer already there, scraping at a patch of mineral dust.

She waited for it to move before kneeling to examine the growth.
