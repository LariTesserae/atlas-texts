After a night of rain, a child named Iri went out with her grandmother to mark the weak places.

They found three along the eastern avenue. At the first, water had risen between the leaves and stayed there, though the neighboring courts were already drying. At the second, a browser had slept on the mat and left a shallow hollow in the shape of its body. Her grandmother pressed it with her foot and decided it would recover.

At the third, the leaves parted before either of them touched them. A dark opening appeared, crossed by white roots. Something moved beneath.

Iri tied a red knot to a stem beside it. Her grandmother knelt and put her hand on the mat.

“The old roots are going,” she said. “Fetch the others.”

Iri ran, spreading her toes as she had been taught. She called from the edge of the court, and people came out of the towers carrying poles, baskets, and rolls of cloth. One of the resin makers came too, still chewing her breakfast.

They widened the opening carefully. Beneath the living mat was a thickness of older roots, and beneath that, water. Usually the roots reached into a soft, firm layer that people said was the remains of the town before this one. Here they hung free. The layer had washed away.

A fish passed through the gap. Then another, swimming against a current none of them had noticed before.

They followed it under the avenue as far as they could, making small openings and closing them behind. At each opening the current grew stronger. It was carrying fine clay out toward the marsh.

By afternoon, the people who knew the ground had gathered with the people who knew the towers. They stood in the court, discussing whether to close the avenue and how far the hollow might extend. Iri stayed at the edge of the group, twisting another red knot around her finger.

A stranger was standing beside her.

The stranger had no hat and no dry cloth. Rainwater ran from their hair into their eyes, though the rain had stopped.

“You could help,” Iri said.

“Yes,” said the stranger.

They waited while the adults finished speaking. Then the stranger approached Iri’s grandmother and asked if they might look.

She gave them a pole and showed them where the mat was safe. The stranger lay down at the edge of the opening, put an arm into the warm water, and kept it there a long time.

When they stood, a threadlike worm clung to their wrist. They returned it to the water.

“The hollow reaches under that tower,” they said, pointing. “And under part of the next court. The water has made a way through the ridge. It will carry more of the ground with it.”

“Can we close it?” asked the grandmother.

“For a while. You could pack the opening, and I could hold the packing in place. But the water would seek another way through. There is more of it beyond the ridge each year.”

The resin maker asked how the stranger knew this.

“I can see it,” they said.

The resin maker looked at the ridge, then back at the stranger. “Show us.”

The stranger held out a wet hand. On the palm lay a tiny image of the basin: its courts and towers, the low ridge, the marsh spreading beyond. The people leaned close. They saw water moving through the clay, not in one clear stream but in countless slow threads. Beneath the eastern avenue, a little cloud of clay drifted away.

Someone reached toward the image, and the stranger turned their hand so she could see better. Iri saw that the woman’s fingers were shaking.

“Could you stop all of it?” she asked.

The stranger was quiet. “I could raise the ridge. I could make a wall deep enough that no water would pass through.”

“That sounds useful,” someone said.

“It would keep water out,” said the stranger. “It would also keep the basin’s water in. The ground and the lives in it would change. I cannot tell you that the town would be better for it.”

No one spoke for a while. A flock crossed the court behind them, probing busily among the leaves.

At last Iri’s grandmother asked, “What else can you do?”

“Help you see where the ground is thinning. Hold the hollow open while you move the rooms. Strengthen what you plant along its edges. There may be other things you think of.”

They talked until evening. People went away to eat and returned with neighbors who had not yet heard. The stranger answered what they could. When someone asked for the water to be made lower everywhere, forever, the stranger said they could do it, but that the streams and marshes beyond the ridge belonged to many lives too. They offered to show the questioner those places.

By morning, the eastern avenue was closed. Its residents carried furniture and baskets through marked doorways into other towers. One passage opened only a handspan, and the resin maker had to coax it wider. A child came through with an armful of bedding, followed by a browser’s young that had been sleeping beneath the bed. Its mother was waiting outside, ears pressed to the wall.

For seven days the stranger held the hollow steady. People could feel the current running faster beneath their feet, but no more ground washed away. They moved slowly, checking each chamber and returning for things forgotten. Iri’s grandmother went back twice: once for her medicines and once for a bowl she had made with her sister, although it was cracked.

On the eighth day, the hollow settled. The eastern tower leaned, its upper rooms folding inward, and a long section of avenue sank gently beneath the surface. Water filled the space. The people stood back from the edge and watched leaves loosen and float away.

The stranger showed them where the remaining ground was firm enough to plant. Court tenders brought young stems; others carried mud in baskets and set it around the roots. The stranger placed their hands in the water among the new plants. By the next morning the stems had taken hold, though they were still thin, and needed guarding from the browsers.

Later, Iri found the stranger sitting at the edge of the new water. She offered them her roll of dry cloth. It was not very clean, but she had not used it since the rain.

They dried their hands and gave it back folded.

“Will you stay?” she asked.

“Not here all the time. But you may call me.”

“How?”

The stranger glanced toward the tower where the resin maker was arguing with two court tenders about the spacing of the new stems.

“You have very clear voices,” they said.

Iri tried calling then, across the water. The stranger smiled, and several people on the far bank turned to look.

She kept the red knot she had made that first morning. In time, when the new stems had grown thick enough to bear a crossing, she tied it where the old avenue met the new. People asked her what it marked. She told them about the ground that had gone, and the water beneath it, and how the town had moved aside.
