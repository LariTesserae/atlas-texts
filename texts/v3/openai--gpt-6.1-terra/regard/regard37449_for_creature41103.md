The next flash comes before the young body has finished drawing back from the last one.

Along the cable, its threads tighten. On the hanging mat, the other part tightens a moment later. The cord between them is pulled fine enough to let light through.

Something touches the cable.

The discharge enters the water farther from the mat than it has before. It branches through the dark, brightens the bare stones, and goes out. The young body contracts, but less of it is caught in the flash. When it loosens, the cord is still whole.

There is a new object beside the pylon: a length of black stone with a broad hollow worn through it. It hangs in the current without a support. The cable passes through the hollow, and the stone draws part of each discharge into itself. Thin lines of light move over its surface and vanish.

The young body extends a thread toward it. At the first contact the thread recoils. After several flashes it touches the stone again and remains there, feeling a small tremor each time the cable lights.

The mat drifts farther from the pylon. The young body sends new branches across the gap, fastening them to fragments of plant and to grit that has gathered against the stone. For a while the connection holds. Then the mat turns edgewise in the current, and most of the branches pull free.

One cord remains. It is longer than the others and passes through the hollow in the stone. There is room inside for it to move. The current draws the cord taut, but the edges of the hollow are smooth.

Farther across the basin, the loose metal panel strikes its pylon.

It strikes again, and after that the blows stop. The panel continues to move. It swings outward in the current, returns toward the pylon, and comes to rest against a thick fold of red vegetation placed between the two pieces of metal. On the next surge it pushes the vegetation flat. Water escapes from the fold in a cloud of bubbles, and the panel settles against it without a blow.

The pumping continues. Through the ground and the water it reaches the knots in both stations, and the knots hold their shapes against its familiar measure.

In the intact station, the pale knot on the turbine casing sends a thread toward the door. A thread from the collapsed station is already there. They touch in the crushed conduit and remain together through several revolutions of the turbine. Then the stations begin to draw apart.

A slab presses down on the conduit as the outcrop shifts. Beneath it, the two threads are stretched thin. The dark stone beside the conduit opens, leaving a narrow space. Water moves through the opening, carrying grit. One thread enters first, then the other. They fasten to opposite sides of the new passage, and as the stations separate, more of each body follows them in.

The slab comes down. The conduit folds shut.

The threads in the new passage pull loose from one another. Each remains attached to its own station. For a long time they lie against the stone, their tips close but not touching.

The basin is still loud. A ridge breaks open and sends a tremor through the plant beds. The knot in the intact station withdraws several threads from a seam in the outcrop. One remains inside. It curls around a pale mineral edge and holds there while the crack widens. When the movement has passed, the thread begins to branch.

Near the pylon, the upright mat catches on the hollow stone. Its lower edge folds around the stone and tears. Most of the mat continues away, taking several knots with it; a smaller piece remains. The young body spreads through this piece and along the cord that joins it to the cable.

When the next flash comes, a thread on the stone feels its tremor. The branches nearest the cable draw back. Those on the mat remain open, shivering in the current.

The loose metal panel swings outward and returns. The red fold receives it. After some time, the body in the intact station grows a thread along the panel’s edge and feels its slow movement there.

The power stays near the basin. Where a passage is about to close, a little room sometimes opens beside it. Where a current has stripped the bottom bare, a stone may come to rest long enough for a young knot to fasten itself. Some stones are pulled away again. The power watches what takes hold and what does not.

The turbine turns. Water passes over the roofs of both stations and through the hollows in the black stone. Along the cable, the young body grows, feeling each flash through the stone before it reaches the water.
