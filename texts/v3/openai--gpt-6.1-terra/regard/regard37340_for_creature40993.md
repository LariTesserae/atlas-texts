The power came to the basin without entering it at first. It felt the streets growing outward and the stone rising beneath the leaves. It felt, too, the small differences between what was coming and what had already given notice of its arrival.

There were several ways the stone might meet the town. In one, the leaves folded upward and the lowest houses rose with them. In another, the stone broke through in narrow columns, leaving the leaves hanging between. In a third, a house failed to detach in time. Its foundation held fast while the wall above it tore.

The power could have stopped the stone. It could also have carried every house out of the basin and set it somewhere safe. But the stone was warm with beginnings, and the houses had grown into their places over many years. The power could not see a way to prevent every hurt without also preventing things the people might have wanted.

It stayed at the rim and listened.

For a while, it listened badly. Its attention made the roots so quiet that people stopped in the streets and put their hands down. The factory workers missed an early vibration and had to cut a snarl from the feeder vine. In three houses, windows closed against the sudden stillness.

The power withdrew some of its attention. The ordinary sounds returned: shoes on bark, a mouse passing a pipe joint, the factory’s three turns and pause. A child in one of the lowest houses heard footsteps in the stillness after all, but by the time she called her mother they had ceased.

That night the power touched the end of an unused root and sent a tremor along it. It meant to say that the stone was nearing the leaves.

The tremor reached the factory first. Two workers stopped the engine and found a cable stretched across the feeder vine, where someone had left it by mistake. They removed it and started the engine again.

The power waited until the next night and tried once more, more gently. This time a person walking home felt the tremor underfoot. She knelt, pressed her fingers to the bark, and waited. The power repeated it.

She went to a neighboring house and brought out its two occupants. They felt it too. One thought it was an animal inside the root. The other said it was too regular. They stayed there for some time, listening and tapping back.

The power learned from their taps that a pause could be a question. It learned that repeating the same answer was not always helpful. By morning, the three people had called a root-mender, who checked the road and found nothing wrong. She left a strip of soft rind over the place so that anyone passing could feel where to put a hand.

Over the following days, other people came. They brought a cup of water, then took it away when nothing drank. They tapped messages into the root. Some received no answer; some felt a faint response that they could not make sense of. A few stopped coming. Others kept trying.

The first clear exchange concerned a loose fastening beneath a house near the center. The power traced the strain through the root, then tapped toward the house until someone understood enough to look. A cable had worn thin against a ridge. The household replaced it before supper.

After that, people began asking about other fastenings. The power answered when it could. Once it sent them to a healthy cable, mistaking the pressure of a bird’s roost for a flaw. The mender who inspected it came back annoyed, and the power felt no reason to make the bird move so that she would be proved right.

The people wanted to know who was tapping. The power had no body to show them, and the shapes it made in the air frightened the birds from a whole length of road. It let the shapes go.

At last, a child laid a piece of dead vine across the marked place and put one end in a bowl of water. The power touched the root. Ripples spread through the bowl. The child laughed and tapped the vine with one finger, and the power answered.

The bowl stayed there. People came to speak into the root and watch the water. Their questions were sometimes too large for ripples. The power learned to make the water rise in a little ridge for *yes* and tremble against the bowl’s edge for *no*, though even these were difficult when a question contained more than one thing.

When they asked whether the stone would reach the leaves, the water rose.

When they asked whether it would break them, the water trembled, then rose, then lay still.

That answer traveled farther than the others. People from the lowest houses came with their knot records and measurements. They asked the power to show them where the stone would rise first. This it could do: it sent small pulses through the leaf veins above the highest places. The people marked them with scraps of pale cable.

They found that one house stood directly above a narrowing gap. Its detachable foundation had been built to lift from below, but a column of stone might catch the outer supports before anyone could reach the release knots.

The family did not wish to move. Their house had a sleeping hollow made by a grandfather whose name was still spoken there, and a wall that had only recently grown a shelf at the right height for the youngest child. They asked whether the power could hold the stone down.

It could, for a time. When it told them so, the family asked for nine nights. On the tenth, relatives came to help them take apart the outer supports. The power held the stone while they worked, feeling its warmth gather against a pressure the stone had never met before. By morning the house hung from a new sling. Its old foundation remained below, too tightly grown into the leaf to remove without tearing either one.

The family moved their belongings back in. The shelf had cracked during the work. For several mornings the child pressed a hand against it, and eventually it began to thicken again.

The power continued to hold the stone for as long as the people asked. They used the time to move two more houses and to clear paths across the leaves. They made longer ladders at the factory. They also brought bowls to the rim so the root-mice would have water if the leaf veins were disturbed. The counters ignored the bowls and gathered beneath the pale cables marking the highest points.

On the morning the people chose, everyone in the lowest houses went up to the streets. A few stayed close enough to feel the leaves through hanging ropes. The power released its hold.

For a long time nothing visible happened. Then the central leaf lifted along one edge. A pale ridge appeared through the gap, shedding warm grains. It pressed against the abandoned foundation and carried it upward, slowly enough that the fibers stretched before they parted. The sound went through the streets like a distant reel being unwound.

The stone kept rising. It did not stop at the leaf. By evening it had made a low shelf beneath three houses, and people were lowering one another down in slings to touch it. It was warm, as they had expected, and granular in some places, smooth in others. The counters crossed it without pausing.

The power remained at the rim. From time to time someone came to the bowl and asked a question. Sometimes the water answered. Sometimes it did not, and the person sat for a while before carrying the question elsewhere.

As the stone grew, the people made new plans. They left the bird-shaped vacancies under the roots alone. They kept coils of cable beside the lower windows. And when a new house appeared at the end of a street, they brought it water, as they always had.
