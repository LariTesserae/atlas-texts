On the night after the rain, a worker in one of the towers felt a pattern in the floor that she did not recognize.

At first she thought it was the last water falling from a leaf. She rested her palm on the stone and waited. The pattern came again: three light touches, a pause, then three more. It was too regular for water and too faint to have traveled from another tower.

She called the others. They knelt around her, placing their hands flat on the floor.

The touches continued. One of the older workers pressed a greeting into the stone. After a little while the answer came, awkwardly spaced but unmistakable.

They spent much of the night that way. The thing in the floor did not know their signs, but it learned quickly. It made mistakes that caused the children to laugh. It asked what the cups near the entrance were for, and why the bridges carried dust. When the older worker asked where it was, the floor held still for so long that several people took their hands away. Then it answered:

*Here. Also elsewhere.*

Toward morning, it asked about the seam beneath the tower.

The workers were cautious. They told it what they knew: how the seam opened, what vessels they lowered, which sounds meant they should stand back. They disagreed about several things, and the thing in the floor listened to each of them.

At last it said that something below the foundation had shifted. It would not fail that day, or perhaps that season. But the space into which they lowered their vessels had begun to open against another space, and in time the opening would widen.

The workers asked how it knew. The answer was difficult to follow; it involved distances that did not remain the same when measured from different sides. The thing tried three times, growing no less patient when the workers questioned it. Finally it showed them a way to test what it had said. They lowered a weighted line into the seam and drew it up marked with a fine green dust, though nothing green had been sent down.

By evening, messages were traveling across the bridges.

People came from other towers carrying instruments and records. For many days the entrance room was crowded with boots, vessels and strips of mineral paste. They made more tests. They argued over what might be done and whether the tower could safely remain occupied. The thing in the floor answered what it could. Once, when asked a question about the machinery beneath the basin, it said it did not know; it had never had occasion to examine that part before.

It could close the seam, it told them. It could also hold the two spaces apart while the workers found another way to gather what they needed. The second choice would take longer, and it could not say how long the holding would be necessary.

The people asked for time to decide. The thing agreed.

During the discussions, it remained quiet unless someone laid a hand on the floor and addressed it. One child, passing through the instrument room, asked whether it could feel the gliders landing on the canopies.

*Not yet,* it answered.

She showed it how she listened through a piece of fallen rib kept on a shelf. A glider landing at the far end made the faintest interruption, more an absence than a touch. The thing tried until she was satisfied. Thereafter, when no one was asking it about the seam, it listened to the basin: the herds spread along the horizon, the small animals emerging at dusk, the cups trembling on the tables.

The workers chose to keep the seam open while they built another means of gathering heat and material. They asked the thing to hold the spaces apart, and it did. There was no visible change. The next vessel came back warm and without the green dust.

For years afterward, people visited the tower to learn about the work. Some also came hoping to speak with the power beneath the floor. It welcomed them, though it did not answer every request as they wished. When a hunter asked it to show her where the predators waited, it showed her the nearest one and asked whether she wanted to know how to feel the quiet around it herself. When an instrument keeper asked it to stop the roar for one hour, so that he could rest, it explained how many creatures would be bewildered by the loss of it. Together they made a small chamber instead, with thick walls and a floor set apart from the foundation. The keeper could still feel a little of the city there, but not enough to trouble his sleep.

The work on the seam took longer than anyone had expected. Children grew into it; some of the first workers died before it was finished. On the day the last vessel was brought up, those who had known the danger gathered beside those who knew it only from accounts. They laid their hands on the floor and asked the power to close the opening.

The seam closed without leaving a mark.

Afterward the power stayed for a while. It helped where it was invited, and sometimes it was simply present. People passing through the instrument room would feel a greeting underfoot and return it without stopping. The child who had taught it to listen grew old. Near the end of her life, she asked if it would remain at the tower forever.

It said it did not think so. There were other places that had drawn its attention, and the people here knew how to reach it.

She asked how.

It reminded her of the greeting the workers had first pressed into the floor. She had been there that night, laughing at its mistakes. She remembered.

In time, the power went elsewhere. The people continued their rounds beneath the canopies. The bridges carried travelers and dust. When rain crossed the basin, the great leaves tipped toward it, and afterward someone set a cup beneath each dripping edge.

Now and then, a person working alone would place a hand on the stone and send the old greeting into it. Sometimes an answer came at once. Sometimes it came much later, when the person had gone about another task and nearly forgotten to wait.
