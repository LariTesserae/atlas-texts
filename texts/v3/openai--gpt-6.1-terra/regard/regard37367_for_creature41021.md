The power came on a day when the eastern wall had moved four millimeters.

The survey crew found it in their readings just after breakfast. Four millimeters was not, by itself, an emergency. They checked the instrument, took the reading again and called the engineer on duty. By noon, the upper walkway was closed and the conveyor below it had been stopped.

The power watched them work. It had not made the wall move, and it did not believe that every moving thing should be held still.

Behind the wall, however, water had found a route no one had mapped. It had passed through an old brick culvert, then into a seam of loose fill beneath the new temporary retaining structure. The seam was widening. The next heavy rain might carry enough material out of it to bring part of the slope down.

The power could have turned the water to stone, or lifted the whole terrace and set it somewhere safer. It could also see the kitchen window above the slope, where a woman had scratched her children’s heights into the frame, and the workshop below, where a man was waiting for a replacement bearing so that he could finish repairing a neighbor’s pump. They would have to leave for a while. They ought to know why.

So it went to the engineer, who was standing in the drizzle with a folded map inside her coat.

“There is a culvert under the old wall,” it said.

It spoke in a voice she could hear over the wind, though she could not tell where the voice came from. She looked up and down the empty walkway.

“Who is this?”

“Someone who can see the water.”

She waited. On the terrace below, a gull landed on the stopped conveyor.

“Where?”

The power gave her three distances and a depth. It told her what the water was doing and how much it thought might fall. It also told her where it was uncertain: there was a layer of old foundations it could not distinguish without looking more closely, and looking more closely might disturb them.

The engineer called the survey crew back. They drilled a small inspection hole where the voice had said, and by late afternoon a camera had found the culvert and the moving water. The engineer ordered the homes above the slope emptied, and the workshop too. Residents packed medicine and dry clothes; one family brought a tray of seedlings. Others carried boxes for neighbors or went to the dining hall to make up beds.

That night the rain came harder than forecast.

The power stayed beside the slope. When a section of the old wall gave way, it slowed the falling brick and fill just enough for the temporary barrier to take the weight. The barrier bent but held. In the morning the crew found a heap of wet masonry against it, and beyond that the ground had opened in a dark, narrow trench.

For three weeks they worked out how to divert the water and rebuild. The power answered questions when asked. It was good at seeing what lay beneath the surface, but the crews knew the place in ways it did not: which valves could be trusted, which access road would bear a loaded hauler, where there was room to put a family and its seedlings for another week. They disagreed with it once about the sequence of the work. The engineer explained her reasons, and the power accepted them.

Before it left, it asked whether there was anything else it could do.

The residents had many answers. Some wanted the rain to stop. One child wanted a bird that would sit on his hand. A pump operator asked for a night without the vibration in her bed, and several people laughed in recognition.

The power could not promise that the rain would stop where they wished without changing it somewhere else. It would not make a bird tame against its nature. But it went with the pump operator to listen.

For years, a housing on the lower terrace had carried a faint tremor through a row of connected foundations. No one had found an economical way to isolate it. Together they traced the vibration to a joint hidden behind two layers of old cladding. The next maintenance crew opened the wall and fitted a flexible coupling.

That night the operator lay awake for a little while in the unfamiliar quiet. Then a different pump started, farther down the basin. She felt it faintly through the floor and knew which one it was. She went to sleep.

By the end of the month the eastern walkway had reopened on a different route. The woman moved back into her home, and the children’s height marks were still on the window frame. The repaired slope was planted with reeds and low willows, with enough space left around the inspection points for a person to get through.

The power had gone by then. The engineer kept the map it had spoken to her over, with the new culvert drawn in red. When she showed it to apprentices, she told them how they had found it.

Some believed her and some did not. All of them learned to check the ground behind the wall.
