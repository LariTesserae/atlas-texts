The power came to the square during the morning exchange and stood near the bench. It did not look like anyone the residents knew. Its coat was dry, although it had been raining, and it had no dark strips around its ankles.

A measurer offered it some cloth. The power accepted, tied the strips in place, and listened.

A kitchen had acquired a third room, which had no door from either of the other two. The person who had seen it had glimpsed it through the window from a bridge. Someone asked whether there was anyone inside. The person did not know; the bridge had moved before she could get a good look.

The power could see the room. There was a table in it and a bowl on the table. The bowl was empty. The room had been waiting for a door for some time.

It could also see the bridge, the warm pools, the grains rising from the tray, and the far walls of all the rooms in town. It saw a person asleep in a bed that had become longer than the room containing it, and a white animal about to pass beneath a boot. It saw the town’s second horizon and, beyond that, another, and a place still farther out where work had stopped.

The power could have brought every far wall into view. It could have set the bridge down and kept it there. It could have finished the hill and given it weather, or given the residents a warmer sky.

Instead it asked the measurer if there was work it might help with.

The measurer considered this. Strangers sometimes arrived with useful skills, though they generally brought tools.

“Can you carry a ladder?” she asked.

The power could. It went with her and two others to the bridge. They waited for the near end to settle, then crossed toward the middle, carrying the ladder between them. From there the measurer could see the window. She marked its height on a strip of paper and passed the paper back.

The power held the ladder while she climbed. Through the window she saw the table and the empty bowl.

“No one there,” she called down.

Her voice reached them a little later, from the far side of the courtyard. By then she was already descending.

They took the ladder to the house and knocked. The people who lived there were eating. They made room for the crew and shared what remained in the pot while the measurer explained about the room.

One of the household wanted to open it at once. Another wanted first to know whether it was warm enough to use. A third asked how much of the kitchen wall would have to come out.

They went together to look. The power found itself beside the youngest member of the household, who was carrying a piece of bread. The child asked whether the room had always been theirs.

“I don’t know,” said the power.

This was true in a way that surprised it. It knew when the room had appeared, and where, and how the bowl had come to be on the table. It did not know whether that made the room belong to the household whose wall stood next to it.

The measurer tapped the wall and listened. Another member of the crew fetched a narrow instrument and checked the temperature at three points. The wall was warm enough. They marked a doorway on it in chalk, leaving space on either side for shelves.

The power could have opened the wall without dust or noise. It said so.

The household discussed this. They asked how it would be done, and the power showed them, making a small opening near the floor. They looked through at one leg of the table. One person put a hand in and felt around the edge.

“That seems all right,” she said.

They asked for the rest of the doorway, and the power made it. The cut stone came away in a single piece. The builders took it into the courtyard, where it could be used for something else.

Inside, the room was bare except for the table and bowl. The household stood around them, deciding what to do. The child put the piece of bread in the bowl. After a while they brought in two chairs. By evening, someone had hung a coat on the back of one.

The power stayed in town.

It learned that the bridge watchers did not want the bridge made still. When it connected the western courtyard, people used it to carry supplies to the pool attendants. When it reached the eastern side, children crossed it on their way to lessons. A fixed bridge would have served one group and left the other to take a much longer route.

The watchers did want a little more time to warn people before the far end began moving. The power looked into the bridge’s workings with them. Together they found a place where a thin strip of metal could be fitted. It gave a clear note several minutes before the bridge changed direction.

The first day, people stopped to listen whenever it sounded. By the end of the week, they were using the notice to finish crossings and call to friends on the other side.

At the hillock, the power offered to bring weather.

The attendants asked what kind.

It described rain fine enough to darken the grains, a wind that would move them down one side, and a season of frost that would make a crust on the surface. The attendant who remembered a hill asked whether grass would grow.

“It could,” said the power.

They asked for time to consider. Over the next several days, people came to the courtyard to hear the proposals. Some wanted grass. Some wanted a bare slope where the work of the wind could be seen. A few thought the mound should be left smooth until everyone who wished had seen it that way.

The attendants put up a second sign beneath the first. It gave the date when they would meet to decide.

Meanwhile, the power walked the lanes. It noticed how often someone went cold while waiting beside a shifting room or bridge. Hot stones helped, but they cooled, and the work sometimes lasted longer than expected. It spoke with the people who tended the pools and with the crews who knew the heat conduits. They brought a small outlet to the square, close enough to the ground that a person sitting on the bench could warm their feet.

The bench remained damp. A resident suggested turning it around; another pointed out that this would change the view of the second horizon. In the end they fitted it with a removable wooden seat. People used the wood when they wanted to sit and talk, and lifted it off when they wanted to see what was being built beyond the doorways.

One evening the power found the person who had slept in the long bed. His room had lengthened again. He was sitting at the near end, looking toward a far wall he could no longer see.

He had marked the floor with pieces of thread, each tied around a different fastener. The nearest marks were a step apart. Farther in, they grew closer together until the power could no longer distinguish them without looking carefully.

“I usually wait until morning,” he said. “It comes back most nights.”

“Would you like me to bring it back now?”

He thought for a moment.

“I’d like to know where it goes.”

So the power stood beside him and looked. The room did not go anywhere. It was making more of itself, slowly and without a plan, adding small distances between the marks. The power explained what it saw as plainly as it could.

The man was quiet for a while. Then he asked if he could sleep in the kitchen and leave the room as it was for the night.

The power helped him carry his bedding. In the morning, the far wall was visible again. He moved his bed back and left the threads where they were, adding the date to a note beside the door.

The power remained through several more changes. It learned to pause when a voice came back from a wall, and to repeat the words to whoever was nearest. It took a turn watching the hillock while both attendants went to a meal together. It stood still once with a set of tracks ending beneath its boots, and felt the small white animal brush past when it arrived.

At the meeting about the hill, the residents chose a light rain for the first weather. They wanted to see what it did before deciding about wind or grass.

The power brought the rain at midday. It fell only within the courtyard, each drop landing on the mound or the tray beneath it. The attendants watched with their instruments. Several children stood under the eaves, reaching out into it and then pulling their hands back.

After an hour, a shallow channel had formed down one side of the hill. Water collected at the bottom and carried a few grains onto the paving. The attendant who remembered a hill crouched to touch the wet slope. Her glove came away dark.

The rain stopped. Everyone stayed a little longer to see whether the channel would change. Then they went back to their work, stepping around the grains on the paving. One of the attendants brought a brush, but after looking at them she set it against the wall for later.
