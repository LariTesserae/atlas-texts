The power came to the city during a light rain. The blue lamps had gone out, and people were waiting beneath the covered walks. The power waited with them.

It could have cleared the veil above the plain. It could have filled the cisterns and brought the distant edges of the city close enough to see. It did none of these things. The collectors were working; the gardens had been planted for this light; and the people under the walk seemed content to wait a little longer, passing a dyed cloth from hand to hand to dry the bench.

The power took a place among them. It had the appearance of a visitor from another district, though no one later agreed which district that might have been. It accepted the cloth, dried a place on the bench, and sat down.

Across the plaza, an unfinished building was rising. Rain darkened its pale foundation. Beside it stood a bed of flowering groundcover, and in the bed the leaves rose in a narrow line, then settled.

The power watched the line approach the foundation. It knew that the machines at work beneath the stone would soon reach the hollow where the creature slept. The machines had no way to recognize a hollow as a home. They would fill it, and continue.

When the rain stopped and the blue lamps returned, the visitor asked the others whether anyone knew who was caring for the new building.

Two people did. They took the visitor to a courtyard where the building’s caretakers were having their midday meal. A dish was offered, and the visitor ate before speaking.

“There is an animal beneath the bed by the foundation,” it said. “Its hollow extends under the stone. The machines will reach it tomorrow.”

One of the caretakers set down her cup. She had seen the raised leaves and marked the bed, but had not known the hollow ran so far. She asked how the visitor knew.

The power considered this. A smaller answer would have been easier, but it gave the true one.

“I can hear it breathing,” it said. “I can hear the machines as well, and I know where they are going.”

The caretakers looked at one another. Then the visitor told them what it was, as plainly as it could. They asked several questions, not all of which had answers that would fit into words. At last one caretaker brought out a plan of the foundation, and another went to fetch the gardener who had planted the bed.

They walked over together. By then the leaves were still. The gardener knelt beside them and put her hand on the soil, though she could feel nothing moving. The caretakers studied the plan and spoke about how much of the foundation could be shifted. The visitor stood nearby, answering when asked.

They stopped the machines for the afternoon. Before evening, they had laid out a changed foundation with a wider space around the bed. The building would be narrower on that side, and one covered walk would have to bend. The gardener thought the bend might make a good place for a bench.

The creature did not emerge while they worked. Afterward, when the plaza had emptied and the weak green light was coming down the walls, the groundcover lifted once beside the new line of stones.

The visitor stayed in the city for several days. Word of it traveled by the crossings and along the longer walks. People came with questions, requests, and things they thought it ought to see. A child brought it to a wall where a lizard hunted beneath a light. Two neighbors took it along an avenue whose old name referred to three cisterns that had since been removed. An engineer showed it a collector fin that had never held its adjustment for more than a week.

The power listened. Where it could help, it explained what it proposed to do, and waited for an answer. It showed the engineer how to make a small change to the fin. Together they watched it turn toward the dim sun and remain there. The engineer wrote down the change so that it could be tried on other fins.

Some people asked about the veil. The power told them what it knew of the air and what might happen to the city if more light reached the plain. The answer took a long time. They continued discussing it after the visitor had gone.

On its last morning, the power walked to the edge of a garden where threads stretched between two towers. Droplets had gathered along them, each one holding a small, blurred piece of the light. The power lifted a hand, and for a moment the droplets hung motionless, even the ones that had begun to fall.

Then it lowered its hand. The water ran into the channels, and the threads began to tremble again.

A gardener had seen this from the covered walk. She came over and asked if the power was leaving.

“Yes,” it said.

“Will you come back?”

“If you want me to.”

She thought about the new building, and the engineer’s fin, and the people still arguing amiably about the sky.

“Perhaps,” she said.

The power thanked her for the welcome. They stood together until the channel began to flow, then went their separate ways: the gardener toward a bed that needed tending, and the power along an avenue that had not yet acquired a name.
