The power came at dawn, when the underside of a stone was wet enough for a droplet to form but not yet heavy enough for it to fall.

It did not lift the stone. There were packed ones beneath it, and several were molting. One had been caught for two days between the edge of the stone and a neighbor grown larger. It could draw in the limbs on one side of its body, but not on the other. Each time the crowd contracted, the pressure returned.

The power attended to that narrow space. A grain of mineral broke loose from the stone’s underside and settled in the damp beside the trapped animal. The stone shifted by less than the width of a limb.

The animal drew itself free. For a while it remained where it was, its ridges compressed on one side. Then, as the ground cooled, it made its way out with the others.

It was not the only creature in difficulty. Across the highland, hunters fed, eggs dried in their casings, and the old and injured found that a familiar crossing had become too long. The power could have stopped these things. It looked at them for a long time.

There was a basin near the middle of the plateau where moisture had failed three seasons in succession. The dome at its edge had emptied; its roof had fallen into the chambers, leaving a ring of skins and dust. Beneath the crust at the basin’s center lay many eggs. Some were still capable of hatching, if dampness reached them and remained.

The power sent no great rain. It cooled the basin a little sooner each evening and held the morning’s condensation there a little longer. The change was enough for a dark patch to persist through the day. It spread by degrees, following the old seams.

The first animals to arrive were shelled ones, moving through the damp ground. Then packed ones came down the shallow paths, and several of the broad grazers altered their courses so slowly that the change could be seen only over weeks. A colony of builders returned to the basin’s rim. They opened chambers beneath the fallen dome and began to carry out the collapsed material.

The eggs in the crust grew damp. Many hatched. Others had been dry too long. Their casings softened into the soil, where smaller things fed on them.

Hunters came too. The power watched one enter a crowded shelter and take a young animal whose body had not yet hardened. The others tightened around the space it left.

The power did not turn away. It remained until the hunter had eaten and until the crowd had loosened again. It remained through the warmth of the day, while water passed from mouth to mouth at the dome’s entrances, and through the evening, when the largest grazer reached the basin and drew a little of the dampness beneath its lobes.

The packed one from under the stone arrived later, among many others. Its injured side had opened into a narrow scar. It grazed for a time, leaving a clean line through the film on the rock, then found a space beneath the edge of the great animal and pressed itself in.

The basin stayed damp for a season, then gradually dried. By then the builders had roofed their chambers, and the young animals had dispersed along the paths. The power let the mornings return to their former temperature.

It continued to visit the highland. Sometimes it found a small place where help was possible: a stone that could be eased upward, a droplet that could be kept from evaporating a little longer. Sometimes it stayed with a creature until it died.

The paths went on joining and dividing. The dome rose, settled, and was rebuilt. On cool mornings, animals crossed the basin so slowly that even the power, watching from one edge of the plateau to the other, chose one and waited.
