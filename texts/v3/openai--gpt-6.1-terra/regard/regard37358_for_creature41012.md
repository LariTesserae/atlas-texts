The power first attends to the noise.

It can hear the reeds separately: each stem’s hollow note, each collision of ice, each thread of water working through the gravel. It hears the stove draw, and Iven’s spoon touch the bottom of the pot. Beneath the willow stand, it hears a vole scratching at a roof that has become a wall.

The vole has taken one of its usual exits. Water filled it from the other end and brought loose peat down behind it. Now the animal is in a pocket between two roots, with its nose above the water and its hind feet swimming.

The power lifts the nearer root a little.

Black water pours through the gap. The vole goes with it, tumbles among loose stems, and catches hold of a reed. It climbs into a thicket where the ground is higher. There it sits for some time, its wet fur standing in points, before it begins to work through the snow toward another of its rooms.

The root settles. By morning, the current will have found the space beneath it and carried some peat away.

Farther along the channel, the anchoring mesh has caught on a buried stone. It is pulling tight in a place where it ought to yield. The machines have not yet registered the strain: the pull is narrow, concentrated along a few strands. When those break, the load will pass quickly to the next section, close to the raised path.

The power could set the strands right. It could gather every scrap of mesh from the roots as well, and lay the loose cables neatly on the workshop floor. But the people here know their machines and the ground around them. They might want to inspect the stone, or move the mesh, or decide the path should be taken up and laid somewhere else.

On the display beside Iven’s stove, a line of pale letters appears:

**The mesh is caught on a stone below the willow stand. It may break near the path.**

Iven looks at it. They touch the screen, opening the pressure map, then close it again. The message remains.

“Who sent that?” they say.

The power answers on the screen.

**Someone who can see beneath the ground.**

Iven reads this twice. They set down their bowl and call the workshop. The person there answers on the third attempt, their face lit blue by their own display.

“Did you send a message about the willow stand?”

“No.”

Iven turns the screen toward the camera. The person in the workshop leans close, frowning.

“Could it be the system?”

“I’ve never seen it write anything.”

The power waits while they talk. The workshop keeper brings up the same pressure map, and they study it together. There is a slight irregularity near the path, though neither can tell from the readings what is causing it.

At last Iven asks the screen, “Can you show us where?”

A dot appears on the map. Beside it, another line:

**Four handspans below the surface. The stone has a sharp edge facing the path.**

The workshop keeper writes down the coordinates. They agree that nobody will go out again tonight unless the readings worsen. In the morning, two people will go with a probe and a spare reel of mesh.

Before ending the call, the workshop keeper says, “Ask it if the other paths are safe.”

Iven asks.

**For now. The water may change them before morning.**

“Well,” the workshop keeper says, “we knew that much.”

After the call, Iven sits by the stove. They have questions, but they are tired, and the wind has not eased. They type:

**Will you be here tomorrow?**

**Yes.**

Iven puts another log on the fire. After a while they ask where the voice comes from, and whether it has a name, and whether it has been here long. The answers appear slowly. Some satisfy them; some leave them staring at the screen, trying to think of a better question.

Outside, the willow stand shifts again and comes to rest. A bird settles in its lee. The vole finds a chamber still dry enough to sleep in, though much of the food stored there has spoiled.

Toward morning the wind turns. When the people from the workshop reach the marker, they find Iven already waiting, holding a probe and a coil of line. Together they locate the stone. Its sharp edge has worn through several strands of mesh.

They free what they can and mark the rest for repair. Then they stand on the gravel ridge, looking at the channel the night has made and discussing where the path might be safest next winter.
