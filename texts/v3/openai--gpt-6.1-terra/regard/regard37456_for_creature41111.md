One morning the mist did not come. The dishes held a few beads, and by the time the sun reached the court, those had been poured together into a single vessel.

The people used the water from the cistern sparingly. They left the cuttings in damp shade for the grazers and carried the smallest hunters on bark to the cool side of the houses. At evening, peel lay untouched outside several doors. The grazers had gone down into the seams, and the hunters waited above them, their limbs spread on the stone.

The power had been watching long enough to know that a dry morning was not unusual. It waited through the next night. When that too passed without mist, it followed the seams down into the mountain.

Water moved there, far below the roots and the places the animals could reach. The power opened a narrow way through the stone, taking care not to disturb the chambers where the grazers had gathered. By dawn a dark line had appeared beside the cistern, and a bead had formed at its lower end.

A child saw it first. She touched the bead and found another forming against her finger. She called the others, and they watched for a while before setting a dish beneath it.

The water came slowly. It filled the dish once that morning and again toward evening. The people poured some into the cistern and shared the rest among the houses. They left a shallow measure beside the new seam, where grazers came to its edge and hunters settled in the damp around it.

The dry weather lasted five days. Each morning the dish filled; each evening it filled again. The people began to cut fewer leaves, choosing those that had already browned at the edges. They kept the paths open and the roof drains clear. On the sixth night the mist returned, and water beaded beneath the collection stones.

The dish beside the cistern continued to fill. The people made a small channel for its overflow, leading it toward a patch of gravel where nothing yet grew. They watched that place over the following days. A few grazers found it, and moss began to show between the stones.

The power stayed through another season. Once, when a slab slipped from a roof, it turned the stone so that it landed flat in an empty stretch of paving. The sound brought people running. They found the slab intact and, under its edge, a hunter that had folded its limbs close. A woman lifted the slab while a child slid bark beneath the animal and carried it into the shade.

After that, the people checked the other roof stones and replaced a support that had split. They cut back the fern that had grown over the roofline, leaving enough for the grazers feeding farther down.

The power watched them finish. Then it went elsewhere, leaving the water to come up through the mountain and the people to tend the new channel as they wished.

In the evenings the child sometimes sat beside the cistern. She could see the bead lengthen, hold for a moment, and drop. When it struck the dish, the smallest animals at the rim drew back. Soon they edged forward again.
