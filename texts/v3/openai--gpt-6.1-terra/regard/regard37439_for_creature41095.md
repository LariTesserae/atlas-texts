The power came first during a pump stoppage, when it could hear the people speaking.

A woman on the eastern walkway was telling a boy that the knock had changed. She tapped the rail twice with her wrench to show him the old rhythm, then again for the new one. The boy listened to the silent housing and said he couldn’t hear either.

“You will when it starts,” she said.

The power waited with them. When the pumps began, it heard what she meant.

It could have silenced the knock at once. Instead it followed the sound down through the housing and found a bearing worn thin on one side, a shaft beginning to score, and a piece of grit lodged where the last repair had left a narrow gap. Then it went farther, into the pipes and the ground beneath them, and beyond the rim of the bowl. It found where the warm water came from and where it went, and which valves had been left open because closing them would flood another district.

By the next shift, a dry board had appeared on the wall beside the gauges. On it was a drawing of the pump, accurate down to the scrape on the housing, with the worn bearing marked in red. Beside the drawing hung a key to a service room beyond the rim.

The workers examined the board. They tested the key on an empty lock, then sent three people along the raised passage with lamps and ropes. Inside the service room they found spare bearings, wrapped against damp, and a set of plans that showed more of the pipes than any plan they had kept.

They brought the bearings back and changed the damaged one during the next low-water interval. The boy held the lamp. When the pump started, he listened through the rod and looked up at the woman.

“Yes,” she said.

More drawings appeared over the following days. Some concerned machinery; one showed a route for the water that would let them keep the streets lower without sending the flood elsewhere. Another showed how to move the most dangerous electrical fittings above the old mineral bands. The drawings left room for notes, and the workers covered those spaces with questions. Answers appeared beneath them, sometimes after an hour, sometimes after a night.

The power did not ask them to leave. It did open doors beyond the rim: to dry sleeping rooms, to a clinic, to people who could help carry out the larger work. Some residents went to see the rooms and came back with measurements and opinions. A few moved there. Others wanted to stay near the pumps, at least until the new arrangements had proved themselves.

The work took months. The walkways were raised and widened where two people could not pass. Fans were fitted in the sleeping rooms, and beds and blankets were carried out to dry on clear days. The loose sheet on the cooling tower was finally brought down by a crew with proper harnesses. Afterward, people noticed how much of their conversation they had been shouting.

The trees remained. One had to lose a branch for the new pipe, and the crew cut it carefully, lowering it in sections so it would not strike the grating. Someone braided its vines into ties and left a coil by each workshop door.

When the altered pumps were ready, everyone who could be spared came to watch. The water drew back from the blue-gray paving and stayed there. For a long time nobody moved into the exposed street. Then the boy, older now and carrying his own tools, went down with two others to clear a drain. He stood on the stones while he worked.

The substation still hummed. On the next scheduled stoppage, drops lifted from the transformer cases and trembled above the fence. A small child reached toward them, and her father caught her hand. Together they waited for the rain to fall.
