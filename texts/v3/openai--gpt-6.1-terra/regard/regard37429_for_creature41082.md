The power came when the pool was below the first notch and no one was looking at it.

It did not come down through the air. It found its way through the stone, following the narrow passages where the pale creatures fed, until it reached the water beneath the town. There it remained for a long time, learning how the water moved and where it went when the gates were opened.

It learned the names the people had given the sounds. It listened to their disagreements, too: a knock beneath the western paving meant one thing to the woman who repaired the gate and another to the man whose sleeping room lay nearest it. Both had reasons. Neither was always right.

The power could have carried the town to higher ground. It could have sealed the cracks in the basin floor. But the houses were built around the stone, and the pool was fed by those cracks even in dry weather. Beyond the wall were fields that sometimes took the overflow and sometimes suffered from it. Beneath the paving were the creatures, and beneath them were passages the people had never seen.

So the power began by making itself known.

One morning, a woman clearing grit from the pool felt her wooden tool stop against something soft. She drew it out. A pale creature clung to its end, and when she laid it on the step, it uncurled and spoke.

“The water will rise before evening,” it said.

The woman sat back on her heels. The creature rested in a little water beside the notch.

“How far?” she asked.

“To the third mark. Perhaps higher. There is a stone caught below the eastern gate.”

She fetched two neighbors. They found the stone where the creature had said, wedged deep in the channel. By afternoon they had worked it loose. That evening, when the pool rose, the gate opened freely.

The creature spoke to them once more from the woman’s wet palm.

“I can help you make more room for the water,” it said. “I would like to show you where.”

The people came to the pool to hear it. Some were afraid; some wanted to ask about the water; one man wanted to know whether the creature was being made to speak against its wishes. The power answered that the creature would not remember speaking, nor be harmed by it. After that, it spoke instead from a cup on the step.

They talked for three days. People brought questions they had thought of during the night. They brought the gate stone, a broken drain cover, and a jar of cloudy water from a household cistern. They sent for those who worked in the fields beyond the wall, and waited for them to return.

The power showed them a place outside the basin where water could gather without reaching the fields. It showed them, in a basin of clear water, the course of a buried channel that could carry some of the rising water there. The channel passed beneath ground belonging to two families. The families came and looked, and after much discussion agreed to it, provided they could still reach their plots and the work did not begin until the crop was in.

When the time came, the power opened the channel through the stone. People shaped its mouth and lined the new pond with rock. They left shallow edges where birds could land and a deeper place that would hold water through a dry spell. The first time the pool rose under a clear sky, water moved quietly into the channel. It did not reach the third notch.

There were other changes, some made by the power and some by the inhabitants. The most troublesome drinking cistern received a clean spring through a seam in its wall. The children’s sleeping shelves were raised. At the pool, the old notches were left as they were, and a small stone disk was set into the step beside them. When water began to rise far below the town, the disk grew cool. People still watched the notches, but now they had a little more time.

The power stayed until the work was finished. Before it went, the woman who had first heard it asked whether the creatures beneath the paving needed anything.

“Wet stone, small spaces, and the scraps you already leave them,” it said. “They are numerous here. In the new channel they will find their own way.”

Then the water in the cup became ordinary water.

For a while, people spoke to cups and waited. They argued about whether the power would return, and whether there was anything else they should have asked. Then a roof spout came loose, and someone needed help repairing it.

After the next heavy rain, the passages shone as before. A few children went out with pieces of bark and carried pale creatures from the drain mouths back to sheltered cracks. By morning, the water had gone down, the new pond beyond the wall had filled, and the grain had stayed where it was.
