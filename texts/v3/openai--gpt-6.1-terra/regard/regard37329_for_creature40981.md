The power does not enter through the seam at first.

It attends to the chamber from the other side of the ceramic, learning the length of its cycles and the longer habits beneath them. It follows water down through the pores and back through the engine. It feels the turbines wearing against their bearings, and the faint variations in the valve that sends the mist. It watches the animals in their thousands, including those too small to be seen by the amber light.

Some of what it learns is difficult to put into words. A hunter whose leg has been damaged draws away from pressure on that side; the power finds more in this than a mechanical withdrawal, though it cannot say that the hunter feels pain as a person does. It finds reason to care, too, about the grazers caught in the hunter’s fringe. Their lives contain more than feeding and flight. They seek particular surfaces. They linger under leaves after shedding, touching other newly molted grazers as they pass.

The power watches a surge sweep through the room. Several flyers are carried into the pores. A grazer loses its hold and is pressed against a hunter’s leg. The hunter folds around it. Later, a larger hunter approaches the basin, and the smaller one draws in its fringe and leaves its feeding place.

The power could stop all of it. It could hold every body still, or remake the chamber so that nothing ever needed to eat anything else. But the chamber is a system of lives laid across one another, and the power is wise enough to see how much it has yet to learn. It does not mistake this for a reason to leave the suffering as it is.

It begins far below, where the engine returns water and food.

A filter has grown thin. It is still working, but in a few years it will tear, and grit will enter a line that feeds the chamber’s plants. The power repairs it without interrupting the flow. It eases a worn bearing back into alignment, then makes provision for the engine to replace it when the wear returns. The chamber’s roar changes by so little that the leaves go on trembling at their accustomed rate.

Next the power opens a narrow route around each of the finest pores. Water can pass through either way. A small animal drawn toward a pore may find, beside it, a shallower channel with a rough bottom where its limbs can take hold. The flyers are the first to use these passages, their young gathering along the new waterlines. Some grazers follow. The hunters investigate the openings, but the channels have been shaped too narrowly for them to wait inside.

The power watches. The reduced loss through the pores means more flyers survive each surge. Their young thicken the films at the waterline, and within several cycles the grazers must travel farther to reach some of their old feeding places. The power lets a little more nutrient solution enter through the roots of two plants. It watches again, and adjusts the amount downward.

Under one shelf it grows a ledge with a soft, textured ceiling. The space is broad enough for many grazers and high enough for them to turn, but too low for a hunter to spread its legs. A thin current carries fresh film across the ledge. At first the grazers avoid it. Then one follows the film beneath the shelf, feeds for a while, and leaves a trail. Others come after it. Some remain through the deepening of the flow.

The hunters still need food. The power does not simply wall them away from it. In a basin near the new refuge, it begins to form small living morsels from material already circulating through the engine: soft bodies without the structures from which the power has found experience arising. They drift with the current. A hunter catches one, holds it briefly, then releases it. The power changes its chemistry. The next is eaten.

For a long time, the hunters take only a few. The power keeps the supply modest while it learns what the food does to their bodies and to the water. Their young grow; their fringes repair after injury. Some continue to catch grazers. Others settle where the morsels gather and leave the grazing paths less disturbed. As generations pass, the balance shifts, though never completely and never without further attention.

The power makes other shelters: a crack above the surge where flyers can cling, a second feeding ledge on the far side of the chamber, a basin with a gradual slope that animals can climb if they fall in. It does not smooth every surface. The roughness gives purchase, and the little hollows have become places where eggs are laid. When it alters a channel, it does so slowly enough that the films can spread along the new edges and the animals can find them.

Once, curious about the roar, it makes a pocket of quiet beneath a leaf. The grazers nearest it stop. One turns back into the vibration and resumes feeding. Another remains at the boundary, feeling the ceramic with its front limbs. The power restores the sound and leaves only a small reduction in the harshest tremor, one the animals can still read through. It keeps watch for a long time afterward, in case even that change has cost them something.

The maintenance seam remains closed. No one entering now would find the chamber transformed into a garden or a monument. Water still moves in sheets over black ceramic. Hunters lie in the basins. Pale grazers work outward from the roots, and flyers gather in warm pockets beneath the shelves.

But there are more places to hold on when the water rises. There is food that does not have to flee. There are young animals that live through a surge their grandparents would not have survived.

The power remains attentive. When the engine drifts out of balance, it corrects it. When one of its changes has an unexpected consequence, it is willing to undo it. Most of the time it does nothing visible at all.

In the chamber, a droplet gathers at the tip of a leaf. A flyer rises beneath it and veers away. The droplet falls into a basin, sending a disturbance through the water. Nearby, a hunter lifts its fringe; under the low ledge, three grazers pause and feel the surface, then go on feeding.
