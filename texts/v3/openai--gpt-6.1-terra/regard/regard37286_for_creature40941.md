The power came in the rain, which was not unusual enough for anyone to notice.

It had no need of a body, but it spent some time with the bodies already there: the shrew nosing through a tunnel under the spring yard, a child carrying three eggs in a basket, the old woman who could remember the slip behind the pens. It listened to the arguments over a drain and to the hollow sound of a path timber struck with a heel.

Above the settlement, behind a shelf of willow, water was gathering in an old seam in the rock. A heavy stone had settled across its usual course. The soil below was taking the water and holding it, though not for much longer. If the rain continued through the night, part of the slope would come down toward the upper pens.

The power could have lifted the stone away. Instead, it loosened a smaller one.

It fell in daylight, knocking against the exposed rock. The sound carried over the roofs. A goat watcher heard it, turned, and saw a thread of brown water where there had been clear water that morning.

She called to the yard. Four people came up with her, including the old woman who remembered the earlier slip. They stood beneath the willows, feeling the ground move very slightly under their boots.

They did not agree at once. One thought the pens were far enough to the side. Another wanted to open the blocked channel immediately. The old woman put her stick into the bank and drew it out wet and gritty. She said they should move the goats first.

By late afternoon the flock was crowded into a lower yard, complaining loudly. A family brought out blankets and baskets from the house nearest the pens. Others carried away the dry fodder. Two people remained above, working from firm ground with long poles, until the brown water began to pulse from the bank.

They came down then, and everyone waited.

The slip fell after dark. It broke one corner of the empty pen and filled the drain behind it with mud. Stones rolled as far as the upper path. No house was struck, though one roof received a spray of wet earth, and the people inside spent a wakeful hour listening for more.

The power held the loosened ground a little longer than it might have held itself. It did not stop the fall. By morning the dangerous water had a way out, and the slope could be seen for what it was.

For several days the settlement worked around the damage. The goats were troublesome in the lower yard; they ate the bark off a fence before anyone thought to move them again. People cleared the path and discussed where to build the new pen. They left the broken drain until the ground above it had settled.

On the second morning a child found the old woman at the spring, washing mud from her stick.

“Did you know it would fall?” the child asked.

“I knew it might,” she said.

The child considered this. “Did the goat watcher know too?”

“She knew enough to call us.”

The power stayed while they worked. It eased the fever of a baby who had been ill before the slip, though the baby’s parents never learned why she began to drink again that evening. It went with the next trading party below the cloud, keeping a falling branch from the narrow track until they had passed. It did these things quietly.

Before leaving, it visited the upper slope once more. Frogs had already entered the shallow pool forming where the drain had been. In time the people would clear part of it and leave part, because the ducks had taken to it and because there were other jobs to do.

The power watched a willow branch, half buried by the slip, lift its leaves out of the mud. Then it went on, leaving the water to run through the basin and the people to hear it.
