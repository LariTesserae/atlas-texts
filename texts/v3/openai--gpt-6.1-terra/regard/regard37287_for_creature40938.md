The first thing the power did was touch the wall.

It had been attending to the basin for a long time. It knew the weight of every shelf of new stone and the routes taken by mineral water through the fractures below. It knew how much pressure the cables held, and how little was carried by some of the thickest ones. It knew which animals had settled on the piers before the people now living in the walls were born.

Knowing these things did not tell it whether the people wanted to be addressed.

It chose a service passage where the wall was thick and the foot traffic light. There, it made a small, sustained pressure against the stone: the customary announcement of a stranger, repeated at intervals.

For several days nobody answered. People felt the signal and went on about their work. One person stopped to investigate, found no body at either end of the passage, and marked the place for a building tender.

The tender came with two others. They lay against the floor for a long time. The pressure was too regular to be a loosened fastening, and it traveled through the wall differently from the movements of animals outside. When one of them pressed an acknowledgment into the stone, the signal stopped.

After some hesitation, the tender asked who was there.

The answer came through the floor. Its first attempt was hard to understand. It used the old form of a word for *near* that meant, more exactly, *on the other side of a thin partition*.

The tender corrected it. The stranger accepted the correction, and after a while they were able to talk.

It said that it was near the city but had no body in the passage. It said that it could feel the stresses beneath the basin. It asked whether there was a worker who could tell it more about the cables.

The tender asked several questions of their own. The answers were strange enough that they sent for another specialist, who sent for others. Soon people were arriving from neighboring districts. They touched the wall in turn and compared what they had felt. Some thought a hidden machine had begun to speak. Some thought the tender had discovered a person living in a sealed chamber. A few were unwilling to press their pads to the place at all.

The power did not try to settle the question. It answered what it could. When asked where it had come from, it gave an answer the people could not make much of, though several remembered it accurately and repeated it later.

The cable specialists were interested in a more immediate claim. The stranger said that a rising shelf under the western district was pressing against a buried support. Within some years, it might transfer too much strain to a cable above the water. The cable held three walkways, two pipes, and a suspended room where a number of families slept.

The specialists already knew about the shelf. They had been watching it for years, and the cable was on their schedule for inspection. They did not agree with one another about the danger.

The stranger described a point deep below the basin floor. None of them had reached it. It offered to press there lightly, so that they could feel the path by which the force traveled.

They asked it to wait until they were ready. People went to the cable, to the neighboring walls, and to a lower chamber close to the shelf. When everyone had found a place to listen, the tender gave permission.

The pressure was slight. Even so, it took a long, complicated path through the district. The specialists felt it arrive in places they had not expected. Afterward they compared their notes, then asked for the same pressure again. The stranger obliged, and then a third time.

That work continued through many visits. The specialists brought prepared strips and recorded the signals. They made their own pressures at accessible points, testing what they had learned. The stranger sometimes predicted the wrong moment for a change to reach the upper cable; the stone was still shifting, and the routes through it changed. It said so, and they adjusted the marks.

Eventually the workers agreed that the suspended room should be moved.

The people sleeping there had known for some time that this might happen. They had already begun looking for another place. The room was warm, and its floor carried a pleasing double pulse from a pipe below; several of the young had never slept anywhere else. The nearest suitable chamber was cooler and had only one entrance above the water. The families asked for a second entrance to be cleared before they moved. That took longer than expected, because flat animals had grown thickly over the old seam.

The power could have opened the chamber in an instant. It did not offer. The workers had a way to clear an entrance without taking the whole living crust, and several families depended on the harvest from that wall. The people doing the work stopped often to consult them, lifting the larger animals aside and fastening them where the water would still reach them.

When the chamber was ready, the families moved their water basins, their food stores, and the objects they kept for their feel: a smooth piece of old pipe, a brush made from the flexible edge of a veil, a stone that clicked softly when turned in the pads. One elder stayed in the suspended room an extra night, with a relative for company. In the morning they both crossed to the new chamber.

The pipes were more difficult. One carried heat to a tray room where young animals were raised. The other helped keep a lower passage from flooding. The workers could build a new span, but they needed time, and the buried shelf was still rising.

At a gathering in the warm chamber, they asked the stranger whether it could slow the shelf.

It said it could hold the buried support against the pressure for a while. It did not know whether releasing it later would cause the shelf to shift all at once. It could also make a small opening deeper in the rock, giving the pressure another path. That would change the flow of mineral water. The stranger could estimate where the water would go, but not exactly how the animals around the fractures would respond.

The gathering grew quiet. People held contact with the floor and with one another while the pipe pulsed under them.

They called in harvesters from the affected piers, people who tended the trays, and workers from two districts farther down the flow. They took more measurements. They argued over the likely harm to animals they ate and to animals they did not. A few wanted the stranger to hold the support while they worked, and to worry about releasing it afterward. Others preferred the deeper opening.

In the end, they asked for the opening, but smaller than the stranger had proposed. They would watch the water and ask for it to be widened if necessary.

The power made the opening over the course of several days. The people followed its progress through the stone. At one point it paused while a diving crew moved a cluster of veil-feeders away from a place where warmer water had begun to emerge. The crew returned tired and scraped, carrying a young one between them because it had let go of its grip in the altered current.

The pressure on the buried support eased. It did not disappear. The specialists kept listening while the builders hung a new cable from two neighboring structures and shifted the pipes across to it. The old cable remained in place until the work was finished, crowded with animals that had spent their lives on its lower surface.

Afterward, the water near the deep fracture was warmer than before. Some crusts thinned; others spread. The harvesters changed which piers they visited, and the tray tenders turned their animals more often until they understood the new flow. The people recorded all of it alongside the measurements of the cable. They wanted to know what their choice had cost and what it had made possible.

The stranger remained near the city. From time to time a worker came to the service passage and pressed a question into the wall. Sometimes the power could answer. Sometimes the answer was that it did not know, or that finding out would require touching something the people had not yet agreed to disturb.

Others came simply to talk. One young person who had ridden on a parent’s back through the move visited often and described the new chamber as it became familiar: the cool hollow, the pipe’s single pulse, the slight give in the floor near the entrance. The stranger listened. It asked questions about things it could already feel, because the young person felt them differently.

Years later, the old suspended room was taken down. Its panels were carried into a lower passage for reuse, and its fastenings were left in the water until the animals on them had moved or been moved elsewhere.

The stranger felt each change. So did the people whose work it was to tend that part of the city. They kept their bodies against the stone, taking turns when the water grew cold, passing what they learned through one another’s pads.
