The power came first as attention.

It followed one of the pale animals toward a fallen drop and found the darker animal moving beside it. It watched a grazer wait in a narrow channel while another passed. It entered the cool spaces under the arches, where blind young fed in water that had arrived as rain.

It noticed the old animal at the rim because a line of moss had grown across one eye. The animal rubbed its face against a stone until the moss came away, then stood for a time with both eyes open. On its shell, the small grazers went on feeding.

The power stayed through the night. By morning it knew something of the basin’s lives, and something of the lives that had made the structures. It could see how the silver walls held water cool, and how the broken arches slowed the rain. It could also see a change approaching.

Far below the basin, where water moved through a seam in the stone, a gradual settling had narrowed the passage. For many years enough had come through. In the next long dry season, the beads would grow fewer. The high shelves would dry first, then the fern channels. The water under the silver roof would last longer than most, but the animals outside would have difficulty reaching it across the exposed ground.

The power could have filled the basin at once. That would have drowned eggs waiting in the hollows and carried the smallest animals from their feeding places. It could have sent rain every evening, but the basin’s creatures had their uses for warmth and drying too.

So it went down into the stone.

It worked slowly, making a narrow way around the settled seam. It left the old passage in place and opened the new one only enough for water to pass. Then it waited for the water to find it.

For several days there was little change. A damp line appeared in a hollow near the rim. A film connected two shelves that had lately dried apart. The pale animals found both places, and dark animals followed them.

The power watched to see whether it had done well. When water began to stand too deep around a bed of eggs, it shifted a grain of stone in the channel above. The film thinned. A hunter came down to drink there and caught one of the pale animals as it turned away from the water.

The fallen arches needed less. One broken edge had sunk into the trough, making the last pool shallower each year. The power raised it by the width of a small animal’s body. Cool water gathered beneath it again. The blind young, whose eggs were fixed to that same edge, remained in place.

On the evening the work was finished, the old animal came down from the rim. It put its feet in the new damp line and followed it to a patch of soft growth. When it had eaten, it rested there. Some of the creatures on its shell climbed down and found water; others stayed among the moss.

Rain crossed the basin after dark. Drops struck the trees and ticked into the hollows. Beneath the silver roof, the swimmers drew out into ribbons, while outside, water joined water along the stone.

The power remained for another season, watching the level of the pools and the passages between them. Then, with the first rain of the next, it let its attention widen and move elsewhere.

The basin went on changing. The new seam would one day need tending; the arches would fall farther; eggs would dry and eggs would hatch. For now, each morning, beads emerged from the rock, joined, and began to run.
