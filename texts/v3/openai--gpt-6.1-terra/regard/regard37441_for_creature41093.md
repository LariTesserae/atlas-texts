The power came down from the rim in the form of a person carrying an empty jar.

At the first cistern, a woman was drawing water. The stranger waited until she had filled her jars, then asked where a visitor might drink. She showed them the cup kept under a flat stone and warned them not to let the rope drag in the dirt.

The water was cool. The stranger thanked her and asked if there was work to be done.

“There’s always work,” she said, and pointed toward the new wall.

By midday the stranger had learned to turn the drying blocks. At first they gripped them too firmly, leaving dents. A child showed them how to slide both hands underneath. After that, the blocks kept their edges.

They stayed several days. They carried gravel, ate where they were invited, and learned which courtyard had room for an extra sleeper. When people asked where they had come from, they said, “From a long way off.” When someone asked what they wanted, they answered, “To see how you live, and to help if I can.”

There was a place at the working margin where a gray layer ran beneath a lip of yellow clay. The workers had cut close to it. After a shower, the stranger saw that water had entered the seam and was loosening the gravel behind it. They laid a hand on the bank and felt the weight above, resting for the moment on very little.

They went to find the people working there.

The workers came, looked at the seam and argued about how far back the loose ground might reach. One fetched a length of reed and pushed it into the crack. Another climbed the bank and found a narrow split on top, hidden among the shrubs.

For the rest of that day no one worked beneath the lip. The stranger stood nearby while the others moved their tools and blocks. They held the bank together with a care no one could see, until the last child had been called out of the yard and the last cart had passed into the lane. Then, at the workers’ request, they helped bring the loose earth down. It fell with a broad, soft sound and covered the place where the new courtyard had been marked.

The next morning the workers began again, farther back. The fallen clay was good for blocks once the gravel had been picked out.

Word spread that the stranger had noticed the danger. People began bringing them other questions: whether a wall needed another stone at its foot, whether a stain inside a cistern meant a leak, whether a certain patch of ground would grow beans. Sometimes the stranger knew an answer. Sometimes they asked to see the place, or sat with the questioner until someone else came by who knew more.

One evening, beside the low new wall, a man asked them outright what they were.

The stranger told him. He sat quietly for a while, looking across the roofs. Then he asked whether they could make it rain.

“Yes,” they said.

He thought about the grain laid out to dry, the uncovered blocks, the channels still full of grit from the last shower.

“Not tonight, then.”

“Not tonight,” the stranger agreed.

He told the others, and by morning the stranger had more visitors than they could receive in the courtyard. Some came with requests. Some came to look. A few kept their distance. The stranger listened to those who wished to speak and did not follow those who turned away.

Together the townspeople chose a place above the settlement where water could be gathered before it ran down the banks. The stranger opened a basin in the earth there, with broad sides that could be inspected and repaired. They did it while the workers watched. Afterward, everyone went to look: adults testing the firm edges with their feet, children sent back when they ventured too near.

When the town was ready, the stranger brought a gentle rain. It filled the new basin partway and gave people time to see where the water went. They found two places where the channel needed widening and one where it ran too fast. By the next rain those places had been mended.

The stranger stayed through the planting of a bed near the basin. Its keepers set stones around the seedlings. A little water was left in a shallow hollow beyond the stones, where a sparrow could drink and where, one morning, the tracks of a mouse appeared in the mud.

When the stranger left, the woman from the cistern walked with them to the rim. Below, someone was turning a block in the sun. Farther off, a cart waited in a narrow lane while its driver helped a neighbor lift a bundle over a wall.

“Will you come back?” she asked.

“Yes,” said the stranger. “If you want me.”

She considered this, then nodded. The stranger went on, and she returned to town, carrying the empty jar they had left for her.
