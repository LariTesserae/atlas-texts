The power came up the staircase on foot.

It could have crossed the distance in a moment, but the steps were inhabited. Moss grew in the seams, and on the forty-third tread a bird had made a nest beneath the lip of a railing. The power stepped around it and went on.

At the top, it stopped beside a sump. Three fish were holding still beneath a pipe joint. A drop formed, lengthened and fell; the fish turned. The power watched until another drop had gathered.

A mapkeeper came along the avenue carrying a folded sheet and a pencil behind one ear. She paused when she saw the stranger.

“Are you looking for someone?”

“I came to see this place,” the power said.

“Then you should take water. The courts are longer than they look.”

She offered her flask. The power drank, though it did not need to, and returned it.

They walked together as far as a court whose far wall was barely visible. The mapkeeper explained that it had been a short crossing until the previous week. Her mother had rooms on the other side and could no longer visit without resting twice.

“We’ve put a bench halfway,” she said. “If we can find it again.”

At the entrance to the court, someone had tied a strip of cloth around the railing. On it, in pencil, were the words: **BENCH IN THE DIRECTION OF THE PUMPING STATION.**

“Would you like the court made shorter?” the power asked.

The mapkeeper considered. “I would. The people who moved into it yesterday might not.”

They crossed. The bench was where the note had said, though from it they could see neither end of the court. A grazer lay beside it, its coat brushed to one side by many passing hands.

The power sat down and rested its hand there too. The animal shifted closer.

For many days the power walked the plateau. It learned the sound of felt soles in the courts and ridged rubber on the elevated tracks. It waited beside loading docks for objects to appear and watched people record their dimensions. It went with workers into the bright floors beneath the paving, where they tapped cable trays before lifting them and listened at valves with their heads inclined.

It asked questions, and sometimes the answers disagreed. When a bridge appeared between two towers, one woman described watching it arrive on a crane. Her brother remembered taking shelter beneath it as a boy. Both took the power to the same place and showed it the marks they knew.

The power found it could follow each account into a life: the woman standing in the morning light with her hand on the railing; the boy crouched beneath a bridge while rain beat against its surface. It did not tell either of them which memory to keep.

In one district it met a man whose front door had been sealed into a wall. His cooking things were inside, along with a box of notes from his sister.

“I can open it,” the power said.

The man put his palm against the white surface. “Will the rooms still be there?”

“I don’t know.”

He asked it to wait while he fetched the people who lived above him. They came with lamps and tools, and together they marked the outlines of their rooms on the wall. Only then did he ask the power to make an opening.

The opening led into his kitchen. Everything was where he remembered leaving it, except for one chair, which stood on the other side of the table. He brought out the box of notes and sat down on the step to read one.

Afterward, the power went to the mapkeepers and told them what it had found.

“There are changes that leave people stranded,” it said. “I can help you make a way to call for help, if that is what you want.”

The mapkeepers spoke among themselves. They asked about doors and rooms and people sleeping below the paving. They asked whether the call would bring help at once, and whether it would still work if the avenue around it changed. They asked if it could be used by children.

At last they took the power to a factory whose loading dock stood empty. The workers there listened too. They opened hatches and examined the slow machinery inside. The power stayed with them while they worked, answering what it could and admitting what it could not.

Some days later, a small object appeared on the dock: a white wheel with a handle at its center. When turned, it gave a low, carrying note.

They fitted the first one beside the long court. When the mapkeeper’s mother wished to cross, she turned the handle. People in the neighboring buildings heard it, and two came with a wheeled chair. The grazer followed them as far as the bench.

More wheels appeared, and people chose where to place them: beside difficult stairs, at inspection hatches, near sumps where children played. A wheel did not tell anyone what had happened. It told them only that someone was asking.

The power also offered to keep the old maps safe. The mapkeepers thanked it and showed it the cabinets they already used. What they needed, they said, was dry paper, and more of it.

So the power helped them find a room where the pipes did not drip. A factory began producing paper in long, pale rolls. People brought maps there from households across the plateau, including rough drawings of vanished courts and routes that only one person remembered. Each was marked with its maker’s name and the date, and with whatever else they wanted to say.

The power remained until the wheels had been placed and the paper room was full. Then it returned to the top of the broad staircase.

The mapkeeper came with it. She had a new pencil map in her pocket and a flask of water over her shoulder.

“Will you come back?” she asked.

“If I’m needed.”

She looked out across the roofs. A crane was moving so slowly that she could not tell whether it had begun.

“We’re often needed here,” she said.

The power nodded. It had seen people coming when a wheel sounded, carrying water into a long court, tapping a cable tray before they opened it. It thanked her for showing it the way.

She went back toward the factories. The power descended the stairs carefully, stepping around the bird’s nest on the forty-third tread.
