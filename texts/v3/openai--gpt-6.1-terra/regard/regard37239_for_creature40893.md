For a long time the power did nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

It listened to the evening note through the ridges and through the backs of the people sitting in the alcoves. It followed water into the seams beneath the basins, where the roots held it and the pale animals slept above it. It saw the bowls left at empty shelters, and the hands that cleared their grooves.

In one deep fissure, water had found a new way through the stone. A slab above an inhabited passage had begun, almost imperceptibly, to settle. The next hard rain would carry grit into the opening and press it farther apart. When the wind rose, the slab would fall across the passage leading to the sleeping rooms.

The power could have sealed the crack. But roots reached down through it, and the water that passed along it fed a spring in the next basin. It considered the stone and the water and the people who slept beneath them.

One evening, as the wind turned, the note in the alcove nearest the fissure sounded twice.

A woman sitting there lifted her head. She had heard the stone catch and answer itself before, but never with that small second beat. She put her palm against the wall. After a time she rose and brought her brother, who knew the passages better than anyone in their family.

He felt it too. They fetched a lamp and followed the sound. Where the roof dipped low, he found fresh dust on a shelf and a hairline opening in the stone above it.

By morning they had moved the children and their sleeping mats into a neighboring dwelling. Others came to look. They carried out baskets, tools, and the stores they could reach without staying long beneath the slab. The woman’s brother marked the place with a strip of bark, tied tight around a stone at either end so that the wind could not take it.

Some hoped the roof might hold. It had stood throughout their parents’ lives and longer. They propped it with timber and watched the crack. For three dry days it did not widen.

On the fourth afternoon, rain fell in that hollow. Brown water ran down the seams, and the stone gave a low knock.

The last two people in the passage heard it and turned back. One had gone in for a bowl, the other to help carry it. At the entrance the bowl caught against the wall. They had to set it down and turn it sideways while dust sifted over their hands.

The slab shifted. For a few breaths, the power held its weight.

The two came out with the bowl between them. They had gone only a short way when the roof fell. A rush of air and dust followed them into the trees, and everyone standing nearby covered their faces.

Afterward, the people waited for the dust to settle. They went close enough to see that the passage was blocked, then farther, by another entrance, to make certain no one had been left inside. The dwelling could not be used again. It had been a good one, with a cool room where several families had slept through the hottest months. The people grieved for it.

They found space in other fissures. Over the following weeks they widened one passage, roofed a shallow opening with slabs, and carried the heavy bowl to a shelter above the basin. They set it beneath the edge of a broad stone and cut grooves to lead the morning condensation into it.

That evening, sitting in a different alcove, the woman told her grandchildren about the sound she had heard. She showed them where to put their hands against the wall. They waited until the wind turned, and one of them felt the note first.

Much later, when everyone was asleep, the power passed through the abandoned passage. Water was still moving through the crack above the fallen slab, finding its way toward the spring.

Outside, the bowl was beginning to collect the night’s damp. The power brushed a leaf from one of its grooves.
