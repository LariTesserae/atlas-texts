The power first noticed the hollow because of the noise.

It had heard forests and oceans, the grinding of stones deep beneath mountains, the thin singing of wires. The sound from the hollow was harder to place: an engine and wings and feet, all occupying the same small space without quite keeping time.

It listened for a long while. It learned the difference between the knocking behind the walls and the knocking of a loose bearing. It listened to the sisters at the pumping station disagree about the water. It listened to the children taking the long way home because the shorter street had become a river of beetles.

It also listened to the root that held up the repair shop.

The root had grown around a piece of iron many years before. The iron had rusted away, leaving a pocket where water sometimes settled. In that pocket lived a blind grazer, which had made a comfortable bed of shavings. The root knew the grazer as a persistent tickle. It knew the repair workers by their warmth and the small, repeated pressures of their hands. It knew the engine as a vibration traveling through the floor.

The power spoke to it first.

There were no words. The power made itself known in the manner of water finding a fine crack: a coolness, a slight easing of pressure. The root tightened around the grazer’s pocket. Then, finding nothing being taken from it, it slowly relaxed.

The power asked whether it hurt.

The root answered with the old shape of the iron.

That was not what the power had meant, but it stayed to hear the whole answer.

Above the hollow, the ventilation shaft bent twice before reaching open air. In its upper length, beyond where the fan could easily be heard, debris had collected: dry leaves, a little soil, the husk of something that had once nested there. Roots had grown through it. Air still passed, but less than the people below needed when the furnace was working and the engine ran hard.

The power could have cleared the shaft in an instant. Instead it followed the roots that crossed it and found one connected to the town’s roof. It touched that root and waited. A faint movement traveled through the woven ceiling; several moths shifted their grip.

The power showed the root where the air was trapped. Over the next two days it helped the living strands loosen their hold. The old nest fell apart. Soil slid down the shaft in a thin stream, and the fan blew it out into the hollow.

The repair workers stopped the engine when they saw dust coming through.

They climbed as far up the shaft as they could, pulling themselves along by the brackets. Finding the way above them clearer than they had ever known it, they came down and argued about what might have happened. One worker thought some creature had gone through. Another thought the roots had moved. They fitted a screen below the bend so that if more debris fell, it would not reach the fan.

When they started the engine again, its exhaust went more readily into the bed of fronds, and the air at the far end of the sleeping rooms lost some of its heaviness. People noticed this at supper. They opened their doors and stood in the passage, feeling the new draft along their ankles.

The power stayed.

At the pumping station, one sister had a sore wrist from turning a stiff valve. It had been sore for months; she had learned to use her other hand and had told almost no one. The power followed her home and sat beside her while she slept. It did not mend the wrist outright. It eased the swelling enough that, in the morning, she could turn her hand without waking the pain. Then it waited to see what she would do.

She flexed her wrist, surprised. At work she showed her sister how the valve caught near the end of its turn. Together they took it apart and found a small crescent of root growing through the housing. They called a repair worker, who shifted the pipe and made room for the root to continue without fouling the mechanism.

That evening the sister told three people that her wrist had been troubling her. One of them brought her a strip of cloth to wrap it in; another offered to take a turn at the valve.

The girl with the silver-flecked beetle encountered the power near the repair-shop bench. She was lying on her stomach, watching the beetle attempt to carry a shaving of wood through a gap narrower than the shaving. Each time it failed, it backed up a little and tried again.

The power touched the shaving, turning it so that it would pass through.

The beetle went on its way.

The girl stared at the empty gap. She had felt no draft. She put a fingertip on the floor and waited. After a little while, something touched her fingertip from beneath, as lightly as a beetle’s foot.

She went to fetch her mother. By the time they returned, the beetle had vanished into the stream, and the floor felt as it always did. Her mother stayed with her anyway. They lay side by side under the bench, looking for the silver fleck among the moving backs.

The power watched them. It had intended to make itself known more clearly, perhaps by shaping a sign in the soft floor. But the girl had turned to her mother, and her mother had come. The power left them to their search.

In time the water came again. Moths crowded the dishes, and the engine workers swept burrowers gently from the belts. One of the pumping sisters, checking the cups, noticed that a channel high on the wall had begun to carry water farther to the left than usual. She called her sister over. They watched it together until the thread divided and ran into two cups instead of one.

The power kept the roots above the shaft from catching the fallen debris a second time. It helped the grazed surfaces of the repair-shop root close over where the mouths had gone too deep. It learned which growth the people cut and which they left alone, and where the burrowers needed the floor to remain soft.

When the dishes were empty, the neighbors opened their doors. A burrower had fallen asleep under the engine worker’s coat. It clung there, its legs folded into the lining, while she carried the coat outside and tried to persuade it onto a leaf. Two children offered advice. A third ran for a better leaf. Soon the passage was crowded, everyone speaking close to one another and touching shoulders to make room.

At last the animal climbed off. The worker held the leaf low while it found its way into a crack.

Then the fan began to turn, and the draft moved through the open doors.
