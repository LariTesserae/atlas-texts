The power came during rain and stayed long enough to learn the sounds.

At first it heard what anyone might have heard from the upper terraces: machinery, birds, water, the long scrape of a herd crossing stone. In time it distinguished the presses in one workshop from those in another, and a loose cover on a drain that rattled only when the water ran high. It learned the calls of the birds at the eastern reservoir, including the short, rough call made by an adult whose chick had strayed.

It watched the people who maintained the channels. They knew where the deposits formed fastest and which old bolts could still be turned. They knew, too, that a wall cultivated for feed could not be scraped bare without making work for someone else farther down the system. The power found no wisdom in making the water stop carrying mineral, or in making the animals keep to places convenient for traffic. Both would have changed more than they appeared to change.

Below the eastern terraces, a grazer with a torn fold of hide over one leg had begun to lag behind its herd. The wound had caught against the broken end of a gate. It was not deep, but it stayed wet and had grown sore. The animal rested frequently, and a calf waited with it, feeding on a narrow strip of film beneath a loading platform.

A woman who cleaned the platform drains noticed them. She brought water in a shallow pan and set it near the animal, then went back to work. The grazer drank after she had moved away. The next morning she brought water again and left the gate to the service yard open, so that the two animals could rest somewhere wider than the passage.

The power could have closed the wound at once. It did so, in the hours before dawn, while the grazer lay with its leg stretched out on the warm pavement. It also turned the broken end of the gate inward, away from the passage. In the morning the woman found the animal standing, the torn hide already joined by a narrow, clean seam. She looked at it for some time. Then she brought the pan of water anyway.

The herd returned that afternoon. The grazer and calf joined it, and the woman closed the service-yard gate behind them.

A few days later, the same woman found a crack in the masonry beside a drain. It was partly hidden by the cream-colored deposits. When she tapped the wall with her tool, a piece of crust fell away, and clear water came through the opening.

She reported it. The repair crew had other work, and the wall appeared sound enough from the street. They marked the place for inspection.

That night the power followed the water behind the wall. A slow leak had washed fine material from beneath the old foundations. There was a hollow there, larger than the crack suggested, and above it lay a street used by trams and by a herd traveling between two feeding grounds.

The power held the wall in place.

In the morning, water emerged through the crack in a steady stream. The repair crew came sooner than planned. They opened the pavement, found the hollow and stopped the trams. By evening they had fenced the street and laid temporary pipes to carry the water away from the excavation.

For eleven days the power held the wall while the crew worked. They brought stone and fill, found another damaged section farther along, and argued over whether the old drain should be replaced entirely. The tram drivers learned a diversion. The herd reached the fence on its first morning and stood there for a long time, calves pressed among the adults, until workers opened a broad route through an adjoining yard. After that, the animals used it readily.

When the new foundations had taken the weight, the power let go. No one felt the moment. A worker was tightening a pipe joint; another was eating from a tin on the edge of the excavation. Above them, rain fell through the open roof of an old warehouse, where the reservoir birds were beginning to nest.

The power remained in the basin after the repairs were finished. It listened when people complained of work that could not be kept up, and when they spoke with pride of a pump that had run for years without failing. It watched animals seek shade beside the warm walls and birds struggle to lift themselves from crowded water. Sometimes it helped in ways a person might notice; sometimes it did work for which no hands were available.

It did not make the basin silent or still. The channels continued to gather deposits. Gates needed repair. There were nights when someone sat beside a sick relative, and mornings when a dead bird had to be lifted from a sluice. The power could not make those labors mean nothing, and did not wish to.

But when the woman who cleaned the drains grew old, the route through the adjoining yard was still in use. She could hear the herd passing below her window: the softer scrape of the adults, the quicker feet among them, and once, after a pause, the hollow steps of an animal walking alone to catch up.
