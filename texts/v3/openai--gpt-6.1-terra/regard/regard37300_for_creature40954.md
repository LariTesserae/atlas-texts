The power came to the rim in the evening, when the works had stopped and the first dampness was gathering on the frames. It did not take a shape. There was no need for one.

For a long time it attended to the town. It followed water down through the walls and light across the roofs. It felt the old iron where roots had tightened around it, and the places where the iron had begun to thin. It listened to the people inside their houses: an argument about a misplaced knife, a child describing something found beneath a roller, a woman coughing after the green dust had settled on her sleeves.

It attended to the roots as well, though there were no words in them to hear. The roots had lived through dry seasons and wet ones. They had been cut and bent and had grown around what they could not move. The power found the places where they were storing water, and the smaller places where they had none to spare.

It could have filled every pocket in the walls. By the next morning the leaves would have opened wider than anyone had seen them open before. Shoots would have climbed over windows and thickened in doorways; the machinery might have stopped with a vine swelling in its rollers. The water would eventually have gone, and the town would still have needed water after that.

So it waited through the night and watched where the rain fell beyond the rim.

In the morning, one of the older workers woke with the memory of a shallow runnel on the far side of the bank. She had known it as a girl. In wet weather, water had gathered there before sinking among the stems. She had not thought of it in years.

After her shift she went to look. A boy who had been helping at the works came with her, carrying a spade because she asked him to. The runnel was still there, though nearly covered. They cleared a short length and found the ground damp beneath it.

They did not dig a channel into the town that day. The worker wanted to know whether there was enough fall, and where the water would go once it arrived. Over the next week, she and several others walked the bank after each brief rain. They marked the dampest places with stakes. One evening they brought a level from the works and set it on the ground beside the square.

The power kept the weather as it was. It helped them notice what was already there: a dark line in the soil, the direction a puddle emptied, the old iron trough lying beneath the runners behind a house. The trough had once caught water from a roof. With a little patching, it could do so again.

By the time they began to dig, nearly everyone in town had come out to see where the channel would run. They argued over the best route. One man wanted it beside the works; the people who lived there objected, because their doorways were damp enough already. They settled on a line that ended near the square, with a gate they could lift when the ground needed water and close when it had enough.

The roots in the channel were cut back. The cuts closed slowly. Some of the severed lengths were taken to the works, and some were laid in the open to dry. A child found two many-legged creatures in the wet soil and carried them on the spade to a seam in the bank.

The power did one thing the people could not have done themselves. Under the square, where the engine shook the ground, an old iron brace had cracked almost through. Each day the vibration widened it a little. The roots around it had grown thick, but they could not hold its two ends together if it gave way.

One night, while the engine was still, the power joined the iron. It left a pale seam at the break. Years later, when someone uncovered the brace for an inspection, they would find the seam and wonder who had made it. By then they would have enough iron to reinforce the whole frame.

The first rain after the channel was finished was light. Most of it passed beyond the basin. The people opened the gate anyway and waited. A thin stream came down from the bank, entered the channel, and stopped short of the square. They left the gate open until evening. By morning the water had reached the end, leaving the soil dark along one side.

The power remained a little longer. It watched the worker who had remembered the runnel wash her hands at the trough. She rubbed the green dust from the creases in her palms, then tipped what was left of the water onto a dry patch beside the path.

The roofs turned toward the light. At the works, someone checked the belts and let the roof rise.

Then the power departed. The channel would need clearing, and the gate would sometimes stick. The people knew how to tend both.
