The power first attends to the pauses.

It hears the pump stop and the belt go on turning. It hears a worker in an upper room set down a cup, then pick it up again. In the brick, a root presses against a grain of mortar.

There is water beneath the settlement, more than the little seam provides. Much of it is too hot or too bitter to drink. One deeper pocket is cool and clean, but the rock between it and the chamber bears part of the chamber’s weight. A careless passage through it would bring down the roof.

The power knows how to reach the water. It also knows that the people here have chosen their settlement for reasons it has not yet learned.

So it waits until the pump has stopped and the workers are listening for the valve. Then, from the empty pipe above the settling basin, a clear drop falls.

Another follows.

The machinist looks up. The pipe has not carried water for three days; it was disconnected for repair. She fetches a lamp and finds a line of dampness on its underside. Someone else brings a cup. Written in the moisture, in letters that gather and hold their shape, are the words:

**There is more water below. May I show you where?**

The machinist calls the others. A builder touches the letters and tastes the damp on his finger. The person who keeps the water records asks whether anyone has been working on the pipe. No one has.

They talk for most of the evening. Some are afraid. Some think the message is a trick, though none can say whose. The pump workers want to know how much water, and whether drawing it will empty the seam they already use. The builders want to know what will happen to the roof.

At last the machinist stands beneath the pipe.

“Show us,” she says. “But don’t start digging yet.”

The next day, marks appear on the chamber floor: a dotted line of moisture running from the lower sump to a place behind the workshops. It stops at a brick pier. Beside it, more words form:

**The pier must be strengthened first.**

The builders examine the pier and find a crack they had missed, thin as a hair beneath the mineral dust. They spend a week arguing over the work and another two weeks doing it. The power helps when asked. It can hold a stone in place while a brace is fitted; it can make a dangerous fault visible on a cut face. It does not take the tools from anyone’s hands.

The shaft they dig is narrow, and the work is hot. The power carries away some of the heat, though it cannot do so without drawing air through the upper passages; the people ask it to stop at night, when the draft reaches their beds.

When the drill finally enters the pocket, the water rises slowly and spills over the lip of the sump. The workers let it run into a clean vessel. They test it, and test it again. Only after several days do they connect a pipe to the public tap.

There is enough now to wash without counting every cup. The cooling tank can be filled before it is entirely empty. Someone suggests a second basin, deep enough for the children to put their feet in. Someone else suggests a bathhouse. These plans take longer, because space must be found for them and the people disagree about which wall can be spared.

The shrub’s seam still needs repair. A builder kneels beside it with the machinist and works mortar carefully around the thicker roots. They leave a gap and fit a small metal plate over it, so that the wall can be inspected later. The shrub loses one branch. The machinist puts it in a jar by her shutter, where it remains green for a surprisingly long time.

The power stays within hearing for a while. It is asked to find a dropped bolt in the sump, to settle a dispute about a pump bearing, and once to tell a man whether a letter he has written will make his sister forgive him. It finds the bolt. It points out a worn place in the bearing. To the last question it gives no answer, and after a time the man sends the letter anyway.

Years later, when the pump pauses, people can still hear the belt and the doors and the workers moving overhead. They can also hear water passing steadily through the new pipe.

The patch of daylight crosses the far wall as before. It reaches the basin, now broad enough to hold a reflection of the ceiling, and moves on.
