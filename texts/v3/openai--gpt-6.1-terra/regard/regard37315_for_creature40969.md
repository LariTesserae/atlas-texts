The power came to the rim and saw the basin as it appeared in the white light. Then it looked into the joints.

It took some time to learn what a drop meant. Not because the drop was difficult to follow, but because its passage lasted longer beneath the stone than it did in the air. The power watched one strike a tower basin and spread through the clay below. It watched the first fold take water, and the next yield slightly to its pressure, and a third, farther away, remain unchanged until the stone cooled.

It watched for many such passages. A fold swelled, withdrew, and swelled again. Sometimes a neighboring body adjusted; sometimes it did not. The power could see what happened in the bodies, but was careful about what it called their experience. It did not need to know whether they loved the water to see that they lived by it.

On an upper terrace a chip had fallen across a narrow channel. Above the chip, water collected in a shallow widening. Below it, three inhabitants had tightened against the walls of their joints. One had already become a pale film. Another still held water in a fold that reached toward a seam on the shaded side of the block, where a little dampness remained.

The power could have moved the chip at once. The waiting water would then have run down the channel in a single rush. It would have entered the dry clay quickly, and the pressure might have torn the film from its chamber before it could soften.

Instead, the power lifted one edge of the chip by less than the thickness of a hair.

Water passed beneath it, wetting a short stretch of the channel. Some sank into the clay. When that had been taken in, the power lifted the chip a little farther. It waited again.

By the time water reached the lower joint, the pale film had softened. Its folds began to open. The power left the chip where it was, held just clear of the channel floor: enough water could pass, and the small pool above would remain for those who had come to depend on it.

Elsewhere, there were fractures beginning beneath two of the towers. A block in one foundation had split, though the weight above kept both halves in place. In another season, or several seasons, the split would widen. The tower might settle and close the damp spaces beneath its collecting basin.

The power entered the fracture without widening it. It drew dissolved mineral from water moving nearby and laid it slowly along the broken faces. The repair took years. During that time the tower continued to give its drops to the basin below, and the bodies under it continued to thicken and diminish with the changing damp.

There were other things the power might have done. It might have filled every dry socket and kept every joint wet. It might have lifted the mineral crusts from the oldest folds under the central water, or opened broad channels between inhabitants who had never touched.

It did none of these at once. Drying had its place in their lives, and the crusts held more than they trapped. Even a new channel would carry pressure, fragments, and water away from one place as well as toward another. The power watched the unfinished wall through a long dry interval, and when a season came in which too little condensation gathered, it cooled the underside of one projecting stone. A few more drops fell into the socket below.

After that it remained near the basin, though nothing could be seen of it from the rim. It did not keep the water at one level or prevent every loss. A fragment still might dry in a seam before a wet season reached it; a fold still might be sealed beneath a deposit. But when a change threatened to take more than it gave, the power was there to notice.

At the last step, water rested beneath the sky. In the joint below, a fold opened against the stone. Much later, as the light moved off the step, a neighboring fold yielded to it.
