The power came at night, when the upper edges of the ribs were cold enough to gather water.

It did not come as a light or a voice. There was already more than enough sound in the room. It attended first to what moved beneath the sound: the strain passing through the bowl, the slow opening of a bud, a pale young animal holding to the felt while a beetle walked past it.

Far below, a bearing in the great fan had begun to fail. Its surface was scored and growing hot. It might have continued turning for years, or it might have broken before the next rain. Either way, its work was nearly done.

The power could have stopped the engines. It could also have made them new.

Instead, it learned their long changes of note, and the way those changes traveled upward through the iron. It followed the sound into the roots of the vines and down the stiff hairs beneath the animals’ bodies. It learned, too, how much of the tower could fall without reaching the room, and which single seam, if it opened, would bring the whole green colonnade down.

Before dawn, it repaired the bearing. At the failing seam it drew the iron close, so that the two surfaces held one another again. It left the old scallops in the ribs, and the ringing of their hollow ends.

On the shaded side of each felt sleeve, the power loosened a few fibers and tucked them back to form small pockets. The next young to hatch there would have places to take hold when the dry wind rose. Some would still be carried away. There was no room on the bowl for all of them to grow, and the things that fed on their dust were living too.

The power considered the animals in the center. One lay with its rim over another’s back; beneath them, in the narrow dark, were two eggs. There had been little rain that season. The adults were thin.

By morning, a new surface had appeared on the inward face of one rib: a line of fine, shallow grooves, beginning high above the vines and ending at the bowl. It looked much like the work of water over a very long time. That night the grooves gathered a little more condensation than the smooth iron around them and led it down, past the felt sleeves, to the floor.

A drop reached the bowl after sunrise. One animal lifted its rim and spread its branches. Another, farther upslope, turned toward the wet line.

The power remained for a while. The fan’s deepest note was swelling, and the animals were beginning to gather. A lizard came down from the ribs and stood at the edge of the bowl, waiting for a moth that had fallen there. The moth was still alive. One of the animals reached it first.

The lizard waited a little longer, then went back to the leaves.
