The power came first to the edge of the plateau, where the country below was hidden.

It could have cleared the haze. It could have stilled the metallic dust and made the whole expanse visible at once. Instead it followed the dust as it moved: down one shallow channel and up another, through the leaning slabs, past the entrance of a dome. It stayed long enough to see that a stone apparently alone on the plain sheltered a copper growth, and that some of the growth was inside the stone.

When a seam opened beneath it, the exposed branches pulled apart. Dust poured into the gap. One half of the growth remained on either side.

The power waited through the closing of the seam and through many seasons afterward. Both halves continued to live.

There were things it learned, and things it could not learn by looking. It could trace the buried threads of a growth for kilometers through the stone. It could see where they had been severed, and where a trace of salt or gas moved into them from the surrounding mineral. But it could not tell whether the growth knew when it had been torn, or whether the two halves that remained were, in any sense that mattered to them, one being or two.

So it did not put them back together.

It watched the grazers and the gliders, too. Beneath a rim of the plateau, in a recess where the high currents seldom reached, it found young gliders clinging to a wall of stone. Their wings were still folded and soft. An adult rested nearby, its pale body almost indistinguishable from the rock.

There were travelers on the plateau when the power arrived. One had been moving between shelters for eleven days, counting distances by the marks of earlier visitors and by a map already wrong in several places. A seam had raised a ridge across the route to the next dome. From the top, the traveler saw a storm coming and no shelter in reach.

The power could have carried the traveler to safety. It could have turned the storm aside.

Instead, a little ahead of the traveler, dust began to collect on a patch of ground where the wind had scoured everything else bare. The grains formed a line, then another line branching from it. The traveler stopped and watched. After a time the lines settled into the shape of the nearby ridges, with a clear space marked behind one of them.

The traveler looked toward the storm, then followed the indicated route.

Behind the ridge was a narrow overhang, not a comfortable place but deep enough to lie beneath. The traveler crawled inside and waited while dust pressed past the opening. For two days the storm continued. The traveler slept, woke, drank carefully, and slept again.

During that time, the power held a loose slab above the overhang. It was not certain the slab would have fallen. When the storm passed and the traveler had gone, it set the slab down more securely on the ledge behind.

Later, the traveler returned with two others and marked the overhang on their maps. They stacked stones at the turn in the route. A year afterward, a shifting seam separated the stacks, and someone corrected the marks.

The power attended to the old domes. It found that some had failed, while others still kept their small chambers of dense air. In one, the clean patch on the floor had begun to shrink. The power examined the work beneath the stone as carefully as it could. Much of it remained beyond even its understanding: not because the making was greater than the power, but because its makers had chosen purposes and means that the power had never encountered.

It repaired what it understood. It left the rest alone.

At the three leaning slabs, a glider landed one evening and folded its wings. It stood for a long time in the even bed of dust, picking up grazers where their tracks converged. The power had considered making another shelter there for travelers, but now it watched the glider, and later the grazers returning beneath the slabs. It chose a place farther away.

Over many years it made several small shelters, each within a day’s walk of another. They were not domes like the old ones. Their entrances faced away from the strongest currents, and inside each was a shelf on which water and food could be left. The power put nothing on the shelves. Travelers eventually did.

At one shelter, copper growths spread into the protected entrance. A traveler scraping dust from a boot damaged them and then, noticing, moved farther inside to finish. The growths continued along the wall.

The power did not remain always at the edge of the plateau. It went among the stones and into the high currents, and sometimes away altogether. Before leaving, it listened to the faint impacts of grains against the shelters and the ridges and the grazing creatures folded tight against the ground.

The storms still crossed the plain. The seams still opened. Copper branches closed before the dust arrived and unrolled afterward, often buried at their edges. In the new shelters, travelers rested beside supplies left by people they had never met. High above them, pale wings held steady against the racing curtains.
