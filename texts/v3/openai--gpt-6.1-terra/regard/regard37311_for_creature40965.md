The power attended first to the mouse, which had pinned a beetle against the ledge.

It did not punish the mouse. The mouse was hungry, and it had found food in the place it knew. But the beetle was struggling, and the power attended to that as well.

A soft larva appeared beneath the mouse’s nose, smelling as any larva should. The mouse released the beetle and ate. The beetle crawled into a crack. The larva had never been alive.

That was a beginning, not a solution. By nightfall the mouse would be hungry again, and there were spiders waiting in their webs, and bats waking above them to fly out over a valley full of insects. There were mites in the damp droppings, living on things too small for the mouse to notice. The power could distinguish them all.

It made more food where food was needed: suitable things in the droppings and under the stones, along the ledges and in the sheltered cracks. In the valley, insects would find flowers a little longer in bloom, and bats would find what nourished them without having to take a struggling creature into their mouths. The food was real enough to sustain a body and return to the soil, though it had no life of its own.

The power made water more reliable in the chamber. It did not flood the dry ribs where other animals sheltered, but kept several small basins from failing at once. It made narrow ways around the places where webs were stretched, so that an animal seeking water need not always pass close to a waiting spider. For the spiders, too, there was food.

It did these things without requiring any creature to understand. The mouse still turned over flakes of stone. The bats still flew out at dusk, and the crickets still called from separate cracks. A spider could repair its web; a beetle could choose between the dark damp layers and the warmer surface. The animals met, mated, raised young, and died. But there was less sickness, less thirst, and much less terror in their lives. When death came, the power was present and made it gentle.

It watched what followed. More food might bring more young than the chamber could hold; a lasting pool might change the warmth of a neighboring crevice. The power could make room beyond the chamber too, and could tend those changes as they came. Its care extended down the slopes with the bats, and into the soil beneath the flowers, because the chamber had never been a world by itself.

The next afternoon, the blade of daylight reached the upper wall. Water beaded on the ceiling. Somewhere below, the mouse found another larva under a stone and ate it. The cricket in the nearest crack went on calling.
