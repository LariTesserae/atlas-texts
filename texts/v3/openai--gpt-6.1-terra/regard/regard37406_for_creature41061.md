The power came in the dry season, when people were measuring the cisterns every morning.

It found plenty to admire. On an upper terrace a child had set three broken tiles around a trickle from a pipe, making a pool shallow enough for insects to drink from without drowning. Far below, a pump tender had repaired a worn bearing with metal cut from the hinge of a door that had not opened in forty years. Someone had planted beans beside the lift stop, where the cage’s passage stirred their leaves.

The power also saw a retaining wall above a busy walkway. Its face carried old repairs, three generations of them, and a fig had grown out over the top. The wall workers had marked a crack in chalk and dated it. The mark was a year old; the crack had widened very little.

Behind the face of the wall, where no one could see, water from a leaking pipe had been washing fine material out of the fill. The wall was holding more weight than it had been made to hold. On the next very hot day, a plate of iron buried in it would lengthen, and several blocks would shift. The fig would come down with them.

The power could have mended the wall without anyone knowing. It considered this. There were other walls with buried iron and leaking pipes. There were people who knew how to find them, if given a reason to look.

That afternoon, a woman nobody recognized arrived at the nearest lift stop carrying a basket of tools. She asked where she might find the wall workers.

They were eating in the shade of a screen. The youngest pointed her toward the foreman, who listened while she described the crack, the buried plate, and the washed-out fill.

“Who sent you?” he asked.

“No one. I came because I think it may fall tomorrow.”

He looked at her basket. It held a hammer, a length of chalk, and a roll of paper.

“You’ve opened it up?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

The woman took a moment to answer. “I can see inside it.”

The youngest worker laughed. The foreman did not. He had been up since dawn and had little patience left for visitors, but the woman’s account of the pipe was precise, and she had named an old kind of iron plate that he had once found in another wall.

He took two workers and went with her. They tapped the blocks and listened. They found the pipe, half hidden under the fig’s roots, and saw that the earth beneath it was wet. The foreman sent the youngest worker for the maintenance records. While they waited, the woman stood back and watched ants crossing the chalk line.

The records showed a plate where she had said it would be. By evening, the walkway had been closed, the pipe shut off, and the households above the wall asked to carry water from the next outlet. There was irritation about this, especially from a man who had just planted seedlings in a long roof bed. He wanted to know how long the repair would take. Nobody could tell him yet.

In the morning, the sun grew strong. A block shifted with a noise like a cupboard being dragged across a floor. Dust came out of the crack. The workers had already moved the lift queue to another landing; they stood under the awning and watched the fig tremble.

Then the plate gave way.

The woman put her hand against the wall. The falling blocks slowed enough for the two workers on the upper path to get clear. One later said he had felt the whole terrace lean beneath him, as if a great weight had been caught and held. By the time the dust settled, the fig lay across the closed walkway, its roots and a long section of masonry beside it.

The workers stared at the woman. She was still standing with her hand raised, though the wall was gone from under it.

After a while, the foreman asked, “Will the rest come down?”

“Not today,” she said.

He sat on an overturned bucket. “You could have told us who you were.”

“I wasn’t sure you would believe me.”

He looked at the fallen tree, then back at her. “I’m not sure now.”

The repair took most of the season. People argued about the cost, the loss of the fig, and whether the new wall should leave room for another tree. The pump tenders found two more leaks in old lines. Wall workers began checking the records for buried plates, and residents showed them cracks they had been watching for years.

The woman stayed to help. She lifted blocks when asked, though she was careful to lift them slowly, and she learned to mix mortar the way the masons wanted it. She answered questions about the hidden parts of the valley, but she did not choose which work should be done first. For that, people met beneath the orchard and laid out their plans on the ground.

The man with the seedlings lost several before the water was turned back on. His neighbors gave him replacements. A mason took cuttings from the fallen fig and put them in pots behind his room; one rooted. The pigeons that had nested in the wall found ledges elsewhere, and for a while their twigs were a nuisance on the path to the lift.

When the work was finished, the woman left her hammer with the wall crew. By then the chalk marks had been washed from the old blocks, and new ones could be seen on other terraces, each with a date written carefully beside it.
