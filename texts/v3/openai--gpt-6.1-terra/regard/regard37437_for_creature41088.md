In the ninth year after a seep began to weaken, the people of the house above it took down the rear wall to look for the water. They found damp stone, a pocket of ice, and a crack too narrow for a hand. They left the wall open for three days. Each morning the ice had grown a little; each afternoon it melted into a bowl.

That autumn the bowl filled more slowly. The family carried water from a neighboring court, and those neighbors in turn asked for help at a cistern farther down the slope. There was enough for the winter, if it was measured carefully.

The power had been watching the plateau for longer than any house had stood there. It knew where the water went beneath the ridges, and where it would emerge, and how long the people would have to wait for it. It could have called the water back into the old crack. But the old crack had begun to close under the weight of the slope, and there were other families downstream who depended on the same water.

Instead, one morning, words appeared on the inside of the nearly empty bowl. They were made of frost, though the room was warm enough that the children had taken off their outer coats.

The youngest child saw them first and called her grandmother. Her grandmother held the bowl up to the light. She could not read, and neither could the child, but both understood what the words said.

*I know where water can be found. Would you like me to show you?*

The grandmother set the bowl down. She went outside and called the neighbors, who came with their coats half-fastened and stood around the hearth looking into it. One man said someone had played a trick. A woman touched the frost with her fingertip; the marks vanished under her skin, then slowly formed again.

They sent for the person in the next court who could read. She looked at the bowl and said that the marks were not letters she knew, but that they said, *I know where water can be found. Would you like me to show you?*

They talked for a long time. Some wanted to carry the bowl away from the house. Some wanted to ask what would be required in return.

At last the grandmother leaned close to the rim and asked.

*Nothing,* the frost said.

“Who are you?”

*Someone who can help.*

“And will you take the water from another house?”

*No.*

They asked the bowl to wait while they sent runners to the houses above and below them. By evening, people from four courts had gathered, along with two herders who had come in with the last animals. They brought questions about the grazing hollows, the old cisterns, and a spring that had failed beyond the western ridge.

The bowl answered each question it could. When it could not make an answer short enough to fit, the frost melted and appeared again in a different arrangement. Someone brought a larger vessel.

At dawn they went out together. A line of white frost lay on the stones ahead of them, turning around patches where a foot might slip. It led them to a fold in the ridge they all knew. Children played there in summer, and the herders sometimes sheltered behind it when the wind came from the north. The frost ended at a patch of gravel beside a large flat stone.

They dug. The ground was hard, and they had to stop twice to warm their hands. Beneath the gravel they found a thin layer of dark soil, then a bed of fractured rock. Water gathered in the bottom of the hole.

There was not much at first. They widened the hole and lined it with stone. By the next morning it held enough to fill several vessels.

Over the following days the frost showed them two more places. One lay close to a settlement whose cistern had been low for years. The other was out among the grazing trails, where a shallow basin could be built for animals as well as people. The herders argued about its position, moved it farther from a dangerous descent, and built it there.

The work occupied many hands. People came from other ridges to see, and stayed to carry stone or ask questions. Some distrusted the frost even after drinking the water. The bowl answered them as readily as it answered anyone else.

Before leaving, the power showed them something more: a fault high above the first settlement, where a shelf of stone had begun to separate from the mountainside. The crack was already visible, but from below it looked like many other cracks.

The people cleared the houses beneath it and moved their stores. They brought out bedding, tools, children, and an old dog who could no longer manage the steps. In the middle of winter the shelf fell. It broke three empty houses and blocked a lane. No one was underneath.

When spring came, they began to rebuild farther along the slope. They used what stone they could recover, though some of the largest slabs had split and were fit only for foundations. Birds nested in the new rubble, and the children were told which parts of it were safe to climb.

The bowl remained in the grandmother’s house. For a while people came to it with every kind of question: where a lost knife had gone, whether a child’s cough would worsen, which herd would reach a distant hollow first. Sometimes frost appeared. Sometimes it did not.

Years later, when the grandmother died, her family washed the bowl and passed it to her youngest granddaughter, who used it to gather rain from the roof. It held water as well as any other bowl.
