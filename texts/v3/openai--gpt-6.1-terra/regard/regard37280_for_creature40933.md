The power came to the low country during a rain that had not yet fallen.

It found the place by the sound of stones knocking beneath the pools. The sound had traveled a long way and had changed on the way, so that what reached the power was not quite a call. Still, it turned its attention toward the country and listened.

For a time it did nothing else.

It listened to a bird pulling a runner from the moss and to the remaining runners settling against the stone. It listened to water rising in a hollow and to the roots that slowly gave ground beneath it. It followed the mineral threads through peat and rock, into places where their faint currents crossed and parted, and found that there was no single point at which to address the marsh.

The power could have joined the threads. It could have held them together after the rain had passed, making a continuous pathway where now there were only brief crossings. It understood, after listening longer, that this might destroy some of what it had come to meet. The silences between showers allowed certain growths to form. A grove that attended to the western pools in the morning might need the afternoon for its own water and roots. Even the disagreements between basins left their traces in the ground, and those traces sometimes became useful years later.

So the power left the threads as they were.

In the western part of the country, beneath a shelf of ribbed stone, water had begun to collect in a seam. It entered from a channel some distance away, one that would not open until a storm arrived three days hence. The seam was narrow, and the water came through slowly. A few drops escaped onto the moss.

The grove above the shelf had already responded. Its hanging growths contracted, and its roots drew tight around the stone. Farther east, leaves closed over an empty place among the reeds.

The power followed these responses into the days ahead. The storm would open many connections at once. The seam would widen, and the shelf would slip into the basin. Water would surge through the pale trunks, carrying peat, branches, nests, and runners into an opening below the ground. Some of what went down would return elsewhere. Much would not.

There were other possible courses. The power saw one in which the stone held, but the trapped water forced a passage beneath the eastern basins instead. It saw another in which the grove survived and a broad stand of reeds dried out. In still another, a bird lifted two young from its nest before the surge and could not return in time for the third.

The power considered these for a long while. Then it made a small change.

Near the seam, it loosened a grain of black stone and let a drop pass through. The drop fell onto a filament in the moss. Across the basin, a shrub lifted one of its branches from the water.

Nothing more happened that day.

The next morning the power loosened another grain. A thin trickle ran out beneath the shelf, too small to alter the water gathering inside. It reached a patch of moss whose filaments had been separated by dry weather. For a few minutes the strands were joined. Pressure passed along them into the grove.

The hanging growths contracted. Their droplets fell. Roots shifted in the peat, and the shrub lowered its branch into the trickle again.

By evening, water was reaching the grove by two routes. The roots did not release the stone; they held it more firmly. But a little way downhill, others began to open a channel through the peat.

The power waited beside the channel. It could not tell whether the movement was an answer to its own act, or simply the grove’s way of responding to water. It did not need to know.

On the second day a bird arrived at the empty place in the eastern reeds. It stepped among the closed leaves, clicked softly, and tested several twigs. Before nightfall it had begun a nest.

The storm reached the western hills on the third day. Its first rain crossed the marsh in a slanting gray sheet. The channel from the future opened beneath the shelf, and water crowded into the seam.

The roots held. For a while the small channel through the peat carried the overflow. Then the water rose faster than it could escape.

The power touched the stone again. This time it held the shelf in place.

Under that weight the threads remained in contact. Water moved among them, carrying pressure from the seam through the grove and out across the basin. Reeds bent. Moss drew tight against the peat. A section of bank gave way where the new channel met an older runnel, and the water found room to spread.

The power kept its hold through the heaviest rain. It could feel the stone trying to slide and the roots pulling against it. It could also feel the bed beneath the grove beginning to move. If the shelf stayed exactly where it was, the pressure would continue to build.

Slowly, it allowed the stone to settle.

The roots stretched. The shelf dropped the width of a leaf and stopped. Water spilled over its edge, tore a gap in the peat below, and rushed down the channel the grove had opened. Several hanging growths were stripped from the pale trunks. One nest fell into the water; the bird that had built it rose through the rain and circled low over the basin.

The surge reached the eastern reeds after dark. Their roots loosened, and a long section of the bank drifted away. The bird on the new nest climbed to a higher stem and remained there until morning. Its nest was gone when it returned, but the reeds around the empty place had begun to lift.

By dawn the seam was draining. The shelf rested in a new position, with water running openly beneath it. The grove’s roots would grow around the stone again, though some had torn and would not mend. The fallen nest lodged among shrubs downstream. Its bird found it there and, after probing the wet twigs, began to pull them apart.

The power released the shelf.

It remained through several more showers, watching the channel change. Runners entered it from the lower pools and grazed on the newly exposed stone. The moss took up minerals from the water and laid down fine threads along both banks. Some reached the grove; others dissolved or broke as the peat shifted.

In a later rain, the grove’s hanging growths contracted before a stone fell far to the south. Their droplets struck the leaves below, and a narrow-bodied creature slipped from one drop into a pool. For a moment the water showed the western shelf, though the shelf was miles away. Then the reflection broke, and the creature went on beneath the moss.

The power listened to the country a while longer. There were other dangers in it, and other lives continuing among them. The birds still hunted. Banks still failed. In one basin, roots had begun to close a channel that another basin was trying to open.

At last the power withdrew its attention. It left no new pathway across the country, only the altered shelf and the channel the water had found. In dry weather the channel became a chain of shallow pools. When rain returned, it filled, and the filaments along its banks carried changes from one end to the other for as long as the water joined them.
