The power first noticed the basin because a train horn sounded where there was no train.

It came from the northern wall, below the cloud, and was answered twice by the workshops. The railway keeper checked the line. No engine was missing, and the crews could account for every horn sounded that morning.

The power listened for a while. It found the trains and their echoes, the presses, the frogs and the people who lifted the cistern covers to let children see them. It found, too, the thin places between streets, where a bird might lose sight of its flock and a bucket might leave its water behind.

Beneath the northern flats, one of the sliding plates had begun to bend. A seam was opening under it, very slowly, and would continue to open until the plate caught against its runner. After that the building would rise unevenly. The staircase would pull away from the third landing. There were people asleep on the upper floors during the day.

The power could have closed the seam. It could have held the building level. It looked farther down and found that the seam carried warmth toward a pond where fish had gathered for many winters. Closing it would cool the pond, though the power could not tell how quickly, or whether the fish would find another place.

So it went to the flats and put a pressure against the brickwork, just strong enough to be felt.

The apprentice on the morning inspection felt it under his hand. He stood still, then called his aunt, who had taught him to listen to the foundations. She came down with a cup of tea and put her own hand beside his.

They went below with lamps. At first the runners looked clean. Then the aunt laid a straightedge across the plate and saw the narrow gap beneath its middle. She sent the apprentice upstairs to knock on doors.

By noon, people were coming out with blankets, cooking pots, documents, cages of birds and children too sleepy to walk. A woman brought the doorframe on which her family’s heights were marked; two neighbors helped her carry it to the square. The foundation crew set jacks beneath the building and argued over where to place the next pair. Someone went for the engineer from the adjacent ward.

A long-bodied animal had made a nest under the bottom step. When the crew began lifting the stair, it ran out carrying one of its young. It came back twice more. The workers paused each time, and afterward one of them moved the remaining nest material under a pipe bridge, where it would stay dry.

By evening the building stood empty and supported. The engineer said it might be possible to replace the bent plate, though they would have to wait for the seam to finish opening. The families were given rooms in a school and in the homes of neighbors. It was crowded, and several people had gone to work without the things they needed. The woman with the doorframe leaned it against a wall in the school corridor, where children from other families began measuring themselves against it.

For a few days the power stayed near the northern district. It learned how much strain the jacks could take and eased the movement of the rock while the crew worked. When the seam broke through the ground, pale shoots appeared along it. The children at the school watched them open into dark fans.

The pond remained warm. The flats were repaired. The apprentice began checking for a gap beneath every straightedge, even where the plates looked sound.

The power continued around the basin after that, paying attention. It did not settle the distances between streets; people and animals both made use of them, though not always safely. At a sharp passage beside the freight depot, where two panes of glass had broken in one week, it left a patch of white mineral dust that shifted whenever the passage opened. The depot workers noticed and put the warning on their ward board.

The dust stayed there. Rain washed some of it away, and a worker brought more from the new seam when it grew thin. After a time the warning no longer needed a date. Children learned to look at the patch before turning into the lane, and to take the longer route when its surface had been disturbed.
