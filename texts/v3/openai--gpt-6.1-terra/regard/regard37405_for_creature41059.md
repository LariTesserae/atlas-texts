A young animal came through an overlap onto a shelf where the water was warmer than the water it had left. It stood with its feet spread and made a small stamp. The answer came at once, then again, farther away. After that there was only the pressure of the pumps and the fine clicking of a shell bed.

The adult remained on the other side. It tested the ground, moved along the edge of a channel, and came back. Each time it set down a foot, the young one answered. The answers reached the adult faintly, but the shelf itself had become difficult to find. Twice the adult followed the pressure and came to the same patch of reeds.

A worker at the nearest pump house felt the stamping through the concrete. She knew the small, hurried beat and the slower one that followed. She had felt them together before.

She took a rope and a jar of oil and went out until the concrete ended. The line under her hand had recently led to the next house, but the water beside it now tasted of the deep seep below the escarpment. She sat down with the rope across her knees. She could feel the animals, but could not tell which way to go.

The power had been attending to the basin for some time. It knew the pressure of the worker’s hand on the rope, the young animal’s repeated stamp, the adult’s feet moving through the reeds. It knew, too, how much passed through the places where the shelves met: water and gravel, animals and roots, the slow spread of the shell beds.

It did not pull the shelves apart. It held them together a little longer.

The young animal stamped, and this time the answer came through the stone with its direction intact. It turned toward the pressure. On the other shelf, the adult felt the small feet move and followed, lifting its own carefully over the reeds.

The worker waited. After a while she felt the two sets of steps draw close, and then the broad, uneven tread she knew: the adult walking with the young one pressed against its side. She stayed where she was until they had passed.

At the place where the shelves had touched, the power left a change. A foot set down there would send a clear pressure through to the other side, while the crossing itself lasted. The pressure was not loud enough to alarm a resting animal. It carried the shape of the step: the divided folds of a heavy foot, the narrow touch of a grazer, the heel and sole of a person.

Other overlaps received the same gift as they formed. It did not make them permanent, and it did not make every crossing safe. It gave those approaching a little more to know.

The workers noticed first that a step could sometimes be felt twice, once beneath the foot and once farther off. They began to stop and test the ground when this happened. They added the double pressure to their records, alongside the knot counts and the scents and the taste of the water. When a route was uncertain, they could sometimes feel someone waiting at the other end.

One worker, returning with a tank, found that the line ahead gave the familiar double pressure while the line behind did not. She set down her load and tapped three times with her rod. A long while later came three taps in answer. She waited until the pump’s vibration agreed with the direction of the reply, then went on.

The power remained near enough to notice when the water rose around a sleeping animal, when a cup by a door dried out, when the pressure of a young foot came alone for too long. There was much in the basin it could not mend without undoing something else. It learned to be patient with that, and attentive.

At the pumping houses the engines continued their slow strokes. Shell creatures settled on the pipes. The workers emptied and refilled the cups. The stone softened under the warm water, and the paths made by heavy feet filled gradually behind them.

Sometimes, when someone stood at an overlap and could not decide whether to cross, the power held the two places together until they had time to choose.
