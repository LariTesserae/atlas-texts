The power came first to a resin bowl.

It had seen the lights across the hollow and thought, for a moment, that they were signals addressed to it. Then it saw the woman tending this one: how she pinched the wick shorter, how she shielded the flame while a bank of mist passed over the platform, how she looked up after each small task to see whether the light opposite was still burning.

That light went out.

The woman waited. She added resin to her own bowl, though it had enough, and turned it slightly in its clay cradle. When the other light did not return, she called into the house behind her.

A man came out carrying a child who had fallen asleep against his shoulder. He looked across the hollow, then went back inside. By the time he returned with a hooked pole and a bundle of food, the woman had tied her hair up and put on a traveling sling.

The power knew what had happened on the distant platform. A root beneath its hearth had tightened and tipped a pot of water into the resin bowl. Nobody was hurt. Someone was already fetching dry moss to light the bowl again.

It considered putting the flame back itself.

Instead, it spoke from the edge of the platform, where the woman could see it.

“The people across from you are safe,” it said.

She took the hooked pole from the man.

The power had chosen a shape with hands and feet, thinking this might make conversation easier. It had not thought to choose clothing, and the woman was looking at it with the suspicion due a naked stranger who had appeared without crossing the bridge.

“How do you know?” she asked.

“I can see them.”

The man moved the child higher on his shoulder. The woman said, “From where?”

“Here.”

The power tried to explain. While it was doing so, the distant light came on again.

Nobody put the pole away immediately.

* * *

The stranger stayed until the resin bowl burned low. The woman gave it a piece of old matting to wrap around itself and asked it several questions, beginning with its name. It had none that would fit in her language. She called it the Visitor, which was accurate enough.

She asked whether it had seen her sister’s household on the terrace beyond the fork of silver trees. The Visitor had not, but looked when she asked. Her sister was asleep. Her sister’s youngest was awake, scratching at an insect bite.

“Is the roof brace holding?” the woman asked.

“It is.”

That answer took longer than it might have. The Visitor had looked through the brace, following the fine pale tunnels made by creatures inside it. Most were empty; one held a borer no longer than the woman’s thumb.

It told her about the borer.

She considered this. “They know about that one,” she said. “They’re waiting to see which way it goes.”

The Visitor looked again. Beside the borer’s opening was a plug of soft fiber, marked with three cuts. It had seen the plug and understood nothing from it.

In the morning, the woman sent a message to her sister anyway.

* * *

The Visitor went with the route tenders when they would take it. It watched them lift fallen bark, replace a broken tie, and set a small white-flowered cutting into a crevice beside a fork. It learned to wait while they compared what each remembered.

At one pool, a tender named Seli counted the floating leaves twice, then sat down. Her companion sat beside her without asking why.

“There should be seven,” Seli told the Visitor at last.

“There are seven,” it said.

“One is new.”

They remained there most of the day, eating from their packs and watching the light move across the water. The path beyond the pool seemed sound. The Visitor could see where it led just then: to a terrace where four people were pulling a grazer out of a garden enclosure. But the route had lately led somewhere else, and the Visitor could not tell Seli that it would continue to lead to the same place after they stepped onto it.

Near evening, Seli took a long stick and drew the new leaf gently beneath an older one. She weighted its stem with a flat stone.

“Will that help?” the Visitor asked.

“It will tell the next people that someone found it,” she said. “They can decide what to do.”

They turned back. Seli’s companion was annoyed at losing the day, and said so. Seli was annoyed too.

The Visitor could have carried them across the hollow in an instant. It had done nothing because neither had asked, and because it was beginning to understand that it could not always tell the difference between a hardship and a choice made in response to one.

That night it asked whether they would have wanted to be carried.

“To the other terrace?” Seli said. “Yes. We were bringing medicine.”

The Visitor went quiet.

“Can you get it there now?”

It could. Seli showed it the wrapped bundle and described the person who needed it. The Visitor found him sitting with his swollen foot in a bowl of cool water, and placed the bundle on the floor beside him. His wife saw it appear and cried out so sharply that he sloshed water over them both.

The Visitor returned and told Seli.

“You might warn them next time,” she said.

* * *

After that, people began to ask the Visitor for things.

Some requests were small: find a lost wedge; look beneath a floor for the source of a tapping; tell a waiting household whether guests had set out yet. Some were not small at all. The Visitor fetched people from bridges where their food had run out. It held a failing limb long enough for the families above it to leave their houses. Once, asked to find a missing girl, it found her too late, and brought her home.

Word traveled unevenly. On one terrace people knew that the Visitor could look through wood. On another they believed it could prevent children from falling, and for a time a few parents stopped checking the ties on their sleeping nets. The Visitor visited that terrace and showed them how the ties had worn. It mended the nets with them, then went to the neighboring platforms to say plainly what it could and could not promise.

It could be present in many places. It could not attend to every falling leaf and every sleeping child at once, not while also listening to what people asked of it. It could make itself larger, but had found that its attention, spread too thin, missed things a careful person would notice.

The people who heard this argued about what it ought to watch. Some wanted it to follow the children. Some wanted it to inspect every support. A woman whose husband had been lost on a bridge wanted the bridges watched first.

The Visitor listened to all of them. Then it watched where it could, and kept asking.

* * *

In time it learned that the route tenders wanted a place where they could leave news that would reach farther than a traveler’s voice.

They had tried such places before. A shelter built at one junction could be reached reliably from the eastern terraces for years, then suddenly not at all. Another remained easy to find but grew so crowded with roots that the messages had to be moved, and the new shelter never became quite as dependable.

The Visitor asked to see the abandoned sites. It looked at the roots and the paths, at the peculiar ways distances folded around them. It found that it could hold one crossing steady without keeping the rest of the high country still.

Seli asked what that would do to the trees.

“I think very little,” the Visitor said. “But I have never done it before.”

So they chose a small crossing, one already tended by three settlements. People from each settlement came to examine it. They marked the living wood and measured the gaps between the roots. They agreed that if the crossing began to strain, the Visitor would let it go.

It held the crossing through one wet season. The roots grew; the gaps changed. The paths leading away continued to wander, but each returned to the crossing as it had before.

They watched through another season. Then they built a shelter there, with a wide floor and a roof of fitted bark. They left food in sealed baskets and hung messages from cords, each cord marked for a different terrace. A traveler who arrived could sleep, eat, leave news, and find a familiar path out.

The Visitor did not make the shelter impossible to reach by accident. One morning a grazer wandered in and ate three messages and the edge of a sleeping mat. After that, people built a gate.

Years later, the woman who had first met the Visitor came to the shelter with her sister. Their children were grown. The woman’s sister brought a cutting from the white-flowered marker beside the pool, where the new leaf had long since become an old one.

They planted it near the gate. The Visitor held the basket while they worked, and when they had finished, the three of them sat for a while in the green light. Travelers came and went. One stopped to ask whether the path home was open, and Seli, who had just arrived from that direction, told him what she knew.
