One morning a cup-dweller woke to find a drop of water hanging above its flower.

It was larger than the drops that gathered on the leaves, and it did not fall. Inside it the animal could see the pale sky, the rim of the cup, and its own narrow body, all turned upside down. It exposed two white marks, then covered them.

The drop waited.

The cup-dweller climbed down into the stem to drink. There was less water than there had been the day before. At the bottom it found a transparent insect caught against the wet wall, its legs moving slowly. The animal ate it and climbed back into the light.

The drop followed, keeping a little distance above the flower.

By noon the cup was warm and nearly dry. The animal moved to a neighboring flower, then another. The third had water, but another cup-dweller was already there. They faced one another, opening and closing the pale marks along their sides. After a time the first animal withdrew and found a shaded cup farther down the slope.

There the drop settled onto a leaf. It divided into several smaller drops, which ran along the veins and gathered at the stem. The cup-dweller drank again. When it had finished, the remaining water passed down into the soil.

The power could have filled every cup on the plateau. It could have drawn rain over the ridges and held it there until the hollows rose. It looked beneath the flowers first.

Under the mats of vegetation, some of the fine channels were drying. The pale young insects clustered in the dampest places. Roots had entered a fracture near the eastern cliffs, and water was moving through it toward the pumping house more quickly than it could be replaced. The pump had drawn water there for many seasons; the fracture had only recently opened wide enough to feed it.

That afternoon the power went to the cliff. It spread through the stone and felt the roots, the water, and the slow movement of the pump. It could close the fracture, but the pressure would find another way. It could fill the ground with water, but the change would be difficult for the workers to notice and might leave them drawing still more.

Instead it waited for them.

Two workers came the next morning. One held a hand against the rock while the other watched the polished strip used to send messages down the valley. The first worker remained there for a long time, then took their hand away and placed it against the stone again.

Beneath the palm, the movement of the water grew distinct. It traced a line downward and toward the pump, then returned to the fracture. The worker called their companion over with a movement of the hand. Both knelt with their palms on the cliff.

The power showed them the line a second time. Then, gently, it showed them where the water had fallen beneath the moss.

The workers went to the pumping house. One lifted the shade from a panel and read the gauges. The other sent a flash toward the tower and waited for an answer. Before evening, more people had come up the maintenance gaps carrying measuring rods and sealed vessels. They tested the damp ground around the fracture and opened a narrow cover beneath the pump. One of them went back to the cliff and held a palm against it for so long that the light left the upper ridge.

They reduced the flow through the pump. For a while the carriers brought less fiber, and some waited empty in the sunlight. The workers examined older records and followed the water through the stone. When they found a way to use less of it for washing, they changed the machinery. They kept checking the ground beneath the moss as well as the gauges inside the house.

In the weeks that followed, moisture returned slowly to the fine channels. The young insects spread out again. Some were eaten when they came above the mats; others reached the leaves and drank from the droplets there.

The cup-dweller moved among the flowers at the cleared edge of the tower. Once, when a worker opened a panel, it was lifted carefully in two hands and set down in a shaded cup. The worker left a little water there and came back later to close the panel.

The power remained on the plateau through the dry season. It watched the cup-dweller turn with the sun and watched the workers alter their figures when the next rain came. At the cliff it kept a place where a patient hand could feel the water moving. It did not make the stone louder.

When the fog arrived over the eastern rim, droplets formed along the leaves. The cup-dweller drank from its stem, climbed back to the flower, and settled around the central cup. Above it, the droplets gathered until they were heavy enough to fall.
