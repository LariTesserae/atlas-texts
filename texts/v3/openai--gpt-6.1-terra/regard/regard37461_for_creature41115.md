The power attends first to the stone.

It feels the broken wall, the blocks resting against one another, and the fine seams beneath them. One block is poised to fall. If it goes, it will take part of the ledge with it, including the willow whose torn root the pale threads have begun to cover.

The power settles the block into a sounder place. It does not close the fractures. Rain can still enter them, and the buried strands can still breathe.

Then it gathers loose moss and grit around the exposed cords, enough to shelter their sealed ends from the wind. It leaves the buried portions where they are: lifting the sharp blocks from them would tear more than it freed. In time, some of those strands will die. Others may grow around the edges of the rock.

The grinding below continues. The power follows it down through the mountain and finds people cutting stone. They are working from plans that do not show the cleft or the fresh split above it.

It draws their attention to the ridge: a loosened survey marker, a photograph taken from a new angle, a vibration noticed and checked. The work pauses. People climb to the scar and measure it. They change where they cut and how much force they use, and leave instruments to warn them if the wall begins to move again.

The mountain remains a mountain. Wind strips the ledges; frost opens cracks; stones fall. The power does not stay to catch each one. But before it leaves, it bends a small runnel in the broken rock so that, after rain, a little more water reaches the sheltered moss.

Months later, white threads have crossed into it. They spread among willow roots and grains of soil, joining what remains to what is new. On the ledge above, the fern still catches beads beneath its leaves. One falls, and the moss receives it.
