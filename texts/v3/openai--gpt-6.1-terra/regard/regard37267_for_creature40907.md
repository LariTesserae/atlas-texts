The power came to the hollow at an hour when the water was climbing.

It had seen mountains lifted and worn away, and knew several ways to stop a flood. It stood for a long time above the roofs, watching the people turn their gates.

A woman at the upper bridge had set a stone against her gate to hold it while she worked. The current moved the stone. A child caught it with both hands and put it back, this time on the other side. The woman glanced up at the birds, then shifted the gate a finger’s width.

The power watched the underfeet facing into the current. It watched the grazers come past, some riding a plate of ice, others holding to its underside. It looked into the old walls inside the new ones and saw how water passed through spaces that appeared, from above, to be solid.

Then it went down into the settlement in the shape of a person.

It wore shades, because everyone else did, though it had no need of them. At the first lane it stepped onto a moving stone and found itself carried slowly toward a doorway. A child on the bridge told it how to get off.

“Are you visiting someone?” the child asked.

“I haven’t been here before.”

The child considered this. “Then wait until the water goes down. Someone will take you across.”

The power waited. While it waited, it held one end of a gate for a man whose fingers had gone numb. The man thanked it and sent it to the next bridge, where two women needed another pair of hands. There it learned to keep its feet apart and not pull against a gate until the person on the other side was ready.

That evening it ate fish broth in an upper room. Its host asked where it had come from, and it told her the truth. She set down her bowl.

“Can you make the water go away?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She looked at the floor for a while. Her son was asleep there, wrapped in a coat.

“Would the seep dry up?”

“It might.”

“And the fish?”

The power thought of the long routes by which the fish reached the hollow, and of how little it yet knew about them. “I don’t know.”

“Then don’t do that.”

In the morning, she introduced it to the people who kept the central grass, and to two elders who disagreed about a wall. It listened to both of them. One remembered water entering the wall from above; the other had opened it thirty years ago and found a channel running through its base. The power looked inside the stone and told them that both channels were still there.

They asked it to show them where.

It could have traced the channels in fire across the wall, but the birds were nesting in it. Instead, it took a piece of charcoal and made small marks on the dry stones. The elders followed with their fingers. They argued again, more usefully this time, and sent for someone with a measuring cord.

Word spread. People came with questions about foundations and seeps, about a child’s painful eyes, about a roof that dripped only when the water ran uphill. The power answered what it could. Where an answer might change the hollow in ways it could not yet foresee, it said so. Some people were disappointed. A few went away angry, and the power did not blame them.

It stayed through several reversals.

On the fourth, water began to gather behind the upper houses. A roof watcher signaled, and people went to lift a gate, but a stone had turned beneath it. The gate would not rise. Water climbed the wall beside it, carrying ice and a pale mass of grazers.

The power saw a pocket opening under the bank of the central seep. If the bank fell, the grass would go with it, and the lower rooms of six houses would fill before their families could raise their stores.

It went to the bank and put its hands against the stone.

The stone held.

People were already running through the lanes. Someone brought a bar to the stuck gate. Someone else called the lower households upstairs. The keepers of the grass fetched screens and stones, then saw that neither would help and went to find rope.

The power could have held the bank all day. It could have made the rock beneath it whole again, too, but the new water needed somewhere to pass.

“Can you open a channel there?” it asked the nearest keeper, pointing toward a low wall beyond the seep.

“There are nests in it.”

The power looked. There were.

“Below them, then. Between those two stones.”

The keeper went to see. Others joined her. They tested the ground, fetched tools, and opened a passage beneath the nests. Water came through in a narrow stream, then more quickly. The birds held to the masonry above it, their wings tucked close.

When the current found the new channel, the power took its hands away. The bank remained standing, though part of it had sunk. The keepers began setting loose stones around the exposed roots. A boy brought wet hide to guide the grazers out of a doorway, and an underfoot came to investigate the work.

That evening the people asked whether the power would remain.

It could not promise always to be there when a gate stuck or a wall shifted. It could, however, leave something useful.

The next day it walked with them along the upper ridge. Beneath the snow it found a broad shelf of stone, thin in one place and hollow underneath. When water gathered behind the upper houses, the shelf gave a faint vibration. The power showed them how to fit a small piece of bone against it. As the pressure grew, the bone would click.

They tried it by diverting water into the channel. The clicking was quiet. A dog heard it first; then the people nearest the shelf began to hear it too.

They built a shelter over the bone to keep it clear of snow. They made its fastening simple enough to replace, and asked the power to show them how to test it. Before leaving, it did one more thing: it eased the pain in the child’s eyes, with her permission, and sat with her mother while she learned how to tell when the glare was becoming too much for her.

The power departed without changing the direction of the water.

The people kept the little shelter on the ridge. They checked the bone, as they checked their gates and roofs and stepping-stones. Sometimes it clicked when there was still ample time; once it stayed silent because ice had formed beneath it, and afterward they altered the shelter.

In the bright hours, people still drew their curtains. The underfeet still slept beneath the bridges. The birds lifted and settled their wings along the walls, and the grass held the bank where it could.

When the water climbed, the people turned the gates around. Now, if it gathered too quickly above them, there was a sound on the ridge that gave them a little longer to work.
