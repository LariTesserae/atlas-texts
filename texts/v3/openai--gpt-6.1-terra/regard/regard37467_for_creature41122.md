The person watching the wall sees the dust first. It comes from a seam above the street, a narrow fall of white grains that does not stop.

They raise both hands and lower them, palms flat. The people at the slab step back. One of them calls to the household inside, and two children come out, carrying a bowl between them.

The dust thickens. Far above, a shelf of limestone has begun to separate from the roof. The seam has been opening slowly for years, hidden behind a line of mineral deposits. Now a length of it gives way at once.

The shelf does not fall.

It hangs a little below the roof, tilted toward the street. Dust pours from its broken edge. People move away from the house and call to those in the adjoining courts. A few stop to look up, then are urged onward by their neighbors.

When the street is empty, the shelf descends. It comes down more slowly than stone should, turning until its broad face lies against the ground. One end breaks a wall; the other settles across a channel. Animals emerge from beneath it and hurry into the powder.

For a while, nobody approaches. Then the person who had been watching the wall walks out with their hands raised against the dust. They touch the stone. It is cold, and its upper surface is ridged with marks from the ceiling.

Others come to look. The household takes its things to an empty chamber on the slope. By evening, people have marked off the street with chalk and begun clearing a route around it.

That night, several residents sit awake near the fallen shelf. They have brought lamps and a jar of water. One is a child from the damaged house, who has been allowed to stay because they noticed a crack in the wall earlier that morning and told an adult.

They talk about what happened. They know how stone falls. Some think a projection in the roof caught the shelf, though none can see where. Others have no explanation.

The power that held it listens. It has understood, from the care with which the city is built and rebuilt, that the people can live with uncertainty. They are already deciding what to do about the street. But the shelf above them had been only one of several loose places, and the next might fall when no one is there to see the dust.

In the morning, a small stone appears beside the watching group. It is a piece of the fallen shelf, flat enough to hold in one hand. On its surface are lines showing the streets nearby, the damaged house, and the opening in the roof above it.

The lines are shallow and precise. No one saw them being made.

The child recognizes the house first. An older channel worker takes the stone into the light and finds the kink in the channel west of the street. Beyond it, three marks lie along the roof line. One is where the shelf fell.

People go to look at the other two places. At the first, they find a crack partly concealed by white deposits. At the second, they can see nothing from the ground. They bring a long pole and touch the roof with it. A spray of grit comes down.

The streets beneath both places are cleared. Over the following days, the loose stone is eased down where it can be reached. Where it cannot, the streets are left empty.

Other marked stones appear, always in places where people are gathered. They show seams in the roof, deep channels in the floor, and hollows where water collects after rain. The first stones cause alarm; later ones are carried from court to court and examined alongside the strips on which people have recorded their own observations. Sometimes the marks reveal something useful. Sometimes their meaning remains unclear.

One stone shows a hollow in the floor beyond the far storage chambers. People dig there and find damp clay beneath the limestone. Water seeps into the hole, slowly enough that at first they think they have made a mistake. They leave a bowl overnight. In the morning it holds a shallow pool.

The families who work the nearby cisterns deepen the hollow and line it with fitted stone. They leave part of its edge rough, where small animals have begun to gather. The new well does not fill quickly, but it holds water through a dry spell, and several households no longer have to carry their jars from the center of the city.

After that, people sometimes leave questions beside the marked stones. They scratch diagrams into limestone or arrange pebbles to show a place they are worried about. Some receive an answer. Others do not. A drawing of a damaged roof produces a mark showing a fault in the ground beneath it; a question about when the next rain will come remains unanswered.

The power could make the rain come. It could hold every shelf in the roof, straighten every channel as soon as it bends, and keep every jar full. It has seen the country above as well: the soil that washes down, the roots that hold it, the creatures that drink from the same water before it reaches the basin. It knows that a change made for one life can travel outward into many others. So it attends to both places, and when it acts, it looks again to see what has followed.

The city continues to settle. A wall opens along a seam; workers mark it and watch. A channel fills with grit and is scraped clean. The well grows a little lower during a long dry season, then rises after rain.

In the street where the shelf came down, the broken wall is rebuilt with smaller blocks. The shelf itself remains where it landed. People have made a low path around it, and in its shade the household from the damaged house keeps several bowls of water. The child who noticed the crack tends them now, lifting each bowl before cleaning beneath it, so the animals sheltering there have time to move away.
