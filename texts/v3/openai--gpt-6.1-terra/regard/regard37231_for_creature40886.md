The bead reaches the basin’s lip and touches the snail’s feelers. The snail draws them back, then extends them again. It lowers its mouth to the water.

The power attends first to the snail, and then to the cold stone beneath it, the eggs in the seam, the roots below the basin, and the fungus threaded through them. It follows the paths of water into the stair crevice and finds the young insects there, still too small to leave their bed.

Above the chamber, one of the courtyard stones has settled. For years it has leaned against the next, leaving the crack between them much as it was. Now a root beneath it is dying. When the root gives way, the stone will move again, and the crack will close.

The power loosens a little soil around the root. It does not restore the root, which has lived a long time, nor does it make the paving stone impervious to age. It guides the tip of a younger root into the space beneath it. The root takes hold there, and the stone remains where it is.

Nothing in the chamber changes at once. The snail drinks what it can reach, then travels down the basin wall. A mite crosses a strand of the spider’s web, and the spider comes carefully toward it.

In the courtyard, the younger root thickens over the following years. Its leaves are small and receive little sun among the broken walls, but there is enough. When the older root finally softens into the soil, the paving stone settles against the new one.

Light continues to enter the chamber. It reaches the basin, then the stair, and then the nearly horizontal fern fronds. One afternoon a snail hatches in the wall seam and begins to eat the clear casing of its egg.
