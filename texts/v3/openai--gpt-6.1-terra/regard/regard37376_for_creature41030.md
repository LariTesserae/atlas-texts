The power first noticed the plateau because of the water.

There were places where rain fell harder and rivers ran deeper, but here a few clear inches could hold the sky so completely that the sky seemed to have come down among the stones. The power watched the pools change shape as the ground rose. It watched the people move their walkways and intakes, and the birds find a basin before the rain had finished filling it.

For a long time it did nothing that could be seen. It listened beneath the rock, where great pressures were making the new folds, and along the surface, where a burrower slept in a chamber lined with grass. It learned how the city’s foundations yielded and settled. It followed a hunter with a torn ear over three basins, and a grazer that had lost a little of one hoof but could still keep pace with her herd.

The power could have stilled the rising ground. It could have made the winters gentler and the forage abundant in every basin. It saw, though, how much depended on the cold, the wet hollows, and even the slow breaking of the rock. It did not know all that would be lost if it remade the place for comfort. So it continued to watch, and learned more.

Late in the wet season, surveyors found that a fold north of the city was rising faster than the models had predicted. One morning a seam crossed their route where no seam had been the evening before. They measured it, sent the figures home, and moved their shelter to higher ground.

Under the fold, a broad sheet of stone was taking weight unevenly. The city’s instruments could tell that something was happening, but not yet where the strain would break. Two herds were feeding along the basin below it. A settlement of water workers stood farther downslope, beside a channel that carried water to the treatment gardens.

The power could feel the narrow place where the stone would fail. It reached into the fold and held it there.

At once the readings changed. The surveyors saw the pressure stop climbing without any sign of release. They checked their instruments, then checked them again by hand. Their report went to the city, where people opened the northern passage and closed the lower walkways. The water workers brought their tools inside and guided their children up to the terraces.

No one could tell the herds what was coming. People walked out toward them in a wide line, leaving room for the animals to turn. The first herd moved readily enough. The second had found good sedge near the water and scattered in several directions before gathering again. By dusk most of its animals had crossed onto the higher rock.

One young grazer remained in the basin. Its leg had sunk through a skin of frost into black mud. When people approached, it pulled harder and went down on its side. A surveyor eased toward it on her hands and knees, but the crust bent under her weight.

The power had held mountains, and could have lifted the animal cleanly from the ground. Instead it warmed the mud around the trapped leg until the ice loosened. The surveyor laid a broad plate on the surface and crawled out over it. She freed the leg with both hands, then backed away as the grazer struggled upright.

It stood for a moment with mud running down its coat. Then it went after the herd, limping, while the people watched from the edge of the basin.

That night the last surveyor left the low shelter. When the lights on the higher route showed that everyone was clear, the power eased its hold.

The break came shortly before morning. A long report traveled under the rain, followed by another, and the water in the northern pools trembled. The fold had opened a new channel. Muddy water spread across the empty basin and reached the lower walkway, but the settlement stood above it, undamaged.

In the following days, people went out to see what had changed. They found stones shifted from their places, a survey light lying on its side, and the low shelter tilted but intact. Birds were already picking along the margins of the new water. The surveyors marked the channel and sent its course to the city. The water workers began planning a different route for their intake.

The young grazer stayed near the rear of its herd for a while. Its leg grew stronger, though in cold weather it still lifted that foot sooner than the others. The hunter with the torn ear came through the basin some weeks later and found tracks on both sides of the water.

The people argued for years about the readings from the night of the break. Some thought an instrument had found a strain in the rock that their models did not yet account for. Others thought the ground had been held. They kept the measurements, including those that made no sense, and taught the episode to new surveyors alongside the old routes and the names of pools that had drained away.

The power stayed near for a time. It listened to the water moving through the fresh channel and to the city making its small adjustments. When another season came, it followed the rain toward the far interior, where clouds were gathering over a basin no one had yet named.
