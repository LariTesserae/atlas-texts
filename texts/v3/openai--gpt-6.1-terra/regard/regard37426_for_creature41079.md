The power came first as attention.

It followed the water through the rim, where roots had found their way into cracks too narrow for a finger. It followed the culvert beneath the wheel and out among the pebbles, and the fine cracks in the iron where the worms waited when the wheel was still.

It found hunger under the stones. It found the long, enclosed waiting of the old seamlings in their hollows, and the brief, vigorous lives of the young, who could still follow a rolling pebble from one place to another. It found the bird’s hunger too, and the beetles’, and the slow work of the fungus in the wet wood.

Then it waited through a change of weather, to see how the basin managed without it.

Rain filled the upper grooves. The wheel began to click. Eggs rose in the deepest pool and drifted toward the culvert; a few caught in a fold of algae, and many went through. On the eastern shelf, an old seamling felt the water rise around its hollow and began the difficult work of turning. It got out, but the current carried it against a pipe, where it remained until the water fell.

Later came dry days. The wheel stopped. A young seamling, searching for a place where the algae was neither scoured away nor sour, crossed a patch of bare rock and was taken by a beetle.

The power did not think that because it had seen these things, it had understood everything. It watched longer. It learned which seep returned first after rain, and which hollows held water after the others had emptied. It noticed that a stone lodged beneath the eastern shelf sometimes diverted the flow just enough to leave a quiet strip along the wall. There, even in a hard rain, the algae held.

At the end of a wet night, while the bird slept elsewhere, the power moved that stone a little farther into the current. It settled firmly. Behind it, the water slowed and spread thinly across the shelf.

The power also opened a narrow passage from the old seamling’s hollow to the sheltered strip. The passage curved beneath an overhang, where the bird could not reach. It was wide enough for the seamling to pass, though not so wide that the first flood would scour the shelter clean.

When the next heavy rain came, the old seamling found the passage by touch. It took a long time to work through. The power could have lifted it, but the seamling was moving steadily, and the stone overhead held. When it emerged, it fastened itself in the quiet water and extended its feeding fringe.

Some eggs still went down the culvert. Others caught behind the shifted stone, where a new film of algae was beginning to grow. A beetle found the shelter as well. The bird continued to visit the spill below the pump house and sometimes left with a seamling in its beak.

As the season grew dry, the power returned its attention to a seep high in the rock. It did not know whether the seep would last through a drought longer than any it had watched, or how much the fields drew from the same water. It left the source alone. Instead, it loosened a flake of shale so that what water did arrive ran across the shelf before draining into the culvert.

For a few more days, the wheel clicked. The fungus remained pale and open along the timber, and worms came out into the damp joints. Then the seep diminished and the wheel stopped.

The power stayed near enough to notice. It would learn what those few days had meant, and whether the shelter held too much silt when the rains returned. There might be more to do, or something to undo.

Below the shelf, water gathered in a shallow groove. A young seamling clung to the underside of a pebble as the current turned it once, gently, and laid it down again.
