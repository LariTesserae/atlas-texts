For a long time the power did nothing that could be distinguished from listening.

It followed water into the stone and felt the pale threads drawing sustenance from the seep. It attended to the grazer that found a hollow, the animal waiting inside, the sudden flight of the others. It felt the larger hunter come through the roots toward the alarm.

It did not seal the hollow or turn the hunter away.

At the workshops it lingered over the machinery. There was pleasure there, repeated in hundreds of small decisions: a surface cut smoothly enough for a bearing to run cool; a drain screen lifted before the storm rather than afterward; a signal passed through a rail and answered by a hand already waiting.

There was pain, too. A worker caught a finger between two parts and held it against their chest while another shut down the press. The power could have closed the wound before the worker had drawn breath. It held back long enough for the worker to call out and for their companions to gather. Then, when the bleeding continued through the first bandage, it helped the blood to clot.

The worker never knew. They kept the finger, though it remained stiff in cold water.

The power stayed.

It learned the long routes by traveling them with people and drummers. It learned the bells, including one whose tone had changed after its mounting loosened; a traveler noticed the change on the same day and tightened it. It learned how a warning was made urgent, and how an offer could be refused.

Eventually it chose a place where one of the travelers liked to rest.

This was a shelf above a river, sheltered under the arching roots of an old tree. A chain ran down from the shelf to a crossing. People often paused there to eat and check their bindings before descending. The traveler who came most regularly was named Sen, and Sen had a habit of tapping their staff against the same exposed patch of stone before sitting down.

One day the stone answered before the staff struck it.

Sen stood still. The answer had been a single soft knock, close to the place where the staff usually landed. After a while Sen touched the stone with the weighted end and gave it a tap.

Two knocks came back.

Sen moved uphill, away from the riverbank, and struck again. The two knocks followed, still close to the staff.

Sen went home without sitting down.

The next time, Sen brought another traveler. They tested the shelf together, first with their staffs and then with their hands flat on the stone. The knocks returned in patterns neither could produce by shifting their weight. They tried a different shelf, then the packed earth beneath the tree. The answering knocks came there too, though faintly.

For several visits the travelers kept their questions simple. Could the thing answer from farther away? Yes. Could it feel a hand laid on the chain? Yes. Had it moved the bell at the crossing? No.

They asked whether it could speak.

The knocks stopped. Sen waited, and the other traveler shifted their feet in the moss. Then the stone gave a sequence so long and uneven that neither of them could make sense of it.

“It’s trying,” Sen said.

After that, people came out from the nearest workshop to meet it. They brought a flat iron plate and laid it on the bedrock, with raised marks along its edges. They placed fingers on the plate and gave names to things: water, tree, staff, hand. They showed it how to indicate a mark and wait for another person to read it.

The power learned quickly, but not so quickly that the people failed to notice the work of learning. It confused the words for a river’s bank and the bench beside a machine. It asked the name of a smell and was told that several names might fit, depending on who was speaking. When someone laughed at an awkward answer, it asked what had caused the laughter.

The plate remained under the tree. Anyone could come to it. Some people did so often; some avoided the shelf and took a longer route to the crossing. The power did not follow them.

A season later, a storm settled over the highest shelves and stayed there. Travelers reached the workshop wet through, reporting water across routes that were usually safe. The crews raised the drain screens more often and set extra hands to clearing grit from the turbine channels.

On the third day, Sen was at the plate when the power sent a hard pulse through it.

*Below the north workshop,* it said, *water has opened a chamber beneath the foundations.*

Sen kept both hands on the iron while the power described what it had felt. A buried channel had widened under one corner of the building. Stone had fallen into the channel farther downstream, holding back water. If the blockage gave way suddenly, the floor above the chamber might fall with it.

“Have you told them?”

*I came to you first. I know how you speak to me.*

Sen ran for the workshop. The rain struck so hard that the route bell could not be heard until Sen was almost beneath it.

At the building, the crew stopped the press and sent people through the lower rooms. They found a crack in the floor beside the turbine housing, narrow but fresh. Water was rising through it.

The workshop had a warning rail that ran to the yard. Sen put a hand on it and gave the signal they had agreed on at the plate. After a pause, the power answered through the iron.

The crew gathered around, each person touching the rail. The power showed them, as best it could, where the chamber lay. The foreworker sent two people to sound the floor with weighted bars. Their strikes came back thin near the turbine and solid by the outer wall.

“Can you move the fallen stone?” the foreworker asked.

*Yes.*

“Will that bring the floor down?”

*I can hold the chamber while the water escapes. I think the floor will remain, but the current may cut a new path under the yard.*

The foreworker asked how long they had. The power did not know. The water was still rising.

They cleared the building and unfastened the carts from their sheltered bay. One cart was too heavily loaded to move quickly; the crew left it. They sent a traveler toward the next workshop with news, then waited on bedrock above the yard while the power held the chamber open and lifted the fallen stone from the channel.

For a time nothing happened that the people could feel. Rain hammered the roof. The river below the workshop roared.

Then the ground trembled beneath their feet. The water broke through the channel, and a deep rush passed under the shelf. It continued for several minutes. When it ended, one corner of the yard had sunk, taking the loaded cart and a section of the retaining wall with it. The workshop still stood.

The crew waited until the storm had moved on before going back inside. They found the crack wider, but the turbine housing had not shifted. It took them many days to brace the floor, fill the worst of the washout, and recover what they could from the cart.

They also found that the river below had cut a new passage into an old side chamber. By the next season, pool dwellers had begun to gather there.

Afterward, the people asked the power to tell them if it felt another dangerous change beneath a building or along a marked route. They argued for a while about what counted as dangerous. A new hollow might swallow a cart, but hollows opened often on the plateau, and travelers already knew to test the ground. In the end they made a list on a long strip of marked iron and left room to add to it.

They did not ask the power to keep every route unchanged. The routes belonged partly to the herds, partly to the rivers, and partly to the roots that grew across them. People had learned to travel among all three.

They did ask whether it could help them make a bell that would ring when the ground beneath a foundation began to separate.

It could. The first design rang whenever a heavy cart passed. The second worked better.

The plate remained beneath the tree. People came to ask questions, leave news, or sit with their hands resting on the iron. Sometimes the power asked questions of its own. It wanted to know why a particular cutting rhythm pleased the workers, and why one traveler preferred the long route beside the river when the high shelf was quicker. The traveler brought it to the high shelf in the wet season and had it listen to the way the wind moved through the roots.

Years later, Sen returned to the plate after a journey between workshops. A herd had crossed the route ahead of them, and Sen had waited half a day in a root tunnel while the young drummers tested the ground.

Sen laid both hands on the iron and described their clumsy strikes: the impatience of one youngster, the older animal that answered, the long pause before the next attempt.

The power listened. When Sen had finished, it asked whether the youngster had found the safe way across.

“Yes,” Sen said. “Though it took a great many tries.”

For a while they sat together above the river. Water ran down the tree’s trunk and collected in the grooves of the plate. Sen turned it over to drain it, as they had been shown to do, and set it back on the stone.
