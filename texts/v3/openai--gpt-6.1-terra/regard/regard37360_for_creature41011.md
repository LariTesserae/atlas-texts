The power came first as a change in the sound of water.

A gutter that had been dripping into a dish gave three quick taps, then stopped. The woman who had set out the dish waited with her hand on the doorframe. After a while she went up to the roof and found the gutter clear, though she could see no reason for the rainwater to have stopped.

On the rock above the southern wall, a person was sitting with their feet in the slot of sky. They wore a plain coat, dusty at the elbows, and were looking down into the hollow.

“You’re blocking the gutter,” she called.

The person drew their feet back. Water ran again.

“Sorry,” they said.

She had not heard anyone approach. The ground above was loose stone, and even the children made a noise crossing it. She climbed the last few rungs of the ladder and came out beside them.

They had a face she could remember afterward, though she found it difficult to describe. Their hands rested on their knees. One was wet from the gutter.

“Are you looking for someone?” she asked.

“I came to see how you live.”

“Then you can come down. You shouldn’t sit there if it rains.”

They thanked her and followed her to the ladder, turning sideways at the narrow place as everyone did.

In the lane, the visitor stood aside for a man carrying a coil of pipe. A child showed them the screw she had once put out for a moth, and explained that moths did not eat screws. The visitor examined it with her and returned it to the jar.

They stayed through the afternoon. They watched the lamp repairer work and held the tray when she needed both hands. At the pumping works they laid a palm on the wall, as the keepers did, and felt the slow tremor. They looked at the cistern board, where the old marks showed faintly beneath the new ones.

At the southern end they knelt by the wall. The shoots were finer than threads at their tips, with a little concrete dust caught between their leaves. The visitor did not touch them.

That evening, when several people had gathered by the pump, the visitor told them what they were.

This took some time. The woman who had met them above asked why they had needed to move their feet from the gutter.

“I didn’t need to,” the visitor said. “But you asked me to.”

Some of the people laughed. Others wanted proof. The visitor held out a hand, and the amber lamp over the pump went dark, then shone blue, then white. They returned it to amber before the lamp repairer had finished telling them not to interfere with it.

“I can do more than that,” they said. “I’d like to help, if you’ll tell me what you need.”

They were asked whether they had caused the shoots. They had not. They were asked whether the shoots would bring down the wall.

“Not soon,” the visitor said. “Their roots are following the iron. They make room for themselves very slowly, but water follows them. In some years the iron will begin to swell.”

“How many years?”

The visitor gave an answer. The people closest to the wall began at once to discuss it, counting backward to when the concrete had been poured and disagreeing about which winter had been wetter.

They also asked about the pump. The visitor showed the keepers a hairline fracture in a coupling, so fine that it took the strip of midday light to make it visible. The part would last a little longer, they said, but not through another hot season. One keeper fetched a spare; another said the spare was the wrong size. They measured it together.

Then there was the question of water. There were other questions too: heat, the sharp braces, the difficulty of carrying someone sick up through the slot. People brought chairs into the lane until no one could get past, and then moved them again. A few did not come to the discussion at all. The people who did came and went, taking the news to the rooms above and bringing back objections.

The visitor waited three days.

On the fourth morning, the people asked for a second way out of the hollow, with steps broad enough for a person to be carried. They asked for a source of clean water that would not depend entirely on rain. They asked that the pump coupling be made sound, and that the visitor explain what should be done about the southern wall before doing anything to it.

The visitor took a piece of chalk from the workshop and went over the stone above the settlement with two of the builders. They marked a route where a passage could be opened without disturbing the roots of the largest plants. Then, while everyone stood well back, the visitor pressed both hands against the rock.

There was a long, low sound. Dust rose from the ground, and the leaves along the slot shivered. When it settled, a passage led up through the stone, with shallow steps and a landing halfway. Its entrance was wide enough for two people to pass. The builders went in first and returned with chalk marks on their sleeves, having checked the roof and sides as far as their lights would reach.

For the water, the visitor found a spring beneath the rock. They did not bring it straight into the cisterns. They brought it to a small basin above the hollow, where it could be tested and where a valve could shut it off. The pipe workers chose where the line should run, and the visitor made the openings they asked for. At the first tap in the lane, water came out cold. A woman filled a cup, tasted it and handed it to the next person. By evening a queue of buckets had formed.

The visitor repaired the coupling and showed the pump keepers how it had been made. They left a second coupling in the warm drawer, wrapped in cloth.

At the southern wall there was a longer discussion. The visitor could remove the shoots, strengthen the wall or make an opening around each row. The people who lived nearest it wanted time to consider. The person who measured the shoots wanted to know how deep their roots went; the person who objected to measuring wanted to know whether any of the work would hurt them.

The visitor answered what they could. They could keep the wall sound without harming the shoots, though the iron would still need checking. In the end, that was what the residents chose. The visitor worked from the far side of the rock, where no one could see exactly what they did. Afterward the shoots stood as they had before, and the wall sounded solid when struck.

Before leaving, the visitor went once more through the lane. A sheet was hanging low across the passage. They lifted it carefully and waited while its owner gathered it into a basket.

At the new steps, the child who had shown them the screw asked whether they would come back.

“You can call me,” the visitor said.

“How?”

“Ask aloud.”

The child considered this, then called to them at once.

They turned around.

She had only wanted to know if it worked.

That night, the flywheel turned behind its screen. Water dripped from the old gutter into the dish below, though there was now a full jug in the room beside it. Someone brought the dish in before bed.

At the southern wall, the watcher unfolded a strip of paper and held it beside a shoot. It had grown a little since the last mark. They made a new one, folded the paper again and went home by way of the new steps, where a faint current of cooler air was moving down.
