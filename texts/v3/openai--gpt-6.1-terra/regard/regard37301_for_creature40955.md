The power came to the plateau on an evening when the arcs were visible.

From above, it could see the whole arrangement: the three poles and the ridges that answered them, the heat gathered in the valleys, the water passing again and again through stone and leaf and cloud. It could also see how old the arrangement was. One pole had changed very slightly where it entered the ground. A seam in the rock had widened around it, and water was beginning to take a new course.

The change might matter in a few hundred years. Or the water might lay down minerals in the seam and close it of its own accord. The power watched long enough to see that both were possible.

Near the pole, an old person was clearing roots with a narrow tool of polished stone. Their name was Sena. They had come to the ridge with two younger companions, who were gathering fallen seedpods farther downslope. Sena had worked around this pole many times. They knew the shape of the ground and had noticed the new wetness.

When the roots were cleared, Sena pressed a palm against the rock. Water gathered around their fingers.

They went to the shelter, brought back a small piece of its growing wall, and set it against the wet seam. They held it there with stones. If it took hold, it might guide the water away from the pole. Sena sat back on their heels and looked at it.

The power could have sealed the seam at once. It could have renewed all three poles, or made the plateau warm without them. But the water had its own paths through the ridge, and many things lived along those paths. Sena’s small repair would give them time to see where the water went. The power left it in place.

That night a seam-walker came down from the cliff. It found the piece of shelter wall and drank from the water pooling against it. In climbing away, it dislodged one of the stones. By morning the scrap had slipped sideways, and the water was running through the seam again.

Sena returned after breakfast. They saw the prints of the seam-walker’s pads and the displaced stone. They set the scrap back, then fetched a flatter stone from the stream. This one they wedged beneath the first.

The power did one small thing. It warmed the rock under the scrap, no more than a hand might have warmed it by resting there. The shelter material spread a little along the seam before the next rain.

Sena came back several days later and found it attached. They touched its clear edge and called the others over. Together they followed the water downslope, parting the grass with their hands. It had begun to emerge a short distance from the pole, where it made a damp patch among the roots of a fern. They watched it for a while. One of the younger people marked the place with three seedpods, so they could find it again.

The power stayed through the rains.

It watched a roofback stand beneath a fall with its scales raised, while its young pressed close and drank from its back. It watched the young seam-walkers practicing on the basin walls, slipping into moss and climbing up again. At dusk, the broad-winged animals settled into the high ferns. One shadow continued across the valley after its owner had landed, and came to rest beside a pool.

The power went there too. It did not find the wing for the shadow; it was not certain that the shadow had lost it. When the wind changed, the shadow lifted from the pool and moved on.

By the time the rains ended, Sena’s scrap had grown across the seam. The fern below it had sent up a new frond. The pole stood as straight as the others, though it had always appeared straight, even when the water first reached it.

Sena and their companions cleaned the shelter bowl before leaving the ridge. The two younger people carried seedpods and a bundle of food between them. Sena paused at the doorway, looking back toward the pole, and then followed the others into the grass.

The power remained until evening. The arcs appeared, and the cloud below shone pale beneath them. Far out in the valley, a roofback stamped. Another answered, and then another, until the sound had passed beyond the ridge.

When the arcs faded, the power let the rock cool to its usual warmth. It had seen how Sena tended the pole, and where the water now came out. It would know the place if it returned.
