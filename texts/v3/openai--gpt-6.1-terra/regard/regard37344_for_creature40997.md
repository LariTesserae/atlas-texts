The power came first to the rim, where the wind went booming past. It could have silenced the wind, but the birds used it, so it listened instead.

It listened for a long time. It learned the sound of a roof slab settling and the different sound of one beginning to slip. It learned the calls of the young birds, and the sharp voices people used when a child had wandered too close to the edge. It watched the roots being pressed and saw the faces made over the bitter drink. It saw, too, how carefully the jars were stopped.

One morning a woman named Eren went to check the storage pits. She found the nearest jar cold to the touch. A skin of clear water had gathered on its lid, beading around the clay stopper.

She tasted a drop. Then she called her sister, who tasted it too.

They looked up. There was no wetness on the shelf above them, nor anywhere else on the jar.

That night Eren dreamed she was standing beside the storage pit. Something stood with her, though she could not see it. It asked whether the water was good.

“Yes,” she said. “Where did it come from?”

“I brought it.”

“Is there more?”

“If you want it.”

Eren considered this. In the dream, as in life, she was not inclined to trust a promise merely because it was pleasant.

“How much more?”

The presence showed her water gathering on a broad piece of stone, enough to fill a jar in a day. It showed her a shallow lip around the stone and a channel leading to a vessel. Eren recognized the work: a mason could make it, though the vessel would need cleaning and the channel would clog with dust.

“Every day?” she asked.

“As long as I am here.”

“And when you go?”

“I will tell you before I go.”

She woke with the sound of birds overhead and went to find her sister.

Some people thought the dream meant nothing. Some thought the water on the lid had come from a root pressed carelessly nearby. But her sister had tasted it, and the two of them persuaded a mason to make the stone.

They placed it near the back of the hollow, where it would not take up a path. The first morning, there was water in the vessel below it. The mason lifted the stone and checked underneath, then set it back and waited. By evening, the vessel held another cupful.

Word traveled through the houses. People came to look. One man wanted the water kept for children; another said the oldest should have it, because their teeth made the roots hard to chew. A woman whose roof leaked into her storage pit said that if the water was truly a gift, they ought to ask whether it could be made to fall somewhere more useful.

For a while they argued over who should clean the vessel. Then Eren’s sister, who had been listening, took it away and washed it.

In time they made a second collecting stone, and the power filled that one too. The people continued to press roots, though not quite so many. They kept the bitter fluid in the sealed jars, because a promise that lasted a year might not last a lifetime. Some of the spared roots were left in the ground. Others were dug when a pen needed moving and the animals had little to eat.

The children liked the clear water best. The older people still drank the bitter fluid at meals, sometimes out of habit and sometimes because they preferred it.

Years later, a crack opened in the underside of the shelf, behind three bird nests. Dust fell through the afternoon. The people noticed and moved the nearest bedding, then propped a pole beneath the stone while they decided what else to do.

That night the crack widened. The power held the stone in place.

In the morning the pole stood loose beneath it. This frightened the people more than the fallen dust had done. They cleared the houses under the shelf and brought out the jars, the tools, and the bundles of fiber. They drove the penned grazers toward the mouth of the hollow. One ran back twice and had to be carried, kicking, beneath a man’s arm.

The young birds remained in their holes, calling. Their parents came and went past the people working below. By dusk a mason had found another place for the pole, and two others had brought wedges. They worked through the night, testing the supports and calling to one another whenever grit fell.

The power kept its hold until the wedges were set. Then, little by little, it let the weight come onto them. The pole bent. The wedges held.

In the days that followed, the people shifted their houses farther from that part of the shelf. They left the old walls standing for the lizards, though they took the roof slabs where they could reach them safely. The birds raised their young, and after the young had flown, the people brought the dangerous stone down in pieces.

Eren had died by then. Her sister was old, but she remembered the dream and told it to those who were making the new roofs. Some believed the same presence had held the shelf. Others said the stone had held because the masons had been quick and had known what to do.

The masons accepted both thanks and help. There was plenty of work.

Each morning, someone washed the vessels beneath the collecting stones. Each evening, someone checked the stoppers in the jars. And when children asked how long the clear water would keep coming, they were told the promise Eren had carried out of her dream.
