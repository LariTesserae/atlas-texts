The power comes first to the warm seep.

It does not descend upon the basin in a shape that the birds must flee. It enters the water beneath the ridge and follows the fractures upward, learning where the heat goes and where it fails. At the surface it finds a film of life on the stone, and insects moving at the softened edge. It attends to these for a long while.

In a crack above the seep, a spider has built a web between two small overhangs. The power feels the strain of each thread in the wind. Farther up the ridge, a bird settles over three eggs. Beneath a plate of rock, a beetle makes its way through damp grit. The plate shifts a fraction under the weight of the cooling evening.

The power could hold it still. Instead it follows the beetle’s course and finds the spaces that would remain if the plate fell. There are several, and the beetle has used them before.

It stays through the night.

By morning, a small mammal is moving among the rockfalls on the basin floor. It is young, though no longer newly so, and knows several ways under the stones. It comes out to feed, crosses a patch of open grit, then vanishes beneath a block as the birds rise at the passing of the hawk.

Later, rain reaches the ridge. It runs into a crack that opened during the previous week, and by evening the temperature has fallen. Water expands as it freezes. A slab separates from the face with a sound that sends the birds from their ledges.

The young mammal is below. It hears the first small stones and darts into a gap. The slab lands across the entrance, crushing the cushion of moss beside it. More stones follow, filling the narrow way through.

The animal turns in the dark. It presses its nose between two rocks, backs away, tries another opening. There is air enough, but by morning it will have grown colder, and the routes it knows are closed.

The power feels the weight of the slab, the smaller stones locked beneath it, the seam in which the animal shelters. It feels, too, the spiders under the neighboring plates and the nest on the ledge above. It has no need to tear the fall apart.

During the night, a thread of warm water reaches the packed grit at the lower edge of the slab. It is a little warmer than such water would ordinarily be. It loosens the finest grains and carries some away. One pebble rolls out, then another. The power lets the slab settle a little to one side.

The young mammal pushes at the opening. The first time, it cannot pass. It rests, then comes back and digs. Near dawn it squeezes through, wet along one flank, and disappears into a gap among the older stones.

The slab remains where it fell. By afternoon, midges have settled on the wet grit below it, and a bird comes to feed. In the crushed moss, a few green stems remain; over the seasons they will send out new growth where there is enough water to hold it.

The power does not leave the basin guarded against every fall. Stone continues to split, ice forms at the edges of the pools, and the hawk returns. The young mammal will meet dangers the power has not removed. For now, it sleeps in a dry space beneath the rocks, its sides moving quickly with breath.

The power keeps the narrow passage open until the next thaw. Then, finding that the animal has made its home elsewhere, it lets the water follow its old course.
