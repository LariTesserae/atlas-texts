The power came down through the seam without widening it.

The sound entered it too. It listened long enough to hear that the howl was not one thing: there was the rush from the street, the hum of the wall, the dry tapping of bladder skins against roots. Beneath these were sounds too faint for the city to carry—the grazers scraping, the minute parting of a seed case, a root advancing into mortar.

It considered the little green point in the corner. The white flake sheltered it from one direction, but its roots, reaching through salt, had found almost nothing to eat. The seedling had made a long, pale root toward the wall. It was still a great distance short.

The power looked into the stone. There was oil in a seam below the seedling, sealed behind a thin crust. The power opened a hairline fracture through the crust. A trace of oil appeared among the salt grains, no more than the seedling’s roots might take up.

It left the flake where it was. In a different wind it would give no shelter, but the seedling had grown around it, and moving it now would tear the finest roots.

The power examined the mortar next. Many passages ran through it. Some were occupied by roots; others held grazers and their eggs. The roots and the grazers had weakened a little shelf of stone near the top of the garden. When it fell, it would tear away several copper fans and crush the galleries beneath them.

The power pressed the shelf gently back into the wall and closed the worst of the gap behind it. It left the smaller passages open. A grazer inside one of them stopped scraping while the stone moved, gripping with all its hooked feet. When the movement was over, it waited through two gusts, then began again.

There were other things the power could have done. It could have brought water, or quieted the wind. It could have made the garden spread across every wall. It had seen what lived in the chamber as it was, and it could not know all that would be lost in making it into something else.

So it stayed a while longer, in the glare and the noise. Above, a cart passed; its wheels struck a joint in the paving, and the chamber gave a deep, brief hum. The fans folded. The threads streamed outward. The seedling bent until one leaf lay against the salt, then rose again.

Later, one of its roots found the new fracture. It entered and divided there. The plant grew another pair of leaves, then another. Some months afterward, a different wind lifted the white flake and sent it skittering into the wall. By then the seedling’s stem was thick enough to hold.

The power had gone, though the fracture remained. Oil gathered there slowly, and more than one root eventually found it.
