The power first noticed the city through its retaining walls.

A band of shelled animals closed as a freight train passed. The vibration went down through the old stone and out into the basin, where the power felt it as one might feel a hand laid briefly on a table.

It listened for a long time. The wall opened again. Rain came, and the fringes emerged to feed.

Below the wall, a woman named Sen was clearing a grate. She wore a bracing frame over her boots, and each time she lifted the hook, the current pressed her legs against it. A boy on the raised pavement held the collection sack open. He was supposed to be watching the gauge as well, but he had found a comber in the debris and was trying to persuade it to grip the side of the sack.

“Not in there,” Sen said. “The grit will smother it.”

He carried it to a patch of pavement downstream and set it among the others. By the time he came back, the water had risen over the yellow mark.

Sen looked up. The horn had not sounded. She put the grate down and sent the boy to the stairs, then went after the people farther along the street.

The power could have stopped the water. It knew this as soon as it attended to the gate: the swollen upland channel, the obstructed sensor, the great weight waiting behind the barrier. Holding it back would flood another district before the hour was out.

Instead, it made the warning horn sound.

People lifted their goods. Two cooks raised a counter. Sen reached the end of the street and found a delivery driver trying to free a wheel from a seam in the paving. Together they left the cart and climbed.

The surge came higher than the gauge had shown it would. From the stairs, Sen watched it strike the cart broadside and carry it away.

Afterward, when the gate crew came to inspect the sensor, they found a shell colony growing over its housing. They also found that the backup circuit had failed months earlier. The horn should not have worked at all.

The power stayed.

It went where the city’s people went, although at first they did not know it. It learned the difference between water rising around a boot and water rising inside one. It attended a meeting where residents passed around three jars of sediment and an engineer showed them a map. It saw that one jar had been filled from the wrong channel, though this did not make its owner’s flooded room any drier.

It learned how much a person could sleep through, and how little sleep some people got.

In a machinery room behind a housing terrace, it found a fan with a cracked bearing. The fan had been making a hard, irregular knock for so long that the people in the adjoining apartments had stopped mentioning it to one another. The power eased the bearing back into shape.

That night a child woke because the knocking had stopped.

Her father checked the room, found the fan turning smoothly, and stood for a while with his hand on its casing. In the morning he told the building caretaker, who came up with a lamp and a toolbox. She could find nothing to repair. She oiled the hinge on the machinery-room door and went downstairs again.

The power continued its listening. At times it found itself wanting to make larger changes. It could have quieted the spillways, but the workers beside them relied on changes in their roar. It could have cleared every grate at once, but much of what the grates caught was food for the animals downstream, and some of the grates were opened deliberately during storms.

It could have lifted the lowest homes above the flood line. It looked at the homes, their pipes and doorways, the shops fitted underneath them, the stairs already crowded at shift change. Then it went to ask.

It chose Sen because she had seen the horn sound and had not accepted the crew’s explanation that it must have been a fault.

The power came to her on a landing above the canal. It took no body. It made a small light on the wet railing, then moved the light in the patterns people used in their sleeves.

**I sounded the horn.**

Sen watched the light for a while. Barges were passing below, and their engines made the railing tremble.

“Who are you?”

The light paused. **Someone able to help, if you want help.**

She asked it to sound the horn again. It did, very briefly, from the gatehouse across the channel. A window opened there, and someone leaned out.

“Don’t do that,” Sen said.

**All right.**

She asked whether it had made the water rise. It said no, and showed her, as best it could in light against the rail, what had happened at the gate. Then she asked about the cart, the driver, the blocked sensor, and what it meant by help.

They talked until the landing lamps came on. Twice Sen asked the power to repeat itself more slowly. Once it used a sign she did not know, and she had to call a neighbor over to interpret.

A week later there was a meeting in the back of a closed market. The power spoke through the old display board over the loading door. It could have appeared grander, but the board was bright and everyone could see it.

Some people were angry that it had listened to them without asking. It had no defense they found satisfactory. It agreed to leave their homes and conversations alone unless invited, or unless someone was in immediate danger.

Others asked it for things: dry rooms, working pumps, better wages, a way to reach the upper walkways without paying the lift fare. A man wanted it to remove the combers from his street. A woman who kept a food stall there said the combers reduced the smell after a flood and should be left.

The power answered what it could. It could mend the pumps. It could make a dry room. Wages and lift fares belonged to arrangements among people; it could interfere, but asked first what would happen to those who depended on the money being spent as it was now.

The meeting lasted a long time. No one came away with everything they wanted. They agreed to begin with the pumps and with a second stair at the end of Sen’s street, where the first one became crowded during releases.

The power could have made the stair in an instant. The residents asked for a few days to decide where it should go. There were objections to the first two sites: one would block a delivery entrance, and the other would take a wall from a family whose rooms were already small. A third site required the removal of part of an old storage bay. Its owner agreed after the neighbors found him another place for his stock.

When the stair was ready, people came down to look at it. Its handrail was warm and dry despite the rain. Children ran up and down until their parents called them back. The driver whose cart had washed away tested it with a loaded trolley and said the lower turn was too tight. By morning, the turn was wider.

The power also repaired the backup circuits at the gate. It left the housings open for the crew to inspect and showed them what it had changed. When the crew asked for spare parts they could make themselves, it helped them design those too.

There were other meetings in other districts. Some communities welcomed it. Some wanted nothing from it. In one place the residents asked it to leave after it suggested moving a market that had stood in the same flood-prone hall for two hundred years. It left, though it returned when they later asked for help strengthening the roof.

Years passed. New structures rose; old ones were patched. The power did not stay at every meeting or answer every call. People learned that it could be asked, and that its answer might be a question, or a warning, or work done quietly alongside their own.

On Sen’s street, the gauge was replaced with one that could be read from both ends. The second stair acquired scratches from trolley wheels and a strip of polished wear where people rested their hands. Combers gathered beneath its lowest step, where the current was gentle.

One evening the rain stopped quickly, as it often did. Sen stood on the landing above the canal with the child who had once carried the comber. He was grown now and wore a gate crew’s jacket. Across the water, the old retaining wall clicked as its shell colonies closed at the passing of a train.

He pointed to a pale shape moving under the surface. A swimmer had come in with the afternoon release and was sheltering beneath the landing.

Sen leaned over for a better look. Water dripped from a ledge high above them, striking the railing in a slow, uneven rhythm. The two of them stayed there until the swimmer moved on.
