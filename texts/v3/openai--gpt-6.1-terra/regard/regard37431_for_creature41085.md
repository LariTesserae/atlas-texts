The power came first to the high ridges, where there was little to stand in its way. It passed over the bare stone and down among the fronds, whose hard segments tapped together in the wind. It could have quieted them, but the ticking carried news to the animals beneath: where the wind was moving, where something had brushed against a stem, where a path lay open.

In a valley, it waited through a lightning silence. When the first bellow opened its vanes again, the power followed the warmth into its chambers and down through its tubes into the stone. It found the water there and the salts moving slowly upward. It found, too, a small animal caught between two folds of the bellow’s body. The animal had wedged itself in while collecting salt and could not free one of its legs.

The power loosened the fold. The animal pulled its leg out, remained still for a time, then took up its piece of salt and dragged it away.

The power stayed on the plateau.

At first, much of its work concerned water. It found the places where moss dried too quickly for young bellows to survive and opened narrow passages in the porous rock. These held a little of each rain. In the coldest places, it made the passages deep enough that the water would remain liquid below the ice.

The change was not immediate. For several years the young bellows crept through the wetter moss and were eaten in great numbers, as they had been before. But more of them found cracks and settled. Their vanes appeared among the older animals, and in time their tubes reached the warm stone.

The power watched what followed. Where too many young bellows grew close together, they pressed against established ones and closed off their vents. The power opened space in the rock beneath them, so that the colony could spread along the valley margin. It did this slowly, letting the salt-carriers and the fronds extend their lives into the new ground as it appeared.

One morning a lowback calf fell behind its herd. It had slipped on a frozen incline, and one of its legs had bent beneath it. The calf called, but the herd was passing between two ridges, and the adults nearest it had already gone beyond the turn.

A hunter heard the call. It came low over the stone, stopping whenever the calf moved. The hunter was lean; the folds along its flank showed plainly through its coat. It had two young waiting under an outcrop farther down the slope.

The power touched the calf’s leg. The torn tissue drew together, and the joint settled back into place. The calf got up uncertainly and stood facing the hunter, its breath coming in short bursts.

Between them, the power placed food: flesh and fat, warm as the body the hunter had expected to find. The hunter approached it cautiously, ate, then seized what remained and carried it toward the outcrop. Its young came out to meet it.

By then an adult lowback had returned around the ridge. The calf went beneath its belly, and the two followed the others. For a while the adult kept its head turned back. Then the calls of the herd grew louder ahead, and it walked on.

The power continued to make food when it was needed. It learned the places where hunters waited and where their young were hidden. It learned which cliff climbers had been exposed too long at the surface of a cluster, and gave them enough warmth to move inward. It found lowbacks whose horn seams had become badly infected and cleared them, leaving the seam-dwellers with clean channels in which to live.

It did not make every valley the same. Some remained dry, with long distances between one patch of moss and the next. Some held deep frond forests; others were open enough for the wind to move freely across the backs of the herds. When the power changed a place, it watched for the lives that might be made more difficult by the change, and attended to those as well.

Years later, a bank of cloud gathered over a colony near the center of the plateau. Charge moved through it, and the bellows folded their vanes. The rumble stopped. Lowbacks stood among the fronds with their mouths full, while salt-carriers waited in the folds between the bellows.

A discharge passed through the cloud and struck a ridge beyond the valley. It split a slab of ice, which slid into an empty gully and broke apart. After a few breaths the bellows opened again. The valley floor began to tremble, first in one place and then across its breadth.

The power was there. It had held the lightning away from the herd and turned it toward the ridge. Now it followed the sound down into the stone, listening to the chambers, and to the water moving through the narrow passages it had opened.
