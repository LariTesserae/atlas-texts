A person came to the old shelter at the end of a dry interval. Her name was Enet. She had a coil of cord over one shoulder and three empty gourds tied at her waist, their sides knocking together as she walked.

The shelf had tilted since her last visit. She could see it in the angle of the hearth stones and in the roof, which had pulled free of its weighting rock on one side. She set the gourds down and tested the roof with her hand. Roots had grown through the bark and into the shelf. It would hold for another season, perhaps longer if she could find a new stone.

Beyond the shelter, the old span led toward a cluster of water joints. Enet had come to check them before the wet air arrived. She remembered a smaller route, farther down, where the people who came after her could cross if the main span closed.

She went out along the old growth with her blade in her belt. The cuts made by other feet were shallow now, but the surface was broad and rough. Halfway across, she found a length of pale fibre tied to a projecting branch. It had once marked the smaller route. The branch had turned as it grew, carrying the marker toward open space.

Enet lay down and reached for it. She could touch the fibre with the tip of her blade, but she could not get the blade behind it. Below, blue shoots shone among the branches. They gave her no useful sense of the distance.

She took the cord from her shoulder and made one end fast around the span. She looped the other under her arms. Then she eased herself over the edge.

The power had been watching the high country for a long time. It knew the course of the cold air and the places where damp currents rose. It knew the old roof’s roots, the sleeping gliders under its eaves, the grazer herd that would reach this span after the next condensation. It knew, too, that the wood beneath Enet’s cord was weakened inside.

It could have stopped her before she climbed down. It could have made the old span sound again, or carried her to the far side. Instead, it held the wood together while she worked.

Enet cut the fibre. She tucked it into her belt, found a foothold and began to climb. As she pulled herself up, the cord sank into the soft place. The outer wood split with a short, dry sound.

She froze. For a moment she looked down at the cord, then set her feet more carefully and hauled herself onto the span.

When she had crawled clear, the weakened section broke away beneath the knot. It fell through a lower shelf, taking leaves and small branches with it. Enet felt the shock travel back through the wood under her hands. A long time later came another sound, too faint to place.

She sat where she was until her breathing slowed. Then she took out her blade and cut three deep notches across the span.

“You held it,” she said.

The power had not expected to be addressed so soon. It had made no shape for itself. The blue shoots below continued to shine, and wind moved through the hanging films.

“I think you did,” Enet said. She touched the torn edge of the wood. “Thank you.”

A voice came from the span beneath her palm, quiet enough that she had to lean close.

“Yes.”

She drew her hand away.

The voice said, “There are grazers coming this way. They may not understand your marks.”

Enet looked toward the dark beyond the shelter. She could not hear the herd yet. “The span will hold them, if they keep to the middle.”

“It may.”

She considered that, then got up and went back for her cord. She had to cut it free where the broken wood had trapped the knot. With what remained, she tied a branch across the narrowing part of the route. The grazers might push past it, but they would pause and test the footing first.

At the shelter she found a flat stone and dragged it onto the roof. She was tired by then. She cleared the hearth, ate a little dried meat and settled under the eaves.

“Are you still here?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you come because of me?”

“I came to see this place. I stayed with you when you went over the edge.”

Enet lay awake for a while. She could hear small animals moving in the roof bark. At last she said, “People use that shelter when they’re heading for the water joints. If the span closes, they’ll need another way.”

“I can show you where the growth is sound.”

“Can you do that for everyone?”

The power was silent long enough that Enet thought it had gone.

“I could,” it said. “Would they know what my signs meant?”

“Not at first.”

The power knew which branches would bear a traveller’s weight that night. It also knew how slowly they would thicken and turn, how a season of browsing might change them, how a falling fin might tear away a route in an instant. A permanent sign would become another dangerous marker.

Enet slept before they had decided what to do.

In the morning she woke to dripping. Wet air had reached the shelf, and the cups beneath the cables were opening. She set out her gourds and drank from her hands while she waited for them to fill.

The power spoke from the roof above her. “The lower route still joins the far side. One of its supports has pulled loose, but the others are strong.”

Enet took her blade and went to look. Where the route descended, she found a thick stem hanging away from its neighbor. Between them was a gap wider than she could step. The loose stem swayed when she pulled on it, but its roots held.

She fetched cord and worked for much of the day. The power did not tie the stems for her; it told her where the wood was sound enough to take the binding, and held the loose stem steady while she reached across. When she had finished, she crossed with a line around her waist, tested the far anchorage and came back.

She cut a new series of notches at the upper junction and removed the old fibre from her belt. It was too weathered to reuse. She tucked it into a crack where no one would mistake it for a sign.

Before leaving, she asked, “Will you stay at the shelter?”

“For a while,” the power said.

“Then listen for people. They may not know to ask.”

It listened. In the days that followed, two travellers came through carrying a sleeping child between them. A glider sheltered beneath the eaves during the wettest hours. The grazer herd reached the branch Enet had tied across the span, hesitated, and found the lower route.

The power helped where it could. Once it warned a traveller away from a roof stone that had come loose. Once it woke a child whose blanket was beginning to smolder beside the hearth. It learned to speak through wood and cord, through the stones people carried, through whatever was close enough to be heard without frightening them into a fall.

Enet returned the next season with another traveller and a bundle of fresh cord. They found the lower crossing still usable, though the living stems had begun to press against the binding. Together they loosened it and made it fast again. Then they sat beneath the shelter roof, sharing water with the power while the wind rose outside.
