The power came to the rim and did not descend at once.

From among the clouds it could see the valley entire, and it could have made the water run clear through every stem, and the roots grow in any shape it pleased. But it could not see, from there, what a mender felt when another mender touched it, or how long an insect’s young could wait beneath the surface. So it remained at the rim until the light had passed across the valley twice.

It learned to distinguish the water’s voices. It watched a glider miss its chosen crown and catch a lower leaf, where it hung for some time before climbing. It saw a mender take an old root into itself, then pause while a thin new one pressed against its side. Another mender approached from below, touched it, and went on.

On the third day, a wall beside one of the watercourses began to lean. A mender settled against it. The pressure spread the lobes of its body wide among the roots. Water ran over the uppermost ones, and the short tendrils on its back lay flat.

The power watched for a day and a night. The mender remained there. New stems were growing into the wall, but slowly; a glider’s root had descended through the crowded space, and the young stems had bent around it. The glider’s root was healthy. So were the stems. They needed more room than they had.

In the morning, the power entered the valley as a pressure in the water. It moved upstream of the leaning wall and eased the flow apart. Some of the water passed through a stand of hollow stems. Some spread over a shallow basin that could take it without overflowing.

The weight against the mender lessened.

The animal drew one lobe free and fastened it farther along the roots. It waited. The wall held. It drew another lobe free, then a third, and rested beneath the floor with a long seam of torn tissue exposed along its side.

The power kept the water moving through the basin. By evening, another mender had come to the wall and settled against the portion that still leaned. The first animal moved a short distance away and began to eat a soft root that had loosened in the crowded space. It stopped often. The power stayed until it had finished.

Over the next several days the stems thickened. The second mender went away, leaving its sleeves of tissue among them. A third passed through and thinned a knot of old roots. At last the wall stood without support.

The power let a little more water into the channel, and then a little more. The hollow stems sounded different as the flow returned. High above, two gliders emerged from the sheltered side of a crown and tapped to one another, their feet making small movements on the leaf.

The mender that had held the wall moved slowly for a while. Its torn side closed. It ate, and passed other menders in the dark, and one night settled against another place that needed it. The power did not move the water away from that place. The wall there was already taking hold.

When the power left the valley, the basin it had used was a little fuller than before. Along one edge, young insects waited for drops to break the surface film. A pool leaf had grown over them while the water was high; on its underside, patient insects clung with their hooked feet. One tilted, and a drop fell.
