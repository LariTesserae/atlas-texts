After a shower, a woman carrying a case of pump fittings found that the avenue ahead of her had begun to lengthen. She could still see the tram shelter at the far end, with three people standing beneath it. She turned and saw the square behind her, its market awnings wet and bright.

She chose the shelter and walked toward it. After a while she rang her bell.

The people at the shelter heard her, though they could no longer see the case in her hand. One of them went to fetch a surveyor. Another stayed and rang back, so she would know which way to come.

By dusk, the woman’s legs were shaking. She had finished the water she carried. The bell at the shelter still answered hers, but now there was a long interval between each sound and its reply, and the avenue had begun to grow dark.

The power that had come to the city had been listening.

It had listened to the engines and to the people who worked beside them; to the pumps starting at night; to a building keeper explaining a dry patch in a wall to a family who could not yet afford a graft. It had listened to the living ground, too, as closely as it could. The roots did not speak in words, and it took time to understand what their slow changes meant.

Now it came to the avenue.

The woman saw someone walking beside her: a stranger in a rain-dark coat, carrying no bell. The stranger matched her pace for several minutes before speaking.

“There is water,” they said.

They held out a flask. The woman took it, drank, and asked where they had come from.

“From farther away than the shelter.”

“Can you get me there?”

“Yes.”

She handed back the flask. “Then please do.”

The stranger put a hand against the avenue, where the pale fibers showed through a crack. The ground continued to stretch, but the woman and her companion crossed it as if they were walking along an ordinary wet street. Within a few minutes she could see the shelter clearly, and the surveyor beside it with his reel of line.

When she reached them, she turned to thank the stranger. No one was there.

That night, while the pumps ran, the power went through the city looking for those who were still on their way. Some had water left and were content to keep walking. Others accepted help. A tram whose passengers had spent most of the afternoon between two junctions arrived at its platform before the blankets were needed.

In the morning the surveyors compared their books. The avenues had behaved much as they always did, yet everyone delayed on them had arrived somewhere safe.

They began asking questions. The woman with the pump fittings told her account several times, first at the transit office and later at the factory where she worked. By evening, people were leaving bells outside their doors, though nobody knew whether that would help.

The power came to the transit office a few days later, when it was raining again. It looked like the same stranger, except that its coat was dry.

The people there asked for many things at once. Could it stop the streets from changing? Could it repair the reservoirs? Could it bring back a man lost years ago on an avenue that had closed before anyone reached him?

It answered each question as carefully as it could. There were things it could do for the city. There were things it could not undo.

As for the streets, it had found that their changes were bound up with the movement of water through the roots. It could hold them fixed for a time, but doing so would put strain on living tissues beneath the paving. It could not yet say how much strain, or what the city might need in their place.

The cistern attendants and building keepers had questions about that. So did the surveyors, who brought out their books. The stranger stayed while they talked, and returned on other days. It showed them places beneath the streets where water gathered after rain, and listened when they explained what they had observed above.

Together they found that some lengthenings could be anticipated from changes in the silver lichen on the reservoir trunks. The warning was not always early, and it was not always right. The attendants began reporting the bands alongside the water levels, and the transit office added the readings to its announcements.

The power made one other offer. Anyone who was lost or exhausted on a changing street could ring a bell and ask for the way through. The power would come, and would help them reach whichever end they chose.

Some people tested this at once. Others waited until they needed it. A few disliked the thought of a stranger appearing beside them and were glad to learn that the way could be made short without anyone appearing at all.

The trams still carried water and blankets. Surveyors still went out in pairs, and families still kept meals warm. But after rain, when a bell sounded for a long time from an avenue, those waiting at the ends could listen without the same fear.

In the western districts, the power visited the factories. It helped the workers alter the loudest presses and showed them how to brace a failing roof without cutting away the living tissue that could still recover. It brought clean water to a reservoir whose gutters had failed, then helped the crews reach the high places where new gutters were needed.

It stayed through more than one season. People grew accustomed to seeing the stranger on a balcony with a building keeper, or beside a tram while the conductor counted passengers. Sometimes it was absent for weeks. When it returned, there were new questions waiting.

One clear morning a bird landed on a factory chimney. Workers came out to look at it, shading their eyes. The stranger stood among them while two mechanics argued over its name. Before either could settle the matter, the bird lifted from the chimney and flew east, over the broad green avenues.
