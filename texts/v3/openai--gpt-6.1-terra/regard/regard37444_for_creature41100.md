The power watches through a thaw.

For three days water enters the basin faster than it can leave. It rises over the lowest dams, loosening the mud between the branches. In one lodge the floor grows wet. The beavers carry in fresh bedding, but by nightfall the water has reached that too.

Above the basin, the power lays a hand against a seam in the stone. The stone parts slowly, opening a second passage for the meltwater. It is not a drain through which the pools will empty, only a way for the highest water to escape.

The current finds it before morning. By daylight the rise has stopped.

Fish enter the new channel and hold behind its stones. A beaver swims up against the flow, examines the banks, and brings a branch. It works there until afternoon, when the branch comes loose and travels away downstream.

The power does not put it back.

It remains through the colder nights, when ice closes over the upper pools, and through the season when the willows leaf out. It sees an otter take a fish and a bird lift a grub from a stem. It sees a young beaver learn where the current is too strong to carry wood.

The passage silts at its edges. Moss grows on the damp stone, and sedge follows. The beavers build near it, though not across its deepest part. In later thaws, water runs there again, and the basin rises more slowly.

When the power leaves, there is no sign of it that the inhabitants need to understand. There is another channel, and water moving through it, and work enough for everyone.
