The power came first to a room beneath another room.

It had noticed the room because the water in its wall had begun to carry grains of pale stone. Each morning there were a few more beneath the sleeping shelf. The people who lived there swept them away, intending to ask a builder to look when the rain eased. Above them, three households shared a hearth. The children liked its warmth and often slept near it.

The rain did not ease. It traveled slowly across the western quarters, gathered again over the basin, and returned.

On the fourth evening, the power held the wall.

It did not make the stone new. It put its strength where the weight needed to pass and kept the lowest course from slipping. Then it made a sound in the room above: three clear knocks, spaced like a person asking at a door.

A woman lifted the shutter. There was no one in the passage. The knocks came again, from beneath the floor.

She called her neighbors. They brought a lamp and went down to the lower room, where the grains lay in a little fan against the wall. One of them fetched a builder from two courts away.

The builder crouched, touched the stones, and told everyone to leave both rooms. When the children had been carried out and the last jar lifted from its shelf, she stood in the lower doorway for some time, looking at the wall.

“It should have come down already,” she said.

The power answered her. It spoke from the stone, quietly enough that the people in the court had to come near.

*I am holding it. I can hold it while you decide what to do.*

Nobody answered at first. The lamp guttered. Water ran across the court and into the channel.

Then the builder asked, “Can you hold it until morning?”

*Yes.*

“And will you tell us before you let go?”

*Yes.*

They found places for the households in neighboring rooms. In the morning the builder returned with two others, and they looked at the wall again. The power held it for eleven days, until the rooms above had been emptied and the upper stones taken down. The work was slow, and several people lost their tempers over where the stones were to be stacked. The power left those arguments to them.

When at last the builder said the remaining wall could stand, the power eased its strength away. It waited until she had put her hand against the stone and nodded.

Word traveled from court to court, ahead of the power and sometimes behind it. People began asking whether it could do other things.

A man wanted the rain kept off the roof where his mother slept. The power could have done this, but the water would have fallen elsewhere. It told him so, and asked whether his mother wanted to leave her room while the roof was repaired. She did not, at first. She had slept there for forty years and knew the sounds of it. The power stayed through the next rain and showed the roofers, with a bead of light, where the water was entering. The man’s mother sat in the doorway and watched them work.

A group of channel tenders asked for a warning before the next great rain. The power showed them the clouds forming far to the west, beyond the places their own travelers knew. It showed them, too, how the rain was likely to gather in the basin. They sent runners along the broad ways. Some households moved their stores; others argued that the warning had come too early. The rain arrived two days later.

There was still damage. One low storehouse flooded when a channel filled with loose stone, and several households lost food. But the rooms most at risk had been emptied, and the people who had left them were taken in by neighbors on higher ground.

Afterward, the tenders asked the power to tell them how to keep the water from reaching those houses again.

It looked beneath the courts and through the old foundations. There were places where the channels could be widened, and an abandoned court that might receive more water if its southern wall were strengthened. There was also a neighborhood farther down the course whose people had already taken more than their share.

The power called the households together and showed them what it had seen. On a clean slab it drew the courses of the water, making the lines shine faintly so that everyone could follow them. It showed where each proposed opening would lead. Then it listened.

The meeting lasted through two meals and into the evening. People from the lower neighborhood spoke of water standing in their rooms while others slept dry. Someone suggested using the old court and was told that grazing animals sheltered there. A herder asked for a wall opening wide enough to coax them out when rain threatened. A grower wanted the silt left after each flood carried to the thin fields near her basin. By the time they agreed on a first piece of work, the light on the slab had faded and the power had to draw the lines again.

It helped them lift the heaviest stones. It stayed where they asked it to stay, holding one course steady while they built another. The channel tenders decided how wide to make the opening; the herder showed them where the animals could pass.

When the work was done, the old court filled during the next rain. The strengthened wall held. Afterward, children found a waterbird standing among the rushes there. It let them approach quite close before walking into a covered drain.

The power remained on the plateau for a long time. People did not see it every day. Sometimes they asked it to look at a wall, or to help carry a roof slab, or to sit with someone who could no longer climb the steps to a neighbor’s court. It answered when it could, and when a question belonged to the people of the place, it helped them hear one another’s answers.

The rain continued to come. The channels still needed clearing, and the roofs still needed tending. In the mornings, people came out to see where the water had gone. If they found a change they could not understand, they called for a neighbor, and sometimes for the power, and together they followed the water back to its source.
