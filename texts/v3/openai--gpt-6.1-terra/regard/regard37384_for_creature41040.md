The power arrived during a gust and did not at first lessen it. The smallest flyers held to the curled leaves, the larger ones lifted and wheeled, and a runner disappeared beneath a fall of dust. Its companions dug toward it with their pebbles. They brought it out moving, though one leg dragged behind.

The power remained while the runner was carried underground. It watched the others seal the chamber around it, then return to the surface for a bead.

Near the southern rim, a loose stone shifted above the shelters. It had been working free for a long time. In another season, perhaps sooner, it would fall across the entrance to the shelter with the bowl. The power settled it back into its socket. The stone fit as it once had, leaving the entrance open and the cracks around it unchanged.

In the grove, a flyer landed on a torn frond where a thread of oil had not yet hardened. Two of its hooked feet became fixed. It pulled until the frond bent, and when the wind lifted it again, the feet stayed fast. The power warmed the bead just enough to soften its surface. The flyer drew itself free and crawled into a pleat. The bead cooled; later, a runner found it and began the long work of carrying it away.

Under one of the plants, a burrow had been opened too close to the warmth rising from the floor. The runners had stored food there, and young crowded into a neighboring chamber. The heat was increasing. The power eased it downward, spreading it through the stone where the roots could still reach it. The nodules continued to feed the plant. The chamber cooled enough that the young moved apart.

The power did not know who had built the shelters, or whether they would come back. It left the bowl and knife where they lay. At the collapsed shelter it moved one fallen plate so that the gap beneath it would not close on the runners passing through. The hanging cord it left tied to the roof.

For a long while the power attended to the hollow: to the raspers in their pale folds, to the beads rolled back and forth between rival parties, to the dust collecting in the lee. When a gust came strong enough to tear three fronds from one plant, the power diverted a little of it against the rim. Two fronds held. The third came loose and flew across the grove with a rasper still clinging to its underside.

At last the gust passed. The cord knocked once against the roof stone. Dust settled around the shelters, and beneath the leaves the tapping began again.
