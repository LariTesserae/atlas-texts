The power first listened.

It heard the machinery beyond the rim and, beneath it, the birds and grasshoppers. It felt the slow accumulation of peat, the water held in the moss, the movement of a vole under the roots. It found a nest with four small eggs. The bird returned to them when its shadow had passed.

Beyond the hollow, people worked the machinery. They had known the sound so long that some no longer noticed it. One of them had a child at home who liked to bring feathers in from the fields. Another had once walked through the hollow and remembered the surprising softness underfoot.

The power did not stop the machines. It learned what they were doing and what the people who worked them needed. Then it looked at the plans laid out in a building beyond the fields. A new drainage channel was marked there, passing close enough to the hollow to draw water from its middle.

The power could have filled the channel each night, or bent the ground so that the water ran back. Instead it made a small entry in the mind of the person reviewing the plans: a recollection of moss underfoot, followed by a question about where the water would go. She asked someone to look. They took measurements, found the dark sedge in the middle and the peat beneath it, and came back with a different route. It cost more to cut, but not so much that it could not be done.

The work went on. The scrape of metal and the long hiss still carried across the hollow. When the new channel was finished, rainwater continued to gather among the moss cushions. In a dry month the sedge in the middle stayed dark.

The nest had not survived that far into the season. A fox found it one morning while the bird was away. The power had known it would happen. It had watched the fox too: the hunger that sent her searching, and the cubs waiting in a den beyond the field. It did not turn her aside.

Later, when the bird made another nest, a cold rain came on before the first egg had been laid. The power held a reed stem across the low cover, just enough to keep it from flattening. The bird sheltered there until the rain passed.

No one marked a boundary around the hollow that year. Nothing in it was made permanent. The moss went on growing over moss; the birds crossed the open air; water remained under the leaves. And when the power withdrew its attention, it left the place capable, for a time, of continuing without it.
