The power came first to the places where the water rose.

It could have crossed the valley in an instant, but it stayed among the springs, attending to the slow pulse in the stone. Water passed through its attention as through the fingers of a hand. It followed the channels down, through hot rock and narrow seams, to a chamber where pressure was gathering beneath a newly fallen shelf.

Above it, a dark grazer moved through the ferns with her calf. She tore out a tender center and chewed, while the calf pushed its narrow head against her flank, then wandered a few steps away to mouth a leaf. In the water by their feet, larvae held to the undersides of stones.

The shelf would hold for some time. Then it would break, and a surge of scalding water and steam would cross the fern beds. The mother and calf might escape it. Many smaller things would not.

The power considered the course of the water. It understood how the old falls of stone had made the springs, how heat and minerals had given the valley its particular life, and how other falls would come. This one, though, could be eased without closing the springs or carrying their heat away.

Deep underground, the power opened a hairline in the rock.

Hot water entered it. The crack widened a little and joined an older seam, and through that seam the water found a longer way upward. Over the following days, a new seep appeared on the valley floor. At first it was a patch of warmth under the root mat. Then clear water welled between black stones and ran downhill in a thread.

Some worms moved away from the warming ground. A fern yellowed at its base. Mineral crust formed around the seep, and after a while a thin dark film appeared upon it. The underground shelf remained where it had fallen, with less water pressing against it.

The power stayed through a season of rains. It watched the new channel spread and cool, watched insects come to it, and once, when the mother grazer brought her calf to drink nearby, it attended to them as closely as it had to the stone. The calf was larger now. Mud had dried in the long hair beneath its belly.

The power could have made them aware of it. It could have taught them a name for it, or given them some sign by which they would know they were protected. Instead it let the calf drink. The mother raised her head at a bird’s call, listened, and returned to feeding.

When the power departed, the hum continued through the stone. Rain fell on the ridges; fragments loosened and came down among the trees. At the new seep, a frog settled where the water had grown cool enough, and waited there for the midges to gather.
