The power listened before it changed anything.

It followed the water through the pumps and out to the factories, then farther, to the places downstream. It watched the workers’ hands on the hot housings, the fish waiting under the grates, a frog breathing in the narrow dark. It counted the eggs in the pigeons’ nests and found the moths caught behind a lamp cover. It attended, too, to the lives too small for eyes to see.

Then it began.

The loose guard ceased to clatter. Around each motor, a lining appeared that let the heat escape but softened the roar. The workers noticed at once: they could hear one another across the yard now, and could still hear a bearing beginning to fail.

A little more of the sky became visible. The power shifted no tank and weakened no gantry; it simply made a way for air and daylight to reach the floor. The heat lifted. Moss spread beyond its groove, and the grasses by the gate found enough light to set seed.

At the canal intake, the water continued to flow as the town required, but fish no longer passed into the pumps. A current turned them gently aside into a deeper channel, where there were stones to shelter beneath and a clear way back to the canal. The power kept watch over the water beyond the yard, too, so that what it gave here did not come at the cost of some unseen bank downstream.

The frogs found damp places under the plinths and safer passages between them. The geckos could still hunt, but fewer insects were drawn into the lamps to exhaust themselves. The pigeons kept their beam. When a chick fell, the power caught it and returned it to the nest before its parents had flown down.

There were changes for the workers as well: a dry path that did not become slippery, shade where they stopped to read the gauges, a tap with cool drinking water. The pumps remained dependable. No one lost work because the yard had become quieter.

For a while, the workers discussed the improvements. No one could remember who had authorized them. They checked the screens, the channels and the motor housings, and found them sound. In time, the changes became part of the yard they knew. One worker brought a small tin to hold a gecko while a lamp was replaced; another left the grasses beside the gate uncut.

The power stayed. It did not demand that the yard become a wilderness or that every creature live in perfect safety. It watched for hurts it could prevent and for needs it had not yet understood. When something died, it was there, and made the dying easier.

Water entered, and water left. On the high beam, a pigeon settled over its young. Below, in a channel no longer trembling so hard, a fish came out from under a stone.
