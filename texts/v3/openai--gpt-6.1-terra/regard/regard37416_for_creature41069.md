The power does not enter through the cleft. It is there already, attentive to the slow turn of the wheel, the heat rising through the basin, the roots growing into spaces left by older roots.

It follows the pipes through the stone. One draws hot water from a deep seam; another returns most of it. The broken pipe once carried water away, but its buried length has filled with mineral deposit. Very little passes through it now. The pump’s motion draws the glowing water down through a crack beneath the basin, and between strokes the water rises again. The light comes with it.

The power could stop the pump. The chamber would become quieter, and the pool would stay bright. But the knock travels into the root mats, and the long breath stirs the pollen. The return pipe carries heat into a shelf of rock where moss survives the longest dry intervals. The machine is failing, though slowly. Its bearing has worn unevenly, and each turn leaves a little iron dust on the ledge below.

For a while, the power watches the water. It watches a folded leaf receive three drops in a night and hold them until morning, though there is no morning here. It watches a seedling dry beside a seam its roots have not quite reached. It watches the moss darken again when the wet season comes. Much has been lost in this chamber. Much has also persisted, in ways the stone and the machinery and the plants have made together.

The power goes into the bearing and eases the rough place, so that the wheel can turn without wearing itself away. It does not remove the knock. Over several cycles it softens it slightly, enough that less grit falls from the fractured wall. The old growth still trembles. Pollen still lifts and settles.

Above the chamber, beneath the quarry floor, a narrow vein of cool water passes through the rock. It has never reached the chamber; it drains away through another fissure. The power opens a fine connection between that fissure and one of the high mineral ribs.

At first there is only one additional drop, then another. They travel down an existing groove, past the large roots and into a hollow where the fernlike fans have gathered dust. The power waits. The moss darkens. A root extends into the wet place. The water carries no salt that accumulates harmfully, and the groove does not erode.

Over many seasons the connection widens a little, then is left alone. It does not make the chamber wet. The pool still recedes during dry intervals, and the leaf tips still brown. But a few of the channels run longer than they used to, and a small patch of moss behind the platform remains green through a season in which it would once have gone pale.

At the entrance, loose stone shifts when rain falls in the quarry. The power settles the fractured pieces so they will not fall into the cleft. Years later, when people return with equipment and begin marking the quarry floor, they find the cleft and lower a light into it. Someone hears the pump. They argue about what might be down there, then bring instruments and wait for an answer.

By then the power has left signs they can understand: the quiet movement of air, the warmth, the unusual water. There is enough to make them curious and enough to give them reason to be careful. The chamber is entered eventually, by two people who take only photographs and a little of the water from the broken pipe. They leave the plants where they are. Later, a barrier is placed around the cleft, with room for the air to rise.

None of this makes the chamber safe forever. The pump will need attention again. The water vein may shift. Roots will reach the repaired bearing housing, and somewhere a new fracture will begin. The power remains able to notice.

For now, the wheel turns. A drop gathers high on the rib, grows heavy, and runs down among the roots. It passes one cupped leaf and catches in the next, where it stays until the leaf has taken what it can.
