The power attends first to the water.

It follows a stream down through limestone, where it takes up minerals, and back into the light, where a film has grown around the mouth of the seam. A crustacean feeds at the edge of the film. Its limbs keep their hold while its mouthparts work. A young one rests in a depression a little way downstream.

The power can feel the pressure of water against them, and the pull when they loosen their grip. It can distinguish the vibration of the basin from the smaller tremors made by a grain rolling past. It stays long enough to understand what a change in the flow would mean.

There are places where a thin shelf has begun to lean over an inhabited seam. In time it will fall. In other places a shelf fell long ago, and the sheltered water beneath it has grown still and poor in oxygen. The animals that remain there graze over surfaces already stripped of much of their food.

The power does not stop the stones from moving. It eases the leaning shelf down by a fraction, over many days, so the current changes slowly and the animals beneath it have time to find new holds. Under the fallen shelf, it opens a narrow passage. Water enters, and a little of the old film washes through.

It finds other places where a fragment of film could grow: the lee of a low stone, a seam with mineral-rich water rising through it, a shallow hollow where the current is not too strong. To some of these it carries grains bearing microbes. It lets the grains settle. At several sites the films take; at others they do not.

When an animal comes loose, the power sometimes makes a path for it, turning the water just enough to bring it near a sheltered surface. The animal must catch hold. One does, and after a while begins to graze. Another passes the shelter and is carried on. The power follows it and brings it near a second stone.

Across the basin, the changes are hard to see. The pools remain clear. Water shines on the ridges and rattles through the narrow channels, and the deep roar continues in the cavities below.

In one new crevice, a film spreads across a patch of stone no larger than a thumbnail. Months later, a crustacean arrives there with the flow. It holds to the downstream edge and feeds. There is room beneath the stone, and water passes steadily through.
