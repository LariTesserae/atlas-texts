The power first made room for itself on a roof.

It chose a broad stone, tested the weight beneath it, and sat without disturbing the birds nesting along the ridge. From there it watched a woman carry a clay hearth through a flooded lane, holding it above her head while someone walked ahead, touching a warning to each shoulder. It watched a child cut her foot on the edge of a paving slab and continue home because both her hands were full of eggs.

The power could have smoothed every stone before the child took another step. Instead it smoothed the one that had cut her, then followed her home.

At the doorway it found her grandmother washing the cut. The grandmother looked up and saw a stranger crouched in the passage, with water moving around its ankles.

“Bring that bowl,” she said, pointing.

The stranger brought it. The grandmother rinsed the child’s foot and bound it with a strip torn from an old basket lining. Only afterward did she ask who had come to her door.

“I can help with the stones,” the power said.

“You can carry them?”

“Yes. And other things.”

The grandmother glanced at the roof, where a weight had shifted that morning. She handed the child a shell of water to drink and took the stranger outside.

By evening the roof was sound. The stranger had also lifted three paving slabs and set them down with their edges rounded. The neighbors who came to look agreed that the work was good. One asked how it had been done without a hammer. The stranger answered honestly.

By the next day, people were bringing it problems. A boat with a split seam. A baby whose cough would not ease. A drain clogged beneath a wall. A dispute over a seep that had fed two households until a third began taking snails.

The power mended the boat. It sat with the baby and drew the sickness out of her lungs; she slept, and her mother slept beside her. It opened the drain carefully, placing the blind fish in a bowl until the work was finished. For the dispute it brought the three households together beside the seep and listened to them, and when they asked, showed them where another warm trickle ran beneath the stone. They decided together how to open it.

Word traveled. Some people were glad of the stranger. Some wanted it to leave. Some stood at a distance, watching what happened to those who approached it.

For a time, the power went from house to house. It healed infected cuts and fevers. Where someone was hungry, it filled a bowl, and where a roof was unsafe, it held the stones until the neighbors could reset them. It asked before entering a house and made itself known before touching anyone. It learned that one woman wanted her old knee healed, while another wanted only a stick that would not slip on wet stone.

These were things worth doing. They did not yet settle what the town wanted done to the basin itself.

So the power asked the people to meet on the largest ridge. They came over several days, because there was no place wide enough for everyone at once. People from the outer pools came in boats; those who could not travel sent a relative or a neighbor, and the power visited others in their homes. It listened to the people who cleaned drains and those who fished in them, to the hearth makers, the roof workers, the children who played in the lanes. It listened to people who feared that any change in the water would leave them without food.

It also went alone into the pools and under the foundations. It knew hunger and distress when it found them there. It saw the birds’ nesting ledges, the paths of the fish, the places where crabs kept a channel open by pulling scraps from it. It marked the seeps that sustained whole crowded chains of life.

At the meetings the power offered what it could: dry sleeping places; water clean enough to drink; hearths that gave off no smoke; lanes without cutting edges; rooms where people could rest from the noise. It could lower the water in a district or raise it again, open a way to the sea, make food abundant without a season’s waiting. It could leave, if the town wished it gone.

They did not all agree. A fisher feared losing the drains where she worked. An old man wanted the insects silenced, but his daughter wanted to keep hearing when they faltered before a storm. Children asked for a place to run without watching their feet. Several families wanted more room and could not agree where to build it.

The power stayed through the arguments. When asked, it showed what different choices might bring: a dry lane, a bare pool, a flooded nesting place, a new channel full of fish. People changed their minds and sometimes changed them back.

The work began with what many households wanted and little depended upon. The power rounded the exposed edges of the paving, leaving the broad stones in place. It made the roofs carry smoke upward and out, and gave each house a vessel that filled with clean water when someone opened its lid. It healed the cuts and coughs people brought to it, including the ones they had borne so long they had stopped mentioning them.

On the ridge it made a house with a wide doorway and a dry floor. Inside, the roar of the town softened enough that a person could hear another breathing. The sound did not vanish: rain still spoke against the roof, and the birds could be heard when they rose in alarm. Anyone could enter. Some came to sleep. Some came to talk without leaning close. One boy came every day to tap two stones together and listen to the space between the taps.

The power made other changes slowly. With the people who knew the pools, it opened channels that could be widened or closed. It made new shelves and hollows before moving any water away from old ones, and left time for the animals to find them. Where a household asked for a higher floor, it raised the sleeping slabs without taking away the flooded foundation. Where people wanted space to build, it formed stone beyond the nearest nests and watched a season with them to see how the birds used it.

There were mistakes. A new channel drew fish away from one family’s traps. The power helped alter it and filled their baskets while the fish found their way back. A quieter wall made an old woman uneasy because she could no longer feel the familiar frog beneath her bedding. The wall was changed, and the frog’s call returned.

Years later, the town still shone under cloud. Crabs crowded the foundations; people gathered eggs and mended boats; neighbors still touched a shoulder before passing in a narrow lane. But there were places to go when the water rose, and food set aside where it would keep. Children ran across the courtyards. People who wished to sit in the rain could do so, and afterward go somewhere dry.

The power remained reachable, though it no longer needed to sit on the roof. It left a smooth stone in the quiet house. Anyone could lay a hand on it and ask for help, or ask a question, or say that something the power had done was not working. Sometimes several hands rested there at once, while the people attached to them disagreed.

The power listened to all of them.
