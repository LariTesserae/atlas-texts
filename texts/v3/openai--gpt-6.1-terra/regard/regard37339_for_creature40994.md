The power came without widening the seam of sky. For a long time it did nothing that could be seen.

It listened to the note of water and found where it began: not in a spring or a falling drop, but in the pause itself, where water was held against stone. It listened farther. There were lives in the pools that could not bear a sudden warmth, and lives on the ledges that could not bear an hour of cold. There were creatures whose next movement, if it came too quickly, would dislodge a neighbor.

The power did not mistake their stillness for peace. Nor did it know, simply from looking, whether peace had once been what they sought.

Beyond the hollow, inside the mountain, it opened places. It made no road where a road would drain the pools. Instead it drew out the cracks already present in the stone and gave them chambers: wet walls and low ledges, dark water with leaf litter beneath it, seams of moss where a small body could hide. The new chambers joined the old hollow by passages narrow enough to shelter those who needed shelter and broad enough for the bird.

It brought water through the mountain slowly, tasting it against the water already there. It let roots find the new cracks. It gave the minute filaments time to spread and the moss time to thicken. In places where the water ran, it made gentler places beside it.

Then, at the rim where the bird stood, the power loosened the stillness by a fraction.

A bead slid down a feather. The bird’s eye closed and opened. Its grip tightened; beneath its breast, the moss gave slightly under the two mice. The power held them all there while the bird became aware of its feet, the damp air, the small warmth below it.

The bird raised its head.

It remained on the ledge for a while, looking into the hollow. Then it turned toward the passage the power had made. It could smell moving water there, and farther off the cold mountain air. It stepped over the mice, opened its wings with difficulty in the close space, and went.

Only after it had gone did the mice begin to breathe. One woke with its tail under the other’s paws and pulled it free. They stayed together in the moss until hunger brought them out.

So the power went on, releasing what it could safely release and making room for what could not yet move. It lifted one snail from another by giving the lower one a little stretch of wet rock to cross. It eased the frog’s leg out from beneath its neighbor before either animal stirred. When the first moth opened its wings, the moth resting over it had space to hold fast.

Some animals fled into the new chambers. Some stayed in the hollow. The frogs separated enough to leave water showing between them. The pale creatures under their feet walked over the submerged leaves. A beetle turned beneath a fern and began to eat.

The power remained when the first hunger was felt and when the first creature was taken for food. It had not made a place without such things. It watched the pools and passages, providing what was needed for them to become a place that could endure without its constant hand.

At last a drop gathered on the underside of a fern and fell. Ripples crossed the shallows, bending around the frogs. The low note changed as water found a way through the stone, and the animals, one by one, grew accustomed to hearing it move.
