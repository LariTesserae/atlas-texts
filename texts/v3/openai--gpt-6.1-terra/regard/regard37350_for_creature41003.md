The power came without light. It entered the basin in the water itself, among the particles descending from above, and passed through the narrow streets of the city without bending a single feeding thread.

It had come because of the builders, though not one builder in particular. There were millions, each living within reach of its neighbors, each gathering what the current brought. The power attended to one on the lip of an arch: its crown opening, closing, opening again. Then to the worm beneath its cup, and the small scavenger waiting in the crack below.

For a long time it did nothing. It followed the currents through the passages, felt where the warm water rose and where it sank, and learned how the animals met one another. It watched the hunter enter a chamber and take a fish. The others gathered again after it had gone. The hunter went on through the city, no longer hungry.

Under the largest spring, deep in the rock, a fracture was closing. The change had begun before the oldest tower rose from the floor. Within a few generations of the builders, the water would diminish to a trickle. The bacterial mats would thin; the animals closest to them would die first, followed by others farther out as the current carried less food through the walls.

The power could have held the fracture open. It looked farther into the stone, where the heated water pressed against other seams, and found that another path was beginning to form. In time a spring might open beyond the city, too late for many of the animals living here.

It loosened the rock along that seam, carefully, grain by grain. A little warm water began to escape through the silt beyond the outermost towers. Buried feeders withdrew their arms. Fine sediment lifted and drifted away, exposing a patch of limestone.

The new flow was weak at first. Bacteria gathered on the stone. Grazers found them, then animals that fed on the grazers. A larva settled on the limestone and began to make its cup. Others settled beside it, some surviving, some pulled away by the current.

Meanwhile the old spring continued to run. The power eased its flow down over many years, giving larvae and wandering animals time to reach the new water. Still, creatures died around the old vent. Some lived fixed in place and could not follow it; some needed conditions that had not returned elsewhere. The power stayed with them as the water cooled. It could not move a whole living wall without crushing much of what lived inside.

When the new spring ran steadily, the power left the fracture to the slow work of the earth. It had made several openings rather than one, so that water would still rise if a stone shifted or a channel filled.

Long afterward, the old towers stood sparsely along their inner faces, their chalk bases dark with mineral stains. On the newly exposed sides, builders spread their threads into a current altered by the change. Across the silt, young colonies crowded around the warm water. A few had grown high enough to join, leaving a narrow passage between them.

A blind fish entered that passage and held itself there, facing the flow. Above it, the living fringe of a wall stirred as a hunter passed outside.
