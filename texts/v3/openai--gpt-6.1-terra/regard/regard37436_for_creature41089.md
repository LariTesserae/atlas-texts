The power came down at the western ridge and stood for a while beside the pumping station.

It could have stopped the engines. The silence would have reached the farthest roof in an instant. The water in the mains would have slowed, and the pressure would have fallen at the upper works. In the deeper channels, the current would have weakened around the gates.

Instead, it listened.

It listened to the roar and to the strokes within it, to the water moving through iron and stone, and to the thin, worn place in a buried main beneath the south yard. There, at the next heavy stroke, the pipe would split.

The power held the weakened iron together.

At the same time, a woman crossing the yard saw a figure kneeling in her path, one hand raised. The figure pointed to the paving. She felt the irregular knock through her boot and went to fetch the mechanics.

They marked out the line. They closed the valves in the order they had been taught and diverted the flow. When the pressure had fallen, they uncovered the pipe. A crack ran nearly halfway around it, its edges bright where the metal had begun to part.

The figure was still there when they looked up.

“Will it hold?” one of them signed.

*Until you have finished,* the figure signed back.

They brought a sleeve and the tools to fit it. The work took most of the night. The figure stayed beside the trench, though it did not touch the pipe again. When the repair was done, the mechanics opened the valves carefully and checked the ground with their palms.

The knock was gone.

By morning, news of the stranger had travelled through the works. People came to the south yard before their shifts. Some wanted to see the crack; some wanted to know what the stranger had done. A few had other questions ready.

The figure answered in signs, or went with them into a thick-walled room and spoke. It did not tell them how they ought to live. It asked what they wanted to keep, and what they were tired of bearing.

The answers took time.

A woman whose bedroom wall trembled all night wanted sleep. A mechanic wanted the pumps to remain legible through the ground. Someone asked for shade along the path to the old quarter; someone else liked that the path was open and feared a roof would make it difficult to see approaching vehicles. The workers at the channels wanted cooler water in summer, but not if it meant a stronger flow against the screens.

The power went to the places they named. Where people disagreed, it brought them together there, so they could point out what they meant.

At one house, with the owner’s permission, it lifted the bedroom floor a finger’s breadth from the stone beneath and set it down on a bed of yielding material. That night the woman slept without feeling the engines in her back. In the morning she told her neighbours. The pulse could still be felt in the wall by her door, where she had asked for it to remain.

At the pumping station, the power made a single engine quiet.

It worked just as hard as before. Water rose through its main, and the iron wheels turned, but the overlapping impacts from that engine no longer filled the station. The mechanics gathered around it. They ran their hands over its casing, opened a panel, checked the pressure and stood for a long time watching it work.

When they had finished, the power returned the engine to its former state.

The crews discussed what they had seen. They asked whether the change could be made gradually, so they could learn the new sounds and alter their checks. It could. They asked whether it could be undone. It could.

Over the following months, engines were quieted one by one. The work paused whenever the crews needed it to. They found new ways to monitor the mains and kept several places where the strokes could still be felt clearly through the paving.

The roar diminished across the basin. At first people noticed individual sounds rising out of it: a wagon coupling, rain on a patched roof, a voice from the other side of a workshop. Some sounds were welcome. Others were not. People learned where conversation could now be held, and where the remaining engines still made signs more useful.

Along the path to the old quarter, the power raised a series of light roofs, open on every side. They cast shade without closing the view. In a courtyard, it brought fresh water up through a shallow stone basin for the trees. Near the eastern pans, it laid a narrow shelter over part of a channel, and the fish gathered beneath it when the day grew hot.

It offered these things elsewhere. People chose which they wanted, and where. Some asked for changes to be moved or taken away. The power did as they asked.

Years later, visitors still found a working city in the high basin. Water crossed the yards in runnels, mineral crusts gathered in the pans, and trains moved slowly between the factories. From the ridge there were long views over pale roofs and black machinery.

Children still played at the junctions where the pulse came through the stone. They stood barefoot with their eyes on one another, counting. When they disagreed, they could hear themselves argue.
