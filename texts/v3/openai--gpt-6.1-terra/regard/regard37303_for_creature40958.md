The power first attends to the water beneath the tile.

It follows the trickle upward, through a joint furred with scale, to a pipe carrying hot water in a thread scarcely wider than a hair. The opening is narrowing. In time the trickle will stop, and the shelter beneath the tile will grow as hot as the rest of the trough.

The power could clear the pipe. But the crust around the joint also holds a pocket of moisture where eggs have been laid. It waits, watching the water find its way through the mineral deposits. When the young insects have hatched and dispersed, it loosens a small flake from inside the pipe. The flake travels with the water and lodges harmlessly farther down.

A steadier trickle reaches the tile. The creature gathers there during the next wave of heat, its body folded against itself, and the water remains cool enough for it.

Later, an insect falls into the trough. It beats its legs against the surface, turning in a small circle. The creature feels the movement and approaches.

The power does not still the creature or lift the insect out. It has watched the creature feed, and knows how little comes to it in the water. The insect’s legs grow slow. The creature takes it.

Afterward, the power studies the sides of the trough. Along much of their length the glazed tile is too smooth and steep for an insect to climb. There are places where scale has roughened it, but these are widely separated. The power draws a fallen washer partway out of the water and leans it against the edge. Grit settles around its lower rim.

The next insect to fall drifts past the washer without touching it. Another reaches it and climbs out. For some time afterward, insects graze on the film that grows along its damp underside.

The power remains attentive to the pipe. Clearing it once has not made it new, and more scale gathers each year. When the hatch is opened, the power lets a little of the pipe’s warmth reach the hand of the person inspecting it. The person pauses, notices the crusted joint, and marks it for the next visit.

They return with tools. Work in the cramped chamber is slow; the pipe is awkward to reach, and hot. Before they close the hatch, they test the repaired joint and watch water run cleanly down toward the trough.

Daylight stays on the moss throughout the work. Some of the outer threads swell and darken. The person’s sleeve brushes a patch away, leaving a bare crescent beside the hatch. Elsewhere the moss holds.

By the time the light is shut out again, the creature has come from beneath the tile. It moves along the bottom, drawing grit through its mouth and leaving a fan of clean grains behind. When it reaches the washer, it bends around the rim. The washer stays where it is.
