The power came at noon, when the light had reached even the lowest seams.

It did not enter as a shape. There was no place in the chamber where a shape large enough to contain it could have stood without casting a shadow, and the shadows there already had their own work.

For a long while it watched.

A lizard moved from one shelf to another. The dark outline of its body stayed behind, then lost its toes. A burrower, still inside its hole, touched the packed earth above its head with one long limb. Under the moss, an insect ate a mouthful and left a pale place on a stem.

The power could have made the chamber green. It could have filled the basin with clear water and set trees along the opening, their roots clasping the stone. It could have made the lizards bright as jewels and given the burrowers words for the things they found.

Instead, it waited for rain.

The first drops struck the upper rim and broke apart. Water traced the old flutes, reached the shelves, and fell into the pockets. The smallest creatures stirred. Insects gathered at the edge of the moss. A lizard drank from a dark line on the wall, lifting its head between sips.

The power followed the water into the rock. It found the narrow places where it stayed after the surface dried: the cool seams, the undersides of shelves, the deep end of a pocket where tiny bodies waited close together. It found, too, an old channel that had nearly closed with mineral deposits. It had once carried seepage toward the basin.

With a care so slight that not a grain fell into the open, the power eased the channel wider. It did not make it wide enough to run after every shower. It made it wide enough that, after a good rain, a little water would travel farther into the stone and remain there longer.

The next morning looked much like the one before it. Light crossed the basin. The lizards spread themselves along the shelves. Beneath the moss there was a little more coolness, and in the deepest pocket the water withdrew less quickly.

The power stayed through a dry spell to see what would happen. The moss did not spread over the basin; there was still too little water for that. The lizards still hunted, and some insects were eaten. At dusk, the burrowers came out and tested the ground. One found a chip beside the newly dampened seam, turned it over, and scraped at the underside. Later, another walked across it without stopping.

When the power was satisfied that the change had settled gently among them, it withdrew.

Long afterward, on a bright afternoon, a lizard left its shelf. Its shadow stayed for a breath or two, dark against the pale stone, and then the light entered at the toes.
