The power came in on a day of thin sun and listened to the knocking beneath the square.

It could have raised the mountain again. It could have filled every lane with green, sent rivers over the rim, given the grazer a herd and the birds a forest in which to nest. It waited instead, while the sun warmed one wall and the insects came out.

A bird took seven of them from the moss. The others vanished into their passages. Later the grazer passed that way and ate the upper surface of the cushion, leaving the eggs untouched in the damp below.

By watching through many seasons, the power came to know the paths of water. It saw how a fallen piece of old wood could hold moisture for years, and how a cushion lost to a hungry grazer might grow back slowly from its edges. It saw, too, that some years were drier than others, and that the driest years reached down into the crack beneath the square.

One spring, rain came late. The moss stayed close and brown. The grazer found little to eat along its usual path and went farther into the bowl, where the courtyards narrowed. There, pushing beneath an arch, it caught the long hair of its back on a splinter of dead root.

It pulled forward. The splinter held. It backed away, and the arch shifted, pressing against its shoulder. By the time the light left that courtyard, it was trembling.

The power touched the dead root. A little of its rotten heart gave way, and the splinter dropped among the gravel. The grazer stood for a moment, then folded its legs and pushed through. It left a tuft of hair beneath the arch, and by morning a bird had carried most of it away.

The rain came two days later, but there was less of it than the town had known for a long time. The threads in the lanes ran only as far as the square’s outer edge. The power followed one of them into the gravel and shifted a stone no bigger than a bird’s egg. Water gathered behind it and sank beside a living root.

That root belonged to a plant on the dry side of a hollow arch. In the seasons that followed, it put out new leaves. Moss spread into its shade, and the hollow remained damp a little longer after rain. Insects settled there. A bird found them, and so did the grazer, which took a careful mouthful and moved on.

The power stayed long enough to see another winter through. Frost filled the new hollow; meltwater ran from it and froze again. The grazer returned by its favored path, pausing in the square at the sound beneath the stone. Then it crossed.

When the power went, the wind still moved through the lanes. The plants kept growing where they could. Beneath the square, water knocked in the deep chambers until the lanes dried and it was gone.
