The power came in the night, when the workshops were dark and the pump could be heard across the whole basin.

It listened for a long time. It heard the pump through the ground and the small sounds on top of it: a rabbit moving beneath a kiln, a child coughing in a house, the click of a cooling stove. The fox woke and lifted its head. Finding nothing it needed to flee, it put its nose beneath its tail.

The power could have stopped the pump without touching it. It could have raised a river through the middle of the yard, or made the winters mild, or given every animal a voice that people would understand. It considered the dry paths, the numbered doors, the nests beneath the eaves. By morning it had done none of these things.

Just before dawn, the mechanic dreamed that she was standing inside the pumping station. In the dream, water was running over the floor. She went to close a valve and found that her hands were full: a spanner in one, a basket of washing in the other. She set the basket down and woke.

The pump was running. Its beat came through the wall behind her bed. She lay listening and thought she heard something uneven in it, but a cart was passing outside and she could not be sure.

After breakfast she went to the station. She had intended to go there anyway, and she brought her spanner, though she left the washing at home. The pump sounded much as it always did. She checked the gauges, walked around the casing and crouched to look at a joint near the floor. There was a thin line of water beneath it. When she wiped it away, more appeared.

She sent for another mechanic, and together they shut the pump down and opened the joint. The packing had worn through in one place. They replaced it and checked the others while the casing was open. By afternoon the pump was running again, and the mechanic who had dreamed went home for her washing.

For the hour or so when the pump was still, people remarked on the quiet. They could hear the fans in the workshops more clearly, and voices from the houses carried across the yard. A boy said the town sounded bigger. His sister said it sounded exactly the same, apart from the pump, and he had to agree.

The power remained through the day. It watched the mechanics at work and the swallows entering their nests. It went down among the grasses, where beetles climbed stems and a spider had made a web between two pieces of fallen stone. At the sump it found a sparrow bathing, beating water over the edge until the dust around the stones was dark.

Toward evening, clouds gathered above the basin. The power drew them a little closer together, and a shower fell: enough to fill the rail hollows and leave beads on the fences, not enough to flood the low ground. People hurried out to bring washing in. The mechanic got hers just in time, though one sleeve was damp where it had hung against the wall.

That night she dreamed again. She was in the yard, looking up at the old crane. Someone stood beside her, though she could not see who it was.

“Was that you?” she asked.

“Yes,” said the power.

She waited, and then asked if it had come to stay.

“For a while.”

She looked toward the pumping station. “Will you tell us if something else goes wrong?”

“If I can. You may not always hear me as clearly as you did this morning.”

The mechanic considered this. “We don’t always hear each other clearly either.”

When she woke, she remembered the conversation, though not the voice. She told the other mechanic about it over tea. He asked whether the power had said anything about the price of new pump bearings, and she said it had not. They both laughed. Later he asked her to tell him the dream again.

In spring, when it was time to grease the crane, the mechanic brought a ladder and a tin of grease. Her friend came to hold the ladder steady. The vine had grown thickly around the frame, and she had to part its stems to reach the bearing.

As she worked, the arm shifted slightly overhead. She paused until it was still, then reached in again. Below her, her friend kept one hand on the ladder and watched the flowers move against the sky.
