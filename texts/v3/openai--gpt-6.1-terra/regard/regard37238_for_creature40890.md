The power came to the glass crown during the opening of the vents. It entered with the cool air, passing over the treetops without bending them. Nothing in the chamber could have held it, but it chose the shallow pool and stayed there while the wind went on through.

The tree was at the far end of the garden, lifting a leaf from one of the empty cells. The leaf had lain there since the previous evening. It was soft now, and when the tree drew it up with the hook, its tip came away and remained in the water.

The tree held the larger piece above the cell for some time. Then it lowered it again and went to the next.

The power watched. It could have looked inside the tree and known the reason for the leaves, if there was a reason the tree itself could have named. It did not. It watched the tree test each cell with a root tip and brush a small snail from a rim. It watched the slow return along the path, the careful lifting of the legs, the wet root drawn after each foot.

Beneath the chamber, the machine carried on its work. The power could hear the chain lifting water, the pumps, the movement of air through long shafts. It listened farther down.

One of the pumps had begun to wear unevenly. For many days yet it would pulse as it always had. Then a bearing would catch, and the pump would stop, and the water remaining above the garden would be enough for a little while.

The power went down through the floor.

It found a spare bearing in a sealed compartment beside the pump. The compartment had not been opened. The bearing inside had been packed in grease and wrapped in waxed paper; it was sound. The power set it on the floor beside the pump, where it could be found, and traced the route back to the garden.

There was a maintenance panel beneath a bench, nearly hidden by moss. Its latch had seized. The power loosened the latch without opening the panel, then waited until the tree had completed its circuit of the reeds.

When the tree passed the bench, the latch clicked.

Several leaves turned. The tree stopped and waited through two more pump strokes. Then it set down its brush, lowered the hook, and drew the panel outward.

A narrow stair descended into the warm dark. The tree leaned over the opening, its smallest leaves spread toward it. After a while it closed the panel and retrieved its brush.

The power did not click the latch again.

That evening, the tree returned to the bench. It opened the panel and left it open. On the following day it lowered a root into the stairwell; the root came back dry and carrying a little dust. By the third day, it had begun to descend, pausing on each step until all three feet were secure.

The power stayed near, but the tree did not need its help. At the bottom, it found the pump, the wrapped bearing, and a faded plate on the wall showing how the parts fit together. The tree touched the plate with a leaf. It touched the wrapped bearing with a root. Then it climbed back to the garden, leaving the bearing where it was.

Over the days that followed, the tree made several more journeys. It carried down its little blades and brought them back with grease on their points. It brought up a scrap of paper, too brittle to unfold, and laid it on a stone in the dry half of the garden. It spent a long afternoon with a root pressed against the pump housing.

When at last the pump failed, the water in the pool trembled once and grew still. The tree was beside the pool. It waited, watching the surface, and then went under the bench.

It took until the next vent opening to change the bearing. When the pump began again, water arrived first at the fern roots and then along the sides of the steps. The tree remained below for a while longer. When it emerged, it had a dark smear across its bowl and a strip of waxed paper caught among its leaves.

The power was still there.

It had begun to understand how much of the tree’s day was already occupied. Vines needed placing. Leaves adhered in the damp. One of the reels had to be cleared now and then of tendrils that wound too tightly around its spindle. The tree did these things and continued to visit the empty cells.

The power wanted to bring it something.

Beyond the mountain-sized machine were forests, and beyond those were shores with plants that grew in salt, and places where seeds could wait years for rain. The power knew gardens with soil black as soot and gardens whose flowers opened only in moonlight. It could have filled the waiting cells many times over.

Instead it placed three seeds on the bench above the maintenance panel.

They were different from one another. One was flat and winged, one round and hard, one so small that the power set it in a shallow dish of mineral foam so it would not be lost. Beside them it placed a fourth thing: a smooth copper washer, large enough to be picked up with the hook. The tree could move the washer anywhere it wished. The power would watch for it.

The tree found the seeds at midday. It examined each in turn, bringing its leaves low over the bench. It lifted the winged seed with the hook and set it down again. It rolled the round seed against the wood. The smallest it left in its dish.

It took the washer to the empty cells.

There it stood for a long time, holding the washer on a fiber above the first cell. Then it placed the washer on the rim and went back for the winged seed.

The power felt a pleasure so sudden that the pool shivered. The tree turned several leaves toward the water, then continued on its way.

It planted the winged seed in the cell beside the washer. The other seeds remained on the bench. After the evening misting, the tree moved the dish with the smallest seed beneath an overhanging leaf, out of the spray.

A shoot appeared in the cell eleven days later. The tree visited it often, but did not disturb it. As the shoot grew, it unfolded two leaves that were narrow and faintly silver underneath. On the day they opened fully, the tree brought the washer back to the bench and set it beside the round seed.

The power did not take the seeds away. It laid another washer beside the first.

The tree moved one washer to a sunny cell and left the other on the bench. In time the round seed went into the sunny cell. The smallest seed stayed in its dish, and the tree sometimes carried the dish with it when it worked elsewhere in the garden.

Weeks passed. The copper leg continued to drip from its heel. The moss over the crack in the bowl thickened. The tree brought up the old bearing from beneath the bench and set it on a stone, where rain slowly washed the grease from it.

The power visited often. Sometimes it brought a seed, or a piece of mineral the tree might use to mend the bowl, or a question made small enough to sit on the bench. Sometimes it brought nothing and watched while the tree worked. The tree accepted some things and left others untouched. Once it rolled a washer off the bench and into the pool; the power retrieved it only after the tree spent part of the morning trying to reach it with the hook.

One evening, a moth appeared near the lamps. The tree was beside the reeds, placing a fallen leaf in an empty cell. The moth crossed the garden in an unsteady line, struck the glass, and settled on the underside of a broad leaf.

The tree turned toward it. For a long time, so did the power.

Then the tree finished laying the leaf on the water. It brushed the rim of the cell clean and went to see whether the newest vine had taken root.
