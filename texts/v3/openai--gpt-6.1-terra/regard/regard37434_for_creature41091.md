The power came with a cloud and stayed after the cloud had gone.

Nothing in the hollow recognized it as a visitor. The birds flew through it. Water crossed it on its way down the stone. A fly, settling on a wet patch of moss, rubbed its forelegs together in the place where it attended most closely.

It listened for a long time.

Below the meadow, frost had worked a crack through the rock. Each winter water entered it, and each winter the ice pressed a little harder. A slab beneath the meadow had begun to lean outward. One day it would fall, taking soil and sedge with it, and the passages of the small mammals, and several nests in the wall below.

The power could have closed the crack. It could have held the slab where it was for a thousand winters.

Instead, it followed the water.

There was another seam a little higher on the wall, shallow and choked with grit. The power loosened a stone no larger than a fingernail. It fell into the seam and dislodged another, and after several days of cloud the water found a new course. Less of it reached the crack beneath the meadow.

The slab would still fall, someday. Mountains came apart that way. But now the meadow might remain through many more warm seasons, and when it went, it might go a little at a time.

The power watched a young bird leave its nest. It missed the shelf it was aiming for and dropped out of sight. Its parents called from the wall. A moment later it rose again, flying awkwardly, and landed among them.

Later, the hunter came. Its shadow passed over the meadow, and the mammals went under the stones. One was too far from an entrance. The hunter took it and flew down the cliff, carrying it toward a nest on another ledge, where its young were waiting.

The power watched them too.

When the person returned, they came slowly, carrying a bundle of sedge and favoring one leg. At the shelter they sat down heavily and pulled back the fur wrapped around their calf. The skin beneath was swollen. They pressed it once with their thumb, then stopped.

Cloud filled the hollow that evening. The person made a small fire and slept beside it, waking often when they turned.

The power could have taken away the pain. It stayed near the shelter instead, considering the injury and the path by which the person had come. By morning, water from the newly opened seam was dripping onto a shelf just outside the shelter. The person noticed it, moved the basin beneath it, and drank. They wet a strip of cloth and laid it against their leg.

For three days they rested. They ate what they had brought and kept the fire small. Once, during a clear afternoon, they sat in the sun and watched the cliff birds making their turns over the falling water. On the fourth morning they tested their weight against the leg, repacked the bundle, and left.

Before going, they placed a flat stone beneath the new drip so the water would not wear a hollow in the soil. They looked up the wall, perhaps trying to see where it came from, then took their staff and followed the path around the mountain.

The power remained through the winter. Snow gathered in the sheltered places; ice hung from the lip. Deep in their holes the birds pressed together, and beneath the meadow the mammals slept in dry sedge.

When the thaw began, the power opened no flowers and summoned no sun. It watched the first water run from the roof of the shelter into the basin, and the first bird emerge to shake its wings. Then it went on, leaving the hollow loud with dripping.
