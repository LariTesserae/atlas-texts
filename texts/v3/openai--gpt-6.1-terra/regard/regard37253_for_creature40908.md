The power listens through the stone.

It feels the blows from above and, beneath them, the smaller pressures: a foot hooked into sulfur, a shell pinned under three others, the clasp of a hunter around a body not yet hardened. It does not mistake stillness for peace. Nor does it know, simply from the shape of these lives, which contacts are welcome and which are endured.

So it begins by learning.

In the long interval after the machinery stops, it attends to each animal in turn: to hunger and its easing, to the effort of breathing in the crowded places, to the bracing that continues even after the last blow. It finds no wish to see the sky, no longing for a green world. It finds needs close at hand.

Farther into the mountain, it opens passages through the glass and rock, low enough for the animals to grip both floor and ceiling. Along their walls it lays seams of sulfur and guides a gentle seep of gas through them. Film spreads over the new seams. The power makes the passages branch and join again, with narrow places for the young and wider places where adults can turn without dislodging their neighbors. It leaves much of the old chamber as it is, but takes the sharpest points from the places where bodies are pressed against them.

Around the inhabited stone it builds a softening layer. When the machinery starts again, the blows still reach the animals, but only as a slow tremor through their feet. The power keeps watch on the work above. If a drill or fracture approaches the passages, it will turn the danger aside without sending it toward the people working there.

The hunters are hungry too. The power changes the film along some of the new seams, making it nourishing to them, and changes their mouths and appetites just enough that they can feed upon it. The first to find such a seam rests there for a long time afterward, its legs unfolded. A young grazer passes beneath it unharmed.

There will still be births. The power gives the animals a way for their bodies to slow that work when space and food grow scarce, and to begin again when there is room. It watches to be sure the change does not leave them troubled or ill.

Some remain in the first chamber, where the familiar crust renews itself. Others follow the fresh currents of gas into the passages. The crowd thins gradually; there is no sudden scattering. A grazer releases one hooked foot and sets it down without finding another shell beneath it. A neighbor moves, and the movement travels only as far as the next body.

Above, the machinery pounds until evening. Below, on the old seam and the new, the animals feed. After the pounding stops, there are still small, dry clicks in the dark, and longer quiet spaces between them.
