The first sign is that a seam does not take.

An old woman has drawn her needle through the same place three times. Each time the fiber comes back slack. She turns the mat over, expecting rotten growth, but its underside is sound: pale knots, black crumbs of stone, a grazer asleep in a pocket no larger than her hand.

She sets the needle down and waits.

Outside, the air has stopped moving. The path between the shelters lies flat. Even the loose ends of the roof hang straight.

Then the warm crack below the ridge gives a single, deep knock.

The woman crawls to the doorway. The other shelters are quiet. Beyond them, a broad-winged animal is descending toward the basin, its wings bent sharply against a current she cannot feel.

She calls the household out. They bring hooked poles and test the stones. One of the younger people puts an ear to the ground and hears a faint grinding, like ice being crushed far below.

They know what to do when a roof shifts. They know where to stand when a mat lifts. But the grinding continues through the next rest, and the stone beneath the shelters grows warm enough to melt the ice at the roof edges. Water runs down the walls.

The old woman takes her pole and goes to the ridge’s end. From there she can see the whole basin: the rain line, the white floor, the distant cliffs. A dark opening has appeared among the plants below. As she watches, it widens. Leaves slide into it without a sound reaching her.

She has walked this way in every season she remembers. There are three households beyond that opening, perhaps four if the travelers have returned. She cannot reach them before the ground breaks, and the air carries her voice only as far as the first slope.

She stands on the bare stone, thinking. Then she hears someone beside her say, “May I help?”

No one has come up the ridge. The voice is quiet, but she hears it clearly over the grinding below.

“What can you do?” she asks.

“I can carry a warning. I can hold the rock for a little while.”

“Long enough for them to get out?”

“Yes.”

The old woman looks down at the widening crack. “Tell them the ridge is open. They know the high path.”

The voice says, “And you?”

“We’ll go when they can see us.”

So the warning travels.

At each shelter beyond the crack, the people hear a voice close by, speaking in words they know. Some come out at once. Others stop to take food, needles, a sleeping child. One man returns for a bundle of wing membrane and is angry to find the doorway tilted when he comes back out.

The rock holds. The crack grows no wider, though the grinding continues.

From the ridge, the old woman sees figures appearing on the high path. She waves her pole above her head. When all she can count have passed the last narrow place, she turns back to her own household.

They carry what they can. They leave the water pools covered and the bone tools tied to their rack. At the last shelter the old woman pauses to lift the unfinished mat from her knees and roll it up. The grazer is still inside. She leaves it there.

They walk until they reach a ridge of older stone. Only then does the voice ask, “Are you all here?”

They count one another. A child has gone back along the path a short way, looking for a dropped mitten. Her father calls her, and she comes running.

“We are,” says the old woman.

The ground breaks open behind them. A section of the basin drops, and the shelters vanish below its edge. Warm air rises through the new fissure, lifting torn sheets of vegetation high into the chamber. Fliers turn toward them. The household watches until the bright leaves have spread so far apart that each looks like a separate weather front.

Later, the old woman hears the voice again.

“I could close it,” it says.

She considers the fissure. A broad current is already rising from it. The mats caught on the broken edges stream upward, their roots exposed. One of the travelers has begun picking a route down to the water that was trapped in the lower basin.

“Would the ground hold afterward?” she asks.

“For a time. It would break somewhere else.”

“Then leave it.”

They make camp on the old ridge. The neighboring households arrive with what they managed to carry, and there is much counting and telling and checking of hands and faces. In the following days they find that the new fissure warms a sheltered hollow where ice had always covered the pools. They also find that the wind there can strip a person off a slope.

The voice remains a while. When asked, it tells them which ledges are sound and which only appear so. It does not mark a route for them: the safest way changes as the torn mats settle, and the people are there to see it.

Before leaving, it asks the old woman if there is anything else she wants.

She is sewing by the doorway of a temporary shelter. She thinks of the rack of needles below the broken edge, and the pools, and a place where her mother used to sit in the light. She would like to see them again. She does not know what to ask for.

At last she says, “There were grazers in the mats.”

“Some are still there,” says the voice. “Others were carried away.”

She nods and draws the fiber tight.

Years afterward, a traveler finds an old mat caught on a cliff far across the basin. Its loose threads have rooted into the rock. Beneath it are several round doors, and inside one is a grazer’s pocket lined with carefully packed roots.

The traveler takes a drink from a nearby pool, presses the path flat with their feet, and goes on.
