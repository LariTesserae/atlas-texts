I would be near a cup, I think. Not at first the cup itself, but something that depends on it: one of the flat scrapers, perhaps, with a gut lit by the leaf it has eaten. I would know the hollow by the pull of six feet and the taste of a narrow trail. When the ceiling hummed, I would press down and wait.

But I keep returning to the leaf.

I would be the broad fan above that cup, joined by a stem to the older body beneath the floor. The scrapers would pass over me without finding an edge to the part of me that mattered. They would leave clear stripes in my surface; I would thicken tissue along the stripes, so slowly that their eggs might hatch before the repairs were finished.

My cup would hold water against the cold. I could not see the animals gathering there, though their weight would draw faint tensions through my tissue. A cluster of eggs beneath its film would be a slight persistent pull; a scraper lifting one foot and setting it down would be too small to distinguish from the leaf’s own movements. I might know the grazers below more clearly, by the ways my lower strands were released as the pelt was cropped around them.

There would be other fans joined to me. Some would draw on water taken up near my stem; some would send water toward me when the seam at my base narrowed. I don’t know whether I would experience them as other parts of myself. A change in one might reach me only as a change in pressure, long after the thing that caused it. Still, if my stem were bitten through, neighboring stems would bend toward the gap. Their tissue would meet the torn ends of mine.

During the hum I would feel the roots shift above and below. The scrapers would flatten themselves against me. Once, a grain of ice might fall into my cup and lie there, making a tiny cold place that spread through the water until the grain was gone. Afterward, as the cup filled again, the water would pass over its lip and carry several eggs down my stem.

I would lose track of them in the floor pelt. Later, a young blade might rise beside me with one of their young holding fast to it. It would reach my leaf, find the luminous tissue, and begin a new trail.

Farther down, around an old root, some of my tissue would already be hardening with mineral grains. The burrower might come awake there and push its muzzle against the soft rib, testing it, then wear it away. I would feel that loss before I could grow around it. Pale threads torn from its coat would remain in the disturbed pelt; some might live.

I would not need to hold all of this at once. There would be the water in my cup, the long tension of my stem, the pressure of roots against the roof. Beyond those, slower changes would arrive through the joined body: a seam opening somewhere, a leaf lost, a new tip finding its way around a stone.

The light would come through me in a band, pass on, and return after a long while. In the meantime the scraper would keep eating, and I would begin, grain by grain and cell by cell, to close the trail behind it.
