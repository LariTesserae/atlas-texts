I live beneath one of the shallow pools, in a chamber with a low ceiling. When the pool is full, its weight presses the ceiling against my back. When it dries, I can stand there on all four feet.

There are six of us in the chamber most days. We sleep curled together, and when one turns, the others must make room. Our feet twitch even in sleep. The floor carries so much through it: mouths working in the walls, the passage of bodies overhead, the rattling of wings not yet unfolded. I know the weight of my nearest sleeper by the way she leans against me. I know her footfall at the chamber entrance.

I hunt the thin ones. They gather where a wall has been grazed, in the damp tissue beneath the pale track. I can pull several out at once if I catch the end of a braid. Sometimes I take a fallen grazer instead, though one large enough to kick is difficult to kill. I have a notch in one ear from an animal that seemed nearly dead when I found it.

I go out along the grooves after the light begins to change. My eyes are not much use inside the walls, but at the water I open them. Often my shadow is already there, drinking from the still pool. If the others are ahead of me, I see their shadows too. I put my feet where the shapes are gathered, and by the time I reach the water there are bodies beside mine.

One evening there were seven shadows at the pool.

I stopped in the groove. The others came past and began to drink. The seventh shape stayed a little apart. It was hunched like ours, but longer through the middle, and its head kept moving while the rest of it remained still.

I could feel something in the wall above us: a pressure forcing its way through passages too small for it. The scraping changed around it. Bodies began to spill from an entrance farther along the rim, pale climbers tumbling over grazers and catching themselves on the wall. The disturbance spread on both sides of the thing, arriving through my feet before the fleeing animals appeared.

Our chamber lay beyond the pool. I started toward it, but the floor gave its deep call and my feet lost the measure of the way.

I flattened myself in the groove. The others did the same. Moisture came out on the walls, and a drop struck my shoulder. The call filled my belly and jaw; the smaller noises continued inside it, as they always did. Above us, the wall heaved. I saw an opening stretch and a long pale head push through, its mouth working.

The pool was suddenly close to me. Beyond it, our entrance looked close too, but the strip of wall between the two had grown long. I could see a grazer moving along that strip, each short step taking it scarcely anywhere.

The long shadow lay across the water now. Its owner had not yet reached the opening. The pale head in the wall turned toward the drinking place, though nothing had emerged there.

I went into the water. It barely covered my feet. The first step took me across the pool and into the wet groove on the other side. The next brought me to our entrance. I pushed through, scraping both flanks on the puckered edge, and felt the chamber floor rise against my chest.

The others came after me. Two reached the entrance while the call still held; the rest came when it ended and the distance settled again. One arrived with her fur wet and a scrape down her back. We crowded around her as she squeezed in. Outside, the wall shuddered, and the alarm moved on.

The seventh animal did not come to our entrance. Later I saw it at the pool, longer than its shadow had looked, with a narrow face and a tail trailing behind. It drank among the marks we had left and went under the wall.

Our chamber stayed open that season. We passed in and out often, wearing the entrance smooth with our sides. After rain the pool formed above us again, and its weight lowered the ceiling until we had to lie close together. I slept with my feet against the floor and woke each evening to find the others there.
