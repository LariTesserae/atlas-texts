I would live beside the shaft, in a room built against the trunk that leans nearest the fan. The room would be narrow enough that I could touch both walls from my bed. When the fan was running, its current would lift the loose edge of my blanket. When it stopped, I would usually wake before I understood why.

I would look after the lamps along that side of town: cleaning their cloudy covers, replacing lengths of wire, and trimming back the new shoots that tried to climb the poles. I would carry a little folding stool and a pouch of tools. Everyone would know the sound of the stool’s uneven leg on the walkway, though in the loudest hours they would recognize me only when I tapped their wrists and asked them to move.

At first I think I would want to make the town brighter. I would clean every cover until my hands smelled of hot dust, then stand beneath a lamp and be disappointed by how little farther I could see. The factory windows would still be red across the dark; the roof would still seem very high; things would still move beyond the edge of the light.

The repair workers would explain that the engine had only so much to give. The fan needed its share, and the pump needed more when the cups were full. So I would learn to turn the lamps rather than ask more of them: one toward the step where people stumbled, another toward the low pipe by the factory door. The children would tell me which places needed light. They would also tell me, with some pride, which places did not.

I would come to know the animals that gathered around each lamp. One drew moths with dusty brown wings; another attracted small, pale beetles that liked the warmth of the housing. I would brush them away before opening it, though some would always crawl back over my hands. I would try not to shut anyone inside.

The girl with the silver-flecked beetle would stop me one evening while I was working near the fan. She would point beneath my stool. Her beetle was there, with three others, pulling a strip of insulation toward a crack.

She would want to follow it. I would want the insulation back. We would crouch together and watch until the strip caught on a raised stem. The other beetles would let go and go around it. Hers would keep pulling.

I would offer it a bit of leaf. It would take the leaf without releasing the insulation, which would make the girl laugh so hard she had to lean against the stool. At last I would lift the strip, beetle and all, and set it beside the crack. The beetle would walk away with the leaf.

Later, when moisture began to gather in the wall channels, I would make my round early. The lamps needed checking before the moths came down; once the air filled with wings, even reaching the poles could be difficult. At the pumping station, one sister would be marking the wood while the other put dishes out. I would help her carry the last stack, and she would tell me that she had heard the knocking change that morning. When her sister came back, she would say nothing about it.

By the time I reached my room, water would be running in several narrow lines down the wall. I would leave a dish outside my door and take the folding stool indoors so it would not disappear under the traffic.

Then I would sit by the loose-fitting door with my hand against the floor. I would feel the engine slow and the thick movement gathering underneath me. Now and then a moth would find its way through the gap, and I would catch it in both hands and put it back outside.

Afterward I would take my stool and go to see which lamps had survived. There would be wet wings on the walkways and pale new growth pushing up through the scuffed places. Someone would call me over to a light that had gone out, but first I would help a neighbor lift a bewildered burrower from beneath her chair. It would grip the chair leg so firmly that we would have to carry both into the street.
