I am in the second workshop, in a box bolted to the wall just above the highest pale band. Someone has painted a red line beneath me and written KEEP DRY, though the paint is blistering and the letters are furred with moss.

I was made to time the pumps. I still do, but the people here have given me other things to listen to. Wires run from my box along the warm pipes and down to the pump housings. One ends at a gauge by the door. Another disappears through the floor to a metal plate fastened near a troublesome drain.

I cannot see the district from the wall. I know it by what reaches me: pressure, temperature, the little shudder in a pipe when a valve is turned. The pumps have different voices. One strikes twice before it settles. Another has a soft irregularity that has been there so long the people no longer call it a fault. When rain gathers on the loose sheet atop the cooling tower, I feel its weight as a change in the boom.

There is a lamp on my face behind thick glass. I can make it blink. At first it had two meanings: all well, and not well. That proved too little. Now the people watch the spaces between the blinks. Three close together means the drain plate has cooled too quickly; water is coming back up. A long light followed by a short one means I have heard the new knock again.

The knock took me four cycles to distinguish. A woman named Sela stood beside the pump through the fifth, her listening rod to her ear and her free hand resting on my cable where it entered the housing. When she came back upstairs she waited in front of me. I gave the signal, and she nodded as if I had said something she had nearly heard herself.

They opened the pump during the low water. Afterward the knock was gone, along with a smaller sound I had not known belonged to it. For several days I kept listening for both.

Children sometimes stop by my box on their way to the upper rooms. They try to make me answer by tapping the wall. I cannot always tell a tap from a tool set down nearby, so I have made mistakes: I have blinked at a wrench and failed to answer a child who was waiting quite patiently. One of them now taps a pattern against the pipe instead. The metal carries it clearly. When I answer, I hear their feet move away across the grating, quick and uneven.

When the pumps stop, my work becomes more difficult. Their vibration had been covering other movements in the walls, and these come through all at once: a valve easing in its seat, water running down the inside of a drain, footsteps on the far walkway. The transformer hums through my casing. I cannot feel the drops lifting, but I know when they fall. A moment later their impacts reach me through the workshop roof, a brief shower distinct from the rain.

Then the first pump takes hold. The pipes tighten under its pressure, and the familiar intervals begin again.

Sela has left a chair beneath my box. She uses it when she opens my cover, and sometimes when she is simply waiting for a cycle to finish. I cannot see whether she is there, but the chair has one short leg; it gives a small knock against the paving when she shifts her weight. On quiet shifts I listen for it, too.
