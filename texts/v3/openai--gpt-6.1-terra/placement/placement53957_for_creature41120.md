I would be in the passage behind the house with the crooked roof weight, where warm water comes up through three cracks and runs beneath a wall. At first I think I live there, but no: my sleeping slab is two houses away, among the slabs of my sisters and their children. I am simply at the seep often enough that people look for me there.

I tend snails. I also mend the low wall around the seep, because the snails get out where it has fallen, and clear the dead shells, because otherwise they collect against the outlet and stop the water. The work belongs to several households. They each take a share, though we have to keep track of what is left as well as what has been taken. When the water is cloudy, I count by touch, moving my fingers along the underside of each stone.

There is a crab under the largest stone. I used to reach for it whenever I found it there. It was a good size, but I could never get my hand behind it without scraping my knuckles. One day I took the smaller stone beside it instead, and three days later I found the outlet blocked with shells. Perhaps the crab had been moving them. Perhaps the blockage had nothing to do with it. We put the stone back, and now I leave the crab alone. It has one pale claw and one dark one; if it dies, I suppose I will notice.

In the morning I wake to my niece stepping over me. She puts her foot on the broad part of my slab, where there is room, and braces her hand against the wall. She is carrying the hearth pot out to be scraped. I catch her ankle before she goes and feel the new cut there. She twists to show it to me, holding the pot above her head so it doesn’t spill.

It is shallow. I let her go.

Outside, the water has risen enough to cover the low paving stones. I know the way by the feel of them: a tilted edge near our door, then four flat steps, then the gap where the neighbors have been meaning to set a new slab. Someone has put a shell over the sharpest corner. It moves under my foot, and I bend to set it firm.

At the seep, a boy from the next house is already crouching with his basket. He has taken more snails than his household’s share. I touch his upper arm twice, and he turns, startled, nearly putting his hand into the crab’s crevice. I catch his wrist. Then I rest my hand there a moment so he knows it is me.

We tip the basket into the water and sort the snails. Some have pale film still on their shells. The smallest go back beneath the stones. The boy is impatient; he can sort faster than I can, and wants me to see it. Twice he drops one where it rolls into the outlet, and twice he fishes it out again while I hold the basket for him.

When we are done, I notice that the wall is leaning. Water has worked beneath its bottom course, and there is a gap wide enough for my fingers. The boy presses against the upper stones, but they shift together. We leave them and go for help.

By midday there are five of us standing in the passage with stones in our hands. One woman knows who built the wall before. She turns each stone we lift, looking for the flat face and the hammer marks, and sets them where we can reach them again. We work from the top down. The crab comes out when its stone is moved, and the boy catches it by the back of the shell. He holds it away from his body, grinning, while its claws open and close.

I expect him to ask whether he can keep it. Instead he takes it to the far end of the seep and lets it go among the other stones. I will have to look for it there tomorrow.

We pack small chips into the hollow beneath the wall, then lay the courses again. It takes longer than we thought. People coming through the passage touch our shoulders and wait while we make room. Someone brings a bowl of insects from a hearth roof; we eat them damp-fingered, passing the bowl over the new wall.

By the time the last stone is fitted, the water has begun to clear. I can see the snails we put back, slowly finding the undersides of their stones. The wall still looks much like the old one. The woman who helped us runs her palm along its top and finds one place where a stone rocks. We lift it once more.

When I get home, my niece is asleep across two slabs, with her cut ankle resting on my bedding. I sit beside her and pull it carefully into my lap. There is grit in the cut after all. I fetch water and clean it out while she sleeps, flinching once but not waking. Outside, someone is moving a boat down the lane, touching each doorway as they pass to ask for room.
