I live at the crossing just below the central basin, in a house whose roof has to be weighted again after every strong wind. The stones do not fall far, but the leaves lift and let the silt through. By morning there will be a fine gray dust on the sleeping cords and a small pile in the hollow where my sister keeps her tools.

I am one of the people of the shelf. My feet are broad, my hands longer than they look when folded. A pale stripe runs down one side of my neck, where a fish bit me when I was a child; I tell children this when they put their hands under the central steps, though it was not the pale fish that did it.

At first I might have said that I mend rails. People bring me loose pins, split lengths of wood, and root cord that has grown too stiff to tie. I carry a rounded stone and a roll of cord at my waist, and I know which posts move when leaned on. But the older I get, the more of my work seems to be listening before I touch anything.

A rail can be firm at one end and working loose at the other. If I put my hand around it and wait, I can feel the difference between the little movements: a child traveling along it, water pressing through the channel, a pin shifting in its hole. Sometimes I ask someone to push from the far post while I hold the near one. We take turns, because the movement is easier to understand from both places.

This morning the rail below the grove house had gone quiet. It was still there, still smooth beneath its coat of silt, but when I tapped it the sound stopped short. One of the roof roots had curled around the post. It had not pulled the post down; it held it so tightly that the wood could scarcely move.

The grove keeper came out through her doorway with three small fish following her hands. She looked at the root, then at me. We had a disagreement about it. She wanted to leave it for a few more days; I wanted to loosen it before the next wind moved the crown above. We settled on clearing the finer growth around it so we could see where it entered the roof. Tomorrow we will look again.

While we worked, her grandson came along the channel. He was practicing without touching the bottom, pushing from post to post with his arms held forward. At the quiet rail he missed his next hold and landed in the plants. We all waited for the silt to settle. Then he found a snail on his shoulder and carried it away to show his mother.

By the time I went home, the broad bands of daylight had moved across our courtyard. My sister was turning the roof stones over in her hands, choosing which ones to put back. We ate folded leaves filled with soft shoots, and afterward I helped her climb onto the wall. She laid the leaves flat while I passed up the stones.

Tonight I will sleep close enough to the crossing to keep a hand on the rail if I wake. Usually there is nothing urgent to feel: a neighbor turning over, someone coming home late, the faint pulse of fish beneath the shelf. In the morning I will go back to the grove house, and the keeper and I will see whether the root has moved.
