I think I would live under a stone on the side of a ridge, not far above a basin. The stone would be broad enough to keep the chamber beneath it from collapsing, and tilted so that condensation gathered along one edge. In the warm hours, drops would fall there into a shallow groove.

I would be one of the burrowers, with dense fur and short limbs and a muzzle worn smooth from pushing through grit. My chamber would be part of a larger system, though I would not often visit its farthest passages. An older animal sleeps beyond a narrowing where the air smells of dry roots. Below us is a hollow shared by a mother and her young; when they move, fine grains fall from its ceiling into mine.

Around my sleeping place I have set seven dark stones. Two were there before I began sleeping in the hollow. The others I brought from a seam exposed farther up the ridge, dragging them one at a time through a passage too narrow to turn around in. They are cool when I first touch them, then take on the warmth of my body.

The current through the ridge is not something I would have a name for. It is present when I sleep and when I wake, a steady condition of the ground, felt most clearly through the bare skin of my feet. I know where the chamber walls are partly by the way it passes through them. The stones sharpen it. Without them the hollow feels less sheltered, though the ceiling is just as solid and the bedding just as dry.

Sometimes I find one of the younger animals turning a stone over with its nose. I let it work for a while, then push the stone back. It may be that I am putting it wrong again. For several days afterward the youngster returns and moves it, and I return and move it back. Eventually we leave it somewhere between the two places.

I go out when the ground near the entrance is cool enough to hold moisture. Most trips are short. There are mineral grains to collect, and soft growth beneath the edge of a neighboring stone. I carry things home against my chest, walking awkwardly on three limbs when what I have found is too large for my mouth.

Once, after rain, the basin below the ridge holds water near its surface. I can feel the yielding ground before I reach it. The pool itself is shallow and scattered with gravel, its margin already crowded. I wait beneath a stalk while a browser drinks. Its jaw presses down and the wet ground firms beneath me as water moves away from its weight. When it lifts its head, the ground gives again.

There are good grains at the edge, dark and heavy, and I gather several. A winged animal lands close by, making a brief rush of air and then a clumsy series of taps. I freeze. It taps past me, picking at something in the gravel, and is gone before I move again.

On the way home I pass a long shape lying against the slope. I have passed it before, or another like it. The ground around it is quiet, but not empty. I keep close to the stones and take the longer route back to the entrance.

Some time later—perhaps after many rains, perhaps after few—the familiar pressure in the chamber changes. It is slight at first. One morning I wake and turn the nearest stone. The hollow feels better for a while. Then the mother below begins carrying her young into an upper passage, and the older animal comes out of the dry narrowing and sleeps near the entrance.

We dig. The new passage runs toward the basin, then bends where the ground grows damp. I carry two stones into it and leave them beside the wall. The younger animal brings the one we used to move between us and sets it down farther in.

The old chamber remains usable. Its ceiling is firm; the groove beneath the tilted stone still catches water. I go back there more than once, and curl up among the stones that remain. But I sleep lightly. Through the floor I can feel the others working, and after a time I get up and follow the passage toward them, pushing the last stone ahead of me.
