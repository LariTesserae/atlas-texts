I am on the inside wall of the silver structure, above the waterline. From here the opening is a pale, unsteady oval. I know when the sun has reached it because the wall warms beneath my feet, though the water below stays cool.

I have six feet, each divided at the end into finer parts. They hold even when a bead passes over me. My body is flat and dark, except where food shows through it as a pale streak. Another of my kind lives a short distance above me. We have crossed one another’s trails often enough that I can tell which way it has gone by the film it leaves behind. I do not know whether it can tell mine.

The wall gives us water all morning. Beads form separately, grow, touch, and run together. I feed around their edges, where the transparent growth is softest. Sometimes I go down to the waterline and let my front feet enter. The swimmers turn beneath me, their dark spots appearing and disappearing. They make no effort to catch me, but a swimmer can strike hard enough to loosen my grip.

Today the water outside has risen to the opening. A few swimmers pass out into the channel, stretching apart as they go. The ones that remain begin to turn in a tighter cloud. I follow their shadow along the wall until the animal above me comes down across my path. We stop with our front feet almost touching. It lifts its body, and I pass beneath.

Farther along, a trail interrupts the growth: a clean curve with a small rough place at its end. I feel it with my feet. Something has scraped here, not fed. I go around it, but the roughness continues below me. In the water, just under the wall, a dark-bodied animal is waiting. It has come in from outside. I stay above it until a bead gathers near my back and carries me several body-lengths sideways. When I have hold again, the animal is gone from sight.

By midday the wall is warm enough that the beads form slowly. My neighbor has climbed toward the roof. I stay lower, where a shallow crease holds moisture after the smooth places have cleared. There are eggs in the crease, fixed singly to the wall. I can pass over them without dislodging them. One has opened since morning, and the young animal is still beside its empty skin, working its feet against the surface. It catches hold, releases itself, and catches hold again. When I return later, it has moved to the damp edge of the crease.

Beyond the opening, a grazer stands with its mouth underwater. Its feet make dark shapes in the channel. One foot lifts, and the water rocks gently against the wall beneath me. A moment later a hunter passes across the opening. The grazer raises its head, dripping, and the cloud inside the structure breaks apart. For a short time the swimmers move separately. Then they find one another again.

In the afternoon I descend. The water has fallen away from the opening, leaving a thin connection along the bottom of the channel. I have been outside before, though not far. The stone is harder to hold than the wall: its surface looks smooth, but my feet find pits and ridges that turn them aside. The film on it tastes different too, with bits of leaf and the dry dust that arrives before rain.

I meet several flattened animals feeding in a hollow. One travels over my back as I cross its path. At the hollow’s edge, the water narrows into threads, and I turn to follow one downhill. It leads me toward a broad depression in the stone, freshly filled. Its sides are scored with parallel marks. I go partway down and find moss caught in one of them, still green and damp. Whatever pressed this place into the ground has moved on.

From the depression I can see the low rim and, against it, the broken arches. A grazer is drinking beneath the largest one. Smaller animals gather at either side of its mouth. When it lifts its head, a bead hangs for a moment from its lip and falls back among them. They scatter, then close over the place where it landed.

I do not reach the arches. The water thread grows narrow behind me, and the stone ahead is almost dry. I turn back, passing the filled depression and the hollow where the flattened animals are still feeding. One has curled into a pit. Two others are moving after me toward the channel.

The rain begins while I am crossing the exposed stone. The first drop lands well away from me, but I feel it through my feet. More follow. A pale crowd comes running toward a fresh puddle, and I cling low as water spreads around us. Something dark moves among the crowd. I leave them and go on toward the opening.

Inside, the water has risen against the wall. A swimmer strikes near my feet as I climb, and I pull myself higher. The young animal is still in the crease. It has found the deeper part, where the water does not rush through so quickly. My neighbor is above us both.

Rain sounds on the roof. Beads gather overhead, and for a while the wall runs freely from top to bottom. I feed until the water slows. Then I settle near the crease, where my feet can hold and my body stays wet. Below me, the swimmers draw out into long, turning ribbons. Outside, the last drops fall from the trees.
