I would be at the rim, on the side where the wind can still find me. There is a narrow shelf there, sloping toward the hollow. I would have to stand with one foot higher than the other, my boot braced against the new black rock. The edge is sharp enough that I would keep my hands in my pockets.

From there I could see a pale tree, three pools, and part of another tree behind the first. The cloud would hide the far side of the ridge. I would hear the wind moving past my ears, but not the hum inside the rock.

At first I think I have come to count the birds. I can see seven on the nearest fern, their throats open at seven different widths. The stem holds them without sagging. Farther in, there are birds whose feathers are almost indistinguishable from the wet stone, until the pale lines along their shafts catch the light. I lose count trying to decide whether one dark shape is a bird or the folded tip of a leaf.

I stay because of the eye among the roots. It is large enough to see from the rim, though much of it is shadowed by liverwort. The hollow is reflected in it: the trees, the cloud, the insects. The figure at the edge is there too, with one foot above the other.

Around the figure’s feet are little shapes I cannot see when I look down at my boots. In the eye, one has a narrow white back; another is dark and round, with something like a tuft of moss growing from it. They may be on the inner slope, hidden from me by the angle of the rock. I shift my weight on the windy shelf to look for them, but the stone cuts off my view.

I would like to get closer to the eye. There is a place a few steps along the rim where the rock looks broad enough to stand on, and I make my way there carefully. From that side I can see more of the pale flank between the pools. It has a line of short fur along it, fine as the roots of grass. I cannot tell how far the body extends. The folded ear beyond the tree might belong to it, or to something lying beside it. Both seem possible when I look.

The figure in the eye is still at the first place on the rim.

I look back. There is nothing standing there in the ordinary wind. In the eye, though, the small figure keeps its uneven stance, and the animals remain gathered around its feet. Its face is too small for me to make out. I can see that its hands are in its pockets.

I think, then, that I belong here as a visitor belongs to a place: not because I tend it or understand it, but because some part of the place holds an image of me. The figure in the eye will not follow me along the rim. I can stand where I choose, and its little feet will remain among those animals I cannot quite see.

I go back to the first shelf and put my boot against the same piece of rock. The wind presses my coat against my legs. In the eye the figure’s outline becomes hard to separate from my own position, and for a while I stand there looking in, trying to see whether the white-backed animal has a face.
