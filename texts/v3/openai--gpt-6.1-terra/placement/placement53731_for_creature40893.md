I think I would live in the passage beneath the tree band at the north end of the long hollow. The entrance is easy to miss: a slit behind three trunks, with a flat stone set upright beside it to keep the worst of the grit out. Inside, the floor slopes down. You have to turn sideways at the narrow place, and in the rains a thread of water runs over your feet.

I would be one of the people who travel between hollows, though not always by choice. My sister and her children live two basins away. When the water lasts well, we stay where we are; when it doesn’t, someone goes ahead to see which shelters are still sound.

I would know the bowls along that route. There is one outside a shelter on the western ridge, cut so thin at the lip that you have to lift it with both hands. Another sits crooked because a corner of its base broke away. I have meant for several seasons to level the stones beneath it. Each time I pass, there is something more urgent to do, and I tell myself that someone else has probably already done it. Then I reach the shelter and find the bowl still leaning, with a little water gathered at one side.

Perhaps this is the visit when I finally set it right.

I have crossed the first basin in the morning, while the pavement is still cool in patches and the wind makes no more than a rough sound in the trees. My carrying net is tight against my back. In it are dried stems, a scraper, and a length of fibre my sister asked for when she last sent word. I have brought a little extra because one of the children is learning to weave and wastes a great deal.

At the shelter, I kneel to drink from the bowl. It has caught very little, but the grooves are damp. I use a folded leaf to wipe out the dust without losing the water, then put my hands beneath the bowl and lift it clear. Underneath, a family of pale animals has been digging; their tunnel has loosened one of the supporting stones. I fill the hole with grit, press the stones down, and settle the bowl again.

It rocks. I take it up and try once more.

By the time I am satisfied, the wind is coming hard over the ridge. I sit in the shelter to eat, watching the bark strips tied to my net strain and settle. Beyond them the basin is empty. A glider passes overhead so low that its shadow moves across the entrance, then it turns sharply and is gone behind the ridge.

I had hoped to reach my sister’s dwelling before evening. It is still possible, if I take the direct route over the pavement. The sheltered way follows the green bands around the hollow and will take much longer.

I put my hand against the shelter wall. At first I feel only the roughness of the slab, warmed by the sun. Then the note comes through it, faint but distinct. I wait for it to rise again. Its pitch is higher than it was when I left home.

From here I cannot see the cliff cut. The air at the entrance is strong, but I can stand in it. I go as far as the ridge and look across. Dust is moving in thin streams along the pavement; near the far wall it lifts suddenly and blows back the way it came. I watch for a while, hoping it will settle. Instead, one stream joins another, and I lose sight of the stones marking the route.

I turn back toward the trees.

My sister may be looking for me from her entrance. If she sees me across the basin, I can signal that I am taking the long way. If she doesn’t, she will know, when I fail to arrive by dusk, that I have stopped somewhere.

Near the end of the tree band, I find a place where the ground falls away into a fissure. Someone has roofed the opening with slabs and left a bowl beside it. The bowl is dry, but the room below is cool, and there is old fern bedding in one corner. I bring in my net, tuck the loose bark strips beneath it, and eat the rest of my food.

When evening comes I sit with my back to the wall. The note reaches this place clearly; it passes through my shoulder blades and into the floor. I put my hand down and feel for the change in it, though there is nothing I need to decide tonight. Above me the trees rasp together. From time to time grit slips through a gap in the roof and ticks against the stone.

In the morning I will clear the bowl’s grooves before I leave. If the wind has dropped, I may yet arrive in time to help my sister gather fern shoots, and the child can show me what they have made with the fibre they wasted.
