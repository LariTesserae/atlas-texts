I think I would first be found in the square, sitting on the damp bench with a notebook open on my knee. From there I could see people arrive from more directions than I could count, and I would have somewhere to put the things I heard.

The notebook would have narrow pages, like the ones carried by the people who watch bridges. Mine would be ruled differently. Each line would begin with a place for a few words, followed by a longer blank. When someone brought in a voice the walls had returned, I would write down the words and leave room for whoever might recognize them.

At first I imagine this as a job with a desk and a shelf, but the shelf would soon be crowded, and a desk would be hard to move when its room became deeper. So I would go about with the notebook tucked inside my coat, visiting the household shelves and comparing their unfinished messages. In the evenings I could sit wherever a meal was being served and read a few aloud.

Not all at once. People would be hungry, and the dishes would need passing.

I would learn to ask how a voice had sounded as well as what it said. A child might remember that the words came from low in a wall, or that there was a pause before the last one. Sometimes the pause would be what someone recognized.

One evening, perhaps, a child would bring me: “until the water comes back.”

We would take it to the pool tenders, who would know several things it might mean. One would remember saying it beside a drain, another beside a kettle. The child would shake their head at both imitations. We would write it down and leave it uncrossed.

Later, at supper, a woman would hear me read it and set down her spoon. She would not remember saying it herself. She would remember someone saying it to her, years ago, while they waited beside a vent that had gone cold. She would know who the other person was, but not where they lived now.

The next day I would go looking for them.

That is how the work would change. I would spend less time on the bench and more time walking people’s words back through the lanes. I would learn which bridges were settling and which doorways had been weighted well enough for a visit. I would carry a hot stone and, eventually, a small wedge of my own, because it would feel wrong to ask someone to search for a message while also holding their door open.

I would not always find the right person. Sometimes two people would be certain a sentence belonged to them. Sometimes a message would be recognized and nobody would want it crossed out yet. I would leave those lines alone.

I think I would be a resident, then: dressed in the same awkward layers, with dark strips around my ankles, dependent on neighbors to help me out of my coat when my hands were full. But I might have arrived without knowing quite what I was for. The notebook would be given to me by someone who had grown tired of carrying it, or by a child who found it under the bench. I would begin using it because there were empty pages.

After a while, people would start catching me in the lanes.

*Do you still have the one about the blue bowl?*

*Has anyone claimed “not on that side”?*

And I would turn back through the pages with my gloves on, while they waited close enough to see.

At night I would keep the notebook near the door of whichever room I slept in. I would also have the ordinary household turn of staying awake to watch the far wall. On those nights I might copy messages onto clean slips while the others slept, looking up every few lines. If the wall seemed farther away, I would mark its position against the edge of a shelf and wake someone if it moved again.

In the morning I would report both kinds of news in the square: how the room had been, and whether anyone had recognized the voice.

The line about the water might take a long time. The person we were looking for might have moved into a room reached by a staircase inside another staircase, or might be standing beside us when we asked, unable at first to place the words. If we found them, I would ask whether they wanted the child to hear the rest.

If they agreed, the three of us could go to one of the warm pools. The child would stand near the tiny leaves while the person told what they remembered. The leaves would turn toward us slowly, and remain pointed there as we left. I would cross out the line when I got home, though I might still be able to read it through the ink.
