I think I would begin in the shade of an older frond, close to the wall where a flue opens. Folded tightly along a pale crease, I would be difficult to distinguish from the other new growth. The leaf above me would bear the scars of years spent rubbing against its neighbors. Every change in the draft would bring it down against me again.

At first I might take myself for the narrow space between those two leaves: the darkness that opens and closes as they knock together. Only in the hottest hours, as my fold eased apart and the older blade was pressed aside, would I become the thing taking up that space.

My roots would be threaded among the roots of the others, drawing from the mineral beneath the paving. Oil would move slowly through me. By evening my tip might reach as far as the flue, where the climbing air could catch it. I would not follow each passing gust; I would bend and settle, bend and settle, until the light shifted and drew me another way.

I would know the courtyard through contact. A neighboring edge, hard and split, would rasp against my surface. Grains would lodge in my crease. One night a spent leaf might fall across my base, and I would grow past it while its tissue darkened the bed around our roots.

There would be long stretches when nothing I touched seemed to move. Then the shutters would begin knocking, or the draft would reverse, and the whole crowded margin would rattle. By morning I would have changed position a little, enough for the first bar of light to find one side of me before it found the other.
