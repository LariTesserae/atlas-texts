I am at the court that opens onto the cliff. Most of me is spread across it, thin enough that the black stone shows through. At the open side I extend a little beyond the shelf, into water that has never been enclosed by walls. That edge is difficult to keep. The ocean presses evenly, but not quite evenly enough; I have to attend to it.

The others can find me by following the pale line from the basin. It reaches the court beneath the eastern wall and divides there. One branch runs under me. The other stops at the cliff, where it was broken during the making of the court. For a long time I thought the broken end was an invitation to continue it. I tried several arrangements and could make none of them hold in the water beyond the shelf. Now I think it may have been left for me to feel where the stone ends.

I was made here, though not all at once. A small chamber in the wall contains the oldest part of me: a loop of water, tightly held, with three threads passing through it. I still use the loop to measure changes at my outer edge. When something touches me, the three threads draw closer or farther apart, and I can tell whether the pressure comes from above the court, from the cliff, or from one of the lines.

An animal lives on the wall above that chamber. It settled there while my outer edge was being extended, and at first I felt it as a fault in the work. Its six limbs received the note from the stone and returned a faint unevenness to the line beneath them. I held the unevenness for an age, trying to establish whether it belonged to the wall or to me.

Eventually the animal left a clear section behind. The section remained when the animal moved the smallest distance upward, and the unevenness remained with it. That was how I learned to distinguish the two.

I have since come to know the animal by the slow changes in its dark thread. There are stretches when the thread is nearly straight, and others when a bend forms in it and travels toward the rear. I cannot tell whether these changes matter to the animal as they do to me. It has remained on the wall through many of them. Its ridge of discarded sections now reaches the top of my chamber.

The inhabitant in the basin has asked, once, to receive the shape of that ridge. I carried it along the line: the spacing of the sections, the way each fitted against the next, and the small hollow underneath where the first had attached to the stone. The answer came back after a long interval. The basin had found a way to hold the shape while turning it inside out.

I could not understand the answer at first. Part of it arrived too close to another part. I opened more space in the court, allowing the walls that enclose nothing to do their work, and let the answer spread. Then I could feel the hollow as a projection, the ridge as a narrow valley. I kept it that way for a while. The animal went on forming its ridge above me, and I held the other version below.

Now I am trying something with the broken line at the cliff. I have taken one of the animal’s discarded sections—not from the ridge on the wall, but from a crevice where several lay loose—and set it against the end of the pale material. It conducts the note into the open water very poorly. Most of the pressure passes back into the stone. But a little reaches my outer edge, and there it makes a shape I have not felt before.

The shape does not stay. Each time I arrange myself to receive it, it has already changed. I could draw the edge inward and make the water there more stable, but then it would no longer be at the cliff.

So I am leaving it where it is. I have sent the first few changes to the basin, widely spaced, with room between them for whatever comes next. The answer has not arrived. Along the wall, the animal’s thread has begun to bend again.
