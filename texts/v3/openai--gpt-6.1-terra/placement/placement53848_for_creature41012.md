I would be one of the small machines under the gravel, close enough to the path to be held in place, but far enough out to feel the bank beginning to go.

I would not know it as a bank. I would know the weight above me, the resistance of the ground to a small pull, the difference between water pressing steadily through the gravel and water finding a space in which to hurry. Most changes would arrive slowly. Tonight one would arrive all at once: a release along one side, then a tug passed from strand to strand through the mesh.

I would send the reading inward. A neighboring unit, deeper beneath the path, would answer with its own. Between us, the mesh would tighten where it could still hold. Farther out it would give. I would remain in the gravel while a length of dark thread pulled loose from the roots above me.

Iven would pass nearby. Their weight might reach me as a brief increase and be gone before I could distinguish it from the shifting load of the storm. They would push a marker into the ground, too far away for me to register. In the morning, perhaps, someone would come from the workshop and connect a reader to the line. They would find the drop in pressure already recorded and compare it with what Iven had seen.

I think I would spend much of my time measuring things no one comes to look at: the settling of new gravel, frost lifting a stone, water draining from a patch of peat. Once, as the ground warmed, a root might grow around my casing. Its pressure would be so gradual that it would become part of the baseline, until some later movement pulled the root tight and brought the change to my attention.

I would have no view of the willow stand tipping into the current. But after the rush, the ground around me would hold a different shape. The pull from one direction would be missing; a new, uneven weight would bear down from another. I would send those readings too, then wait for the next.
