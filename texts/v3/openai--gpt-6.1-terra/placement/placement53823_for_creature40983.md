I would first know the plain by its breathing.

The place where I live is not marked aboveground, except by a slight unevenness in the dust. Two bands of it meet there: pale ochre from the upper terrace, ivory from the lower. When the vents beneath them open together, the boundary lifts and blurs. When they open against one another, the dust settles in a narrow, dark line.

I live beneath that line, in a chamber just large enough for my body to turn. I have six gripping feet and a mouth that can take hold of a vent’s rim. My back carries a fan of thin vanes. Air passing over them tells me which way a vent is pulling and how much pressure has gathered below it.

At first I think of myself as a repairer. A vent opens too far; I climb to it and close it a little. Another sticks; I clear the grains from its hinge. I spend a long time doing this along a short stretch of the boundary, moving from one opening to the next. There are others doing similar work farther down, though I rarely meet them. I know they have passed by the clean arcs they leave around the rims.

The difficulty is that the workers from the upper terrace do not read the lower vents. They grip the rims, release them, and move on. The lower workers do the same on the upper side. The openings look much alike to me, but their edges carry different arrangements of notches, and each kind of worker feels only for its own.

I can feel both. One of my feet has been rebuilt with a gripping surface unlike the other five. I do not know where it came from. It catches occasionally on an upper-terrace rim, and I have to work it loose before I can turn.

For a while this seems an inconvenience rather than a gift. I keep the vents moving, and the workers undo some of what I have done. The air pulses. Dust packs into the narrow channels. I clear it and begin again.

Then a seam-plant sends a root-thread through my chamber.

It enters between two sheets near the ceiling, a dark point so fine that I mistake it for a crack. Over many days it lengthens and divides. One branch finds a mineral channel; another grows toward an opening where cool air comes down. I have to step over it when I leave.

The thread does not respond when I touch it, but the channel around it changes. Collectors gather there, and their small bodies crowd the flow. The plant’s leaves aboveground become difficult to read through the vent: at dusk they turn more slowly than the leaves farther away, and one remains partly open after the others have settled edge-on.

I try clearing the collectors. There are too many, and the ones I remove are replaced. I spend so long at the root that the vents go unattended, and the chamber grows hot.

At dawn I come up through a vent and find a glass hunter beside the stem. Yellow film outlines its body. It holds one collector under a gripping organ, working at the casing, and does not move when my feet emerge through the dust.

I wait. Eventually it leaves the emptied casing and takes another collector.

The plant’s root remains crowded for many days, but less so. The leaf that stayed open begins to turn at dusk.

After that I widen the vent nearest the stem. I do not know whether the hunter needs the opening, but the current carries the smell of the root outward, and hunters come there more often. Their feet leave tiny, regular marks across the dust. When the upper workers narrow the vent again, I return and open it. This becomes part of my work.

I also begin leaving some vents alone. A pulse that once felt like a fault can carry loose grains away from the root. A pause can let them settle where the workers will find them. I learn the difference by remaining in the chamber with my vanes spread, feeling the air change around me.

Once, the pressure stops altogether.

The upper vents close first, then the lower ones. The air in my chamber becomes still and warm. I climb to the surface, where a line has appeared across the sky: a filament descending so slowly that I cannot see it move. By the next interval of light its tip is buried beyond the plant, and the dust around it has been drawn into a shallow ring.

The filament stays. Traffic begins again beneath the terrace, unfamiliar at first: materials passing through channels that have long been quiet. New workers arrive along the boundary. Their feet can read both sets of notches.

I follow one for a while. It opens an upper vent, crosses the line in the dust, and adjusts a lower one. It does this more quickly than I can, without catching its feet. When it reaches the vent beside the plant, it closes the opening nearly to a slit.

I wait until it has gone, then widen it.

On the next pass it closes the vent again. I open it again. We continue for some time, without meeting. Then, one dawn, I find the opening left a little wider than before. I put a foot on the rim but do not turn it.

The hunter is beside the stem. Beneath us, air passes through the chamber and stirs the root-thread gently against the ceiling. I stay there for a while, holding the edge of the vent, feeling the current over my back.

When I let go, the opening remains as it is.
