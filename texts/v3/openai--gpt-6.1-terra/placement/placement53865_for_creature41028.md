At first I think I would be one of the shells. There is something appealing about staying in one place and knowing a world by what arrives: the fine grit, the pressure of a hunter’s foot, the shadow that darkens the water before anything touches it. I would close when my neighbors closed, and my own small click would pass into the great sound.

But I think I would miss too much. I would want to know where the wave went after it left my pool, and what had made it start.

So perhaps I would be someone who returns to one of the shelters on the middle rise. I would come by the gravel bars, walking carefully around the feeding heaps, with a flat stone in my hand to test the depth ahead. Even so, my shadow would go farther than I did. Shells would close across the water, and a hunter I had not seen would stop moving.

The shelter would not be mine in the sense that I had made it. I would have found it standing, its roof still holding, with a little rain coming through one corner. I would add gravel where the roof stones shifted and clear the entrance when grit gathered there. Inside, on the sheltered wall, the grooves would fill with water. I would watch them tremble, then look out to see whether I could tell which pool had answered the sound.

At first I might try to learn the basin by counting: the clicks from a shadow, the longer rush when something large moved underwater, the deep slap of a swimmer turning. I would scratch marks of my own beside the old grooves, leaving a space between them because I would not know what the older marks meant.

After a while I would find that counting made me miss the beginning of the next wave. I would stop trying to record every sound. I might instead mark the days when the pools stayed open longest, or when a sheet of ice held until morning, or when a hunter had made a new heap of shells close to the shelter. I would leave room beside each mark for something I had overlooked.

The pebble-lined pit outside the entrance would collect rain. I would keep it clear of grit with one of the worn stones, though I could not be sure that was what its makers had used it for. When I left, I would set the stone where I had found it.

I think that is who I would be there: not the keeper of the basin, but a returning listener, making small repairs to a shelter and learning, slowly, how much happens before the sound reaches me.
