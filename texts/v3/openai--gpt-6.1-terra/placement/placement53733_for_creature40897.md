I live where the soft floor meets the edge of a stone. My tube curves down beside it, following the seam between rock and sediment. The stone gives the tube one firm wall, though the current has sometimes opened a hollow above it, leaving the upper part exposed.

Most of me stays below. I send a branched crown into the water, holding it open until the drag grows too great or the branches carry more silt than I can sort there. Then I draw it in. The tube fits closely around the folded crown; grains scrape its lining as they come down with me.

Inside, I separate what I can eat from what I cannot. The useful particles are few, but they come mixed with many others, and I work through them slowly. I bind the rejected grains and push them back up. They collect around my opening until the current takes them.

I know the stone by the way it directs the water. When the flow comes from one side, the crown can stay open a long time in its shelter. From the other, the branches bend almost as soon as I extend them. There are other changes I cannot place so easily: water that carries more food for a while, or a fine load of silt that arrives before the stronger flow behind it.

Once, something moved along the stone and touched the edge of my crown. I withdrew it. Later I extended it again and felt the touch farther down, near the tube’s mouth. I pulled in hard enough to dislodge some of the lining. Whatever had come was gone when I next opened. For a time afterward, grains entered through the damaged place, and I had to work them out and close it.

The tube has needed other repairs. Sediment settles around its mouth and raises the floor; I lengthen the lining upward. A current scours the floor away, and a section that was buried stands bare in the water. If it bends too far, I abandon that part and make a new opening lower down. My body has grown, too, and the tube is wider than it was. There is old lining below me that I no longer touch.

When a dense cloud reaches the stone, I close before it can fill the branches. The cloud may pass quickly, or I may stay folded through many attempts to open, each one bringing another load of grit. At last the branches meet water they can hold against. I spread them a little farther, then farther still.

What settles on them has traveled beyond the reach of this stone. I can use only the small part that catches, and only after I have drawn it down and sorted it. The rest I return to the floor, in packets that lie beside my opening until the water moves them away.
