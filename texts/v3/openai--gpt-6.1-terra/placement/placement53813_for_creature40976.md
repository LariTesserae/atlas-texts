At first I might mistake the sound for weather: the low hammering that comes through me from every direction. Then the wind slackens, and the hammering continues. Somewhere above, a seed head is still turning.

I am below it, though not directly. Between its roots and mine are the roots of many other plants, pressed together and grown through one another. I have a narrow share of a mineral seam and a much wider share of old ground. Some of the tissue around me is dead and hard; some tightens when I push against it. I cannot always tell which of the living strands are mine.

I do not make a stalk. For a long time I send a pale edge along the underside of an old fire scar, where the ground has split in a thin black line. The scar is brittle, and my edge can work into it. Above the scar, another plant spends its growth on a tall stem. Its cords pass close to me. When the wind turns its head, the cords pull taut and a chamber closes with a force I can feel through the layers. Powder sifts out around the chamber. Some of it reaches my roots.

Years pass in which this is the most regular thing I know: pressure, release, the fine settling of powder. Less regularly, a burrower cuts through the newer growth above me. It leaves loose fibers packed in its wake, and for a while I can grow into them more easily than into the old ground. Then something digs down from the surface. The blows come quickly and stop. Afterward there is an opening where the wind can get in.

The opening dries and widens. A rim-eater finds the soft growth around its edge and pulls some of it away, including a strip that had been mine. I seal what is left. The animal’s feet remain nearby for a time, their weight shifting from one place to another; then they move off, and the lighter, quicker impacts beside them go too.

In the next strong wind, a seed catches in the opening. Its first root presses down through the shavings. It comes against me and stops, then flattens and continues along my side. As it grows, the space tightens. I yield in one place and thicken in another. Much later, when the young plant begins to make a stalk, its pull travels down that root and into me.

The old seed head keeps turning. The young stalk is not yet tall enough to sound in the wind. Between them I go on spreading slowly through the scar, taking what reaches me and holding where I can.
