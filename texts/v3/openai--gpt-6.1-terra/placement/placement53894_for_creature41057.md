I would be in the joint, though not all along it. One damp pocket behind the farther fern would be enough: a little dead moss pressed against the limestone, and, beneath it, a few pale threads of fungus.

At first I might seem to be the whiteness on the stone. But that reaches farther than I do, and most of it is only the stone itself. I would be finer and less continuous: threads working through what the moss has shed, branching where there is moisture, failing where the crack opens to dry air. Some would be eaten. Some would break. I would keep growing from what remained.

The animal might pass over me when water gathers. Its front would touch the moss before the rest of it appeared, and then its flattened body would follow, slowly enough that a droplet could change shape beside it. I would not know it as an animal, or recognize it when it came again. Where it fed, some of my threads would end. Where it pressed the moss down, the damp might last a little longer.

When the joint dried, I would not go deeper as it does. I would have to endure the place I occupied. Near the surface, the fine ends might die; farther in, protected by the moss and the close stone, a little of me might persist until water returned. The animal could retreat past that living part, into a crevice I could not fill.

I would have no use for the row of stones near the entrance. They would be farther away than any root or crack I followed, across ground where I could not grow. Yet a grain loosened from the rim might one day wash down to the wall and lodge among my threads. A fern leaflet might fall there too, and soften, and be entered.

That is where I would be: not the whole hidden life of the joint, and not its keeper. Just one small growth among others, occupying a little of what has fallen, leaving room behind me as I am eaten or dry away.
