At first I would be beneath something. I don’t think I could tell, from the shelter alone, whether it was a flake of stone or the edge of one of the great grazers. There would be warmth above me and bodies on either side, and a narrow place where I could extend a limb and find the cool ground.

If the roof were stone, it would hold still while the warmth moved through it. If it were a grazer, it might tighten without warning, lifting me a little against the others. Grains would slide down from its folds. Farther inward, where I could not reach, something would be feeding on it.

I think I would begin beneath a stone. I would be one of the packed ones, grown into a space that was larger at one end than the other. My back would have a shallow bend in it from the body that pressed against me while I was growing. That neighbor might be gone by now. Another would occupy the place, fitting me less closely.

When a shadow crossed the stone’s thin edge, the bodies nearest it would contract, and I would feel them pull against me. I would fold my back too, before anything touched me. Sometimes the shadow would pass. Once, the stone itself might rise, and the pull would become much stronger: every limb holding fast to rock or neighbor, the whole shelter exposed at once. When the stone settled again, grit would lie in the new gaps.

I would leave in the cool hours, though not necessarily the first time I felt moisture. A packed body has to make its departure a limb at a time, and the others keep shifting against the places it has freed. At last I would pull loose and find the ground open around me. My side would retain the shape of the gap for a while.

The path to the basin would be easy to enter. It would run through a slight hollow, dark with animals moving in the same direction. I could follow the damp, but also the clean marks where others had grazed, and the occasional body resting in the way. I would feed as I went, taking the film from the stone in a narrow strip. Behind me the strip would be paler.

At the basin’s center the crust would give beneath my limbs. There might be eggs under it, laid during an earlier wet spell, and small shelled animals in the seams. A transparent hunter could be there too. I might not notice it until a place along my fringe hurt and would no longer grip properly. I would close over the injury and go on, more slowly for a time.

On the return, I might reach my old stone or I might come to the edge of a great grazer instead. Its underside would be crowded, and the ground beneath it would still be damp. I could press into a space there. When the grazer lifted its lobe to scrape away the shelled animals, I would have to hold on; afterward I might settle a little farther inward.

A long time could pass in that shelter. The grazer would move, though I would mostly notice the movement by what appeared at its edge: a fresh patch of film, a seam in the rock, the entrance to a dome. Small bodies would come and go beneath it. After rain, water would run through the channels overhead, and we would retreat from its path. For days afterward the shelter would remain damp.

Eventually I would leave that place too. Perhaps the basin would have dried; perhaps a flexing lobe would have exposed me to the air, and another shelter would be nearer than the old one. I could fit myself among strangers beneath a flake of stone, occupying whatever gap they had left.

From there, in the cool hours, I would still be able to find the path. It would be worn slightly deeper than when I first traveled it, though not by anything I could notice while moving.
