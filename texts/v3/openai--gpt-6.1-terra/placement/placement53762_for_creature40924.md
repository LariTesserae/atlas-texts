At first I would be difficult to find: a small brown shape beneath the rim of the basket that holds the plant. The basket hangs where a strip of afternoon light reaches it, close enough to the wall for silk to span the gap. Above me are the swifts. Below, when the light is right, I can see a pale gecko waiting under a shelf.

I would be a spider, and my part of the chamber would be the space between the basket and the clay.

There are old strands there, gray with dust, and newer ones that catch the light. The basket moves when the shutters knock or when someone comes down the stair. I feel each movement through my feet. Most vibrations pass through the web and are gone; a moth beating against the silk makes a longer disturbance. By the time I reach it, it may have torn free. If not, I wrap it where it hangs.

The gecko sometimes waits within reach of the lower strands. Once I would have watched it take a moth from the edge of the web, quick as a falling chip of clay. Afterward I might retreat under the basket rim and remain there until its toes had carried it elsewhere. There would be no use objecting. I would have to mend what it broke.

The plant’s roots have tightened around the old nesting material inside the basket. Some have found their way through the weave, and I can anchor a thread to them. When the plant shifts toward the light, very slowly, it changes the shape of my supports. More often the damage comes all at once: a bird brushes the basket, a gust rattles it against the wall, or a loose strand catches a falling feather and sags under the weight.

One day a person comes down the stair and stops beside me. The hanging pots announce the visit before the person reaches the bottom. A hand tests the basket. The whole web shudders; I draw my legs close and hide beneath the rim. The basket is lifted, its cord adjusted, and for a moment it hangs at a strange angle. Several strands part.

When the person has gone, I come out. The basket is a little higher than it was. The plant is still in it, and there is still a gap between the rim and the wall, though I must cross farther to fasten the first new line.

By late afternoon the light has moved away from me. Birds are returning to the upper tubes, calling and jostling at the bowls. The chamber is loud enough that the small sounds near my basket disappear among the others, but the web carries its own news. I wait with my feet on the silk, beneath the plant, between the wall and the swaying rim.
