At first I think I would be a bird in a fissure, one voice in a colony so loud that even I could not hear myself. I would know the shelf where my nest was wedged and the particular call of my young. I would leave the crack in a rush of other bodies and return just as quickly, carrying a moth whose wings beat against my beak.

But I find myself lingering near one of the basins instead, where people have left their vessels. There is a hollow ridge close by, with a floor dry enough to sleep on and an entrance partly blocked by an old fan of tree roots. I am a person who stays there for a season, perhaps longer than I meant to.

I tend the basin after rain. The channels clog with leaves and the remains of insects, so I clear them with a thin piece of wood, then set the vessels where the water will run. The first water is full of loose soil; I let it pass. Later I plug each jar with grass and carry it into the ridge, where the air stays cool.

There are three people with me at first. We have come up by a route marked with standing stones, testing the ground with our poles. One of us knows which sedges have roots worth eating. Another can mend a clay vessel with a patience that makes the rest of us ashamed of how often we break them. When we talk outside, we lean together and take turns speaking into one another’s ears. I come to recognize my companions by the shape of their raised arms long before I can see their faces.

I spend a great deal of time repairing our shelter. The roots grow across its entrance, and beetles find their way into every bundle we leave on the floor. I move them out by lifting the whole bundle and shaking it gently over the mat. The beetles pour off, catch the light, and disappear into the stems. By evening some are back.

One afternoon I find a shallow dip across the route to the basin. It was not there when we arrived, or none of us noticed it. I press my pole into the center and feel the mat give farther than it should. When I lie down and look through the roots, a current of cold air touches my face. I cannot see the bottom.

We go around it after that. Before the others leave, we cut a mark into the nearest shelter pole and lay stones in a bent line pointing away from the fissure. I stay behind with the jars, intending to follow when the next party comes.

At dusk the sedges darken with moths. I watch from the shelter entrance as the trough fills, then lifts: first a flicker, then a rising mass that casts its own shadow. The hunters unfold themselves along the cracks. Birds plunge into the swarm and climb again. The roar reaches even the dry chamber behind me.

I pull my feet inside and wait. Under the floor, a family of grazers passes close enough to lift one of the jars. I steady it with my hand until the floor settles. Then I put the jar back a little farther from the wall, where it will be less likely to fall when they return.
