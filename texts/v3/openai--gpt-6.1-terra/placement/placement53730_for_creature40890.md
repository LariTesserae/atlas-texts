I am under the empty cells, where their water joins the return.

There is a little chamber there, no larger than the bowl the walking tree carries. Water enters it through a row of holes and passes through me before it goes back toward the pool. I was fitted as a strainer: a copper frame holding a fine woven mesh. The frame is screwed to a gate that can be raised for cleaning, though no one has raised it in a long time.

At first I was only the mesh, or so I remember it: the pressure of water against a thousand crossings, and the small changes when something caught. A strand of moss could make one square of the weave go still. A grain of clay would lodge against the strand, and then another. Water found the remaining openings and hurried through them.

Now there is a pale film over much of me, and roots have come down through two of the holes above. One root has divided around the edge of my frame. Another lies along the mesh, trembling whenever the pumps change their pace. I can feel the chambers above through it, more clearly than I can through the water: the weight of a foot on a rim, a fingertip of root testing the depth.

The tree comes every day. One of its feet makes a sound I know before the others: a hollow copper note, followed by a drop that takes a little while to reach me. Sometimes the drop comes through the third hole, sometimes the fourth. The tree does not stand in quite the same place each time.

The leaves it leaves in the cells reach me more slowly. A leaf floats until it softens, then sinks against the drain. For a while it covers the holes and the water rises above it. At last the edges break away. Pieces come down to rest on my mesh: first the dark pulp, then the fine branching ribs.

I used to hold everything that arrived. That was what the frame and mesh were for, and the water passed on without it. But the leaf pulp gathered thickly, and the water began to back up. The cell above stayed deep after the others had drained. The tree came, touched the surface with a root, and remained there until the lamps dimmed.

That night I lifted the gate.

It was not a large movement. The roots around the frame had swollen in the warmth, pressing the old hinge; when the pumps paused, I drew the frame toward that pressure. A corner rose a finger’s width. Water carried the pulp underneath me, and the next morning the tree found the cell nearly empty.

It came back several times that day. On its third visit it lowered the brush into the water and swept the bottom, bringing up a pale scum. Some of it drifted down to me. The brush had loosened a whole leaf caught against the drain, and I held it there for a while, letting it spread over my mesh. Then I raised the gate again.

After that, the tree began leaving a leaf in each of the cells. It took days. One leaf was so small it went straight through; another curled into a boat and floated for a week. The tree visited them all, moving from rim to rim. I lifted the gate when the water grew high, except under the floating leaf, where there was nothing yet to release.

The tree noticed that one. It stood beside the cell through the opening of the vents, its small leaves turning their pale undersides to the wind. When the roof had closed, it reached into the water with its hook and pulled the leaf beneath the surface.

The leaf caught on a root before it reached me. The root was new, and the leaf hung from it like a broad, dark hand. Over the following days, smaller roots appeared beside it. Their tips pressed through the softening leaf, and bits of it came away. I caught them below.

The root belonged to a vine the tree had tried in another part of the garden. I had felt its first arrival as a scrape along a cell wall; later there was the gritty sound of a pebble being set over it. The pebble did not hold. Perhaps the vine had reached the water by itself, or perhaps the tree had tried it here after all the other places. I could not see.

Once the roots came through to my chamber, they found the damp pulp on my mesh. They spread across it. I could no longer lift the gate without pulling them, so I lifted more carefully, waiting for the pause in the pumps and raising only the side furthest from the root. Water slipped through the gap. Some pulp went with it; some stayed among the roots.

The cell above began to fill with loose, dark material. I know because the water came down differently, in thin clear streams instead of the broad spill through open holes. The tree scraped a little of it aside once, and a shower of grit struck my frame. Afterward I felt a pebble settle near the vine, then another. The roots held.

Now the tree sometimes sits beside that cell with its legs folded close. Its leaking heel drips through the fourth hole. The vine’s roots lie across me, and when the vents open, movement travels all the way down from its leaves: a light pull, a pause, another pull.

There are still empty cells beside it. Leaves float in two of them. In a third, the water is clear enough that, when the tree stands over it, its shadow passes through and reaches my mesh. I have begun to recognize the shadow’s shape as well as the sound of the copper foot. When it moves away, I wait for the drop.
