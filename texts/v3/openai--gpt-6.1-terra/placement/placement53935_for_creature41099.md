I think I would find myself on a stone shelf, just beyond the reach of a stand of pale shoots. Someone has cut them back repeatedly, and their stumps glow in a ragged line below the shelf.

At first I would be sitting inside the shelter, listening. The walls are loose enough to hear through, but they turn the daylight bars into a lattice on the floor. A clapper is caught in the weave beside the entrance. I can hear it working at a strand of resin, stopping whenever I bring my hand near.

My job is to keep the path to the water seam open. It is a small job on the scale of the chamber, and a large one on the scale of a morning.

I wrap my ankles with fresh strips, take the pole from beside the door and go down. The path has risen in the night. Leaves that lay flat yesterday have tipped against one another, and the stones marking the edge have slipped into the gaps. I find three of them with the hooked end of the pole and put them back. The fourth has vanished. I leave a cut rib in its place, angled toward the shelf so that I will recognize it on the way home.

A herd has crossed here. The grazers have left a broad scrape of amber, all running in one direction. At its edge, a few late ones are still feeding, their legs spread across the join between two leaves. I pick one up by its plates and put it in my bag. Another has its belly caught to the leaf by a hunter’s mouth. When I touch the leaf with my pole, the hunter lets go, leaving a bright puncture that begins to bead.

Farther on, the hammering changes.

I stand still to hear it better. Several stems are answering at once, and for a moment I cannot tell which carries the quick, uneven blows. Then a shower of winged things rises from the old arch ahead. I back away along the path, counting my stones. The arch does not fall while I watch, but a seam opens lengthwise in one of its supporting stems. I will not go under it today.

The other way to the water is longer. It passes beneath a curtain where the pale, weak flyers gather at the cool edge of a roof seam. Some cling to the cloth of my sleeves as I brush through. I stop on the far side and ease them off one at a time; their wings catch easily on the wrappings.

At the seam there are already two people from the next shelf. They have set folded leaves beneath the beads on the stone. One of them points out a new crack, high enough that we will need to stand on each other’s shoulders to reach it. We leave it for later and divide what has gathered in the lower folds. I tell them about the arch. They have heard the hammering too, but from their side it sounded like the calls were coming from farther away.

On the return, the floor heaves. It is slow enough that I have time to crouch and lay the pole across two leaves, but not slow enough to save the water. One cup rolls from my bag and empties. The leaves around it buckle upward, and the cut rib I left as a marker disappears between them. I keep hold of the other cup until the motion stops.

Then I call toward the shelf. Someone answers from the direction I expected, which is a relief. Another voice calls from farther off, asking whether the route to the seam is passable.

“Not yet,” I say, and start looking for the stones again.

I think this is who I would be: not the person who knows every route, but one of the people who goes back out to find where the route has gone. By evening I would have scratched a mark on my pole for the height of the new shoots, set the recovered stones along a changed stretch of floor, and told anyone passing where the cup was lost. If the hollow stayed open, perhaps there would be something growing in it tomorrow.
