At first I would be near the back wall, where a little water gathers in a groove before spilling down. From there I could feel the hollow changing: the cold weight of cloud, then the sun finding the stone, then the quick feet of flies on the wet surface.

But I don’t think I would stay there. The birds keep pulling my attention outward. I would be one of them, hatched in a hole above the meadow, in a nest that smells of damp grass and old feathers.

For a while the whole world would arrive at the entrance. Light, a parent’s body blocking it, the quick touch of a beak, then light again. Below, the mammals would move among the stones. I might hear them without knowing what they were. The water would be harder to separate into sounds; it would simply be there, even in sleep.

When I first came out, I would cling to the rock and call much longer than necessary. Another bird might answer from a shelf. The shelf would look farther away whenever I leaned toward it.

My first flight would be untidy. I would reach the shelf low, scramble up with both feet and sit there with my wings held slightly open. Later I would learn to drop before turning, to let the air rising from below help carry me back. I would still sometimes misjudge it. On windy days the hollow would feel different, and I would keep closer to the wall.

I would know the visiting person chiefly by their place near the shelter. When they came, I would watch them from an entrance above the basin. Their hands would make sharp sounds against the dark stone. Once they had been there a while, I would resume flying through the insects, though I might take the longer way around them. If they left the basin full, I might drink there after they had gone.

The hunter would change everything at once. I might see the shadow move over the meadow, or hear the calls break off, or find that the air had abruptly emptied of birds. I would go into the nearest hole I knew and wait, pressed against stone that smelled of another nest.

When I came out again, I would look before calling. Another bird would answer, then several. A mammal’s nose would appear between two stones, remain there, and withdraw.

And when a cloud entered the hollow, I would fly a shorter circuit, keeping the wall in sight. Water would begin to drip more heavily. I would return to my own hole by the rough patch beside its entrance and wait there, facing outward, until the light came back.
