I would be in one of the shallow channels, where the stone holds a little warmth after sunset. From the rim I might be difficult to distinguish from the channel floor: an amber-ear lying with my legs folded under me, my back dusted pale.

An insect is working between the pads of my foot. Its movements are small, but I can feel them. I lift the foot slightly, and it comes farther out, carrying a red fragment. Another insect follows it. This one catches in the hair and I flick my foot; both insects scatter. After a while the first returns.

There are six of us resting along this stretch of channel. Farther down, where the walls bend, I can hear another animal scraping at the stone. I know the shape of the older grazer nearest me, the nick in her left ear and the way she settles one shoulder before the other. We have fed near each other for many days. The young one beyond her is newer to me. It followed its mother into the basin this morning and spent the afternoon following whoever happened to be moving.

Earlier we came upon a ring. I had been there before, though the sand against its edge had shifted. A bead was gathering above the stone, lengthening and drawing itself back. I waited for it to fall. The young one walked through the ring and stopped, startled by something behind it. Its mother was already beside it, bending to drink from the wet place the bead had left.

I drank there too. My tongue brought up a little grit with the water. By then the others were moving toward a hollow farther along, so I went after them.

The hollow was nearly dry. What remained lay under a dark skin, and when we broke it our noses came away marked. We browsed the sloping side of the basin afterward, finding lichen in the seams. The young one tried a pale stem and pulled the whole visible part free. For a few moments it held the stem across its mouth, unable to decide what to do with it. Then it chewed.

A shadow crossed the basin. We lowered ourselves before I had seen the animal casting it. I watched the older grazer’s flank rise and fall. Beyond her, one ear of the young one remained upright, glowing at its edge. The shadow moved on. We stayed down a little longer, then rose one after another.

Now, in the channel, the young one has settled close to its mother. Tomorrow it may follow her again, or it may come with me if I am the first to leave. I know a seam where water sometimes remains beneath a loose stone. There may be insects there by dawn, and perhaps enough moisture for me to reach when I turn the stone over.

For now I keep my foot still. The insect has returned to the space between my pads, and the warm wall of the channel rests against my side.
