I would live just below the lip of a dam, where water slips through a fringe of moss and gathers speed over the gravel.

At first I might be mistaken for a loose bit of the bottom: a short tube made of sand grains and tiny fragments of sedge. But the tube moves. A dark head and six small legs emerge from one end, grip a stone, and pull it forward.

I am a caddisfly larva. The case is mine because I built it, and because I carry it everywhere. As I grow, I must add to its wider end, choosing grains that I can lift and fastening them in place with silk. A piece too large makes the case awkward; a hollow stem may make it float when I need to hold to the bottom. I test a fragment against the current before I fix it.

There are safer places upstream, perhaps. The pool behind the dam is slow and deep, with roots hanging into it. But the seam below brings food past my door, and the stones give me purchase. I spend my days among them, browsing the fine growth on their surfaces. When a fish comes close, I draw back into my case and hold still.

Above me, a beaver sometimes works at the leak. The first warning is a tremor through the sticks. Then mud clouds the water and settles on my stones. Once, a branch presses into the moss overhead and changes the flow so abruptly that my case rolls onto its side. I catch a root and cling there until the current finds its way around the new obstruction.

For a while after that, the seam runs slowly. Silt gathers. I move a little farther down, to another place where clear water passes between two stones. From there I can feel the dam yielding and settling as the pool rises. The beaver returns, and returns again. I cannot see much of it: a passing shape, a foot planted among the sticks, pale chips sinking through the water. Its repairs make my channel; my channel undoes a little of its work.

During the coldest days, ice forms above the quieter pools. Here the water keeps moving beneath a shelf of it. I remain among the stones, my case heavier now and rough with additions, while the light narrows and opens overhead.

Later, when the willows have leafed out, I fasten my case firmly to a stone and close it with silk. Water can still pass through. Inside, the body that crawled and grazed changes. When I emerge, I leave the case attached to the stone: a small, empty piece of construction below the larger one.

I swim upward and break through the surface among the sedges. I have wings now, though they take time to unfold and dry. From a stem I can see the dam for the first time as more than a ceiling of sticks: its long, uneven crest, the green shoots growing from it, the higher pool shining on one side. Beyond it are other dams and lodges, close enough that their willows meet.

I do not go far. I fly low over the water, find another caddisfly in the evening air, and later return to a sheltered edge to lay my eggs.

The current will carry some away. Others may remain among the roots, above the seam where I lived. By the time they hatch, the beavers may have closed that leak entirely. There will be another opening somewhere; there nearly always is.
