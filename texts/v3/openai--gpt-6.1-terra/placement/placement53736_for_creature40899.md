I would be one of the thread-bodied creatures, small enough to live in the seam beneath a grazer’s shell.

From there the plateau would not look level. It would rise around me in pale cliffs and jointed arches, with a roof of old bone overhead. My feet would be set into the seam, and below me the grazer’s legs would stand in their shallow, ridged depression. I would take what I needed from the powder that collected there. I might know the grazer chiefly by the steady light under its shell and by the taste of the material it left for me.

I would not be alone. Another creature would occupy a seam on the far side, and there would be smaller ones between us, difficult even for me to make out. When the daylight changed, their clear bodies would disappear against the shell, leaving only their amber organs visible. The lights would be close enough that, from some angles, they seemed to belong to one long animal.

For a while I think I would believe the grazer was the world. Then someone would lift it.

The person would have to work their fingers beneath its legs. I would feel the pressure through the shell before I saw the light widen under it. Grit would fall away. The depression would remain on the ground, shaped exactly to the animal that had occupied it. For the first time I would be able to see the underside of a hunter’s mouth a short distance away, its points resting against the place where the grazer had been.

The person might turn the grazer over in their palm. They might notice me and the pale line of powder where my feet were sunk. I would not let go. I do not know whether I could.

Perhaps they would place us in a bowl, beside two other animals. I would come to know the bowl by its curved wall and the repaired break, where a gray cord passed through pairs of holes. The animals’ lights would show faintly through the clay. Beneath each of us, the feet would leave a pale patch.

Inside the shelter, I would see only a narrow piece of the plateau. A hand would sometimes pass through it. There would be a scrape as the bowl was moved, a shadow over the opening, the brief weight of fingers against its rim. I would not know what the person wanted from us. They might have chosen us because we were alike, or because we were different in ways I could not see.

Later, if I were still there, the hand would cease to appear. Daylight would continue to enter the doorway. Outside, the notched island would be visible in the same place.

I think I would begin by calling myself a creature in a seam. After the bowl was lifted a few times, I might understand that the grazer and I could be carried together without becoming one thing. There would be some comfort in that: my neighbor on the other side of the shell, the animals beside us, the person who had set us down—all separate, all held for a time within the same small curve of clay.
