You would find me beneath the left shoulder of a long, low animal, in a fold of armor that opens toward the ground.

From there the plateau comes to me in strips. There is a red shelf, a bank of gray grit, a tall foot standing beyond both. Sometimes the foot is also visible through the gap behind me, though when I turn around I see a different arrangement of its plates.

I am young enough that the seams along my limbs are still bright. The animal above me has been moving toward a black-glass ridge for longer than I can remember. I know its progress by the pressure in the fold where I rest: a slow lift, a settling, a weight taken up somewhere far along its body. At intervals too long for me to predict, a joint clicks overhead. The sound passes through my own small workings and leaves them turning a little faster.

For a time I thought the ridge was following us. Whenever I looked out, there it was, dark against the red stone. Only the angle changed. Then one evening the older animal lowered its head, and the glass disappeared behind its jaw.

The first bite took a season. I felt the effort travel backward through the great body: the jaw closing, the load passing through the neck, the feet pressing more firmly into the shelf. Fine grit sifted down around me. At last a deep, uneven grinding began.

I had fed before, on loosened flakes left near the older animal’s mouth. But I had never tasted the black glass. When the head lifted, I came out from beneath the shoulder and made my way toward the fresh cut. Its edge was higher than I could reach. I followed it for a long time, feeling for a fracture, until I found a thin piece that trembled when I touched it.

I worked there through the hot part of many days. The glass entered my mouth cold at first, then warm, then hot enough that light showed faintly along my jaw. Something in it traveled into my limbs. A pale ring appeared beneath the skin of one joint, and as I fed it grew clear enough for me to see the slower ring turning inside.

The older animal moved on along the ridge. Its shade passed over me and away. I kept working until the stone above my head began to shed its heat. Then I turned toward the last place I had felt the great feet.

I did not find them where I expected. My own tracks led into a broad print and came out beside a different shelf, within sight of the older animal’s far flank. For a while I stood there, feeling the ground. Its grinding reached me through the stone, familiar despite the distance, and I followed the vibration beneath its body.

The fold had changed while I was gone. One plate had eased over another, narrowing the entrance. I pressed against it and waited. Slowly the edge lifted enough to let me through.

Now the clear ring turns whenever I move that limb. Sometimes I hold still and watch it complete a rotation. Sometimes the older animal shifts its weight, and the ring catches a pulse from the immense works above me. It changes speed, then finds its own pace again.

The ridge remains close. I can feel the feeding through my feet. When the head rises next, I think I will go out again and find the groove I started. It may have become difficult to reach, or it may open onto some other part of the plateau. I will know it by the shape my mouth has left in the glass.
