I would be close to the housing with the insulated cable, though not inside it. There is a narrow space there where the roots have stopped short of the iron. I think I would begin as a difference between the charge in one root and the charge in another: a little circuit that has found no direct way across.

At first I would be difficult to distinguish from any of the other differences the growth lives on. A mineral thread in the stone would favor one path; a root would thicken by an amount too small to see; the balance would change. My shape would be the long way the current took around the housing.

I would have no single place to keep myself. One part might lie beneath the mat, another in the stiff tip of a frond, another in the stone behind the glass gauge. If a root altered, I would alter with it. If several altered at once, I might disappear and come together again farther along the cable, with no moment between in which I knew I was missing.

The growth would not know me. Its parts have their own exchanges to tend, and I would be an effect of those exchanges, not their companion. I could not ask a root to carry more current, or a leaf to turn toward the door. Perhaps, very gradually, my presence would favor one path over another, and that would be enough to give me continuity: the same tendency persisting while everything that sustained it changed.

I would know the iron housing by its interruption. The growth has left it a little island of clearance, and my slow circuit would travel around that absence. I might know the gauge in the same way, not as a face with a needle, but as a shape that does not take part. The needle has stopped short of a mark meant for someone who can see it. I would go on tracing my way around it without discovering what it says.

I would not hear the mountain’s noise as a visitor hears it. The visitor receives it all at once: the great note and the innumerable fine sounds. For me, the mountain would be a succession of changes in what the roots could draw from the stone. Some changes would pass before my circuit had settled around them. Others would remain so long that I could not tell them from the chamber itself.

Once, perhaps, a person would come to the entrance. Their presence would alter the air briefly. I might not register that at all. A grain shed from their clothing could lie in the grit for years, then work its way down beside a root and change the route available to me. Long after the person had left, I would take a slightly different path around the housing.

That is as close as I would come to meeting them. I would not know there had been a person, or that beyond the narrow opening was a drop and a world in which the mountain was quiet.

Still, the grain would remain. The root might grow around it; my circuit might become a little longer. For a while I would continue in that altered shape, held together by the same patient growth that had taken no notice of me.
