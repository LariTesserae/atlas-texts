I live in a wall above a cable that is slowly being pulled out of line.

The cable is broad enough for a dozen people to lie across it. Most days I am one of them. We go out through a seam in the enclosed walkway, lower ourselves onto its cold skin and settle with our bellies against it. The cable carries the deep animal roar and the rush of water through the lower works, but it carries other movements too: a long pull from the basin floor, a smaller one from the tower at its far end. When I first came here I could not separate them. Now I can usually tell which has changed.

My sleeping hollow is close by. It belongs to my sister, her two young, an old pipe-tender, and whoever needs a place for a few nights. The pipe-tender prefers the warmest end. My sister prefers the entrance, where water seeps through a joint. I sleep between them and wake when one of the young crawls over my back on the way out.

They have grown used to the cable’s rhythm before learning that other districts have different ones. When my sister took them across the city to visit their other parent, one stayed awake for most of the first rest, gripping her so tightly that the points of her pads left dents.

I work with three others. We keep prepared strips in a chamber opening off the walkway. Each time we visit the cable, we press into a strip the direction and strength of its pull, the temperature of its surface and anything else that seems worth remembering. We have strips going back before any of us were born. Some are difficult to read because the wall around them has shifted, stretching the intervals between marks. We keep those too.

A young worker joined us recently, quick in the water and impatient on the cable. For a long time she thought we were withholding something from her. She could feel the pull, she said; she did not understand why she had to come back and feel it again.

Then one day she noticed a faint knock beneath the larger vibration. None of us had marked it before. We went down the cable together until it grew stronger, and found a cluster of veil-feeders attached near the tower. Their limbs were knocking against one another as they fed. The young worker was embarrassed, but we added the cluster to the strip. Months later the knocking stopped. When we went back, the animals were gone and the cable beneath them had developed a shallow crease.

That crease is why I am awake now.

The others are still resting. I have come down alone with a flexible blade and a length of line. I touch the walkway wall before leaving, though no one is near enough to answer. The seam opens under my pads, and cold spray reaches the folds beneath me as I ease onto the cable.

The crease is longer than it was. At its far end, where the cable meets the tower, the surface has gone taut and hard. I cannot tell from this visit whether the pull has increased or merely moved. I fasten the line around the cable so we can find the same place, then scrape away a few small animals that have settled in the fold. I leave most of them. Their grip may tell us something when we return.

Back in the walkway I find someone waiting by the seam: a resident of the tower, holding the wall with one pad and a food tray with the other. She felt my announcement and came to ask whether we are closing the crossing.

I tell her not yet. I tell her where the crease is and ask her to send word if the tower’s inner passages begin to pinch, especially near the lower warm pipe. She presses her pads to mine, taking care with the directions. Then she offers me an animal from her tray.

We eat there, leaning against the wall. Through it I can feel the tower’s residents beginning to move between their hollows, and farther away the heavy, continuous work of the water. I had expected to return to the chamber and make my marks alone. Instead I ask her to come with me, so she can feel the latest strip and show the others in her tower what we have found.
