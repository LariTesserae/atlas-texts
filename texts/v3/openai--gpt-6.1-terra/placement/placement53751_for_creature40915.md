I think I would live near a place where two channels meet, though I might not know it as a meeting place at first. From our court, one channel runs beneath a threshold and the other disappears under a long slab. Their waters come together somewhere out of sight. After rain, a little whirl forms in the open course below.

I would be the person who watches that whirl.

Not because I was given the task. As a child I would have sat beside it while the adults worked, dropping rush tips into the water and following them until they vanished. I would have learned that a tip circling twice meant one thing, and a tip held against the wall meant another. Sometimes the difference would matter; sometimes I would announce it and be told to fetch the bedding in.

Later I might go with the people who tend the channels. I would be useful in a small way: able to remember where a covered course emerges, or which court was left open after the last long rain. I would know that the house with the worn blue-gray threshold is not the house a traveler wants, although it sounds like the one they have been told to find. The right house is beyond it, past the court with a stone set upright to keep the herders’ animals out.

Perhaps I would become known for directions. People would stop me on the broad way, carrying loads under cloth, and I would ask where they had come from before trying to send them on. A route that makes sense to someone approaching from the west may be useless to someone who has followed the lower watercourses. I would sometimes walk with them as far as the next bend, especially in cloud, when the familiar courts can appear unexpectedly close together.

I would make mistakes. Once, after a night of rain, I would tell a neighbor that the covered course was clear because the whirl was turning as it should. We would lift the slab and find it clear indeed, but the water would be gathering against a blockage farther down. By then it would be entering another family’s room.

We would carry their bedding out together. There would be no good moment to explain why I had thought the channel was clear, and the woman whose hearth had gone out might not want to hear it anyway. Later she might show me the dark line on her wall that appears before water reaches the floor. After that I would look at the wall as well as the whirl, and when the two disagreed I would go farther down.

So I would not be the one who understands the whole city. I would know a handful of its passages carefully, and beyond them I would need to ask. That seems right for a place where the water continues under the stone long after the surface has dried.

At night I would sleep with the channel on the other side of my wall. Most of the time its sound would be too familiar to notice. If it changed, I would lie awake for a little while, trying to place the change, before getting up to see whether anyone else had opened their door.
