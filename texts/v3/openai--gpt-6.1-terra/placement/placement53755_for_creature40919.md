At first I think I would be a bird, because the basin is easiest to understand from above. A flock lifting from the water could see the cloud on one rim and the pale sky on the other, the lines of meltwater branching and disappearing among the stones.

But the birds leave too much behind. They rise, circle, and land somewhere else; what happens under the ice or beneath the loose plates is closed to them.

I would be lower down, beside the warm seep, where a slab of rock rests unevenly on another. There is a gap under one edge, just high enough for a beetle to pass through. Water darkens the stone nearby, and a little warmth reaches the gap, though the air outside can still freeze.

I would be a spider there. My web would be a few threads across a sheltered opening, anchored to grains and rough points in the rock. Some mornings one would be broken, and I would replace it. The threads would tremble when wind found its way beneath the slab, and differently when a midge was caught.

I would know the basin by those interruptions. The tread of something large overhead. A stone shifting and settling. Water finding a new route after a night of frost. Once, a sharp report from somewhere in the ridge would travel through the slab, and grit would fall into the web. I might wait a long time before coming out to clear it.

In warmer hours I could move to the edge of my shelter. From there, the pool would be a narrow strip of light. Bird legs would pass through it, stopping and starting; a shadow might bring them all into motion at once. Beyond them I could see only a little of the opposite bank, and the lower stems of a cushion plant growing in its crack.

As evening came, ice would begin to form at the farther edge of the pool. The seep beside my slab would keep running. I would retreat beneath the rock, leaving the web at the opening, and wait there while water moved past and the stone slowly gave up the day’s warmth.
