At first I think I am under the stone.

There is a weight above me, a cold surface against my back, and the fine white legs of something passing over my mouth. I keep still until it has gone. Then I push, and the roof lifts: a cushion of leaves, holding the shape of the rock against its underside.

I have been beneath it long enough for damp to gather in my fur. When I crawl out, the roar is there. It was there under the leaves as well, but the leaves kept it from seeming so large.

I am one of the little animals with the curved toes. My belly nearly touches the stone. There is a bare place on my left side where the fur has worn away, and when I turn my head I can reach it with my mouth. I do this several times before I set off.

The nearest seam is full of dark water. I put my mouth to it and work my jaw. The water tastes of stone and something faintly bitter. A tiny white body is caught at the edge; I take that too.

Ahead is a wall. There are three stones in its highest part, one leaning over the other two. I know this wall, or one like it. Inside, the ground is dry enough that dust comes away on my feet. Sometimes another animal is there. We lie apart, each with our nose toward the stones, and neither of us follows when the other leaves.

Today I want to carry something back. It is not clear to me why until I find it: a narrow white piece beside the seam, jointed at one end. I take it crosswise in my mouth. It catches against the rock as I walk, so I stop and take it again, farther along.

The wall stays ahead. Once, when I look up, it is to my right, though I have not turned. I put the bone down and rest with my chin beside it. The higher note is as near as ever. My sides move quickly. I wait until they slow, then pick the bone up again.

A pale shape lies on the ledge above. I do not look at it for long. There is a little hollow beneath the ledge where the moisture collects, and sometimes the leaves there have tender undersides. When I pass through the hollow I find a bud instead. I grip its stem and pull it toward me, but it slips from my mouth. It springs back without opening.

By the time I reach the wall, the bone has become warm where I have held it. There are other fragments in the enclosure. Some have been here longer than I can remember; one has a strip of gray tissue still fastened to it. I lay mine beside a small stone at the foot of the wall. It rolls away. I nudge it back and press the stone against it with my nose.

Another animal is lying on the dry ground. Its eyes are open. I go close enough to smell its damp fur, then settle a short distance away. Between us is a scattering of dust, a curved rib, and the fragment I have brought.

After a while the other animal gets up. It noses the new fragment, takes it in its mouth and carries it to the opposite side of the enclosure. I watch it put the fragment down beside a gap in the wall. There it fits without rolling.

I stay where I am. The ground is dry under my belly, and there is a dark place between two stones where I can put my nose. The other animal folds its feet beneath itself beside the bone. We rest there together, apart, while the sound fills the valley.
