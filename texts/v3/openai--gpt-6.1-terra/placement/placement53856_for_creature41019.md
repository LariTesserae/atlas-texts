At first I would take myself for the water moving under the moss. I would feel my way around roots, find the soft undersides of things, and know the plain by the slight differences in its pressure: here a foot resting above me, here the slow opening of an amphibian’s throat.

But water passes too easily. I have to work my way through.

I am one of the many-footed animals, brown above and pale beneath. My back fits close under the moss. When I push forward, the stems lift a little and settle behind me, and the water carries a fine cloud from my mouth. Pale fish follow it. Sometimes one comes close enough that I feel its nose against my hindmost plates. I draw my feet in until it passes, then go on eating.

There is a place I return to where a dead stem crosses a channel. Its submerged side has softened. I can put my head beneath it and scrape for a long time, braced against two roots, while particles drift out into the pool. Above me, the living plant bears a broad leaf. I know when its grazers gather at the tip because the leaf lowers and touches the water near the end of my stem. A clear body may let go. A fish may turn.

The leaf rises again. I keep my hold.

From below, the plain is full of changes that do not travel far. A browser’s foot presses down nearby, and water enters my passage in a warm rush. I climb over a root to let it through. Later the weight lifts, the moss loosens, and I can pass beneath it again. I do not see the browser, except once, perhaps, the dark spreading tips of its toes.

When I reach an open pool I hesitate at its edge. There is more room there, but also a broad flat head almost indistinguishable from the bottom. The throat beneath it opens and closes. I wait among the roots until the head turns toward a low leaf. Droplets strike the pool, and I cross while the surface is still trembling.

I would not have much use for the mineral backs, though I might come upon one where it meets the wet ground. Its surface would be smooth against my feelers. I would follow its edge until I found moss again, and under the moss the familiar yielding stems, the water, and something soft enough to eat.
