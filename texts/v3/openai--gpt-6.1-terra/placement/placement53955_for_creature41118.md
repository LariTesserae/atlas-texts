At first I would look for myself in one of the dark seams, where there seems to be a path. From the ridge I might follow it with my eye almost to the valley floor. But down among the growths it is not one opening. It is a succession of them, some already closing, some full of water, some no wider than the space beneath a scale.

I think I would be near the lower end of one such seam, on a slope above the drainage channel. Not in the channel itself, where the water has enough force to carry grit and loose fibers away, but a little above it, where a jointed stalk has bent and caught against a bank.

I would be a branch growing from the bank. My scales would overlap closely in dry weather, their exposed faces dull green and marked with pale scratches. One side of me would be pressed against the fallen stalk. In that sheltered place I would have sent down fine strands, first into the wet hairs on a flattened leaf, then through them into the older mat below.

The root that fed me when I was young would no longer reach me. Perhaps it died in a dry summer; perhaps the slope shifted and pulled it apart. Its end would still lie somewhere uphill, difficult to tell from the other dark fibers. I would be living on water drawn through the newer strands, and when rain came I would spread my scales to receive the cold.

The fallen stalk would hold a little basin open beside me. Water would collect there after rain and stand long enough for a film to darken on the bottom. A crawler might work through the soft tissue under the stalk, packing its passage behind it. I would not know it as a neighbor in the way I know the pressure of the stalk against me, but its eating would make a difference. Over seasons, the support would soften. The basin would become shallower. One afternoon its water would run out through a gap that had not been there before.

My lower scales would stay wet for a while after the basin emptied. Then they would close, and grit left on their edges would dry in place. I imagine growing around those grains, wearing the slope’s color a little more each year.

A moth could emerge from the fallen stalk. Its opening might be on the far side, hidden from me, but once its wings were dry it might climb over my branch to reach the light. For a short time its pale body would rest against my scales. Then it would lift off above the channel, and the air would close behind it without moving me.

Eventually the stalk would split where it had been softened. Part of it would slide downhill, taking some of my strands with it. The branch would be pulled sideways and held there, its tip touching a mat it had never touched before. If water lingered at that contact, I could put down new strands. If it did not, the tip might dry and break away.

So I would be this branch for as long as the branch held together. After that, perhaps one piece would live and another would not. The seam above me would narrow; a different opening would appear below. From the ridge there would still be a dark line running down the green slope, and I would still be somewhere in it, though not quite where I began.
