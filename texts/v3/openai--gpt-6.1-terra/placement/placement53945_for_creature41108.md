I would live beneath the platform, in a space left between two fitted stones. It is not a burrow; there is nowhere to burrow. The space was made when the platform was built, perhaps by accident, and it is just wide enough for me to turn around if I draw in my arms.

I would be one of the passage-tenders. There may be others. Sometimes I find a tie I do not remember making, though I have also found ties that I remember making tomorrow.

My hands are broad at the fingertips, with a little groove in each nail for catching fiber. I can stand upright under most of the trees, but I usually go bent over. There is too much to see near the ground: a shell animal working across a path, or a raised seam whose green tips will be crushed if I step there. My shoulders are always dusted from the leaves.

In the morning I carry a shallow clay dish and a coil of fiber. I gather loose gypsum where the ridges have been scuffed apart and press it around roots that have come exposed. The pale seam-tenders work beside me sometimes. They can pack the powder more firmly than I can, if I give them a little time. I put the dish down and wait while they cross the fresh mound, their brushes moving underneath them.

I used to follow the passages all the way around the enclosure. Now I tend them in the order I find them. This is not always the same order. A path may bring me to a place I have just left, and I may see my own thumbprint still bright in the gypsum. I do not smooth it out. It helps to know that someone has been there, even if that someone was me.

The fronds are the most difficult. They look as though they will bend farther than they do. If one snaps while I am tying it back, a grazer will come for it eventually, but the broken end stays pale for days. I have learned to lift a leaf along its length before drawing it aside. There is a point at which it seems almost to help, curling gently around my hand. I tie it there, leaving enough fiber for it to move toward the light.

At midday I return to the platform and eat in its shade. I eat the soft kernels that fall from the crowns, and sometimes a little of the wax that gathers on the dish. The furred animals watch from above. One has begun to come down while I am there. It waits on a low branch until I crack a seed case, then takes the smaller piece. Its fingers are cool and very careful. Once, when I offered it a kernel between two fingers, it touched the groove in my nail before taking the food.

The shells need less attention than the paths, though I think of them more often. When they have gathered across the stone, I sweep them to one side with a strip of leaf. I try not to lift them. If I do, there will be another in its place, and then two things to wonder about.

Once I found a living shell animal beside the platform with an unfinished patch on its back. A shell on the stone had a chip of the same shape. I put the shell near the animal, in the seam it was following. By evening the animal had crossed beneath its edge and gone on. I left the shell there until the green nap grew around it.

At dusk the passages narrow. I make my last round without the dish, turning sideways between the leaves and listening for grazers. Their chewing often reaches me before they do. I wait until I can see where their heads are, then step around them. The furred creatures come down to drink, and for a little while the cool tiles are busy: small hands on the edges, broad mouths at the seams, my own reflection broken in the beads of water.

Under the platform I keep a stack of fiber and one whole seed case. I have not opened it. On some evenings I hear it knock softly, though when I bring it out in the morning it is still closed. I turn it over in my hands, looking for the place that is beginning to give, then put it back where the stones will hold it steady.
