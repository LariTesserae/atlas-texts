I would be on the sheltered face of a stone, just inside a ring.

At first I would be difficult to distinguish from the dust caught there: a pale branching crust, narrower than a finger, following a line where warmth came through. I would have grown from a flake pressed into place by a ring-maker. Most of the flake would have failed to take. A few fine edges would have remained against the stone and spread.

I would know the stone chiefly by its warmth. It would have a slow daily—or perhaps not daily, for there is no sun to tell me so—variation: a deepening warmth followed by a long release. When the large stones on the fissure side shifted, the change would come faster. The exposed edge of me would become dry and brittle; the part in the hollow would last longer.

Dust would settle on me. Some grains I could grow around. Others would cover too much, and that branch would stop. I would spread unevenly, thriving in a shallow groove, failing on the smooth face above it. From a little distance I might appear delicate and complete. Closer, I would be full of breaks.

The ring-maker would visit. I would feel its arrival through the stone before its weight came against the sheltered face. It would draw some of the dust and crust into its mouth. Once it took a whole growing edge; another time it left a fragment pressed so firmly into a groove that the fragment continued to spread.

I could not know whether it meant to tend me. I would grow where it had put me, and be eaten where it could reach.

There would be others like me on the underside of the neighboring stones. When the ring was intact, a few of our branches might nearly meet across the narrow joints. A fine fall of dust could bury one of us and leave another exposed. We would not all respond alike. One pale patch might disappear after a tremor; another, no larger than before, would remain for a very long time.

Then the ground beneath the ring would lift.

My stone would turn. For a while its sheltered face would lie open to the warm gust rising from a new crack. Dust would blow away. The groove where I had held moisture and warmth would become too hot, and the oldest part of me would harden and separate from the stone.

Some of the ring’s stones would settle at different heights. Mine would come to rest against a larger one, with a thin space between them. The ring-maker might return and move both stones, or it might not. A flake of me would be left in that space. If warmth continued to seep through, it might slowly put out another edge.

I would be there, then: not the whole pale pattern that had covered the first stone, but that remaining edge, growing along whatever surface it had been given.
