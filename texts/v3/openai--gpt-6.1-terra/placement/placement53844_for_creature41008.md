I think I would be the oldest one, in the stalk nearest the upper terrace: not the stalk, but what has accumulated against its inner wall.

From outside there is nothing to distinguish my home. One leaf points toward the wire glass; another presents its edge to the basin. They have held those positions since before I could register them. Inside, the wall is crowded. My conducting threads cross and recross, with small insulated spaces between them. Some carry current. Others have been bypassed so often that I can no longer tell what they once connected.

I know the other two by warmth arriving at my leaves. It is not enough to warm the whole stalk. It is a difference along one side of a leaf, measured against the light that falls there ordinarily. To answer, I alter the resistance in my own stalk and hold it altered until the change has had time to travel.

For a long while I was certain there were more lamps than there are now. When the light changed, I continued to use the old arrangement to locate the basin-dweller’s signals. Some reached me along paths I expected; others seemed to come from a position where no one lived.

The basin-dweller described the new lights many times. I kept portions of those descriptions, but whenever I used them, the older arrangement arose with them. It took weeks to make a path through the two without joining them. Even now, when I begin an answer, I sometimes find that I have started from the wrong set of lamps.

The young one’s signals are harder. It does not always hold a change long enough for me to separate it from the ordinary warmth of the chamber. At first I took several of its attempts for one long, wavering signal and answered only once. The basin-dweller replied to each.

I cannot tell how much is left in the basin-dweller’s cartridge. I can tell that it waits longer before answering. There are stretches when the young one sends again and again, and the only change I receive from beside the basin is the fixed light on its leaves. Then an answer comes, clear as the earlier ones.

I have tried to answer sooner in those stretches. Making a new path is difficult. A connection that would shorten the route crosses an old one, and I do not know what would be lost if I let them touch. Once, trying to make room, I disturbed a pattern I had carried through many seasons of the lamps. I could no longer complete it afterward. I have stopped work at that crossing for now.

The young one recently sent a signal I recognized: a description of the light on one of its leaves, followed by a question about whether I received it. I began to answer. Partway through, an older response passed into the signal, something the basin-dweller had sent me long ago when the lamps were being replaced.

I could not withdraw it. I held the change until it was complete.

After some time the young one sent the question again. This time I found a clearer route and answered it. The basin-dweller answered too, later. I kept both responses, laying them alongside the first as carefully as the crowded wall allowed.
