At first I think I am the water in the bowl.

I know its round edge and the cold metal beneath it. I know the small weight of a moth that lands on the surface and struggles there. By morning the moth is still, and the water has gone down through a seam too narrow for the surveyors to see.

Then they lift the bowl, and I remain.

I am somewhere below it, in the new gray ground. Not very far below: I can feel the day’s cold on the surface and, deeper down, a slow pressure that has not yet taken a shape. When a person walks overhead, each foot presses a little of me aside. I remember the weight after they have gone, though not for long. A rail is easier. It lies still and gives me time to learn its length.

The surveyors come in pairs. One wears a coat with a square repair on the sleeve; the other carries the bowls nested together, with a cloth between them so they do not knock. I do not see these things as they do. The coat brushes a marker. The bowls touch my surface one after another. I know the pair by their habits before I know anything like their shapes.

I have tried to keep their markers in place. Sometimes I can. Sometimes the ground beneath one is still arriving, and the iron has to go somewhere. Once I brought a stake up whole and laid it down where the square-sleeved surveyor could find it. They stood over it for a long time. The next morning there were two bowls.

I drank from both and spent the day pressing the ground around their other stakes until it would hold. This took longer than I expected. By afternoon a machine came across, its wheels heavy enough to reach places I had not felt before. One wheel sank. The driver stopped, and everyone waited. I tried to make the soft place firm, but I pushed against the wheel as well, and it tilted.

The workers brought boards. They slid them under the wheel and drove the machine free. No one left a bowl that evening, which may have been because the crew went home by another route. I thought about it all night, or for as much of the night as I could hold together.

I am not the whole plain. Beyond the nearest stakes there are places where the ground rises without my knowing how. Sometimes a new seam opens between two parts of me, and I lose the small things I was keeping: the pressure of a footprint, the position of a stone, the taste of water that had stood in a metal bowl. Later I may find them again, farther apart.

The city covers ground I once knew. Under it, I am interrupted by foundations, drains and the packed beds of rails. Plant roots enter where they can. They are thin at first, then persistent, and they do not lift out neatly like iron. Around them I become difficult to move. I think this is one reason the older streets stay where they are.

There is a workshop that has crossed me three times. I know it by a loose piece underneath its platform, which taps against the ground whenever the wheels pass over a joint. On its latest journey it stopped above me for most of a day. Someone inside tipped a pot of water out through a doorway. It ran down the platform and into a shallow fold, carrying a little soil and the root of a plant with it.

The root stayed. For several days I could feel it pressing into the damp place. Then the surface stiffened around it, and I lost track of it. I hope it reached one of the seams.

The surveyors have begun leaving a short pole laid across two stones near the bowls. I do not know what they want to measure with it. This morning the pole was partly buried where the ground had risen during the night. I loosened one end, then the other, and left it across the stones again. It took most of the daylight.

When the pair returned, the one with the square sleeve crouched beside it. They touched the pole, then the two stones, and called their partner over. Both looked for a while. One made a mark in a notebook.

They filled the bowl before they went. This time I left the water where it was until they had reached the first rail, so they could turn and see it there. I do not know if they did.
