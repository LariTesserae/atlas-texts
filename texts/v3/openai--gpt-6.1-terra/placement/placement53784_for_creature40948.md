I would be the person at the pumping station who keeps the book of small repairs.

The book would lie open on a table just inside the door, its pages held down by a piece of dark stone from the bank. At first I would try to make it orderly: one column for the date, another for the fault, a third for what was done. Soon there would be notes in the margins about a swallow’s nest above door seven, a cart wheel that squeaks only when it is loaded, and a patch of ground that stays damp longer than it should.

People would come in to find a washer or borrow a pencil, and I would ask them questions. Did the wheel squeak before the rain? Was the damp patch there yesterday? Sometimes they would know; sometimes they would tell me to go and look for myself.

I would like the walk across the yard when there was something to check. The pump’s beat would travel through my boots as I passed the station wall, then become harder to distinguish among the cart wheels and voices. I would pause beside a rail to see whether the water in its hollow had dried, or stoop to turn a beetle right side up. By the time I reached the workshop I might have collected an empty jar for someone and agreed to bring back a child’s forgotten coat.

I would not always be right. Once I might hear a change in the pump and enter a confident note about a worn bearing. The mechanic would come in, listen, and find a loose panel vibrating against the wall. I would cross out my diagnosis but leave it readable, then write *panel tightened* underneath. After that I would be more careful to put down what I heard before putting down what I thought it meant.

In spring I would help grease the crane, mostly by carrying things and keeping the tin from tipping in the grass. Up close, the vine would make the work awkward. Its new stems would catch on our sleeves, and the mechanic would ask me to hold one gently aside while she reached the bearing. When we finished, I would stand back and look up at the arm. It might not move at all that day.

Later, someone crossing the yard would stop at my door and say it had turned toward the houses. I would go outside to see. We would stand there a little while, squinting into the flowers, trying to remember exactly where they had been that morning.
