I live under the raised sidewalk behind the northern power house, in the third space between its supports. The ground there is dry except when the drain backs up, and a pipe passes close enough to keep the nest warm. I have lined it with grass, blue feathers and the soft gray stuffing from a split in the pipe. The feathers work their way to the outside. Sometimes I find one caught in my whiskers when I wake.

From the nest I can hear feet overhead. Most pass quickly, with the same pause between steps as they reach the loose plank. One person always stops there. Through a gap I see the toes of her boots turn toward the power house, then toward the empty ground. She stands long enough for the warmth to leave the wood above her before she moves on.

I come out after the shift has passed. The supports give way to a clear strip of dust, then a channel with a stone cover missing. Beetles gather on the damp sides of the channel. I eat them there, leaving the wings in a pile because they stick between my teeth.

On the far side is a workshop with birds in its upper windows. They know me. If I climb the downpipe in daylight, the nearest one calls before I have reached the first bracket, and the others come to the sill with their wings half open. At night they are slower. I have taken eggs from the shelf inside the broken ventilator, though once an egg rolled from my mouth and broke on the paving below. The birds called over it until morning.

In the nest there are two young ones. They are old enough to follow me out but not yet as far as the workshop. One has learned to catch beetles; the other paws at their wings and lets the bodies escape. I bring them what I can carry.

One evening I return with half a fish from the grate at the pond. The fish is cold against my throat. I pass the missing channel cover, cross the dust and go under the sidewalk. The pipe is there, and the nest is there, but the young are not.

I put down the fish and search the neighboring spaces. A dry leaf has come in from the open ground. There is a new crack across one of the supports. I go back to the nest and listen.

I can hear them moving. The sound is faint, as if they are deep inside the insulation, though there is no opening large enough for them to enter. I scratch at the gray wrapping until a piece comes away. The sound stops.

Then one of them calls from behind me.

I turn and find them both on the other side of the support. They push past me into the nest and begin eating the fish. There is fish skin stuck to the whiskers of the smaller one. I clean it off, and afterward I carry the remains farther under the pipe, where the beetles cannot reach them so easily.

The next night the young follow me out. We go as far as the channel. I cross it in one bound, but when I turn, they are still beside the sidewalk. They can see me; their heads follow as I move. I call them and the larger one runs forward.

It disappears behind a stem of dry grass. It does not come out the other side.

The smaller one waits. I go back across the channel. There is no stem beside the sidewalk, and no sign of the other young one in the dust. I search beneath the boards, calling now and then. At last I hear it answering from somewhere above me.

I find it on the power-house steps, standing beside the door where the workers go in. It has never been there before. When I approach, it runs to me so quickly that it bumps my shoulder.

We go home along the wall, avoiding the open dust. It is a longer way. The smaller one tires and I have to wait while it rests with its belly on the cold paving.

In the morning the person with the stopping feet comes to the loose plank. She stays longer than usual. I hear chalk against the board on the wall above us, then her boots crossing the dust toward the channel. She comes back by the wall. Before she leaves, she sets a small stone beside the gap under the sidewalk.

For several nights afterward, people use the longer way. The young grow stronger and begin to range beyond the channel on their own. Sometimes they come back together; sometimes one arrives wet from the pond while the other is still hunting beetles. I wait near the nest until I hear both of them under the boards.

The stone remains beside the gap. Rain darkens it, and then it dries again. One evening the larger young one climbs onto it to look across the empty ground. From there it can see the workshop windows. The birds are gathering on the sill, calling to others still out over the ponds.
