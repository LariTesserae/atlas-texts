I might begin as a grain of grit in the cup beneath the overhang. There is room under me for several animals, and when a drop falls I turn over. The animals scatter, then some of them come back.

But the grain does not have to find another shelter if the cup dries too completely. I think I would be one of the animals beneath it.

For much of the time I would be contracted among the mineral grains, with too little water in my body for anything to happen quickly. I would not know how long the cold had lasted. When meltwater reached me, I would take it in and unfold, and the hairs at my mouth would begin to beat.

The cup would be a small place even to me. There would be its sloping stone floor, the grain above me, the other grains I could move between. Food would come from elsewhere: bacteria in the water, scraps carried in on the wind. I would draw them toward me for as long as the water remained.

A drop from the slab could overturn that whole arrangement. I would tumble, come to rest against a different piece of grit, and begin again. Perhaps another animal would settle beside me. We would feed in the same narrow current without needing to recognize one another.

When the water froze, I would contract. I might be held under the ice for a time, or left exposed as the last liquid retreated. The same cup could thaw again tomorrow; I could remain there through many such days. Or, dried and light enough, I could be lifted out by a gust.

From the air the basin might be crossed in an instant. Most of it would offer me no place to wake. I could pass the old ring, the worked flake, the stacked stones, and be carried over the rim. If I landed instead in another hollow where water gathered, I could unfold there, under a grain I had never sheltered beneath before.

I would not have a name for the mountain or a way to know how many cups it held. My life would be measured in periods of water, each one giving me time to feed before the cold closed in again.
