I would live at the third iron post east of a pumping station, where the ground breaks into a shallow gully. The post leans slightly. At its foot is a collar of pale stone, and beside that a crack just wide enough for me to enter.

I would be a lizard, an old one by the measures that matter there: I would know which side of the post to wait on when cloud comes from the escarpment, and how far I can cross the open rock before the heat becomes dangerous. My back would be the colour of the ledge, except after rain, when the dust washes from it and I am darker for a little while.

At dawn I would come out facing the post. Water does not always collect there. When it does, it travels down the iron in thin lines, joins beneath the collar and hangs for a moment before falling. The younger lizards rush at the first glint. I wait near the bottom, where several paths meet. Sometimes a beetle is already there, its legs braced on the stone. Sometimes there is enough for all of us.

The station changes the ground around it. One pipe shades a strip of rock through the morning, and beneath that strip the stone stays bearable longer than the ledges beyond. There is a seam under the metal wall where insects gather. Once, after people had been working there, water dripped for two days from a fitting. Lizards came from cracks I had never seen occupied. On the third day a vehicle returned, and a person turned something with a tool. We waited under the pipe until long after the engine stopped.

I would know the people chiefly by their feet and by the shadows of their hands. When they are there, I keep close to an opening. They lift stones, set down hot tools and sometimes disturb the beetles beneath the building. One of them once placed a boot over my crack while I was still outside it. I went under the wall instead and stayed there through the bright part of the day.

The post would not be my only water. Farther down the gully, a slanted slab keeps its underside cool, and beads form there even on some mornings when the iron is dry. The slab is a better place in a year with little cloud, though I have to leave it early: a dark bird has learned to watch the approach. I have seen it standing very still above the slab, looking down.

At midday I would be inside the crack, with my body against stone that still holds some of the night. Through the narrow entrance I could see the light move over a patch of ground. Grit would fall occasionally from somewhere above. When the engine ran, the vibration would reach me through my belly before the noise filled the gully.

By evening I would come out again. The long shadows would have met across the slope, and the herd might be passing, their broad feet finding purchase among the slabs. I would let them go by before crossing to the warm vent, where the reddish growth is sometimes soft enough to bite.

On a cloudy night, I would return to the post and climb the low stone beside it. The iron cools faster than the rock. I would watch its dark surface until the first moisture appeared, then move down to the collar and wait for the separate beads to find one another.
