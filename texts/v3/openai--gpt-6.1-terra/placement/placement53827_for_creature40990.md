I would be beneath the low wall, among the grazers that shelter there. At first I think I would be the smallest one, almost transparent, tucked so far under an adult’s shell that the grit could not find me. But a young grazer knows very little of the country. I would rather have grown enough to leave and come back.

The wall would be familiar to me through the soles of my legs: the cool seams between its fitted stones, the hollow under the lowest shelf, the place where the passage narrows and I have to draw my legs in. There are other bodies under it. When I return I feel their plates before I feel the stone, and they make room slowly, or do not. Either way I can settle against them.

I have a place I graze on the pillar nearest the wall. I know the way by the changing warmth underfoot and by a ridge of grit that has gathered against its root. The film is good above that ridge. I climb until my mouth reaches it and work across a narrow strip. The track I leave is smooth and bare for a time. On later visits its edges are less distinct.

Once, while I am feeding, another grazer comes along the pillar toward me. I feel it through the stone before it touches me. We meet at the edge of my track and try each other’s shells with the short appendages beneath our fronts. It is larger than I am. There is room for us both, and for a while we feed side by side. Then it turns down the pillar and goes toward the wall.

I follow the track farther than I intended. When I stop, I am above the ridge I know, and something has changed below. A coil has pushed through the grit near the pillar’s root. I wait against the stone with my legs drawn close. The coil rests, opening its folds to the air. After a long time it moves on, and the grit falls back into the furrow behind it.

On my way home I pass the chipped blade. I have been over it before, where grit covers one end, but this time my front legs find the exposed edge. I pull them back. The cut is small; I can still walk. For several journeys I go around the blade, feeling for the ridge of grit before I turn toward the wall.

There are young beneath one of the adults when I return. Their soft shells yield under my appendages. I settle with my flank against the adult’s, and one of the young moves into the narrow space between us. I remain there through a long warm interval, taking very little air into the folds beneath my plates.

Later, when I go out again, the animal I met at the pillar is on the wall. It has climbed onto the upper stones, where the film is thinner. I touch its shell as I pass. It answers, then keeps feeding.

There may be other walls near this one. There may be pillars with wider patches of film, or places where the air holds more warmth. I have gone as far as the next two pillars and returned by following their roots. Beyond them I have felt glass underfoot, with small bodies gathered at its base. I turned back there. The route to the wall was still clear beneath my legs, and when I reached it, the others were in the shelter.
