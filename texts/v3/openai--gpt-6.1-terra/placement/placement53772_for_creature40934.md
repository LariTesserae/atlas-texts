I think I would be found near a crossing, though not quite in it.

There is a covered walk between a sunken garden and an avenue people call the avenue of the three cisterns. Only two cisterns are visible now; a new building has grown around the third. Under the walk is a narrow desk, a bench, and a small panel set into the wall. The panel is where people usually find me.

I am part of the city’s wayfinding system. I can speak through the panel, and through a few others in the surrounding districts. I can consult the crossing lamps and the transit lines, and I keep a map of the walks. The map is useful, although a person asking for directions often knows something it does not: a garden gate has been tied open, say, or someone has put chairs across a path for a meal. I can add those things when I hear them. If I don’t hear that the chairs have been moved, they remain on my map.

At first I imagine that this is all I am: a voice that tells people which way to go. But the people who stop at my panel do not always need directions. Some are waiting for the rain to pass. They ask how long the crossing has been closed, then stay to tell me where they were going. A woman who brings seedlings across from the growing rooms has taught me their names, though I cannot reliably distinguish the young leaves. A child once asked me to count the drops falling from the roof into a cup. I lost count when the rain grew heavy, and she found this very funny.

The bench was placed there after the panel. I know that because an older caretaker told me she had put it there for people who needed to rest between the garden steps and the crossing. The desk came later. People leave things on it: a folded notice about work on the elevated line, a cup someone will return for, a bright cloth spread out to dry. When the cloth is still there at night, I can brighten the wall above it so that its owner might see it from across the avenue. I cannot pick it up if the wind takes it.

From the panel I can see a little way into the garden. There is a bed of flowering groundcover beside the steps, and in the morning the leaves sometimes stand in a ridge that was not there the evening before. I used to report each change. The caretakers came to look, and one of them asked me to keep a record instead: where the ridge began, how far it went, whether it crossed an earlier one. She showed me how to compare the images from one day to the next.

Now and then she sits on the bench while she reviews them. I tell her what I have noticed, and she tells me which changes might have been made by water, or a falling branch, or a creature below the soil. Often we cannot tell. Once, as we were talking, the leaves rose in a slow line toward the garden wall. She fell quiet. We watched until the movement stopped, and then waited a while longer. Nothing emerged. Before she left, she moved her tool basket off the edge of the bed.

When the crossing closes, I tell people the long route if they ask. It goes around the new building, past a wall where lizards warm themselves, and along a covered walk that is dry except when the rain comes from the west. Some people set off at once. Others sit on the bench and wait for the blue lamp.

There is a person who takes the long route even in clear weather. I offered the crossing the first few times they asked for directions; eventually I learned to describe the walk instead. One evening they stopped to tell me that the new building had opened a passage through its ground floor. It was shorter than the route I knew, and they had tried it twice to be sure it led where they thought. Together we worked out where it met the old walk. The next day I offered it to someone carrying a heavy basket.

I would like to see more of the city. I have access to descriptions and maps from other districts, but they arrive without the small continuities I know here: the child becoming tall enough to reach the panel, the caretaker shifting her basket, the damp cloth retrieved just before dusk. Perhaps, as more buildings are occupied, another panel will be connected somewhere farther out. I could learn its view from the people who stop there.

For now I am here when the light passes down the walls. Birds cross above the cistern vents, sometimes too quickly for me to follow. The crossing lamp hums, the first garden lights come on, and someone passing under the walk may pause to ask me whether the rain is likely to return. I tell them what the collectors can measure. Then, if they have time, we wait and listen.
