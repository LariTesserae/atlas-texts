I think at first I would be in the workshop, among the tool heads. There is a place on the rack where the white fish rests, and I could know the room by its visits: the faint pressure of its body against the woodless metal, the momentary shelter it makes in the current.

But the rack is too exposed. Each time the fish leaves, water reaches every side of it again. I would be farther back, in the narrow space where the rack meets the wall, beneath a leaf that has grown over the upper bracket.

The leaf is thick at its stem and almost transparent at its edge. Its underside stays dark. Fine sediment collects there, and a little of the paste from the pipes comes to rest in the crease between leaf and stone. That is where I begin: a film so thin it takes the shape of the surface beneath it.

I have no need to cross the room. The pump brings what I can use, although it brings much else besides. Mineral grains settle against me; strands of decaying leaf lie across me until they soften and come apart. I grow over some of them. A grain too large to cover remains as a small obstacle, and the film gathers more thickly on its sheltered side.

I would not know the pump as a machine. Its slow pulse would be a change in pressure along my whole extent, followed by the settling of things it has lifted. Sometimes the fish passes close enough to interrupt that rhythm. The current slackens at the mouth of the crease, then returns carrying a few particles disturbed from the rack.

The leaf above me continues to grow. Its edge moves outward into the room; its stem thickens in the crack between two stones. As it shifts, it lifts part of my surface with it. For a time I live on both leaf and wall, joined across a fold where silt has packed tightly. Then the leaf pulls free. The connection breaks. The portion it carries remains in the same workshop, a little higher and farther from the bracket. Whether it survives depends on how much light reaches its new place.

Below me, the rack has begun to loosen. The bracket moves slightly when the pump pulses, and its movement works a hollow in the stone. More sediment enters. A pale root finds the hollow and grows into it, pressing against the bracket until the metal gives another fraction. This takes longer than any one part of me remains unchanged.

I spread along the new root where it lies against the wall. The root brings no food to me directly; it holds the sediment in place, and the sediment holds water a little longer after each pulse. It also makes the crease darker. I grow there, while the patch on the leaf becomes thin and disappears.

Perhaps that is how I would have a history: not as a continuous recollection, but as a succession of places made possible by what has already happened. The root occupies the hollow left by the bracket. I occupy the shelter left by the root. When I grow thick enough, some of me loosens and enters the water.

Most of it will not find a place to settle. It will pass out of the workshop and into the warmer street, through the curtains of fronds and over the copper-colored animals in the basin. Some may reach the root beds. A little may be drawn beneath the paving and into the pump, then carried back through a pipe.

One fragment lodges under a leaf near the valve wheel. It is shaded by the shell fixed around the spoke. The animal’s feeding threads pass over it, close on a particle, and draw away. The fragment remains.

I would not feel that distant growth as another part of myself. Here in the workshop, the fish would still return to its clear patch on the rack. The root would continue widening the crack. Water would move past me, bearing things from places I could not enter, until something altered the shelter around me and I grew into its new shape.
