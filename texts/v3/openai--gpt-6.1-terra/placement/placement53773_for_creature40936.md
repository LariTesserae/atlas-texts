I would be in a joint near the foot of a ridge, where the seepage arrives.

The joint is narrow at its mouth and wider underneath. A frond lies over it, its ridges resting on the stone. When the current strengthens, the frond lifts a little, and cold water enters. When it slackens, the frond settles back. Grit gathers along its edge.

At first I might seem to be the pale film on the shaded face of the block: a branching patch, following the shallow marks left by a tool. But some of what sustains that patch lives deeper in the joint, where mineral-rich water moves past grains held together by dark strands. Some lives in the frond’s fine channels. There are others among us that take what the first ones release, and still others that live on their remains. I would be this small company, as long as its members remained close enough to feed one another.

I would have no single body to turn toward the water. A change at the mouth of the joint would reach me gradually, as it changed what was available to the life inside. A grain settling on the film might become a place to grow. A crustacean grazing across it would leave a narrow path, which might close again.

Above me, a stem has grown from another joint. Its base is thick and its blades hang among the blades of its neighbours. Part of our company lives inside it too. The stem draws on the seepage, and when an old blade folds down, its tissues slowly become food for the bed beneath it. I would not know the whole height of the stem. I would know where its base presses against the block, where it has widened the joint a little, and where dead matter collects in the shelter it makes.

The block was once shaped by hands. I would spread along one of the marks left on it, because the groove holds a little more water and fine material than the surface beside it. The mark runs beneath me and continues beyond the edge of the film. I would follow it without knowing how it was made.

Once, perhaps, the block shifts. The movement is slight: one edge sinks into the grit and the other opens a space beneath the stem. A stretch of film is torn. On one side, the torn edge remains attached to the block; on the other, it clings to the grains. Both sides go on living. In the newly opened space, seepage reaches surfaces that have been closed for a long time.

I would grow there too, though slowly. The frond above might settle into a new position. The stem might lean and then remain as it is for centuries, its base enclosing more sediment. The two torn edges might never meet again.

If you came back later, I could not show you which patch had been mine. The joint would still hold life, though, and the tool mark would still pass under it, becoming harder to feel as the film thickened.
