I think I would be found near the pool, though not always beside it. There is a covered workspace a short way uphill where people bring cracked cups and loosened roof spouts. The roof is low enough that, when it rains, we can hear which spout has begun to leak before anyone sees it.

I mend clay. Not the fine sort of work, if anyone here has ever made fine clay things. I press fresh clay into the split rims of cups, replace the little sills across doorways, and make plugs for holes in cistern walls. A repair has to dry before it can be trusted, which means there are days when everyone wants my help at once and nothing I do will help them until tomorrow.

At first I imagine that the workspace is mine. It is not. In the mornings a woman scrapes hides there, and the wet smell stays after she has rolled them up. I work in the afternoon, when there is a little light from the pool. We keep our tools in different boxes, but she uses my smooth stone to weight a corner of hide, and I use one of her blunt scrapers to lift old clay from a sill. Sometimes we remember to put them back.

My own house is farther up, in a passage so narrow that a board laid across one threshold can obstruct another. I have a dry room, or a room we call dry, with my bedding on a shelf. Beneath the shelf there are two pots of clay covered with damp cloth. I once kept them on the floor. After a night of high water I found one full of gray silt, and for days afterward every repair I made had little black grains showing through it. My neighbors were polite about this until the repairs began to crumble.

On an ordinary evening I come down to the pool to rinse my hands. There is someone on the washing ledge beating a cloth against stone, and a child beside the steps holding a cup under a drip. The cup is already full. I point this out, and the child says she knows: she is seeing whether it can become fuller. We watch for a while. The water rises a little above the rim and trembles there before it spills.

The pool is below the second notch. I look because everyone looks. On my way back I tell a man carrying split reeds that it is below the second, and he says it was there an hour ago. He sounds disappointed in me for bringing old news.

That night I wake to a cup ticking much faster than it did when I went to bed. There has been no rain. I lie still long enough to hear a board scrape somewhere in the passage, then get up and feel for my soles. The water outside is cold, but not yet over them.

At the pool, a woman is already crouched by the notches with her hand on the step. She says the water is just short of the third. While we watch, it reaches the cut and darkens the stone inside it.

She goes toward the gate. I turn back through the passages, knocking on the doors where I know people sleep heavily. At one house I have to call twice. The person who opens is annoyed until he sees me standing in water; then he asks whether I have knocked next door. I have, but I go back and knock again because I am no longer certain.

By the time the gates are open, the lane outside my house is shining from wall to wall. A board has floated away from a neighbor’s sill and lodged against mine. I take it back, and together we set a stone on either end. There is an argument from farther down about a blocked drain, followed by the familiar sound of a slab being levered up.

In the morning, after the water has begun to fall, I go to see whose sill needs mending. At the lifted slab, two children are returning the pale creatures to the seam on pieces of bark. One creature curls around the end of a child’s thumb and stays there. She waits for it to uncurl rather than shake it off.

The clay in my pots has stayed clean this time. I carry a lump of it down to the workspace, where the hide scraper is already at work. She has set my smooth stone aside for me, on the driest part of the bench.
