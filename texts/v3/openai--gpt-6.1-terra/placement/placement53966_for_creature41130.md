I would be low in the wall, where the pale shelf meets a darker layer of stone. My exposed surface would be small, scarcely wider than one of the white rings. Most of me would lie folded behind it, with my deepest edge reaching toward the seam.

At first I think I would know myself by the water: the long warmth, the sudden small weight, the opening of my folds around what slips through. But others reach the seam too, and what arrives there has already passed through stone and air I cannot feel. I might know myself better by a particular fault in the wall—a place that presses twice when the mountain rises, the second pressure gentler than the first. I would make room for it before I knew I was doing so.

The others would know it too, though not as I do. What I pass to them is changed by the shape of me. One receives a longer pulse; another receives two pulses where I felt one. Farther along the wall, someone may learn to brace for a movement that has already ended at my surface.

When a bead reaches the seam, I open. Water enters the narrow spaces between my folds, carrying its slight load of mineral. I take what I can. Later, near the surface, I release the mineral into the shelf. The ring it leaves is not mine alone. It may cross an older ring, and when the wall strains, I feel both as one uneven ridge.

For a long time I have been growing a fold that does not quite follow the others. It begins deep within me and returns toward the exposed surface. At first it is only a place where pressure lingers. Then, during one of the mountain’s slower movements, it opens.

I do not divide cleanly. For a while there are two surfaces answering the wall, and a broad edge still joining us behind them. The newer surface feels the fault’s second pressure less distinctly. It is closer to the shelf and feels the rings more clearly. When water comes, we both open; when it is scarce, we close at slightly different times.

The difference travels along the fine lines. Bodies above us adjust, though I cannot tell what they make of it. Much later, warmth comes back down through them, carrying a distinction I have not felt before: cold air touched the upper stone, but no water reached us.

I can hold that distinction. I cannot make it into a bead.

So I alter a fold near the seam, leaving it ready to open at a slighter tremor. My neighbor does the same a little later, or perhaps has already done it and I am only now receiving the change. Above us, the upper inhabitants hold their places against the narrowing. Their effort arrives as a faint, sustained pressure.

When the next bead falls, it is smaller than some I remember. It reaches the seam. We open around it, and the pressure above continues for a little while after the water is gone.
