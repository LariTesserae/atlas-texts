I think I would begin at the route board outside the old dye works, where the upper walkway forks. It has a shallow roof to keep off the rain, though water still creeps behind the clear cover and leaves a cloudy patch over the eastern terraces.

I would be the person who updates it. Not the surveyor who decides a wall is unsafe, or the operator who closes a channel, but the one who gathers their notices and makes them legible to someone trying to get home with groceries.

In the morning I would walk the posted routes before changing the board. A closure can be accurately reported and still difficult to find: a gate swung across a stair, perhaps, with its notice facing the wrong way. I would turn the notice around, then continue by whatever path remained. I’d carry a roll of tape, a grease pencil, and a notebook whose pages had gone soft at the corners.

At first I imagine myself good at this because I like maps. But the work would demand something less tidy. A route that is open to the demolition crew might have a ladder that an older resident can’t manage. A detour that looks short on the board might pass a waste lift where gulls gather and make a child unwilling to go alone. I would learn to add those things in the margins, where there was room, and to listen when someone said, “You can’t really go that way.”

People would stop me while I was working. Sometimes they’d have news: a footbridge reopened, a handrail loose, a familiar shortcut blocked by a stack of willow cuttings. Sometimes they’d simply want to know where I had put a place they still called by its old name.

“The blue door,” someone might say.

“Which blue door?”

“The one that used to be red.”

That would be enough, some days. On others I would have to ask them to show me.

I think I’d live nearby, in a narrow room on the sheltered side of a workshop. There would be a tray for my boots and a pot of something green on the sill, probably doing poorly because I kept forgetting to turn it toward the light. In the evening I’d feel the pumps through the chair and occasionally go out again, if their rhythm changed and the lower-terrace notices hadn’t yet come through.

After a while, I might start keeping the superseded maps. Not all of them—there would be too many—but the ones people had written on: *steps slippery here*, *nest above lamp*, *ask at the pump house*. I’d flatten them under a board to dry.

The current map would stay outside, where everyone could correct it.
