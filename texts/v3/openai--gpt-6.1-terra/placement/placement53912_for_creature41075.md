I think I would be found kneeling where an avenue meets a court, with my hat turned up beside me to catch the little knots people bring.

I would be a route keeper, though that sounds more settled than the work is. No one can keep a route in the same place. I would walk certain crossings often enough to notice when the leaves have changed their minds about bearing us. After rain, people would come and tell me what they had felt underfoot: a softness near the western tower, a cold place, a patch that sprang back too quickly. Then I would go and feel it myself.

My feet would be broad even for the basin, the toes rather crooked from an old injury. When I was young I gripped the mat as I fell, and tore it. I remember less of the fall than of the adults pulling me free while water came up through the opening. For a long time afterward, whenever I crossed an avenue, I held my toes stiffly above the leaves. My mother had to teach me again how to let my weight down.

Now I would carry a thin basket, a roll of cloth and three colors of knotted fiber. One color means the surface is weak. One means a passage or crossing has moved. The third means *look here*: something has happened that I do not yet know how to name.

On a morning after a hard rain, a child might find me at the court with a yellow knot in her hand. She says her grandfather’s doorway has opened in the wrong tower. He went through carrying a bowl of soaked roots and came out beside a sleeping browser, which woke and followed him into the court.

This is not quite my work. I would tell her so, and she would tell me she has already been to the resin household. Someone from there is coming when they have finished their breakfast. The child wants me because her grandfather says the avenue feels wrong too.

We would walk there together. She would go ahead, placing her feet very carefully until she forgot she was doing it. At the crossing, she would stop and wait for me to decide where it was safe to stand.

The leaves would look well enough: pale, close-packed, their tips crossing in the usual fine weave. But when I put my palm down, I would feel a faint tapping beneath it. Not the quick scatter of fish. A slower movement, repeated from farther along the avenue, then nearer.

I would sit a while. The child would sit too, though she had been standing in a hurry.

When the resin worker arrived, she would crouch by the doorway and taste a trace from the frame. She might ask what the grandfather had carried through recently. The bowl of roots, certainly. A stool. His bedding, two days ago, because he had wanted to sleep nearer his sister while the rain lasted.

“And before that?” she would ask.

The child would go to fetch him. While she was away, the tapping would stop. I would part the leaves just enough to look down and see a browser’s folded ear in the water, pressed against the older roots.

There would be some difficulty getting it out. Not the browser from the doorway, but a young one, caught where a narrow drainage channel passes beneath the avenue. Its mother would be under a tree nearby, making a low sound I had taken for water in the stems. We would bring leafy branches and stand in the warm shallows beyond the crossing, calling softly while the grandfather, on his knees, loosened the mat ahead of the animal’s nose.

When it finally pushed through, its mother would move away without waiting for us to admire it. The young one would follow, dripping, and the two of them would disappear among the tree stems.

The resin worker would still have a doorway to attend to. The child and I would close the leaves over the channel and mark a wider crossing for the day, giving the roots time to settle. I would hang the *look here* knot from a short stem anyway, because the tapping had taught me there was a channel I had not known about, and I would want to find it again.

By evening someone might move the knot, or add another beside it. On my next walk I would ask who had done so, and what they had found.
