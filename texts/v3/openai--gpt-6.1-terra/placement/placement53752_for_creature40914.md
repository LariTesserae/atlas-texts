I am under the folded lip of a chamber in the outer district, on the side sheltered from the drop.

My oldest branches grip the wall above a seam. The younger ones hang outward, fine and hollow, and catch the grit that comes through the chamber. At first I would have said that I live on this grit. It enters where a branch has split, and the split closes afterward. But the branches also close when they are empty, and some of my best growth is on the wall behind them, where no grit reaches. I have had a long time to consider this without arriving at an answer.

There are seven plates in the seam below me. For a while there were eight. One left during a still interval, crossing the floor toward a doorway on the far side of the chamber. It did not come back that way. Much later, an eighth animal appeared at the seam, but its leading scale was chipped and I could not tell whether it was the same one.

I know them by the pressure of their passing and by the small disturbances they make among my branches. They climb into me to feed on the dark film sheltered there. Their weight bends the finest growth almost to the wall. Sometimes a branch breaks. The plate carries the broken piece away against its underside, and the remaining tip begins to close.

The wall has been changing for some time. The seam below me is narrowing at one end and widening at the other. The plates have moved their resting place toward the wide end, where a thin tongue of new stone has lifted from the chamber floor. The stone is pale. There is no film on it yet, and the animals cross it quickly.

One of them has left disks beneath the tongue. I can feel their faint weight through the wall when the ground is quiet. At first I took them for grains worked into the crack. Then their clear fastening spread against the stone, and the disks began to take its color.

There is a place in my older growth where two branches have fused. Powder gathers in the little cup they form. Lately, whenever the ground shifts, the powder leaves the cup and settles farther along the wall. It does not spill over the rim. It is simply gone from one place and present in the other.

I have sent a branch toward that place. It grows slowly, and the wall carries it a little farther each time the terrace rises. I do not know whether the powder’s path will hold when the branch reaches it. The plates enter seams like this one and sometimes come out at another doorway. My branch may reach an adjacent chamber, or it may press against stone and grow no farther.

The young have emerged. They graze close together beneath the tongue, too lightly for me to distinguish one from another. When an adult passes, they make room against the wall, then spread over the darkening seam again.

The powder has left my cup once more. This time a young plate was resting beneath it. The animal has gone too. The others remain under the tongue, moving over the stone. One adult comes up through my branches and pauses there, its weight bending them downward.

I hold as much of the wall as I can.
