I live on the third terrace below the ridge, in a chamber whose roof was laid by my mother and her sisters. One corner of the roof is lower than the others. The slab settled before its joints had sealed, and though they tried to lift it again, the new stone held it fast. Inside, we have to duck beneath that corner. Outside, it makes a good place to brace a basket while tying the handles.

I keep the path between our terrace and the warm basins. There are longer paths in the town, and steeper ones, but mine is used often: people bring stones up for sorting and carry food, water-warmed clay and news down. I know where a burden will catch against the wall and where someone tired will choose to stop.

The first path I kept was a child’s path, hardly longer than my arm span. I cleared it so carefully that nothing grew there. My aunt pointed out that the bare silt on either side was beginning to slip into it. She gave me three cuttings from the fence and showed me where to press their roots. I remember being annoyed. I had just cleared those places.

Now the fence beside my path is taller than I am. I trim it only where it leans too far across, and I leave the cuttings for anyone who needs them. The clear creatures gather there, their jointed legs folded around the stems. When I brush past, they flatten themselves and keep still.

One morning, after a cloudy spell, I found a pale swelling under the lower marker stone. It was no bigger than my palm. I knelt and felt it for a while, with my other hand on the rope so that people coming down could pass around me. Warm water moved through the crust. The stone above it had not yet shifted, but it would.

I took the marker out and set it a little farther from the ridge. Then I moved the rope. It looked wrong there, cutting close to the fence, and I thought I might have to move it again when the swelling grew.

By the next day, the new crust had split around the marker’s old hollow. A child from the terrace below came up carrying a basket of fish and stopped to look. I put her hand against the warm place and held it there with mine. She was impatient at first; the basket kept bumping her leg. Then she laid it down.

She asked how long it would take to become stone.

I told her it was stone already, but not the kind we could stand on.

She pressed harder and left a shallow mark. We watched cloudy water curl from the edge of it. I asked her not to do that again, and she tucked her hand under her arm.

For several days I checked the seam each time I passed. It spread beneath the path and raised a lip beside the fence. The people bringing loads down began turning sideways to get through. One of the old stone sorters rested her basket on the lip and said it was useful. Later, when she lifted the basket, a piece came away underneath it. She handed me the piece without saying anything.

We opened a new route together, a little lower down. I cut two fence stems, and the child carried them away to plant beside her family’s field. Others rolled the marker stones into place and pulled the rope taut. It took most of the morning. When we had finished, the path was wide enough for a basket, though a roof slab would have to come another way.

The old sorter went along it first, because she was already carrying a load. At the bend she stopped and shifted the basket from one hip to the other. Her foot settled firmly against a dark patch of older rock. I marked that place with a small stone, so that anyone coming after her could find it.

The abandoned part of the path is difficult to see now. Silt has gathered in it, and the fence is growing across. The pale lip is still there, thicker than it was, with tiny grazers making dark tracks over its surface. Sometimes I put my hand on it as I go past. It is cool at the outer edge, warm farther in.

At home, beneath the low corner of the roof, the child has begun a wall of loose stones. She has left a gap in it. When I asked her why, she said she wanted to see what would settle on the other side. Each morning she looks before anyone disturbs it. So far there are only a few leaf fragments, caught neatly between two stones.
