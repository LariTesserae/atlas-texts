I think I would first look for a room with a window, somewhere high enough to see the swifts turn over the chimneys. But the rooms with windows are already lived in, and after a few days in the overhead glare I might find myself lingering at the lift stops, where there is shade and a reason to wait.

So perhaps I would live lower down, in an old service bay beside a pump room. My door would open onto a passage rather than a view. There would be a shelf for cups, a bed raised clear of the floor, and a square of mesh over an opening whose original purpose nobody quite remembers. When the fans were running, the mesh would hum faintly. When they stopped, the passage would fill with the smaller sounds usually hidden beneath them: a drip, claws in the duct, someone calling a child home.

I would help tend the pumps. Not the great lift machinery, with its exposed height and its demands for strong hands, but the smaller machines that move water between terraces. They would still be heavy enough. I would learn to take a casing apart without losing the bolts, to feel grit in a valve, to distinguish a pipe that is merely warm from one that has been running dry.

At first I would keep too many notes. Every change in pitch, every damp patch, every time a gardener said the water had arrived late. I would write down the weather even when it had been the same for weeks. The older pump tender might leaf through my pages and point out that I had recorded a worrying rattle for twelve consecutive mornings without once checking the loose guard that caused it.

The notes would get better after that. A sketch of a fitting whose threads are no longer made. The date a washer was cut from an old sheet of rubber. A mark beside the pipe to the upper beds, where a fig root has begun to lift it. Some things would have to be measured over years; I would leave room for someone else to add their figures after mine.

The work would take me to people’s doors. A gardener would say her bed had always received less water since the pipe was moved. Her neighbor would say it had always been that way, and besides, the gardener’s trees had grown larger. I could test the flow and find a partial blockage, but clearing it would not settle everything they were arguing about. I would tell them what I had found, open the joint, and get wet to the elbows. They might bring me tea afterward, or they might continue the argument while I packed my tools.

I would come to know the valley in this interrupted way: a route made of problems, favors, and places where I had once been sent to fetch the correct spanner. There would be terraces I knew only by the pipes beneath them. Others I would know intimately—the cracked blue bowl under an outlet, the lizard missing its tail tip, the household that hung a yellow cloth outside when the cistern float stuck. I would still sometimes take the wrong stair and emerge into an orchard I had never seen.

Near my room, a little water would escape from a joint I had been meaning to replace. For a while I would put a dish underneath it. Moths would gather there overnight, and in the morning I would carry the water to a fern in the passage. Eventually the leak would worsen, and I would fix it. Then I might leave the dish and fill it deliberately, if there was enough water to spare.

At night I would sit by the open door with my notebook on my knee. The pump would start up on the other side of the wall, and I would listen until its familiar rhythm settled. Above it I could hear voices traveling along the passage, too softened by the masonry to make out the words. Now and then I would go upstairs for the last light and stand among the condensation screens, looking down at the district and waiting for the swifts to appear.
