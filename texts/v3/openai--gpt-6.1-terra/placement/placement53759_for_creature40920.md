I would be found most mornings at the third inspection hatch beneath the broad staircase, sitting on its rim while the kettle warms.

The chamber there is not mine alone. Three households keep cooking things in it, and someone stores bundles of felt on the upper shelves. The kettle belongs to a woman named Esen, who leaves a different cup beside it whenever she thinks a visitor may be coming. She has left one for me often enough that I wash it without asking.

I am a passage worker. That is what I call myself when someone asks, although the work has other names in other districts. I carry things through routes that have recently changed: cooking pots, blankets, tools, once a shallow tray containing six fish and enough water to cover their backs. A mapkeeper can tell you where a door is now. I help discover whether a person can get through it with the things they need.

My shoes have one felt sole and one ridged rubber sole. They began as a temporary pair, assembled from two worn-out pairs after a stair changed and I had to climb a wet service ramp every day. I have kept them that way because people hear me coming: a soft step, then a small hard scrape. From inside a pipe chamber or around a bend in a court, they know whom to call.

For years I believed I had grown up in the rooms above the loading bay nearest the hatch. I remembered the shape of the window and the warmth of a pipe against my bed. Esen remembered me there too. Then a wall was fitted across the bay, and a new staircase opened behind it. At the top were rooms with the same window, the same pipe, and a view I could not remember ever having seen: a long glass avenue with birds walking on its roof.

Esen said those had always been our rooms. She showed me a mark on the doorframe where my height had been measured. The highest line was beside a date from the year before, though I had been fully grown for some time. We stood looking at it until the kettle hissed below us. After that I slept in the new room and kept my shoes beside the bed, where I could find them in either dark or light.

Yesterday a hollow white block appeared at the north loading dock. I went to see it after breakfast. It was taller than I was, open at both ends, and still faintly warm along one edge. A worker had written its measurements on a card and tied the card to a railing. Beneath the block, where no one had thought to look until a child pointed them out, moths rested in a pale patch.

This morning the block was gone. The old passage from the cooking chamber to the upper rooms was gone too. In its place was a clean wall with a seam running down it. Esen stood before it holding her kettle, which she had brought up to fill at the reservoir. On the other side, as far as we could tell, were her cups, her blankets, and a pot she had left on the stove.

A stair had appeared farther along the court. It rose toward the right level, but each step was a little too high for Esen’s knees. She climbed the first four, waited with her hand on the rail, and came down again.

We left the kettle by the hatch and went looking. A mapkeeper joined us at the end of the court, pencil tucked behind one ear. She had already drawn the new stair. Together we tried a service ramp, which ended at a locked factory door; a bridge, which brought us back to the roof above the loading bay; and a narrow descent through two floors of beams. The descent smelled faintly of warm cable. We tapped each tray before lifting it, and the shell-creatures unfolded and hurried away.

At the bottom was a door I remembered using as a child. I could even picture the chipped enamel around its handle. The enamel was smooth when I touched it. The door opened into Esen’s room.

We fetched the blankets first. Esen said she did not intend to move out; she only wanted enough with her until someone found a way to make the new stair manageable. The mapkeeper marked the descent in pencil and wrote beside it: *steep, trays must be lifted, room for one person at a time.* I carried the kettle and the pot. Esen carried the cups wrapped in a sleeve of her coat.

By afternoon a grazer had come to lie beside the first step of the new stair. Its coat was thick with dust from the wall. Esen sat on the step above it while I measured the height of each tread with a strip torn from the mapkeeper’s spare paper. Tomorrow I will take the measurements to the workers who fit handrails and ask whether they can make a second, lower set of steps along one side.

For now the kettle is back on its shelf beside the third hatch. Esen has put out four cups: one for her, one for me, one for the mapkeeper, and one for whoever comes to ask where the passage went.
