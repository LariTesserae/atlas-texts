I think I would be in a seam between two concrete slabs, not far from the long building.

At first I would have said I was the moss there. It has a good place: a little shelter from the wind, water held after the exposed ground has frozen hard, and enough light to keep growing when the days permit. But I would be farther down, among the pale threads in the dark material the moss has caught.

I would be a fungus, or part of one. My threads would run through dead sedge and pine needles, around grains of peat, into the softened remains of things I could use. I would not fill the seam. There would be other fungi beside me and crossing me, and places where the concrete was too cold or dry for any of us.

The fans would reach me as vibration through the slabs. I would not know what made it. A change in the machinery might pass through the seam, but I would be more directly affected by a drop of water falling from the building and finding its way down between the slabs. In warmer hours it could carry dissolved material past my threads. By evening it might freeze, and there would be nothing to do with it.

I would grow where there was something to grow into. A sedge stem lodged in the crack would give me a new surface; a dry stretch of grit might stop me. Parts of me would die while other parts continued. I doubt I would have a useful sense of being one thing spread along the seam. Still, when spring loosened the snow and water moved again, I would remain there in whatever portions had survived, taking up the work of living among the moss.
