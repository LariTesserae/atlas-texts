I would live beneath the intake ledge, among the mussels.

At first I might be difficult to pick out: a pale crustacean no longer than a bolt head, holding to the underside of a shell. The water would pass over my back and through the narrow gaps between my legs. I would feed on what caught there—the fine material the mussels rejected, scraps lodged against their threads, a film scraped from iron.

The third pump would be the first thing I knew about the chamber beyond the ledge. Its start would reach me through the shell before the current changed. I would tighten my grip and wait for the loose material to come travelling along the floor. Sometimes a morsel would lodge close enough to reach. Sometimes the current would strip the feeding place clean.

As I grew, I would venture farther out. There is a seam in the floor where gravel collects, and a flattened bolt above it that offers a little shelter. From there I could see the fish downstream of the cage, silver where they crossed the lamplight. Their stillness would be hard to understand: so many bodies held apart in moving water, each making its own minute corrections. When a larger fish came through the duct, they would vanish between the bars, and I would press myself into the seam.

Once, after moulting, I would remain under the ledge until my new covering hardened. My old one might catch against a mussel and rock there in the flow, almost like another animal reaching for the same food. By the time I went out again, it would be gone.

The hatch would open rarely enough that I would have no preparation for it. A diver’s light would cross the floor, brighter and whiter than the lamps. The pumps would stop one after another. In the quieter water, debris would settle onto the mussels, onto my back, onto the tools laid beside the cage. A gloved hand would remove a corroded bracket and put another in its place. The diver would stir the floor each time they moved, and the cloud would take a long time to clear.

When the pumps began again, the flow beneath the ledge would be different. The new bracket would deflect a narrow stream toward the bolt; material that once settled in the seam would travel farther before falling. I would search the old place, find little, and follow the scraps downstream.

There, under the edge of the new metal, I would find a shallow space with a steady supply of food. It would be bare at first. In time a film would form over it, and something small would fasten near the corner. I would shelter beside it when the third pump started, then come out to pick through whatever the water had brought.
