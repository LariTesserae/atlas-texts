At first I would take myself for a wet mark on the inside of the black glass.

Rain is always gathering there, though it has not begun outside. The sound comes before the water: a fine pattering above me, growing quicker. I spread toward it, branching over the glass in lines so narrow that a grazer sheltering beside me might not notice. Then the first drops strike the basin and my edges thicken.

I am not the shelter. I can leave it, though I do not go far. When the glass dries I draw myself together into a little clear knot at its foot, with grains of black stone held in my folds. A dark flyer once tried to take me from there and found me too firmly attached. I carried the impression of its mouth for several rains.

From the shelter I know the basin mostly by arrivals. The hinged drinkers come in with their backs knocking; the grazers step carefully over them and stand with their feet folded. Flyers settle overhead. A few drops shaken loose from their bodies run down the glass, and I can taste where they have been: the sharp mineral at a new pool, the faint green of the moss, sometimes something colder from under the rising lip.

When the booming comes, I feel it through the glass. I tighten. The others do too, each in its own way. I have tried to remain spread through a boom, to see what would happen, but I always draw in before the sound reaches me.

One day a young grazer comes into the shelter without rain. It is dark-bodied and unsteady, with one foot curled around a pellet of soft mineral. It pulls the foot up and sets it down again, then calls. The call is astonishingly loud inside the glass. Nothing answers.

I reach toward it along a line of condensation. It lifts the foot away from me, so I wait. After a while it lowers the foot onto the wet glass, and the pellet begins to soften. I do not know whether it notices me moving around the rays. By the time the pellet falls free, I have spread farther than I meant to, and some of me remains on the foot when the grazer steps back.

It leaves a clear mark on the stone outside. Then another. I feel the first one for a little while, cooling in the open air, and then I do not.

The grazer goes toward the moss. I watch its dark body diminish among the pale stones until an adult comes striding past, calls once, and the young one turns to follow. Neither comes back for the part of me left behind.

That evening rain approaches again. I hear it gathering on the glass and open slowly toward the sound. The shelter is filling; a hinged creature has wedged itself beside my dry knot, and a flyer clings overhead. Outside, the grazer’s marks are no longer visible. Water finds its way down the glass, over me, and onto the floor, where the little crowd drinks.
