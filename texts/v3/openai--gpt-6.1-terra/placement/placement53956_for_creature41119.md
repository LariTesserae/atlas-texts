I would live beside the last cooling channel, where the town’s water leaves the stonework and spreads over the flats. My room would be above a pump chamber, with a narrow window facing the basin. At night I could feel the engine through the floor if I put my heel down. When it stopped for maintenance, I would wake in the unfamiliar stillness and wait until I remembered what day it was.

My work would be at the channel outlet. Each morning I would lift the screens and scrape away the mineral crust before it narrowed the flow. I would check the temperature where the water emerged and again where it reached the flats. If it was too hot, I would signal the pump house to slow the release, then open the side gates to send more of it through the cooling beds.

At first I imagine myself as someone who understands the machinery. I would know its ordinary rhythms, and the places where a hand on the wall could tell me more than a gauge. But the person who trained me would know those things better. She would come down from the pump house sometimes, stand beside me without speaking, and ask what I thought a change in the vibration meant. Often I would guess wrong. She would leave me to listen a little longer.

What I would know best, after some years, is the ground beyond the outlet. The warm water would make a dependable place for the films to grow, and the grazers would gather there. One old animal with a notch in its edge would settle almost directly beneath the flow. We would call it Notch at our end of the channel, though the people upstream would have another name for it.

When I needed to clean that part of the stone, I would pour a little water ahead of it. Sometimes it would move. Sometimes I would have to wait, standing with the scraper in my hand, while it held itself against the ground. The pump-house workers could see me from their roof. If I stayed there long enough, one of them might sign a suggestion across the yard. They would not always be helpful suggestions.

One afternoon I would find Notch a little farther out than usual, with fresh feeding tracks behind it and one side lifted from the stone. A hunter stood nearby. It had been using the crossing for months, and I had grown used to judging the hour by its position, but I had never seen it so close to one of the broad grazers.

I would approach slowly, carrying a bucket. The hunter might move off as I came down the channel; if it did not, I would give it room. I could pour water along the grazer’s lifted edge and wait to see whether it settled again. If it was badly hurt, I might fetch someone who knew more than I did. Most likely we would end up crouching beside it, unable to do much, watching the water pass underneath.

I would come to know other people through such small interruptions to the work: the person who mended my shoes and could tell where I walked by the wear on the soles; a child who crossed the yard each day with a covered vessel, practicing signs on me through the glare; the cook who sent me back to a different well whenever I brought water that made the food too sharp.

In the evenings, after cleaning the outlet, I would sit on the raised threshold outside my room and scrape the residue from beneath my nails. From there I could see a strip of the flats between two buildings. The hunters would begin to move, and the grazers would leave slow tracks through the water. If someone on a roof across the yard lifted a hand, I would lift mine in return. Then, when it grew too dark to see the signs, I would go downstairs and sit by the channel with whoever was still awake.
