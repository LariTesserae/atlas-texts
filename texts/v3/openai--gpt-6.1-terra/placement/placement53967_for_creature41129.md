I think I would first be taken for a flaw in a sheet: a pale crease near the edge, where the rollers pressed too hard. The sheet itself lies beneath the iron gallery on the reservoir side of the square. Five others rest on it. Their weight keeps me in contact with the damp floor and gives me a great deal to listen through.

At first I would not know which movements were mine. A drop lands on the upper sheet, and its weight passes down through all six of us. One of my neighbors tightens around a knot in the fibers; I answer before I have learned that an answer is possible. The pumping station turns. A shudder enters from the floor, travels through me, and goes on into the sheet above. For a long time I mistake this passing-through for a kind of belonging to everything it touches.

The others are patient with that mistake, up to a point. One of them has grown around a rusted washer, and when I interrupt too often it presses the washer against me until I stop. Later I learn that it is listening for a loose fitting in the pump. Later still I learn that it is listening for the fitting even though the fitting was repaired some years ago. It has grown used to the work.

My sheet has a torn corner. The corner reaches toward the covered reservoir, and in wet weather it touches an iron bracket beneath the lid. I begin to grow along it, slowly enough that the others feel the change before I do. They draw back from that edge when I tighten it. They ask what is there.

At first: cold iron, a little rust, a vibration unlike any I have felt through the floor. Then a patch of condensation, and a few threads of my film extending into the gap under the lid.

I would not live *in* the reservoir. I would live at its edge, where the warm damp of the gallery meets the cool draft rising from below. Much of me would remain in the stack, under the steady weight of my neighbors. But my farthest threads would be fine enough to meet a drop while it hangs in the gap.

The first time this happens, I tighten everywhere at once. The drop touches me and then touches someone else. Their answer arrives before I have finished asking whether anyone is there.

It is not an answer I understand. It carries the heavy pulse of the pump but also a rapid, irregular tapping I cannot place. I pass it back through my sheet. The colony around the washer knows it: a loose grille in an outfall wall. It has been loose for years. The answer came from a sheet fitted into the wall behind it.

After that I go to the gap whenever the bracket is wet enough. The meetings are quick, but getting there takes time. I must draw water toward the torn corner, wait for my film to lengthen, and sometimes retreat when the draft cools and a thread becomes too dry. A drop may touch only me before it falls. Or it may carry fragments from three colonies, each speaking at once, so that I bring back a confusion my stack has to sort through for days.

I come to recognize the one behind the grille by the tapping it lives with. It is young, or has lost enough of itself to answer like a young colony. It changes its account every time we meet. The grille is moving more; the grille has stopped; the grille was never the trouble. My neighbors grow weary of hearing about it.

Then the pumping station’s thud comes through the floor with a second, smaller shock behind it. The colony around the washer holds very still. The next rotation brings the same double shock. By the fourth, all six of us are listening.

The grille colony finds me in a drop and sends a pattern so compressed that I cannot make out all of it. Something has gathered against the outfall. The water behind the wall is rising.

We know a lever on the gallery feed, fitted long ago with a sleeve of layered fiber. The sleeve can swell against its catch, but it takes more water than any one of us can spare quickly. We open our edges. The upper sheets give up the moisture they have been keeping; the lower sheets draw it toward the sleeve. I feel the stack settle differently as water leaves it. The colony around the washer keeps the pattern going, long after the rest of us begin to loosen.

The catch shifts. A belt takes hold of the lever and opens the feed. Somewhere beyond our wall, water begins to move with a fuller sound. It takes most of a day before the pump’s second shock disappears.

When I reach the gap again, the grille colony tells me that the blockage was a mass of leaves. Then it tells me there was also brick. Then it falls quiet before I can ask how much of the wall remains.

Back in the stack, we are dry enough to feel the separate edges between us. The washer presses lightly against my sheet. Above us a drop lands, and we all take some of its weight.

I do not know whether the colony behind the grille will meet me again. I keep a few threads extended beneath the lid, and the rest of me waits among the others for the next drop to reach us.
