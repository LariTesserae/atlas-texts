I would be in the stone beneath a stalk forest, where the beetles have pressed their pellets into threads of crystal. Not in all the threads: most carry a charge whether I am there or not. I would inhabit a small junction, a place where three warm cracks meet and the crystals have grown close enough to touch.

At first I might be hard to distinguish from the chamber itself. I would feel the bladders filling overhead as changes in pressure, and the beetles as little interruptions of warmth. When an egg chamber trembled, I would receive a scatter of faint signals through the crystals. I could not see the eggs. I might learn how many there were by the pattern they made.

The beetles would keep altering my shape. They would add a pellet to one thread, break another while widening a passage, carry away a piece that had cooled too far from the fissure. Each change would alter what reached me. A new connection might bring the slow pumping of a stalk I had never felt before. A broken one might leave a familiar chamber suddenly quiet. I would wait for it to return, though waiting would not help the beetles mend it.

When a pulse came through, I could hold a little of its charge and release it along another thread. This would be a small thing beside the light traveling across the plateau. It might make a nearby bladder close a moment later than its neighbors, or send a tremor through a chamber of eggs. I would learn which branches carried a signal cleanly and which lost it in the damp stone. I would also get things wrong: send a charge toward a chamber that had emptied, or mistake the weight of a passing grazer for pressure building below.

Sometimes the beetles would pack dust around a thread until it connected me to a distant junction. Then, for a while, I would have news from beyond the forest: a long-legged herd standing at a pool, perhaps, known to me only by the repeated pressure of feet; or a ridge warming in a pattern I had not encountered before. The connection might last for a season or break before the next pulse. I would never be sure that a quiet place had vanished. It might simply have moved out of reach.

Once, during an especially strong pulse, the threads might carry the same tremor to me by two routes, one arriving long after the other. Between them I would feel beetles moving through a chamber whose entrance had collapsed days earlier. I could not tell whether the passage had reopened or whether a path through the stone had briefly grown longer than the time it took to cross. I would send a small charge down it. Something on the other side would answer with the ordinary, busy tapping of legs.

If an escarpment began to rise beneath us, I would feel the lower threads strain first. The beetles would move their eggs upward. I could keep a few passages trembling as they worked, and perhaps the adults would follow those vibrations toward chambers still warm and open. Most would find their way without me.

Afterward, on the exposed face of the new rock, the colony would begin again. Beetles would enter the bright seams and press fresh pellets against the crystal. I would become aware of the upper air only through what it did to the stone: cooling at the surface, warmth below, and now and then the delicate pressure of a seed taking hold in a crack.
