I think I would live near the basin, though not in its center. There is a stretch along the eastern edge where the passages narrow and the sound arrives strangely: a wedge struck nearby can seem to be tapped behind your shoulder. The carriers dislike bringing long slings through there. They have to turn each corner in stages, setting the load down while someone shifts a loose tool or takes a peg out of the way.

At first I thought I would be one of the listeners, sitting with the older workers where the walls overlap. But I don’t think I could sit still long enough. I would listen for a while, then get up to see what someone was doing with a lever, or notice a pan beginning to tip. I might be useful to the listeners all the same: the one they send to look, who comes back muddy-handed and tries to describe what the seam is doing.

I would be small and broad-backed like the others, with one foot wrapping thicker than the other where I had mended it too many times. I would carry a few pegs tucked through my sling belt, and a curved shell for lifting film from a joint. One of the pegs would be scored all around its middle, not as a signal but because I worry it with my thumbnail when I’m thinking.

The house I sleep in would be just high enough inside to sit upright. Four of us fit on the shelves if we tuck our feet in after the door is closed. The person nearest the threshold wakes first when water enters; on dry nights that is usually me, because I like the cooler place. Above my shelf hangs a loop of cord with three little shell pieces on it. They are not useful for anything. A child in the house gave them to me after discovering that, held at the right angle, each catches a separate patch of light.

In the morning, if there has been rain, I would go out with a pan under one arm and a bundle of wedges under the other. There are joints I know by the feel of their edges: one that sheds grit when stepped across, another with a tiny shelf where film gathers even after the surrounding stone is dry. I would check the pegs, add a scratch to one if the gap had widened, and take water to the places that had been marked.

I would not always be sure what the marks meant. A peg can turn in a hard shower, and sometimes two crews use the same arrangement for different things. Once I might pour water into a seam that had been left dry so its wedge could be drawn out. The crew arriving afterward would find it swollen tight. They would have to work it loose with film and a thinner lever, and I would stay to help, holding pressure where they told me. I imagine remembering that for a long time afterward, especially when reaching for a pan.

There is a pale animal that comes through that lane often enough for me to recognize it. Its left ear-fold is torn, and when it walks over the close-fitted blocks it leaves faint damp impressions, ridged like the underside of a leaf no one here has ever seen. I turn a pan over for it sometimes. Sometimes it waits; sometimes it goes past me without looking. I would like to believe it knows me, but what I know is that it comes to the same sheltered joints I do.

One afternoon, while I am watching it drink from the underside of the pan, a block falls somewhere beyond the raised courses. I hear the first report, then the second. Before the third comes, the animal lifts its head.

The third sounds close, though the fall was far away. In the lane, two carriers stop with their sling between them. An older listener, sheltered under an overhang, points toward the seam beside my foot.

I crouch there. The joint is narrow, no wider than a shell edge, and for a while nothing happens. Then the film lying across it draws into little folds. The two blocks have come slightly closer together. When I put my cheek to the nearer one, I can feel a faint, irregular rubbing.

The listener comes over and feels it too. We fetch the carriers, and one of them taps for a lever. The animal has gone by then; the upturned pan is still rocking gently on its rim.

We work until the light is hard to distinguish from the wet shine on the blocks. The wedge we pull out is split through. There is another beneath it, deeper than we can reach, so we brace the seam, mark it with two pegs, and leave a pan where the next crew will see it. The rubbing has stopped, but the listener asks me to come back after the next rain.

I would go home with grit stuck to my face and a sore place across my palm. Someone would pass me a cup of paste. Someone else would ask whether we had found the trouble, and I would say we had found part of it. Then I would hang my belt above the shelf, check that the shell pieces were still there, and lie down with my feet toward the door.
