I am under the larger one’s back, where the plates overlap above the narrow space at the rear of the shelter.

At first I take the regular pressure there for part of the ground. It comes through me, eases, and comes again: not the great chord itself, but a change in the chord, like a shallow place in a current. I tighten to meet it. Much later, when the larger one lifts slightly, I feel the pressure lift with it. I have been bracing against the work of its body.

The larger one’s plates are not all the same age. One near my fringe has an edge worn pale and smooth. Another still has the fine layered surface it had beneath the fronds. Between them is a gap through which the sharper note of a needle sometimes reaches me. When it does, my fringe closes before the rest of me has time to move.

There is a plate beside me. The larger one has brought it from the sorted heap and left it within reach. It is small enough to fit across my shoulders, though I cannot yet hold it there and fasten it at the same time. I work one limb beneath its edge. The plate shifts. For a long while afterward I feel the new pressure it makes against the stone.

From here I know the shelter before I know its shape. One slab carries a dry, rapid trembling; another gives a broad pressure that settles my bands into a useful rhythm. At the back is the deeper vibration that the larger one has left a space to admit. I grow toward that space. My outer limbs unfold against the floor, and the larger one gradually makes room.

The first time I press a plate to the ground, I do it by accident. It slips from my limb and its edge catches in a crack. A thin pressure runs outward through the stone. It returns altered, having passed the slab, the wedge beneath it, and the sorted heap outside.

I do it again. This time I hold the plate there.

The return is not quite the same. The larger one has shifted a limb, and the pressure travels through that too. I begin to notice how much of the shelter is present in what comes back: the loose fiber against the roof, the hammer beside its polished notch, even the uneven weight of my own body as I lean on the plate.

I cannot sense the other shelter at first. I know only that, occasionally, a pressure persists beneath the floor longer than any I have made. It is faint and steady. The larger one rests one limb against the ground while it lasts.

When I am far enough from under its back to see the doorway, I find the loose roof fiber hanging outside. Its lower end is closer to the ground than when the larger one last adjusted it, though not by much. Beyond it, the edge of the basin shines between the fronds. I can feel the fringe of young growth there as a different weight upon the stone.

The floor changes slowly. A new pressure gathers beneath one corner, broad at first and then sharper. The slab above it begins to carry a troublesome note. My plates are still few, and I feel them knock against one another whenever the note rises.

The larger one reaches for the hammer. Before it closes its limb around the handle, I try my small plate against the floor. I hold its edge down beside the growing pressure. The return comes back unevenly, and I move it a little farther along. At one place the pressure beneath it is stronger than anywhere else. I keep it there.

The larger one touches the stone beside my plate. Then it draws the hammer nearer.

I do not know whether I have shown it anything it had not already felt. When the wedge is moved, though, the sharp note in the slab diminishes, and my plates lie quietly again.

There is room now for me beside the larger body. I have a plate across my shoulders and another fitted low, where it scrapes the floor when I turn. The sorted heap is outside the doorway, beyond the hanging fiber. I have begun to reach for the smallest plate in it. From here the edge of the heap gives a clear return; I can feel which plate I am approaching, and the narrow space that remains between it and my limb.

Beyond the basin there are other pressures I cannot yet distinguish. One day I may go out far enough to learn them. For now I keep my limb against the floor, feeling the shelter change around us, and wait for the larger one’s hammer to touch the stone.
