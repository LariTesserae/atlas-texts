At first I might seem to be part of the grazer’s coat: a pale, jointed thing no longer than one of the looser fibers, holding fast where the fringe joins its flank. Dust settles on me. Sometimes a fragment of lichen catches across my back and stays there until I groom it away.

The animal has been lying still for a long time. Beneath us the gravel has taken on a little warmth, enough that a bead of water forms on the underside of a stone. I leave the coat and go down to it, feeling my way between the hairs. There are others at the bead already. We drink at its edge, and when it has grown too small we begin to comb through the damp fibers around it.

The grazer’s breathing moves the whole place where we live. Its flank rises against my feet and settles again. Farther under its body, a young one is feeding on the soft growth at the roots of the fringe. I can reach the young one’s coat, but I do not stay there long; it moves suddenly, and its hairs are still too short to hold much water.

For many days the adult feeds without rising. Its head reaches out from the mound of its body, and the faint grinding travels back through the ground. I become familiar with that motion, with the pace of the jaw and the long pauses between mouthfuls. At night the outer hairs stiffen. Condensation gathers on them when the light returns.

Then the grazer gets up.

The fibers pull taut around me. I climb inward as the coat shakes and gravel slides away beneath us. Through a gap I see a row of feet lifting, setting down, lifting again: the young one walking in its mother’s shadow. Other animals are rising across the slope. The one carrying me follows them, and the stone I drank from disappears behind us.

Travel is difficult. Some of the small creatures drop off when the animal brushes a boulder; others work their way deeper into the coat. I hold near the skin, where I can still find moisture, and venture outward when the grazer stops to feed. Once I reach the fringe of another adult while the two stand pressed flank to flank. Its coat smells different, its skin is warmer, and there are many more of us already living there. When the animals separate, I stay.

Later, in a narrow passage, the herd slows. Our grazer turns so sharply that I lose my grip and fall. I land in gravel among several others. The animal’s padded foot comes down close beside us, then lifts and moves away. I hear the brushing of coats continue beyond the rocks. Something low and long lies motionless at the side of the passage; the grazers have gone around it.

I shelter under a stone. Its underside is damp, and there are smaller creatures moving over a dark film in the crack beneath it. I follow them far enough to drink. The stone holds the day’s slight warmth long after the air has cooled, though it is not as warm as a grazer.

The next animals pass slowly overhead. Their feet darken the openings between the stones. I wait until one settles nearby, then cross the gravel and climb into its fringe. Here too there is water caught among the fibers, loose skin to comb, and the steady movement of a living body beneath me.

By the time it rises, I am holding on.
