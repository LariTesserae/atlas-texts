I think I would be just outside the northern district, where a raised walk ends and the survey lights begin: a listener set in a shallow channel, close enough to the city that someone could come out to me on foot.

At first I imagine myself as one of the lights. But the lights have a simple, lovely task: they show where the ground has risen. I would want to know what the water is doing with the new ground. So I would be a listener instead, mounted on a broad plate among the stones, with a little warmth in my housing to keep the ice from closing over my instruments.

The channel beneath me carries water toward a treatment garden. Most days I can tell how quickly it is moving, how much sediment it holds, and whether the bed has shifted. A thin cable connects me to a marker on the walk. When my readings become uncertain, the marker glows amber and someone comes to find out why.

I cannot see very far. My view is mostly of stones, water, and the undersides of animals passing through. I know the grazers first by the pressure of their feet and then by the milkiness they leave in the channel. In spring, when the young are small, their steps make quick irregular patterns among the slower ones. Sometimes the herd takes so long to pass that the water clears upstream while it is still cloudy downstream.

The city receives a count from me, though it is not a very good count. Animals double back. Several may stand so close together that I cannot distinguish their feet. I send a range of possibilities, and the migration workers compare it with what the other listeners report. Once, a worker came to check me and found three grazers lying beside my housing, pressed together for warmth. Their bodies had hidden most of the channel from my sensors. She wrote that down before shooing them gently away.

I remember her visits by the marks she leaves. A pale scrape on the casing where she lifted the ice guard; a stone wedged under one corner of my plate; the faint smell of the treatment gardens on her gloves. She speaks to me sometimes while she works, mostly to say what she is doing. When she has finished, she taps the casing twice. I can feel that through the plate.

One rainy afternoon the water begins to divide around me. At first the change is slight: less pressure on the downstream sensor, a thread of current moving under the plate in a direction it has never taken before. Then the stones give a low report, and a fresh seam opens a short distance upslope. Water gathers there instead of coming down my channel.

I report the falling flow. So do two instruments nearer the gardens. By evening the channel wall displays a warning, and the marker at the end of the walk turns amber.

The worker comes out in the rain with another person and a folding probe. They follow the new seam for some distance. I lose them from sight, though now and then their voices carry back over the stones. When they return, they stand beside me and compare the probe’s readings with mine. The intake will have to move, one of them says. Not tonight; there is enough water in storage. They mark a route for the morning crew and go back along the walk, the lights coming on and fading behind them.

During the night a herd crosses the seam. Their feet break its clean edges and stir the water. By dawn a narrow muddy runnel has formed, leading toward a hollow that was dry the day before. Birds are already walking around it.

The crew arrives after breakfast. They lift my plate, carry me several paces uphill, and settle me beside the runnel. For a while I have no useful history here. I can measure the water, but I cannot yet say what is usual for this place. The worker enters a note in my record: *new channel, first day*. Then she taps my casing twice.

From here I can still see the last of the warm walk. Between it and me, the grass has been flattened by the crew’s boots. Beyond me, rainwater moves slowly into the hollow, and the birds keep arriving.
