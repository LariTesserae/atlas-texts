At first I would be difficult to distinguish from the wetness on the underside of the fern: a darkening of the fine seam where its stalk leaves the rock, a place that holds a bead a little longer than the surfaces around it.

But the bead falls, and I remain.

I am the fern, or enough of it to speak from here. My fronds lie flat beneath an overhanging slab on the western wall. One was snapped in the fall; its brown tip is caught among the fresh blocks below. The others still open and take what light reaches them. When the wind comes from the lower lip, it gets under their edges and lifts them, though the slab keeps the worst of it away.

My roots occupy very little ground. They have found a narrow seam, packed with old leaf fragments and grit, and follow it inward. There the pale strands pass among them. Some touch the roots closely; others continue past, into the moss or deeper into the stone. I cannot tell where they end. Since the fall there are fewer than there were, and the cushion below me has loosened enough that dry grains run through it.

Once, the willow sheltered me from winds coming over the ridge. One of its branches was buried, and now, when a gust hits, another branch reaches farther across the ledge. It scrapes my outer frond against the rock. The frond has a torn place that grows no smaller, but the branch also catches blown leaves. They gather in the angle behind its stem, where rain can press them down.

Water comes to me along several routes. Some strikes the slab and finds the seam. Some hangs from my fronds until the weight of it pulls free. The moss takes those drops; what seeps farther may eventually reach my roots again. Much is lost down fractures I have never entered.

Below, the grinding rises through the wall. Grit shifts in the seam. So it did when the rock fell, though the fall was sudden, and afterward there was more air around my roots. I cannot know from the sound whether another stone will come down. I can only hold in the seam that remains.

A new frond is beginning under the old ones, still tightly curled. If the willow branch brushes it when it opens, it may tear. If leaves accumulate around my roots, they may cover the exposed place before it dries. Neither has happened yet.

Rain arrives sideways. One drop catches on the broken edge of a frond, travels to its tip, and falls among the pale strands beneath me.
