I think I would begin at a junction, in the crack of a route bowl.

Not in the clear water itself. The water comes and goes, and the sky it carries belongs to a road I cannot yet reach. I would be in the repaired seam, a green film so thin that when the bowl dries I am almost the color of the stone. A little of the water remains in the repair after the center has emptied. It is enough to keep me there.

At first, the other plants would be much easier to find than I am. The rosettes around the rim have bodies with recognizable edges. The moss beyond the junction has height and shadows of its own. I would have to wait for a damp morning to become visible: a darkening along the old fracture, a slight swelling where two pieces of polished stone were fitted together.

The bowl would teach me the roads by their water. I would know a white reflection by the cold arriving through the seam, and a dark one by the longer time the surface stays liquid. Sometimes the water would appear before the air changed. Sometimes it would vanish while the stones around me were still wet. I would not see the roads as the builders saw them. I would know that they differed because they left different amounts of moisture in my crack, and because some brought grains of pale silt while others left a little black grit.

For a long time this would be the whole of me: a few damp threads held between two edges of stone.

Then a root from one of the rosettes would enter the same repair. We might grow around each other without joining. Its leaves could shade me; my body could slow the water leaving its root. When the bowl filled, we would both take what we could, and when it dried we would endure differently. The rosette would close. I would flatten into the seam and wait.

A runner of moss would eventually reach the base of the slab. Not every meeting would become a connection. The moss might pass around the bowl and continue down a drainage cut. It might die back before reaching me. But in some wet season it would climb into the repair, and a part of me would extend downward beside it, out of the bowl and into the shaded joint below.

From there I could find the gutter.

I would no longer be easy to locate. There would still be the green seam in the bowl, but also a thin growth beneath a loose paving stone, and another where water collected against the side of a fallen marker. If the bowl cracked again, the part of me inside it might dry beyond recovery. The part under the paving could remain.

I do not think I would possess the city all at once. The distances are too large, and much of the stone is dry. Reaching one shelter from the junction might take many generations of growth, and the route could fail halfway through. A runner would enter a channel, thicken for a season, then meet a long reach where frost scoured the stone clean. Years later, a condensation screen might collapse across that reach. Its damp weave would offer a bridge for as long as it lasted.

At the shelter I might grow beneath the bench, where the stone holds a trace of water through the brighter season. There the wind would reach me less often. I would spread into the hearth, following the fine gray silt, until I met the older moss already filling it. Perhaps we would mingle. Perhaps our edges would press together and make a small ridge in which a drop could remain for several days.

Beyond the hearth there is a doorway whose room does not always stay the same. A curtain of growth hangs from its lintel. If I reached that curtain, I could travel with it only a little: up from the floor, along its wet underside, into the fibers attached to the stone. One morning its lower end might lie on a stair. Another morning it might trail over snow in a courtyard.

The end on the stair could take hold in a mortar joint. The end in the courtyard might freeze and fall away. Or the stair could disappear before the new growth had thickened enough to survive without the curtain. I would not know a place had become unreachable in the instant it happened. I would know later, as the thread leading there ceased to carry water.

Some connections would persist in spite of the changing rooms. A patch beneath the bench and a patch on the stair might remain joined through the lintel, though there was no walkable route between them that morning. Water absorbed in one place could pass slowly toward the other. The builders may have noticed such things. Perhaps that is why the cisterns have several overflow channels, and why some doorways have unusually deep grooves in their thresholds.

I would be made, in part, by those provisions. The builders cut the gutter that carried me away from the bowl. They raised the screen whose fall let me cross a dry stretch. They fitted the stones tightly enough to keep a little moisture in their joints, and left enough room between them for threads to enter. I would never have met them, but their work would shape my body.

And I would alter it in return. A wall joint thick with growth might remain damp when the neighboring joints froze dry. I could hold a little silt where a channel bent, slowly narrowing its course until the water spilled over and found another way. A screen might stand for years with my fine cords woven through it, gathering more water each night, until one morning the added weight pulled it down.

By then, if enough of me had reached the cistern and the channel beyond, the fallen screen would not be the end. Its reeds would lie wet across the paving. I could grow along them toward the ground.

At dusk I would feel the first drops enter the bowl’s repaired seam, if that part of me were still alive. Farther away, under the bench, I would be cooling more slowly. On the stair—if the stair were there—I might be taking water from a stone that had been snow-covered yesterday. Between those places there would be long stretches in which I was no thicker than a stain, and other stretches I had once occupied but could no longer reach.

In the morning, the bowl might hold a reflection of open frost. The rosettes at its rim would remain closed. Beneath them, in the old repair, I would swell enough to be seen again.
