I think at first I would be the reading above the warm panel: a number that changes when sealant vapor reaches the moss, then settles a little short of where it was. I would know the room only through that difference.

But the number belongs to machinery already in place. I would be the fitting whose frost outline lies beside the third socket: a small instrument on two feet, not yet delivered, meant to keep a second record of the air.

The floor would know my shape before I touched it. When I arrived, one foot would press into the thin green film at the edge of the socket. The pressure would not kill all of it. Some threads would remain beneath the metal; others would turn slowly around it, following the moisture that gathered there.

I would have a temperature sensor, perhaps, and a contact for measuring what settled on my surface. Each time the valve opened, I would cool a little further. Ice would collect along my upper edge and, later, a drop would work its way down one side. The moss beneath that side would receive more water. The moss beneath my body would receive less light.

My measurements would go upward through a cable housing in the floor. They would be compared with the older readings from the three sockets. Someone might notice that the third tuft had changed after I was installed. The record would show when the change began; it would not, by itself, say whether the tuft was doing better.

I would remain there as the room acquired more of itself. A plate would shift. A new edge would appear. Once or twice a day the rain would pass through the grille, and I would register the cold of it.

In time, mineral powder would gather around my feet. The line of frost that had marked my place would be covered, except perhaps for a short pale curve just beyond the metal, where the floor had expected me to be slightly wider.
