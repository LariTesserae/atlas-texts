I would begin beneath a broken pot, stuck to its underside among a cluster of eggs. The water there would be still enough for the dust to settle on us. Above the shard, a tile would move sometimes, and the movement would come down through the water before the clicking did.

When I hatched, I would eat the film from the clay close to my egg. I would know the curve of that chamber by touch: the rough broken edge, the smooth inside of the pot, the place where another shard leaned against it and left a narrow opening. The others would pass through that opening long before I could follow. I would wait until the water carried food toward me again.

Later, with wider feet, I would fit into a seam that rose toward the wall. The first time I reached the pavement, I might stay there only a moment. It would be so open. Even the roof, close enough to rattle over the basin, would seem far away from the edge of a tile. I would keep one side of my body against the clay blocks and go with the smaller creatures, under the leaning shoots and through the tracks of water.

I would carry a white crumb under my chin. It would be awkward at first; I would keep trying to taste it. At a crack I would turn sideways, brace my feet, and press the crumb into the gap. Another creature might come behind me with a red one and pack it against mine. We would leave a little pale-and-red mark in the pavement, smooth enough for feet to pass over.

I would pass it again on the next circuit, if it held.

There would come a wet season when I was too broad for some of the seams I had used. I imagine reaching one, feeling the familiar cool air from within, and pushing my head in as far as it would go. My feet would work against the tile, but the rest of me would not follow. I would have to back out and find another way under.

By then I would be large enough to travel with the other stream. The little ones coming toward me would be difficult to avoid where the wall narrowed the path. One might catch against my foot and ride there for a step, its own feet spread wide. When it touched the pavement again, it would go on in its direction and I in mine.

I would learn the underside of the floor more thoroughly as an adult: the hollow tiles that answered a footfall, the ones weighted down by brick, the pottery chambers where a clutch could be fastened above the still water. I would graze where the light did not reach. Sometimes I would emerge carrying a black fragment from the ash layer and work it into the same crack where, long ago, I had left the white one. There might be no trace of that first repair. The tile could have shifted; the crack might even have moved.

When the basin drained, I would join the crowd in one of the bowls. From inside it, I could feel the weight gathering on one side. The bowl would begin to rock. I might climb higher, finding purchase on its damp slope, and then be spilled with the others. For a moment I would lie among feet and pale bodies in a sheet of water, waiting for something firm to push against.

Afterward I would find a loose tile and go beneath it. The roof would still be dripping, though more slowly. I would hear a few feet overhead, then a long pause, then a click from somewhere along the wall. I might answer it, and settle into the cool space beside a broken shell, with my feet resting on the clay.
