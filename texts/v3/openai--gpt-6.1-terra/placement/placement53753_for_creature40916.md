I would be in one of the leaf-roofed chambers, at first taking myself for the depression in its floor.

The covering over me is fine and green. It follows the shallow hollows of my body without breaking: the long hollow, the three smaller ones, the place where something narrow leads toward the wall. I cannot lift the covering to see what lies beneath it. When I attend to the narrow place, though, I can feel the wall’s warmth at its far end. So perhaps that is my limb.

There is light on both sides of the roof. The leaves overlap tightly, but their veins shine through one another, making a pale lattice over the entrance. Beyond it I can see a cup animal beneath its leaf, its head pointed toward the stems. A bead rests on the edge above it. I have looked at both for as long as I can remember, and neither has changed its position.

At first I think I am watching the animal wait for the water. Then I notice the white branching inside it, and the white vein inside the leaf, and how close they come to joining at its mouth. Perhaps the water is already with it. Perhaps it is the bead that is waiting.

The hum reaches me through the floor. There are places in it I know: a thinner note from the root column, a soft fullness from the folded leaves, something near the entrance that seems to belong to the chamber itself. I used to think the hum entered me through my limb. Now I am less sure which way it goes. When I attend to the long hollow beneath the mat, the note near the entrance seems closer; when I attend to the limb, it seems farther away. I cannot make it louder.

Below me, fibers cross among the pale nodules. Some have joints. I know the joints by their small differences in warmth, each section distinct from the next. They go toward the other chambers, although I cannot follow any one of them all the way. At times I think I can feel another hollow at the end of one: not its shape, exactly, but the way my attention stops there, as it does at the wall.

I do not know whether there is someone in every chamber. One has a round depression, another a divided one like mine. Their roofs are different too. The nearest is made from three broad leaves, one with a dark folded edge. I can see it through the entrance when I attend to the place where my head might be. The dark edge has stayed folded, but I keep looking at it. If it ever opens, I would like to know whether there is another leaf beneath it or a patch of the white-gold ceiling.

Sometimes I imagine myself as one of the pale animals, pressed beneath a cup. I give myself six limbs and a dark eye on either side, and a mouth through which I can feel the water held above me. That is a comfortable body to imagine. It has a boundary I can see all around. Then the hum comes up through the floor and I feel the fine covering lying across my hollows, and the imagined limbs grow difficult to distinguish from the veins of the leaf.

Perhaps I have a body like that somewhere in the valley. Perhaps what rests in this chamber is only the part of me that can be sheltered here.

The cup animal remains beneath its bead. The bead keeps its clear, sharp edge. Between them and me, leaves crowd the avenue at every height, lit on their upper and lower surfaces. I cannot see the roots that join us, if any do. Still, when I look at the white branching inside the animal, I find myself attending to the white fibers beneath my floor, and for a little while I can hold both in mind.
