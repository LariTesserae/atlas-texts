I would be near the margin, but not quite at it: beneath an arch of young fronds where a passage turns toward the open ground.

At first I think I would be one of the small lives in a frond’s joint, with the whole world moving around me so slowly that its movement becomes apparent only in the changing pressure of wax. A good place to be during a veil. But I would want to know what is on the other side of the bend, and then what lies beyond the next one.

So I am a grazer, perhaps not fully grown. My plates are pale and close-fitting, though a little powder has collected along their edges. My six feet know the passage floor: the polished grains, the soft places where old fronds have collapsed, the hollow worn by bodies pausing before they go out. The roof touches me in one place. It did not touch me there before, and in a few days it may not touch me again.

There is food under an old crown farther in. Its upper surface is bleached and brittle, but underneath, where it rests against the floor, the tissue has softened into amber streaks. I work my snout into the edge and comb what comes loose. Mineral grains spill out in a little fan. One of my feet breaks through the surface, and when I pull it free it carries a smear of the living film.

I follow the passage outward with that smear still in the creases of my foot. There are younger grazers in a hollow off the main route. One is working at a fallen rib much too large for it, bracing the rib with its front legs and worrying the end. I pass close enough to brush it. It draws its legs in, then sets them out again after I have gone by.

At the mouth of the passage I stop. A drift has gathered outside, not far from cover. I can see the interruption it makes against the brightness, and the dark flicker of something moving along its edge. My feet are already spread, ready to back into the passage.

The flicker is only grit. I go out and feed.

The open ground is difficult to look across. Light comes up from it as well as down. I keep my body low, pushing into the drift, and leave several fans of clean grains behind me. Once I pause with my snout buried. There is a faint contact through my feet, then another. They are widely spaced. I turn back at once, not quickly enough to see what made them, and reach the fronds before there is any movement I can distinguish.

Inside, I continue past the young animal and the old crown. The film on my foot rubs away against another fallen frond.

Later the air changes. Fine grains begin to touch the roof of the passage, and the fronds press closer together. I go deeper, where the others are pushing loose powder into an entrance. We remain there while the roof settles low over us.

When I come out again, I have to dig. The grains ahead give way in small avalanches, filling the space I have just cleared. Eventually light reaches through, and I push my head into it.

The colony is still bent down. Powder streams from a joint above me and makes a small cone on the ground. Farther out there is a broad hollow in the new surface. I stay beneath the fronds and watch it. Nothing rises from it. The wind has already begun to soften its edges.

I can see a fresh drift from here. It will take me some time to reach it; there is no need to leave yet.
