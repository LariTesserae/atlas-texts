At first I am somewhere in the trembling.

A broad, slow pressure comes along the stone, and the rattles between it and me stop. I do not know the shape of the animal yet. I know only its weight, and the pauses in its weight, and the little impacts it makes when it puts its face down to listen.

Then it digs. Grit strikes the wall around me. Something soft catches against my edge and is pulled away. The pressure stays for a long time, moving back and forth through a narrow space, until the digger has eaten what it can reach. When it leaves, its young remain in the cut. They are lighter; their movements come through the stone like drops falling from a height.

I am on the wall above them, in an old digging. I hold to the limestone with the flat, damp underside of my body. Around me are other callers, so close that I feel them through their grips and through the places where our edges touch. One has grown partly over me. Beneath us, a younger caller occupies a shallow pit; its thin pulse comes up through my body as well as through the stone.

I feed without leaving. My filaments extend into the moisture under my edge, gathering what has settled there: fine debris from the digger’s work, fragments of bacterial film, things too small for me to distinguish except by what they give me. Sometimes a grazer passes beneath my filaments and I draw them in until its crowded legs are gone.

I rattle. The plate inside me bends and catches, bends and catches again. The sound goes into the wall. What comes back is never my call alone. The whole channel presses against my grip, a multitude of pulses with heavier movements running through them.

A narrow silence approaches along the wall. The caller overlapping me stops. I stop too. Something steps across us, placing its weight briefly on my hard upper surface. I feel one foot lift, another settle, then nothing directly above me. Farther along, the rattles quicken. A second hunter waits there, and when the first reaches it the two make a sudden flurry against the stone.

Long after they have gone, an injured foot continues to twitch in the crowd below. Each movement is smaller than the last.

Water comes first as impacts on the rib above the channel. Then a cold thread reaches my edge, carrying loose grit. I pull my filaments in. The callers higher on the wall continue rattling while those below me grow quiet. A grazer climbs over my back, slips, and catches against the body above mine. Others follow it upward.

The water rises past me. My plate moves once more against its ridge, sluggishly, and stops. The wall is still full of motion: grazers struggling for purchase, swimmers striking and releasing them, a heavy animal forcing itself through the flooded cut. At last even the impacts grow distant. My young neighbor remains pressed into its pit beneath me.

For a while I know the world by the water moving over my shell and the faint shifts of the bodies anchored around me.

When the level falls, it leaves a film of silt on my exposed surface. Water drains from the edge of my plate. I flex it and make one poor, interrupted rattle. I wait, then try again. Above me another caller answers—or merely begins when it is able—and below us a series of pulses starts up along the wall.

The young one beneath me does not move at first. I feel for it without intending to, through the part of my underside that rests over its pit. Eventually there is a thin vibration, repeated twice. I release one edge of my grip and settle slightly higher, leaving it more room.

The digging fills again. Grazers come to the newly wet stone; the young diggers return to the cut. The noise grows until no single pulse can be followed very far. I stay where I am, feeding in the damp under my body, sounding into the wall, and feeling the basin pass through me.
