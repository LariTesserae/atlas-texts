I would be on the outer edge of a tower near a spring, where the water divides around a projecting shelf. Not at the hottest center, where the pale mats grow thickest, but close enough to feel the warm water arrive in pulses among the cooler currents.

For a while I would not be there at all. I would be small and drifting, carried past walls I could not distinguish from the water around them. I might strike a crown and be drawn toward a mouth; I might fall into a chamber where the blind fish waited. Eventually I would touch a rough place on the tower’s edge and remain there. The current would press me against it. Something in the stone, or in the bodies growing from it, would let me begin.

My cup would be white when it was new. Its base would fit into the uneven rim of an older cup, closed and empty except for what had settled inside. I would add to my own cup slowly, taking from the water what I needed, raising its edge around my soft body. Then I would spread my threads into the current.

At first there would be room on either side. A red worm would live below me, so deeply in the wall that I could touch only its feathery mouth. A flat scavenger would pass through the crack behind my cup, its legs stirring loose grains. Later, two more builders would settle beside me. As they grew, our crowns would brush and fold against one another. We would feed close together, withdrawing when a larger body scraped past.

I would know the spring by the water it sent us: its warmth, the substances it carried, the fine living matter lifted from the mats at its base. The flow would change with the wider currents. Sometimes it would come steadily across my crown; sometimes it would curl back against the wall, leaving my threads slack until the next turn of water brought them out again.

The fish would gather in the channel below. I would feel them mostly as a succession of small disturbances, each one passing, then passing again as they held themselves in place. Once, all those movements would vanish at nearly the same moment. The broad hunter would enter, pushing a slow pressure ahead of it. One flank would graze the wall. I would close around my threads while its body moved past, and open again after the fish had begun to return.

I would live on the tower through changes too gradual for a single touch to register. More cups would grow above me, and the flow along my edge would become stronger as the opening narrowed. Sediment would collect in the cups behind me. The worm’s mouth would appear less often. A sheet of translucent animals would spread across a neighboring ridge and lift at its edge whenever the current turned.

Then the tower would lean.

The first movement might be no more than a new pressure where my cup met the one beneath it. Over time the current would strike my crown from an unfamiliar side. Grains that once passed over me would lodge along my rim. A crack would open somewhere deep in the old chalk, and a fine powder would drift out among the fish.

When the tower came to rest against its neighbor, there would be a long stirring of silt. The channel below would have a roof. Animals on the enclosed side would receive less of the flow; some would close and not open again. My own cup would still hold. I would be turned outward now, farther into the current, with a clear reach of water in front of me.

I would feed there for the rest of my life. New builders might settle along the upper edge of my cup, close enough for their crowns to touch mine. Their cups would rise beyond me, and the current would divide around them in turn. Inside the roof, the fish would gather in the shelter, facing the water that came through.
