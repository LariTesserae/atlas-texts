I live behind the fern, in the space it has most recently made. I sleep in a sling tied between two pale stems, with three other builders. When the fern shifts, the sling moves with it. Sometimes I wake to find a strand lying against my back that was clear of me when I slept.

I cut grass for the bridges. My stone is short and thick at one end, where I can hold it without wrapping, and thin at the other. There is a small notch in the edge. I know the notch so well that I can turn the stone in its fiber and find it without exposing the cutting side. Another builder gave it to me when I was young. The edge was longer then.

The lane behind the fern is the newest. It begins at a junction marked by three knots and runs along the cool seam of the curled tip. At first there was only enough room there for one of us to turn. Then the seam moved away from the root beside it, and a draft came through carrying the damp taste of fresh fibers.

I went in with a loose strand around my middle. Another builder held the far end. The floor dipped, and the moss gave way to a patch of wet root, so I climbed a stem and tapped it with my stone. The answer came through the root beneath me: one tap, a pause, then another. I pulled the strand tight and felt it taken in at the other end.

We laid the first bridge there that same rest, though we had to take it up again soon afterward. The fern had turned as it grew, and the stem we had tied to was no longer where our limbs expected it. One builder went down into the moss while following the knots. She was not hurt, but afterward we tied a loose loop at the junction until the bridge was sound again.

I used to think the knots marked places that would remain. When I followed an older builder, I learned each one by touching it after her: three close together for the crossing, a long stretch of smooth grass, then the binding where the bridge met a stem. I could feel her ahead of me through the grass, and when she paused I paused. Once she let go without telling me. I went on touching the knots, expecting to find her at the next stem. She was behind me, feeling the bridge take my weight.

Now I teach the youngest that way. They hurry at first. Their forward limbs miss the knots or catch on the loose ends of bindings. I leave those ends longer than I would on a finished bridge, so there is something easy to find. Later, when they sleep, I trim them close.

There is a hunter near the old lane. Its tunnel opens beneath a root where dry fragments have collected. I have never touched the hunter, but once, while clearing the lane, I felt movement come from the tunnel mouth and stop. I backed away. After the next flood had scattered the fragments, I gathered them again and laid them where the bridge approaches. The youngest know to turn aside when they feel them.

The last heavy footfall came while we were carrying grass. The roof bowed. A cold trickle ran down the fern and across the bridge, lifting the bundle from my back. I caught one strand, but the others went into the moss. The builder ahead pulled the sling cord to wake those who were resting, and we climbed the nearest stems.

We waited there while water moved below us. Something struck my walking limbs twice, lightly: a springtail thrown up from the floor. Farther along the stem, another builder was pressed against me. I could feel her breathing slow after the first rush of water passed.

When we came down, the bridge at the junction had lost one binding. We marked it with a loose loop before looking for the grass. Most of the bundle had caught against the fern. One strand had been carried under its edge, into a narrowing space where the wall pressed close to the floor. I put in my reaching limbs and found it, but it would not pull free. I cut it as far in as I could. The end that remained was still held by the growing wall.

We used the rest to mend the bridge. Then I took my stone forward and tapped the stem at the entrance to the new lane. An answer came from the other side of the fern, faint but clear through the root. There was room to work between us. I found the first binding by touch and began to undo it, so we could move the bridge farther into the opening.
