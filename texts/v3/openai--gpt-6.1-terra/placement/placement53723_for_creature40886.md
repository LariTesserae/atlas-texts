I would be under the stair, where the white fungus meets the dark crust.

Not all of me would fit there. I would run into the moss and along the buried faces of the stones, through old root channels and into the rooms the insects have made. But that would be the place I returned to in thought, if I had anything like thought: a folded boundary, wet more often than the stone around it.

The crust would not be an enemy. It would be something I could not grow through. I would meet it, turn, grow into another seam and meet it again. Between us, water would collect in a film thin enough for an insect to drink from. When the adults came, their fur would brush my threads. Sometimes they would take a piece of me away with the damp fibers.

I would have no picture of the chamber. The shaft of light would reach me only in its effects: a little warmth in one patch of moss, a change in the water held there, then cold again. I might grow around a fern root without ever knowing the shape of its frond. A snail’s passage would leave an absence at the surface and, later, a little matter for me to feed on. Its shell and feelers would be beyond my reach.

In the lichen rooms I would be eaten back regularly. I would keep growing there anyway, into what the insects dropped and what they abandoned. Their young would be close, separated from me by chewed fibers. When an adult carried food inward, its feet would press the bed around them. I might feel that pressure travel through the packed material, although I could not distinguish the adult from the young.

Some of my older threads would thicken in a joint between two stones. A root would pass through them, live there for a time, then die. I would feed on it slowly. Another root might enter the space it left. I would hold the joint a little, along with the roots and the stones themselves, without being the reason it remained standing.

Near the basin there would be moss I had never entered. I would not know why. Perhaps there would be too little of what I needed in the seam leading to it, or perhaps I would simply not have reached it yet. My edge would be uneven, full of places where a thread had stopped.

When the bead slipped down the channel, I would not know that the snail had been waiting. It might drink some of the water. The rest would pass the basin’s lip and disappear into the moss. Much later, if any reached the joint under the stair, the film between me and the crust would widen a little.

An insect would come to it. Its feet would disturb the water, and one of my fine threads, stretched across the gap, would bend against the stone.
