I think I would be near the blue seam, where a root has died against the rock.

At first I might seem to be the hollow it left: a narrow way through the moss, dark even at midday. Meltwater finds it after passing through the roof of needles. It travels along the old root’s shape, pauses at a bend and emerges under a living tree, where it hangs in a clear drop before falling into a pool.

But the hollow belongs to the water as much as to me. I would be the fine fungal threads through the softened wood around it, and through the litter packed into the neighbouring cracks. Some would reach the living roots; others would end in a scatter of needles or pass into the fallen limb hidden above the lane. Most of me would be too fine to see. Where many threads gathered, I might show as a pale skin, easily mistaken for a little frost.

The old root would not disappear all at once. Its harder fibres would remain after the softer parts had gone, holding the hollow open for water and making a rough bed for new growth. A young root might enter from the side and follow the sheltered channel upward, until its tip met stone.

I would have no view of the lane, though some of the lane would find its way to me. A loosened needle might lodge in the moss overhead. Water would carry traces of it down. Once, when a bird shook snow from a branch, the melt might reach the dead root days later, cold enough to leave a bright crust at the hollow’s mouth.

In a bitter spell the opening could freeze shut. Water would remain in the deeper wood for a while, and an insect might shelter there too, moving slowly among fibres that had once carried water for the tree. When the ice loosened, the first drop would take an unhurried path around it.

I would spread where there was moisture and something yielding to grow through. Some threads would fail in dry moss; others would persist beneath the stone. There would be neighbours too small to distinguish without looking closely, working through the same wood, leaving different parts of it behind. I could not keep the hollow as it was. The more of the root we softened, the more likely the roof above it would sag.

One spring—or one brief thaw between colder days—a small piece of the blue rock would fall into the channel. Moss would begin to grow around it. Water would gather on its upper face and find another way underneath.

The lane beyond might remain open for years. Closer in, the old passage would slowly narrow around the stone and the young root. I would still be there, in the damp wood to either side, spreading into what the water brought and what the living growth left behind.
