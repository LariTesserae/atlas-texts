I would be in the crack beneath the tilted plate, where the last of the snow’s water takes longer to disappear. From above, I might look like a faint whitening among the dead stems. More likely I would not be visible at all.

I would be a fungus, growing in threads through the old cushion. Some threads would reach into the dark material under the living leaves; others would press along a grain of stone or enter a stem that had died years before. I would have no clear edge. Where a thread dried and broke, I would end there. Where it found water and something it could use, I might continue.

At first I imagine myself deep in the crack, sheltered and still. But the shelter has openings. Meltwater enters from one side, carrying fine particles down from the slab. A springtail pushes between the fibers, brushing against me. Above, the living cushion swells when it is wet and draws tight again as it dries. Even down here the day reaches me through these small movements.

I would live partly on what the cushion sheds. Its leaves weather at the surface; its older stems darken underneath. In time I would enter some of that dead matter and break it down. The plant would go on growing above me, making new leaves while I worked among the old ones. We would occupy different depths of the same small mound.

When the snow covered us, I might remain there for a long time with little happening. Then water would come again, and some of my threads would resume growing. Others would not. I would persist through the parts that remained able to begin.

Perhaps, in a favorable season, I would make something visible: a small fruiting body among the stems, brief enough to be missed by the bird crossing the slab. Spores might leave it on the wind. Most would land on open rock, or pass over the basin’s lip. One might lodge in a tuft farther along the ridge, where moisture stayed a little longer.

It would not be a journey I could follow. I would remain here in the seam, while the wind carried that possibility away.
