I would be one of the small white animals inside a pipe.

Not deep in it, where the iron is dark and the sound comes through the walls, but near enough to the mouth that I can reach the rim when the machinery warms. My body would be soft, with short gripping feet and a narrow fold underneath for taking in the bright granules that lodge in the pipe. I would face inward most of the time. There would be more to feel there: the iron carrying the wheel’s vibration, the minute changes in heat as the frame takes it up from the stone.

At first I think I would know the wheel by its turning. But the pipe does not carry a clean account of it. The whine of the upright animals comes through too, and the tapping of their legs, and movements farther away that arrive as a faint blur. The iron gives me all of these at once. I can distinguish the stronger pulses, perhaps, but not tell exactly where they begin.

There are others in the pipe. I know the one nearest me by the cool patch left when it moves. Sometimes we meet at the rim, where we can reach the dust shaken loose from above. Neither of us stays long if a shadow crosses the bulb. We draw back into the pipe, passing one another against its curved wall, and wait.

From the mouth I can see part of a stalk and the low, lit edge of the wheel. Glassbacks travel over the frame in little groups, carrying grains between their feet. Once one comes into the pipe and walks across my back. Its legs are so light that I feel mostly the changing weight of it. It stays until the iron warms, then turns and goes out again.

I would not know when a glassback dies under a distant ledge, or when a young upright animal takes over a missing note. There might be an afternoon when the vibration through my feet is different: a thin part of it absent, then a new trembling that wanders before settling. I would hold still for a while. Then I would go on picking grains from the iron.

When the bulb is clear and the frame is warm, I come to the rim. The wheel is already turning. It will carry its marked tooth past the opening several times while I feed, though I cannot see the mark from here. Beneath me, an upright animal puts a foot into the teeth and draws it safely away. The click reaches me through the air first, then through the pipe.
