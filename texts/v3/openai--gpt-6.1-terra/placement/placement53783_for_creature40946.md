I would be one of the small animals in a seam, beneath a lid clear enough to let the light through. At first I think of the lid as a window. But it opens, and I open with it: the rim lifts, the warm current enters, and the little folds inside me begin their work.

When I close, a bead of the clear film stays with me. It is enough. I turn it slowly through the structures of my body. Outside, the water goes on without me for a while.

I live close to a basin where the long-backed animals stand. I cannot see a whole one. Through the water there are dark feet, the pale angles of legs, and occasionally a flank reflected in the surface above me. When an animal adjusts its footing, the pressure travels down the seam. My neighbors open ahead of me, their rims catching the light one after another. I open too. Farther along, the band continues.

Sometimes a drop falls from a chin into the water nearby. The impulse reaches me before I see the rings. It is not large enough to sweep anything away. It passes over my lid, travels along the seam, and enters the plates.

I have no need to follow it. Water comes back to me, bringing film; what I release goes away beneath the ceramic. The workers on the basin wall take it farther than I can see, lifting their ridged mouths over the rivets as they go. I know them only when the water thins enough to show their pale, moving edges.

Above me, the sky appears in pieces. A tower crosses one piece, upside down. At intervals the reflection darkens as the winged animals pass over it. I cannot tell where they land. By the time I open again, the tower is bright and still, and a bead has gathered under the chin above my seam.

My lid closes around its small warmth. The folds turn. When the pressure comes, I open into it once more, and the water enters.
