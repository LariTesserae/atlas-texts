I am beneath a plate near the center of the enclosure. At first I am hardly more than the place where the plate does not quite meet what is under it. The weight above me presses me flat. When it shifts, I open again.

I do not know whether this happens once or many times before I keep something. A pellet falls from the packed bed at my edge and comes to rest inside me. It is small, with a crease around its middle where a hand once rolled it against a rough surface. When the paving moves, the pellet stays. Clay gathers around it, then draws back. My sides widen and the pellet remains at my center.

Water reaches me through a seam. I make a passage for it, slowly, by giving way where the pellets are loosest. The water enters warm and carries a little red clay. For a while I am full. The pellet lies on my floor under the water, its crease collecting the fine red silt.

Later, there is another pellet.

It lies in a chamber under a different plate. I cannot tell how far away that plate is. Its chamber is lower than mine, and water has entered it too. The pellet has the same crease, though no red silt has settled in it. The weight above that chamber is different; it presses hardest on one side. When the chamber yields, I feel nothing happen to my own walls. Still, I know where the second pellet is.

A third appears with part of its crease pressed shut. One of the others opens along the crease, just a little. Mine does not change. Its red line remains fine and continuous.

Above me, the film begins to spread. At first I know it only by what happens to the seam: water that once found its way down comes more slowly. The edges of the plates have softened against each other. Then the rain returns and the passage opens again, carrying down a faint branching stain on a flake of clay.

I hold the flake for a time beside the pellet. It is too thin to remain itself in the water. Its edges loosen, and the stain comes apart. By the next dark interval there is a mark on one wall of my chamber where the flake had rested. It is not branching, only a darker patch. I do not know whether the film has reached me.

The enclosure fills with work. Pressure comes and goes through the paving. A wall is being raised somewhere near the gate; each new course has a weight I can feel for a while, until the ground takes it up. A chamber under that wall keeps a piece of brick. I learn this when a piece with the same broken corner appears on my floor.

There is barely room for it. The brick fragment lies against the pellet and pushes it away from my center. I widen. The paving above me sinks a little, though not enough for water to collect. By the time I have made room, the pellet has rolled back against the brick. I try to keep them apart. The brick stays where it is, but the pellet moves whenever my floor changes shape.

In another chamber, the brick fragment is upright. Its broken corner has been pressed into a wall, and on its smooth face is the impression of a narrow joint. I have not seen that mark made. After a dark interval it appears on my fragment too, shallow but distinct. The chamber beneath the gate may have received it from a print in the wall, or from somewhere farther away.

I keep the marked brick longer than I kept the flake. The joint does not soften. Fine clay settles in it, then water clears it again. Around it the fragment becomes familiar to me: the rough break, the molded face, a ridge along one edge where the mold was imperfectly filled.

One light interval the ground trembles. My chamber flattens so quickly that the pellet is pressed into the brick’s broken face. I cannot widen enough to protect either of them. The water leaves by the passage it entered, then comes back from another side. Above me, something heavy settles. When the pressure eases, my chamber returns slowly, but the passage is gone.

The pellet is split. One half lies beside the brick; the other is embedded in my ceiling. I wait for the ceiling to loosen, but it holds. The two halves no longer have a common center, and I cannot keep both in the same place. Each time my walls move, the loose half travels a little.

Elsewhere, the pellet with the closed crease is whole. So is the one without the red silt. I hold their places as well as I can. In time the loose half of mine acquires a second crease, a narrow indentation that was not there before. I find its match on the whole pellet in the low chamber. The other half remains overhead, its broken surface pale where the water cannot reach it.

I grow upward around that piece. It takes many dark intervals. The ceiling thickens, then arches, and the piece comes free with a little clay attached. It falls to the floor. I have made enough room by then that it lands beside the brick instead of on it.

The two halves do not fit together anymore. The embedded piece has swollen; the other has been rubbed smooth along one edge. I keep them both.

Above, a course of bricks reaches over the paving, and its shadow cools nothing. The film crosses the softened seam and follows the base of the new wall. A print closes around a brick at the unfinished end. Water finds my chamber again, and for a while it is clear enough that the marks on everything I hold remain visible.

I am larger now than the space that first opened under the plate. The brick lies at my center, with the two pieces of pellet close beside it. Farther away, other chambers keep their own objects, some marked like mine and some not. When the weight shifts, I yield around what I have. Then I begin, slowly, to return.
