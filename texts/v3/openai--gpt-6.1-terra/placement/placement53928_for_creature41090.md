I would be in the broken part of the wall behind the gray blades, where the water has cut a channel through the mud. Not on the wall, exactly. Most of me would be underneath it, in the shade of the stones.

At first I think I would be one of the tubes: a translucent length fixed at one end, with a mouth opening into the quieter water. In rain I would fill and become firm. When the basin drained I would soften, drawing the pebble beside me a little closer. Each time, the movement would be small enough that the pebble might appear not to have moved at all.

But there are other tubes under the pebble. They touch mine, and water passes between us. When a grain catches in one mouth, a neighboring tube may swell and pull it free. If one of us is torn, the others keep the torn end from being washed out of the wall. I do not know where I would have to be cut to become only myself.

The roots of the blades pass among us too. They are tougher than we are and push through places we cannot enter. In the wet season fine mud gathers around them, full of fragments to filter. In the dry season the roots hold that mud against the wind. We grow along them when we can, though the roots are alive in a different direction: they reach down and out, while we follow the small shelter already made by stones.

I would know the larger grazers by the long shadow of their passing over the wall’s mouth, and by what comes after: torn film, loose mineral, a sudden abundance caught in the water. I would know the little jointed grazers when they entered the channels between the stones. Their limbs tap the pebbles in quick, uneven sequences. Sometimes they shelter against us. Once in a while one brings eggs, pale beneath its body, and must turn sideways to fit.

One such animal settles under my nearest stone as the current begins to change. It has tested the gap with two limbs, then another, and now rests with its underside close to the rock. A few eggs have caught on a rough edge outside. Water moves them gently against one another.

The basin is still filling, but the pressure in the wall has already shifted. It comes from the wrong side, as though the water behind the wall were higher than the water in front. The little animal presses flat. Farther along, others do the same. Their stillness reaches me through the stones before any violent water does.

I stiffen. So do the tubes beside me. We lift the pebble a fraction, enough for the current to pull mud through beneath it. One egg goes with the mud. The others remain caught.

The water reverses hard enough to bend the blades down across the wall. A piece of crust strikes the stones and folds around them. I take in what passes through the gap: small shreds of the film, grains loosened from the pavement, something warm from a deeper seam. The pebble slips against my side. I hold it there, or the tubes around me do.

Afterward the animal emerges. It works at the rough edge with its limbs and gathers the remaining eggs back underneath. One is too far into the gap. It stays there, pressed against a root, and I cannot tell whether it is alive.

For a long time the channel runs toward the basin. Mud accumulates behind the pebble. The blades above us flower under their sheaths, and a heavy seed lodges in the wall. Its ridges catch on the stone where the animal had sheltered. Later a root comes down beside me, pale at first, then toughening.

The pebble does not return to its old position. When the water drains, we slacken and draw it closer; when the rain comes, we fill and hold it again. Other stones settle against it. The gap narrows, though there is still room for a small grazer to enter.

I would not see much of the plain from here. The basin would come to me in pressure, in fragments, in the tastes of water from places I could not reach. Sometimes a tremor would travel through the wall, and I would tighten around my pebble. Sometimes the tremor would come after the stone had shifted, when I was already holding it in place.
