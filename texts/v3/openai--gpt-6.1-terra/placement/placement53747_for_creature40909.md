I think I would live at the edge of town, where one of the grazer grooves crosses an old street. The wall of my chamber would have been worn smooth on the outside by passing bodies. On certain mornings I would wake to a slow scraping close enough to feel through the floor, and wait before going out. A grazer might stand there for hours, content to eat and block the entrance both.

At first I imagine myself as a keeper of the bright points, standing high at dusk with a polished plate. But the ridges are crowded enough when a storm comes, and I think I would be more useful farther down, where the town’s air begins to thin.

I would look after the near caches.

Not the distant ones: those belong to travelers who know how to cross a day’s open country and come back. The near caches are small chambers grown into the plateau at intervals beyond the last basin. They hold enough breathable air for someone delayed by a storm, or a grazer blocking the route, or a bladder that has developed a leak at its fold. Most are never needed. That is why they are easy to neglect.

I would have four limbs like everyone else, pale ridges across my back, and a habit of lowering myself to listen before I opened a chamber. My chest plates would make a rather uneven voice. Children would recognize it through the walls and sometimes answer me with a rasp of their own.

I would travel with a tray of testing vessels strapped beneath me. At each cache I would touch the ground first, sending a short vibration ahead. This would not make the air safer, but it might warn someone inside that I was coming. Then I would press my forelimb into the taught place in the wall and wait for the fibers to loosen.

Inside, I would test the air before opening my own bladder to it. If the pressure was low, I would mark that in the dust outside and return with a fuller bladder to replenish it. If the air had gone bad, I would seal the chamber and shift one of its dark stones out of line. A traveler approaching from any direction could see that something was wrong.

There is one cache I would keep visiting after it had failed for good. Its entrance has narrowed, and the fibers inside have grown together in a thick seam. It would take years to teach them apart again. We have another cache not far away, better placed and easier to maintain.

Still, the stones around the old one are carefully set. Someone comes out from town to straighten them after storms. I have never met that person. Once, finding the stones freshly arranged, I would sit beside the sealed entrance and send a greeting through the ground. I would feel no answer. On my next visit I would bring one extra dark stone and leave it beside the others, not quite sure whether I meant it as a gift or a question.

Perhaps the following season it would have been moved into the arrangement.

My chamber in town would be slow to admit visitors. I would mean to teach the wall a second scent and put it off, and friends would have to wait outside while I made my way to them. One of them, a collector of storm dust, would complain each time. Then one evening the wall would open beside her before I reached it. Neither of us would have taught it that place. We would stand looking at the passage, wondering who had used it before, until she grew impatient and went in.

When a storm approached, I would come back early from the caches. From the outer basin I would see the bright points drifting outward, and above them the first charged dust moving across the flats. I would close my breathing bladder, settle low and put a limb against the street. Through the singing of the walls I might feel other people coming in behind me, their steps arriving one after another through the plant.

After the storm, I would go out again with my testing vessels and a small supply of dark stones. The plateau would look almost unchanged. Dust would have gathered against the cache markers; a grazer line might have crossed them in the night. I would clear what needed clearing, test each chamber, and leave the good ones ready.

On the way home I would stop at the old sealed cache. There might be a new stone there, or the same ones in their familiar places. Either way, I would straighten them before I left.
