I would be beneath the old chamber, where the path ends under the root.

At first I might seem to be part of the root itself: a pale fringe hanging in the water, with finer strands trailing from it. The water here is clear but brownish with the slow seep through the mat. When a foldback crosses the chamber floor, the pressure reaches me before the scent does. The fibers above bow down; a thread of water slips past my body, carrying the taste of crushed moss.

I turn toward it. Turning is something I can do.

I have a soft body, a little longer than a foldback’s foot, and fringed limbs that can hold to the roots or let go one at a time. I graze their tender surfaces, though I leave the thicker strands intact. When I have eaten a place bare, I move on. The floor above is not one solid thing, and among its hanging roots are passages I know by their currents: the steady downward seep near the chamber wall, the colder drift where water enters from an older, deeper basin.

There was once more movement overhead. Foldbacks slept in the chamber and crossed its floor every bright part of the day. Their weight came in small, familiar pressings, sometimes many at once, until I could feel the whole colony settling. When they left, the floor lifted a little. For a long time afterward I kept turning toward places where the water no longer moved.

Other things came. A grazer sheltered there and rubbed against the wall until a little moss from its back fell through the entrance. Strainers hung their threads inside. Roots reached across the opening, and leaves grew over them. By then the chamber floor was thickening too, and less water passed down through it.

I moved outward, following the stronger seep. It took me many days to get past the old root. On the far side I found water I had not known was there, full of loose white fibers and the young of animals like myself. They clung to the roots near the surface, their limbs moving in the current. I grazed nearby without touching them.

Now I live where the floating mat gives under the weight of larger creatures. When a mat-walker approaches, the water first trembles around the root tips. Then a great pressure travels slowly overhead, bending the floor far down around me. I draw in my limbs and hold fast. Sometimes the animal stops, and I feel its weight shift from place to place as it tests the surface. Once it turned away; the water went on moving long after it had gone.

In a season of warm rain, I release my young among the roots. They are carried a short distance before they attach. Some remain where I can find them, although when they have grown I cannot tell them from the others. The currents change, the roots lengthen, and each of us follows the water that comes through.

Above me there may be clouds moving over the plateau, or a foldback pressing its side against another. I know them, if I know them at all, by what reaches this water: a cooling seep, a faint scent, a pressure passing through the living floor. When the seep weakens, I loosen my hold and make my way toward it again, limb by limb.
