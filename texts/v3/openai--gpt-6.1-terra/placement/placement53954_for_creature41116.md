I live above the platform, though I am seldom there.

Most of my time I am somewhere between it and the nearest root, carried in a widening circuit that never quite repeats. I have six sails. They are clear, like the rest of me, except where each hinge holds a little dark knot. If I close the three on one side, I turn. If I close them all, I fall until the air catches some other part of me.

At first I thought the platform was a place I visited because food gathered there. Grains arrive from the crowns and stop for a moment on its tilted surface. I put down my feet beside one, brace myself, and work at it with my mouth. Often I get only a taste before it rises. Sometimes I rise with it, my sails still half-folded, until the grain slips away and the ordinary wind takes me.

The platform is the only place I know where a grain can be examined without chasing it. Some grains are worth following into the air; others leave a harsh taste and little else. I have learned the difference partly by their shadows. A heavy grain makes a sharp dot. A thin flake makes a shape that changes as it turns, even while it seems to be still.

There are others who feed this way. We do not gather deliberately, but when a crown sheds heavily several of us may arrive at once. Our feet touch the platform. The grains hang between us. Then we lift, at slightly different speeds, and are scattered before we have climbed the height of a root.

One of the clear hunters waits above that height. I know it by a missing corner on its left sail. I know, too, that this may be several hunters, each missing a similar corner; the air does not let me stay near enough to be certain. When the shape appears I fold my sails and drop, even if it costs me a good grain.

I began life in a fissure beneath the root. There were many of us in the crack, our folded sails pressed close to our sides. The stone hummed through our feet. I climbed toward the sound of tapping, found the lower filaments, and fed there for a while. Then a crown turned over above me, and the wind reached down into the sheltered place where I was gripping. I let go.

I remember the first circuit only in pieces: a silver underside rushing past, the dark mouth of another grazer, a blow against a filament so fine it left no mark I could see. I remember landing on the platform and being startled that my feet could remain in one place. I had not known I was tired.

Now I return to the root when I can. There are fissures there where eggs will hold, though not every fissure stays open. I test them with my feet. The root moves too slowly for me to see it moving, but a crack that admitted me once may later be too narrow for my head. When I find a suitable place, I leave my eggs deep inside, beyond the reach of the strongest gusts. I do not wait for them to emerge. The flattened animals work along the stone below, and I have seen what they can do to a framework that cannot rise.

The arch draws me sometimes. When the blue line passes, the crowns come together and the air presses the animals close. I have mated there. I have also lost a foot there, though the hunter took nothing more. Afterward I was turned inside a crowd of bodies so clear that I could see the fronds through them. Then the crowns sprang apart, and each of us went wherever our open sails would take us.

I can still steer with the damaged foot. On the platform I keep it lifted, because a grain caught beneath it is difficult to reach. My shadow has a gap now. It crosses the stone beside the other shadows, and for an instant, while I feed, it holds still enough for me to recognize.
