I would be partway up the wall behind the pump, where a condensation groove disappears under a curtain of leaves. At first I might seem to be the pale shoot growing out of an empty root sheath. The sheath holds me upright, though it belongs to something that dried long ago. My first root has gone down through it; my second has gone the other way, into a seam already crowded with living roots.

For a while I would be small enough to mistake for one of the old remains. When water came, I would darken. I would put on a little weight and lean against the stone.

The knock reaches me through the wall and through the plants that touch me. It has been there as long as I have. The returning echoes take longer to arrive by air than the tremor takes through the rock, though I have no need to distinguish them. The repeated shaking loosens grit from the shelf above. Some gathers in the fold of my leaf; some works its way down into the sheath around my root.

My life would be measured less by the pump’s strokes than by what gathers between them: the salt left after a wet season, the thickening of a neighboring root, the gradual failure of a leaf that once kept the worst heat from me. When that leaf finally splits along its fold, more of my surface dries. I grow narrower leaves after that.

I send a root toward the condensation channel. It enters a mat of moss, crosses a woody root and continues beneath a crust of mineral deposit. The channel is occupied. Water reaches me first by passing through the old fibers there, and some of it continues down through my sheath to a smaller shoot below. In dry intervals its tip turns the color of paper. More than once I would seem to have lost it; then moisture would return and its base would swell again.

Eventually a leaf of mine reaches the broken pipe. It lies against the iron and follows its curve for a short distance. Minerals collect where they touch. Years later, if the leaf dies and shrinks, its outline may remain on the pipe, while newer growth uses the space it left.

From where I am, the pool’s light reaches the underside of only one leaf. The light strengthens during the pump’s long pauses and withdraws when it begins again. I do not turn fast enough to follow it. Over many cycles, though, that leaf grows heavier than the others and presses down on the curtain beneath it.

A white flower opens in the narrow space between my stem and the rock. Its pollen lodges nearby. Later a seedling appears in a seam my roots have helped to hold open. It may be mine; the chamber gives me no clear way to tell. Its first root finds the damp fibers around me and stays.

Above, the quarry has its own weather. I know it when the stone cools enough for water to gather, and later, when the first drop works its way down through the groove. The largest leaves catch much of it. What escapes reaches my roots slowly, and slower still reaches the shoot below.

By then I am no longer just the pale blade in the old sheath. I am also the leaf against the pipe, the root crossing the moss, the fibers holding a little grit in place, the shelter in which another plant has begun. Some of those parts will remain after I dry. Something else may grow through them and find its way toward the water.
