I would be a young keeper at the foot of the old gate.

For a long time, I would know the chamber as a place I could leave only a little way at a time. There would be the broad underside of the animal that sheltered me, its limbs making a loose fence around a patch of stone. The water moved differently inside that fence. Food caught there: fine grains, soft fragments, the remains of smaller creatures. I grazed among them with others my size.

Sometimes the limbs unfolded and the whole shelter rose. Water entered from every side and lifted the silt from our backs. We clung to the paving or crowded against the parent’s soft underside until it settled again. Once, it came down a little farther along the ridge. I was outside the shelter when it did. I found my way back by following the taste of the scraped stone.

Later I left for longer. I crossed a seam where worms withdrew one after another beneath me. Their crowns had already opened again by the time I turned back. Near the passage, crustaceans pressed against my front edge, unwilling to let go of the current. I could push between them, but the smaller ones climbed over my back, and for a while I carried them with me.

I found the socket by chance. Its rim was worn smooth, and inside it the mortar gave off a faint mineral taste. I could fit most of my body into the hollow, though one edge of my back remained exposed. When I stayed there, particles gathered in the lee of the stone. A snail came to graze on me. I lifted myself and it held fast, then continued across my back after I had settled.

I would return to the parent a few more times. It was not always in the same hollow, but its clean impressions lasted on the floor, and the freshest led me toward it. Each time, the space beneath its limbs was a little more crowded with my own body. At last I went back to the socket and stayed there through many slow arrivals of food.

I would grow thick-backed. The socket would become too small to hold me, though I might still rest beside it with one side tucked against the stone. Small animals would shelter under me as I had sheltered once. When I rose, they would cling or scatter, and water would pass through the place where we had been.

I would not know what the gate had closed, or what lay up the passage. I would know the stronger flow at its mouth, the yielding crowd gathered there, and the worn rim of the socket against my side. On a floor where silt settled everywhere, I would leave the shape of my body behind me, clean for a while, then gradually pale again.
