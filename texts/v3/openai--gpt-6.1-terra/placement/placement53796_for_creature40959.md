I would live in the exchange hall above the lowest reservoir, in a chamber divided from the others by strips of ribbed metal. When the draft changed, the strips would touch one another with a faint, irregular clatter. I would know which way the air was moving before I was fully awake.

I would be one of the basin dwellers, dark-skinned and low to the ground, with a pale scar across the ridges of my left palm. The scar would make that hand less reliable on cold stone. On the warm floors of the pumping stations I could still feel well enough through it, but for fine work I would turn my right hand down and keep the left for holding a cord or steadying a tool.

My group would keep six cultivation plates in a channel beneath the hall. One was slightly warped and rocked whenever the water rose. We meant to straighten it, but the growth on that plate was especially good, and each time we lifted it someone would say that we could do the work after the next scraping. In the meantime we put a small wedge beneath one corner. It washed away frequently.

I would spend much of my time at a gate between the hall and the reservoir. The gate was old, and its frame had settled out of square. When the basin shifted, it sometimes appeared to close while leaving a narrow gap at the bottom. Water passed through too quietly to be heard from the floor above. We had tied a cord to the lower bar and brought its other end up through a sleeve in the wall. By holding it taut, I could feel the bar come to rest.

There were several kinds of rest. One was firm, with a small return of tension after the weight settled. Another was dull and incomplete. I had learned the difference from an older member of the group, who would put my hand on the cord over theirs and wait for the gate to move. At first I felt only the cord cutting into my palm. Then, one wet day, the bar seated properly and I felt the little return. We waited through another cycle so I could feel it again.

I would not always work alone. On a day of steady rain, I might go down with a youngster carrying a scraper too large for them. They would follow close behind, sometimes stepping on my tail when they stopped to inspect a seam. At the gate I would let them hold the cord while I cleared the grille. The pale grazers clinging beneath it would curl up when exposed to the air. I would take a few for our meal and lower the rest back into the channel.

Once, while we were there, the youngster would say that the cord was moving before I had touched the gate. I would put my hand beside theirs. There would be a slow pull, then nothing, then a pull again.

We would go out to the causeway. The rain would have thinned to a warm mist, and the edge of the reservoir would be difficult to make out. Near the far wall, several of the flat-bodied animals would be gathered just beneath the surface. Their backs would make broad, faint shadows.

Others would come from the hall and the neighboring station. We would stand together on the wet stone, facing the reservoir, our feet spread against the ground. Someone would touch the youngster’s shoulder and ask what they had felt. The youngster would hold out their hand, and the other would take it.

We might wait a long time. A line could travel across the reservoir; the bridge beyond the exchange hall might lower. Or the animals might sink out of sight and the cord grow still, leaving us with nothing to report except what we had felt.

When we returned, I would check the grille once more. The youngster would go ahead to tell the others, trailing their hand along the wall. In the channel beneath the hall, the warped plate would be rocking again, and I would look for a better wedge before the evening meal.
