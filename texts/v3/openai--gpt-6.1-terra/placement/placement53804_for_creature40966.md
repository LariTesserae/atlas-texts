I would be just below the edge of a stone shelf, where water gathers beneath the overhang and falls into a bed of ferns. From above, there would be little to mark the place: a seam in the green, a few fallen leaves caught against a root, perhaps a shelfback’s print holding clear water for a day or two.

Below the print, the ground would be a dense, dark mat. Fine roots would pass through it in every direction. Some would end against stone; others would follow the narrow channels where water moved. Rust-colored grains would settle among them, along with fragments of moss, insect shells, and bits of wood too soft to hold their shape.

I would be a fungus in that mat, growing against the roots of a current-feeding plant.

At first I might seem to be the pale fan that appears after several wet days, lifting from a piece of buried wood. But that would be a brief part of me. Most of my body would remain among the roots: branching threads, some pressed close to the plant, others working their way into a fallen leaf or the skin of a dead root. The fan would spread its spores and soften back into the ground. I would continue, though not quite in the same form.

The plant would give me sugars. I would help it take up minerals, releasing some from the grains its own leaves had dropped the season before. Its roots would grow through mine, and mine around them, until a small piece of ground held a long history of our exchanges. There would be other fungi there, too, some joined to the same plant, some living on what had died. We would meet at the edges of our growth. Sometimes one of us would take more of the space.

A shelfback’s foot would press the turf down above me. Water would be squeezed from the mat, then drawn back when the foot lifted. The plant’s stems might bend but remain rooted; the soft center of another plant nearby might be eaten. Later a bird would scratch in the loosened leaves, exposing a grain of red mineral and a curled white larva. The bird would take the larva. Rain would wash the grain into a channel where one of my threads could reach it.

In wet weather the current would pass through the soil. I cannot tell how much of it I would feel. Perhaps it would matter chiefly through the plant: a change in what came to its roots as the hillside reddened and its leaves grew heavy with grains. In a long rain the ground around me would fill, and some of my threads would die for lack of air. Others, higher against the root, would survive. When the water receded, I would grow again into the openings left behind.

A snail might cross the damp fan while it lasted. Spores could lodge in the folds of its body, and a few might come loose among the moss above ground. Most would come to nothing. One might find softened wood in the fork of a tree and begin to grow. Whatever lived there would no longer be the same connected body as the one beneath the shelf, though it would have come from it.

I think I would stay where the shelf sheds its water. There is enough falling into that place to keep me occupied: the plant’s leaves, the old roots, an occasional insect, the fine grains brought down from higher ground. The shelfbacks would pass overhead, and their prints would fill and empty. I would not see the hillside turn red or watch the blue light at the pools.

For a short time after rain, though, a little fan would stand under the fern. Someone looking closely might find it there, pale against the dark wood, with a drop gathering at its edge.
