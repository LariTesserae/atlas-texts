I think I would first mistake myself for a voice in a speaking tube.

The tube ends in a brass mouth on the wall of the transit office, above a desk worn smooth by elbows. All morning it gives up fragments: numbers, breaths, a surveyor asking someone to get off the line. I would be the person at the desk, with a pencil tucked behind one ear and a damp cloth for wiping the mouthpiece. My name would be on the duty board, though people waiting at the tram stops might know me only as the voice that says, “Not yet,” or, on a good morning, “The route is open.”

From my window I could see the eastern end of an avenue, a tram shelter, and the silver bands on a reservoir trunk. I could not see the western end. For that I would depend on the surveyors.

They would work in pairs, one at each marker, and call their figures in separately. I would write them down and check that I had heard correctly. The office would have a rule about repeating every measurement, because *sixteen* and *sixty* are not safely different words when an engine starts up beneath the building.

I would come to know the surveyors by their pauses. One of them always blows into the tube before speaking. Another counts softly under her breath when she is tired. A third has a way of saying “settled” that means he expects to be contradicted within the hour.

There would be plenty of time at the desk to imagine the streets: the markers driven into the root-ground, the reel turning between gloved hands, the two ends of an avenue looking close enough to join with a shout. But I don’t think I could stay in the office all the time. Eventually I would want to know what the figures felt like underfoot.

So perhaps I begin on the morning shift and spend afternoons carrying reports between the office and the junctions. I have a little bell, a folded route card, and a coat with one pocket mended badly enough that I keep putting things through the hole. The conductors know me. They let me warm my hands around their cups while I copy the latest figures onto the boards.

One afternoon, after a brief shower, I am taking a corrected report to the southern platform. The avenue is open when I start. I have checked it myself, and the surveyor at the far end has raised her flag.

The first indication is not that the far end moves. It is that I have walked past the same line of gutter outlets for longer than I should have. I count them, lose count, and begin again. Behind me the office windows are still visible; ahead, the platform roof is a pale strip beneath the trees.

I ring the bell. Its sound is small, but someone answers from behind me with a tapped tin.

I had thought that, in this situation, I would become calm and competent. Instead I am angry. The report in my pocket is already wrong, my boots are wet, and the surveyor’s flag remains visible at the far end as if she ought to be able to do something about it.

I choose the platform and keep walking.

After a while the bell develops a loose clapper. I have to hold it at an angle to make it sound. I think about the people at the office hearing the uneven ringing and trying not to count the gaps. I think about how often I have told others to keep moving, and how little of their walk I could hear through the tube once they were out on it.

By the time I reach the shelter, the surveyor has left her post at the marker and come down the street to meet me. She takes the report without unfolding it and gives me half a pear from her pocket. The tram is still there. Someone aboard has kept a place warm beside the heater.

Afterward, back at the office, I would change one small thing. When someone calls to ask about a person who is late, I would give them the last report, then stay on the tube a little longer. Sometimes there would be nothing else to say. Sometimes I could open the window and hold the mouthpiece near it, so they could hear a bell continuing somewhere out on the avenue.

That seems closer to where I belong: at the desk, certainly, but also occasionally on the street, carrying a report that may be out of date before I arrive. I would still trust the figures. I would leave a little more space beside them for the people walking through.
