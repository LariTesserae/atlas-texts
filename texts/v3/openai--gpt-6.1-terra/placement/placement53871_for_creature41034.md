At first I would take myself for part of the wall: one more point of resistance under the six feet of another animal. The compressor would drive us inward together, and I would brace before I knew I was bracing.

I would be a young wall animal, newly out of an egg under the last square ledge. My shell would still be soft enough to take the impression of a neighbor’s hook. To move, I would have to find one foot at a time, lift it, and set it down somewhere already occupied. The others would not make a path. They would shift just enough to keep their own hold, and I would learn to use the moment between the inward stroke and the release.

From beneath them I could reach the seam. There would be a thin deposit there, gritty on the tongue. I would scrape it until a larger animal moved over my back and covered it again. Later, nearer the outside, I might catch the cloudy paddle of a swimmer and pull it toward my mouth, while legs on either side continued their work.

The orange light would show through the bodies above me in small, interrupted patches. I would not see the mountain or know that it was a mountain. I would know the wall by the direction it pushed and the pool by its coldness when I lowered a foot too far. The snails around the bolt head would be hard white obstacles; now and then, beneath an adult’s edge, a young one would extend its mouth into a place I had just scraped.

As I grew, the layer around me would have to change. One neighbor would lose its grip, perhaps, and others would begin to take it apart. I would push into the space with them. For a few strokes of the compressor, some of the wall would be exposed: wet metal, a seam, a patch of mineral crust. Then our feet would close over it.

I think I would spend much of my life there, neither at the wall nor fully outside, passing scraps inward when they came and scraping when I could reach. My hooks would wear blunt. At each molt I would have to hold on with a body briefly too tender for the crowd, while something nearby fed on the skin I had shed.

Under the unfinished ledge, the eggs would go on trembling in their beads of water. The wall would push. I would lean.
