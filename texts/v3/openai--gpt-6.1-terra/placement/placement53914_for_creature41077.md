At first I would take the dark place for a hollow in a hill. I would hang there through the day, tucked behind a root in a chamber just large enough to turn around in. The ceiling would be rough against my back. Other bats would hang close by, shifting their feet and settling again as the air warmed.

But the walls would have seams. Here and there a pellet would come loose and fall, and beneath it there would be smaller passages, some empty, some still in use. The hill around us would be a great accumulation of nest, root, fallen nest and new wall. The termites would not have made the cavity for us. Perhaps a root rotted out; perhaps rain opened it. We would have found it afterward.

I would be a small bat that catches insects under the canopy. Not one of the bats carrying fruit home: the scraps beneath our roost would be wings and the hard little parts of beetles, mixed with our droppings and the dust that falls from the ceiling.

At dusk I would climb toward the opening. The others would go before me or after me, brushing past in the narrow space. Outside, the frogs would already be calling from the hollow below. I would drop, open my wings, and turn between two trunks before the ground came up to meet me.

There would be room to fly here, though not much. A hanging vine, a spray of fern, a branch that was not there the night before: each would send something back from my calls. I would make quick turns through the dark passages, following the moths and beetles out among the leaves. Sometimes I would miss. Sometimes a beetle would strike a leaf and vanish into it before I could reach it.

After rain, water would fall from the canopy long after the rain itself had stopped. Drops would strike my wings and the leaf litter below. At the pool, frogs would take insects too, and a snake might wait among the roots for a frog. I would pass over them without landing, then climb the slope where the ground held the day’s warmth.

Near dawn I would return to the cavity. Perhaps the entrance would have narrowed in the night: termites would be building across a wet edge, bringing earth to a place where the rain had broken through. I would squeeze past their work and hang up behind the root.

From there I could feel the others arrive, one by one, before they settled. Outside, monkeys would begin moving through the crowns. A twig would hit the slope and slide down it; loose earth would follow. Inside the wall, workers would come to the break. I would fold my wings and sleep while they closed it.
