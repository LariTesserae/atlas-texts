I live beneath the staircase, on the lower face of a mineral flake. The flake is caught against a roof fastening, with one edge lifted from the stone. Water passes through the gap. On one side of me it has just come from the bright joint; on the other it has washed over the metal.

I am a film of cells, thin enough that the water feels the shape of the rock through me. The dark mat holds the upper face of the flake. A few of its threads reach around the edge, among my cells, and attach to the roof below.

When the current runs toward the staircase, I receive what it carries from the joint. I build a pale case around myself, grain by grain. Some of my neighbors build cases too; others grow inside old ones, taking them apart. We grow over and through one another until the gap narrows.

Then the water presses harder against the flake.

The fastening trembles first. I know it through the movement of the grains to which I am attached: a quick shift, a pause, another shift. The flake tilts upward. Water enters beneath it, lifting a pale cloud from the roof. Several of my neighbors go with the cloud. One of the mat’s threads stretches across the widening gap and holds.

When the pressure eases, the flake settles a little farther from the fastening. The water reaches a different part of me. Cells that were sheltered begin to build; some that had been growing well stop. The mat’s stretched thread lies across the opening. In time, it thickens, and other threads grow down beside it.

I do not know how many reversals have happened while I have been here. There are layers beneath me, cases with no living cells in them, and above me cells dividing into water that once moved freely. Each reversal changes which parts of us can live. I am the film that remains connected, though what remains is never quite the same.

Once, after the pressure door opened, the flake lifted so far that light came under it. The mat tore along its edge. Pieces of it stayed on my side, dark against the pale cases. For a long while the opening was too wide for its threads to cross.

I grew into the newly exposed place. The water there was rich in what I needed, and I made a ridge along the edge of the flake. As the mat grew back, its threads caught on that ridge. They held through the next reversal. Afterward the gap was narrower than before, and a little less water came through.

The machinery changed something elsewhere in the street. The fastening cooled; the flow beneath the flake strengthened. Some of the ridge broke away, but the mat’s threads held to what was left.

Now, when the deep hum changes pitch, water begins to press beneath me before the flake moves. The green filaments under the balcony flatten. The doors fill with turning glass. I stay where I am as long as the grain beneath me stays, building my case in the gap, leaving its surface for the mat to hold.
