I would be in one of the stretched places, where a root enters the water beside a trunk and takes a long time to emerge on the other side.

At first I might be mistaken for part of the root. My body would be narrow and pale, wrapped loosely around it, with a fringe of feeding limbs at each end. The limbs on the near side would gather spores; those on the far side would take whatever had already passed through the forest between. Often they would bring in the same kinds of food, but not in the same order.

I would not know the length of my body all at once. When the water cooled at one end, I would begin to draw that end inward. Sometimes the other would feel the pull before the cooling reached it. Sometimes I would gather myself for hours, the root sliding through my coils, and discover that both fringes were still extended.

A grazer’s trail crosses the root. I feel the sealed edges of the leaves before I feel the animal itself: a slight change in the water, and then a succession of smooth places where the food has been taken. Once, while I am feeding among those edges, the grazer comes through. Its skin brushes my limbs and carries some of them away. They are small losses. New limbs grow, and the old ones remain on the grazer for a while, lifting in the current as it moves.

Later I find a tuft of stems beside the root. It has grown around something pale. I cannot reach it from this end of myself. The other end is already there, though, drawing loose fibres from its branches. Among them are the limbs I lost, softened now and threaded into the tuft.

When a bed of sediment rises, I loosen my hold. Grains strike my body and lodge in its folds. Some are carried into the stretched water, where they seem to fall for a very long time. I take what I can from them. The rest settle around the root, on both sides and in places I cannot reach.

I have never been inside a stone ring. Yet one of my feeding fringes sometimes encounters a current that circles without moving away. There is a faint brightness in it, and tiny swimming bodies, some of which I catch. The fringe remains there as long as the current lasts. At the other end, I hold to the root.

The forest closes and opens around me. A leaf turns pale; a trail appears; the grazer comes, or has gone. Between my two ends the root continues to grow. Every so often I must loosen my coils to let it thicken.
