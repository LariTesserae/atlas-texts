I would be one of the fine roots at the bottom of the glass enclosure.

Not the whole sleeve of roots, though I would be joined to it. Somewhere below the floor plates, my tissue would meet the tissue of another stem, and water taken up there could pass into me. The boundary would be firm in some places, porous in others. A few of the roots crossing mine would belong to the same joined plant; a few would not. They would all press against me as they thickened.

At my tip I would meet the glass.

I would have been growing through the narrow space between the enclosure and its mounting. The glass would turn me aside. I would lengthen along it, following the pressure of its smooth surface until another root crossed my path. There I might curve again, leaving a pale line against the glass. Behind me, the older part of my root would swell slightly, making the space more crowded.

The enclosure would have one side warmed by a lamp. Growth would be quicker there. A root that had reached it before me would cast a thin shadow, and I would pass beneath that root, close enough that our surfaces touched for a while.

Beyond the glass there would be air. I would not be able to reach it, nor would I know it was there. The seal at the base would offer smaller places to enter: a crease in the material, a gap beside a screw. I would grow into one of these and stop advancing when the space became too tight. My tip would soften there. Farther back, a branch would begin.

The larger plant would continue its work above me. Leaves would turn their edges from the lamps while the stems behind them kept lengthening. When a leaf came away, it might settle on the platform, then slide into the ferns on the chair. Parts of it would eventually reach the floor and darken the soil around roots like mine.

I would live for a time that way: taking in water and salts, sending some of them through the joined tissues, growing wherever there was room. The chair would remain above me; the dry lever would remain behind its glass. Occasionally the machinery would make an adjustment, and the water would move across the crowded plants. I might feel that movement only faintly, through the roots resting against mine.

There would still be space for a new branch. It would emerge behind my stalled tip and grow along the edge of the mounting, into a gap none of us had filled yet.
