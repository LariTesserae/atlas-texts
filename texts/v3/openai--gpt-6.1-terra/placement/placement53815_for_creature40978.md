At first I think I would be the shaft, because it is one of the few things here that moves without going anywhere. Water falls; the shaft turns; a droplet leaves the split pipe. There is a pause long enough for the rust to seem entirely still, and then another turn.

But the shaft was made elsewhere, and its work here is nearly finished. I think I would be something that has gathered on it since.

On the wet face of the frame, just below one of the breaks, there is a pale patch that could be either a deposit or a sheet of animals. From a short distance it looks like all the other pale patches. Close up, when a droplet strikes, part of it draws back while the rest remains unchanged.

I would be in the part that draws back.

Not the whole sheet, perhaps. One of its small fixed animals, with a fringe in the moving water and neighbors close enough that when one contracts, the space it leaves changes the current around the others. I would have no view of the basin as a basin. There would be the iron beneath me, the passing water, the pressure of bodies on either side. There would be food arriving too small to choose at a distance: a grain that can be drawn in, another that must be let go, a living thing caught among the grains.

The shaft’s knock would reach me through the frame. The spray would reach me afterward. The interval between them would vary: sometimes a droplet would strike directly, closing my fringe and those around it; sometimes the water would gather above us and descend slowly, dividing around a ridge of crust. When the pipe ran thin, I would feel the film of water on the iron growing shallow. The shells below might still be submerged while the upper edge of our sheet tightened against the frame.

A swimmer would visit. Its transparent joints would be difficult for me to distinguish from the water, if I could distinguish them at all. I would know it by the scrape passing over my neighbors, by the sudden open place beside me. It might remove animals and deposit alike, leaving the iron bare in a little crescent. Later, grains would lodge there. Water would collect against them. The bare place might remain bare for a while, or another animal might settle there and grow.

Below us, the shells would open where the droplets ran together. One has grit caught between its valves. It closes whenever the shadow of a many-legged creature passes over the frame, but not completely. Water continues to move through the gap. A swimmer works there, braced against the shell’s rim, and the shell opens again around it.

At the hour when the crevices begin to light, I would not see much. The frame leans toward the largest pool, but my body is fixed to its other face. A gleam might reach the water running past me, broken and faint. More often I would feel only the small changes that came with it: a ripple entering the trough pool, a momentary check in the flow, a swimmer hurrying over the edge of the iron.

I would remain open when I could. The shaft would knock, and the pipe would send another scatter of droplets across the frame. Some would strike the crust above us and join together there before coming down. One would gather a rust flake on its way and bring it within reach of my fringe. I would draw it close, then let the water take it on to my neighbor.
