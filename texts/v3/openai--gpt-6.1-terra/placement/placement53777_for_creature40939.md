At first I think I would live in the stairwell behind the frosted pane. It is one of the few places where a person can be visible without being entirely seen. Someone has wiped a circle in the glass, and through it I can watch the upper walkway: the passing pipe, the shoulders beneath it, the pause at each corner.

But there is no room to put down my tools in the stairwell, so I move across to the service passage. I am the person called when something has begun to loosen but has not yet fallen: a bracket pulling out of a wall, a stair tread rocking underfoot, mortar crumbling around a pipe. The builders are still here and could do most of what I do, but they have larger work in hand. I have a tin of fasteners, a narrow chisel, and a piece of cloth I fold beneath anything likely to ring when dropped.

From the passage I can see the shrub’s guard. It leans a little into the walkway where someone has bent it back around the stem. I mean to straighten it, but the machinist has already done so by the time I bring my pliers. For a while I stand there holding them, looking for another loose wire. Then the pump starts, and the pipe above me cools slowly enough that I can feel the change by keeping my hand near it.

I know the pump’s knock, though not as well as the people who work on it. I know the little tap made by a bracket near the cooling tank when the belt is running; I have tightened it twice, and the tap returns after a few days. I know which ladder has a repaired rung, because I repaired it and still place my foot carefully on that rung. At night, sounds come through the wall of my room without telling me whose hands are making them. I have learned not to answer every one.

One morning a worker asks me to look at the brick seam beside the shrub. A corner of mortar has come away, leaving a pale chip on the floor. The root beneath it is no thicker than a cord, but it has entered deeply enough that I cannot see where it ends.

I bring the chisel and leave it on the cloth. The person who keeps the settling basin comes by with a scraper and stops to look. We discuss whether the root can be cut. Neither of us knows. The shrub has only a few branches, and this one runs toward the leaves that catch the afternoon light. We decide to ask the machinist, who has been here longer than either of us.

The machinist comes out with dust on one sleeve. She presses the brick above the crack, then the brick below it. The lower one moves under her thumb.

“That one needs doing,” she says.

We wait until after the tank has been drained. Someone lifts the trailing stem out of the channel, and the warm water passes, darkening the stone. When it has gone, I take off the wire guard and set it where no one will step on it. I work the loose brick free with my fingers, then clear away the broken mortar around the root. There is more root than I expected: a thin strand branching into finer ones, several already dry where the mortar has separated.

The machinist cuts a shallow notch in the edge of a replacement brick. We try it in place twice. The first time it presses against the root; the second time there is a little space. I pack mortar around the other edges and leave the notch open. It is not as neat as the original seam, and the opening may give us trouble later. I make a note of it on the scrap of card where I keep the dates of repairs.

By then the light from the shaft has reached the basin. The person with the scraper has gone back to work, and each time the scraper lifts, a few white grains fall into the shallow water. I wash my hands with a damp rag rather than at the tap. There is mortar under my nails that will stay there through the evening.

Before I put the guard back, I tuck its lower end farther from the channel. The next person carrying pipe may bend it again. The shrub may thicken into the space we have left and loosen the new brick in its turn. I will be able to reach that brick without taking the whole seam apart.

When the pump stops, I stay where I am for a moment, listening to the belt and to the scraper at the basin. Then I gather the chisel from its cloth and carry it back through the passage, turning it carefully at the corner.
