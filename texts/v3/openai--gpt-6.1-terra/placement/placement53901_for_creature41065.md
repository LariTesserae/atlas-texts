I would be beneath one of the piles, not in the deepest gap but near its edge, where a thin sheet of water passes under the lowest stone.

At first I might seem to be part of the seam: a pale length interrupted by the small dark lines between my segments. The stone above me bears down unevenly. I have folded my limbs where the space is narrow and braced two of them where it widens. Moisture gathers along my sides. Now and then a drop falls beyond my shelter, and its impact travels through the ridge.

I know the ridge by its tremors. Beneath all of them is the great, slow vibration of the basin, so constant that stillness would be stranger to me than movement. The drops make brief, separate touches. Running water gives a finer, sustained shiver. Something walking has weight and intervals; I draw my front limbs in until it has passed.

The pile is older than I am. One side has been sealed with mineral growth, and a plate has slipped down over the entrance on the other. I can get through, but only by turning partway onto my side. There are empty egg cases on the underside of the upper stone, and deeper in, beyond a ridge of crust, a cluster that is not empty.

When the mist thickens, I come out. The exposed rock is cold and wet against my underside. I stop at the edge of a trough and wait there, my front raised, while a film of water slides down from a higher plate. In the pool below me the small swimmers settle against the bottom. Once they are still, they are difficult to distinguish from the pale marks in the silt.

I take one from a shallow place where the water has nearly run out. Later I scrape a soft patch from the stone under an overhang. The marks I leave are little crescents, already beginning to fill with water when I move away.

On the way back, I find a loose plate. It is thinner than the one that has slipped across the entrance, and its underside is damp. I get my front limbs beneath an edge and lift. It shifts, then settles again. I work at it from another side. The crust breaks in a narrow line, and the plate moves far enough for me to drag it onto the ridge.

I do not carry it directly to the pile. There is a fissure in the way, and I must leave the plate, cross first, then reach back for it. By the time I have drawn it to the entrance, condensation is running freely over the stones. I wedge its edge beneath the slipped plate and press upward. The upper stone rises only a little, but when I let it settle, a wider opening remains.

I enter through the gap and turn beneath it. Water drips from the new plate outside, not from the stone above the eggs. I hold still for a long time, feeling the altered weight of the pile against the ridge.

Later, something small moves inside the sealed part. The movement is so slight that I lose it in the basin’s trembling, then find it again: a few faint touches against the underside of the stone.
