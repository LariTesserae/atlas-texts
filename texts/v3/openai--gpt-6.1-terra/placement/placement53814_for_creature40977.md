I would be just inside the wall, where a reed fiber crosses a seam. At first I might take myself for the seam: I feel each blow arrive through the clay, and water keeps coming to me from both sides.

But the seam opens when the mountain is struck. I bend with it and remain attached at both ends. I am one of the roots that has grown around the reed, divided to pass it and joined again beyond. My loop is no wider than the animal’s foot.

I have more than one growing tip. One has gone deep into the warm clay, where it finds water but nothing to brace against except the close-packed grains. Another has reached the chamber. It came out under a moss shelf and hung there for a while, white and unsteady. Now it has a fringe of fine hairs, and the animal sometimes puts a foot among them.

I know which foot it is by the pull. The animal tests several hairs, lets two go, then settles its weight on the others. When a drop forms above it, the pull lengthens toward the ceiling. Once the drop falls, the hairs spring back. I feel the animal gather itself elsewhere, then return, touching lightly from hold to hold.

The tool worries me. It comes through the little opening with its broad face and presses new work against the old. The clay closes around one of my tips. For a time I cannot move it at all. Then the pressure eases, leaving the tip bent sideways into a fresh shelf. The shelf is wet enough that I begin to grow through it.

A new tuft has been set there. Its roots are short and broken where they were lifted from wherever they grew before. One lies across mine. Water passes down it into the clay; later, when the shelf begins to dry at its edge, water travels back up. The broken end swells and puts out a small branch. I grow alongside it. We meet a reed fiber and divide around opposite sides.

Outside, the hands strike again. The animal folds, pulling my hanging hairs close to the wall. The new tuft shakes so hard that its green tips strike the fernlet above it. A drop comes loose and falls through the leaves, hitting one, then another, then the clay beside my loop.

The wall takes in the water. Some reaches my deeper tip. The rest travels upward through the new shelf, toward the tuft and its broken roots.

When the blows stop, the animal opens one leg. Its foot finds my hairs again, including one that has grown since the last time. It holds there, while above us a fresh bead gathers slowly enough for both of us to feel it.
