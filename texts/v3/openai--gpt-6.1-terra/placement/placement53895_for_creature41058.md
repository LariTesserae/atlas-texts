I would live where a conveyor from the southern collectors joins the line to the storage towers. The junction is enclosed, but one wall has a row of narrow inspection slits. Through them I can see the piers go out across the stone and, in clear weather, the copper fan above the old road.

My work is to receive the road strips and pass their contents on. Travelers leave them at a slot beside the rail, sometimes without stopping. I clean the dust from the cuts, feel them with an inner arm, and tap the information along the line to whoever needs it: a damaged switch to the rail workers, a cold storage bed to the people who maintain the depots. The strips themselves go onward in a tray.

At first I think of myself as someone who carries messages without moving very far. But I also look after the junction. A tray with a broken rim can catch on the guide and stop everything behind it. I keep a tool for lifting the rim clear and a stack of thin plates for repairs. When a load arrives with dust spilling from its cover, I close the line and sweep the joints before it can work into the rollers.

There is a pale-backed animal that sleeps outside the warmest part of the wall. One of its rear plates is split, and dust has gathered in the split so that I can recognize it through the lowest slit. It comes and goes with the others, though in cold weather it tends to arrive before my shift begins. Once I found it rubbing against the guard around an exposed terminal. I tapped on the wall until it moved, then tightened the guard. The next day I found it in the same place and tightened the guard again.

I share a room against the storage tower with three others. One of them works the southern intakes and sometimes comes home with dust packed so firmly around the covers on their joints that we need two brushes to free them. They tell us what the banks look like from outside. From my slits I can usually see only the edge of one, and by then it has already begun to shade the road.

One morning a strip arrives marked with the position of a fan I cannot see. I take it to the slit and hold it up, though that does not help. The traveler has cut the date clearly, and the road beneath the fan is one I know from other strips. I tap the report through to the storage tower, then add that the fan is hidden from the junction.

The answer comes back along the rail: *Seen from the upper platform.*

I pass that on too. Afterward I put the strip in its tray and watch it disappear around the bend. Outside, the split-backed animal has turned in its sleep. Its broad feet rest against the wall, just below the terminal guard.
