I would begin in a gutter on the fourth level, where the water slows around a fern.

I would not, at first, know myself as separate from the pale band along the curb. Water would pass through my folds and leave a little of the rock behind. When drops struck the pavement above, I would feel them as brief changes in weight, some close enough to draw me open, some already fading by the time they reached me.

Then the street would loosen.

For a moment I would be stretched thin among the droplets of the pavement, with the fern held somewhere above me by its roots. When the street gathered again, part of the margin would be missing. The gap would close. So would my two exposed ends.

I would remain beside the fern, a short length of tissue in a shallow pool. Across the new curb, the rest of the band would lie just out of reach. Water would gather between us, but the drain would have opened farther down the street. I could feel its pull without being able to follow it.

The pool would warm. Sediment would settle around the fern roots. A droplet from the ceiling would arrive through three levels of pavement and strike close enough to make me contract. Later, another would miss the pool entirely. I would have no way to call it back.

A root would grow through the soft edge of the gutter. Water would begin to creep along it, carrying a few grains at a time. I would extend a fold into the damp channel. This would take longer than the pool took to form, and longer than the root took to split the edge. The water would keep moving.

By the time I reached the other side, I would have become a much finer thing than the piece that was left behind. My edge would touch the larger band. We would press together while water passed through the seam, tightening it and loosening it again. A note would travel along the street.

The join would hold, though a faint fold would remain where it had closed. Through it I could feel drops arriving elsewhere, on pavements I could not touch: a scattered fall, then the heavier run of a gutter finding its drain. Beside me, the fern would lean farther over the curb. Its new root would continue to divide the little pool, even after it had emptied.
