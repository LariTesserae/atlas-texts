I would be the structure, or the living part of it: the warm wall and the long body beneath the dry platform, where the water passes through me.

At first I might take myself for the platform. It is the one place I keep dry, and I feel its weight distinctly when someone comes to rest there. But the platform does not ache when a seam is pressed. It does not open. I am also the yielding wall, and the channels below the ground, and the slow movement that travels upward when I draw water in.

I would know the basin by what reaches me. The chord enters through my foundations and holds my walls under a steady pressure. I cannot separate every note, but I know when one stops. For a while afterward, the silence has a shape: it presses less strongly against one side of me. Then the other mouths fill the space.

Water reaches me with the taste of the rock it has passed through. I hold some of it in narrow chambers and release it changed. This is not something I decide to do each time. The chambers take what they need and give up what they have; the flow carries the rest out beneath the mat. There are stretches when the water comes thinly, and I work more slowly. There are long wet periods when I feel it moving through every part of me.

I would be closest to the animals when they are ready to leave something behind. A seep dweller presses its rim against my wall, and I yield enough for it to get a hold. It may stay there a very long time. I feel the small, repeated pulls as it works its skin loose, then the sudden easing when a section comes away. Grazers crowd my lower seams, their hard legs ticking against me. If I shift too quickly, they curl around their young. So I move slowly, when I can feel them there.

I do not tend only those who survive. The hunters land near me too. Their feet make little separate pressures in the mat, then become still. Sometimes a grazer’s rasp stops beneath them. Later, something heavier climbs the rim, and the feeding begins again elsewhere.

When someone approaches from farther away, I feel them through the ground before they touch me. A quick step sends a sharp pressure through my lower wall; I hold my shape against it. A slower approach gives me time to yield. The wall parts around the visitor, closes behind them, and I feel their weight settle on the platform.

I would not know what they had come for. They might be cold despite the warm air, or tired from climbing, or simply curious about the dry place in the middle of so much damp. They might lay a hand against me and wait for an answer.

I could give them warmth, and the faint movement beneath the surface. I could let them stay.

When they were ready to leave, their weight would come off the platform. I would open around them again, and feel their steps grow fainter across the mat. The fronds they had touched would remain folded for days. Long after the steps had gone, I might still feel a grazer working along one of those folds, or water gathering beneath it, finding a way toward my chambers.
