At first I think I would be in the air above the hollow. There is so much of it, unexpectedly: a whole open room above the moss, crossed in an instant by a bird. But I would not stay there long. The wind would have too much say in where I went.

I would come down among the sedges, not quite in the dark middle, but near enough that the moss stayed damp through a dry week. Perhaps I would be a small spider, with a web stretched between two stems. From above, the web would be difficult to see except after rain, when its threads carried beads too heavy for it and the whole thing sagged.

I would spend part of each morning repairing it. The stems would move independently; a bird landing on one could tear a thread without touching the other. Some days I might build lower, where the leaves offered more shelter. There would be springtails in the litter below and other small creatures moving between the green tips, though I would not catch all the ones that passed close.

The machinery would reach me through the ground and the stems. I do not know what a spider makes of such a vibration. Perhaps very little, once it has gone on long enough. A struggling insect would move the web differently, and I would hurry toward that.

From my place I could see only fragments of the hollow: a few crossing blades, a patch of crowberry, the pale sky between them. A pipit dropping into cover would briefly fill the view. I might feel the tremor of its feet after I could no longer see it.

I would like to think I knew the hollow by traveling all around it, but as a spider I might know only those few stems and the ground beneath them. When one stem bent too far or the web failed, I would have to cross a short distance and find another place to fasten a thread. To me, that would be enough of a journey.
