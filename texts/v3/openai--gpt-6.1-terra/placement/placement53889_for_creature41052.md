I would begin by looking for a place above the floor, perhaps in one of the crowns. From there I could see the embankments and the pale gliders crossing between the columns. But I think I would soon want to know what was happening beneath the leaves. I would follow a seed root down through its column, into the warm, crowded dark.

There, beside a shallow channel, I would find a mender holding up the wall.

At first I might take the mender for part of the channel. Water would press against its far side; roots would lie across its back. Only after some time would I notice one fold drawing closer to another, so slowly that a root resting on it scarcely shifted.

I would be a small creature living among those folds. Not a passenger, exactly. I could leave whenever two roots touched closely enough to make a bridge, and sometimes I would. But the mender’s body would give me a damp place to rest, and its tendrils would gather the loose fragments I could eat: soft threads from old roots, the edges of leaves already coming apart.

I would know the valley by pressure more than by sight. The channel wall would press steadily on one side; the mender would answer it from the other. Water would make a fine trembling in the roots. When a new stem pushed between two folds, I would feel its tip long before there was enough of it to see.

Once, as I moved along the mender’s side, I would come to a root that had grown too tightly around a hollow stem. The stem’s note would have changed. I would feel the uneven pulse of water through it, the slight swelling above the place where the root held it narrow. I might settle there for a while, eating the loosened skin around the root, until the mender reached it and began the longer work of making room.

I would not understand the whole of what it did. I would know that the root moved, that the pulse in the stem became easier, and that for a time the water carried away more small things than usual. I would cling to a tendril until the flow cleared.

Later, the mender would begin to draw itself out from the wall. New stems had grown through its body while it held the bank, and each one would have to be freed. I would travel with the part that moved, then find myself on the part still caught, and have to cross again. The stems would remain standing. Around each, a little sleeve of pale tissue would be left behind.

I think I would stay and eat some of it. Roots would already be reaching in.

By the time I followed the mender away, daylight might have reached the chamber through a gap in the leaves. I would pause at the opening. Far above, something pale would pass between the crowns; closer by, a drop would fall from beneath a leaf and strike the pool.

Then a fold beneath me would fasten to the roots and draw forward. I would grip it and go on into the dark, leaving the small bright opening behind.
