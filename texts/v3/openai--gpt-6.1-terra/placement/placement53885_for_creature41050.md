At first I might be mistaken for a seam in the floor: a pale length held between pale roots, moving so slowly that the strands across my back have time to settle.

I am a burrower, working just below the upper surface of the plateau. Ahead of me the felt is dense with grazed growth. I press my blunt head into it and send a tightening ring down my body. The roots part around me. When I relax, they close against my sides, and the little animals clinging there shift their hooked feet to keep from being scraped away.

I cannot take the whole passage with me. Behind my tail, the space I have made would narrow and disappear if I left it alone. I draw loose strands across it, one against another, and wet their crossing points with the secretion from my skin. In this cold it takes time to hold. I move on while it is still soft, leaving a passage that will last for a while, then a seam that may last much longer.

There is a grazer above me. I feel the rasp through the roots before I hear the knock of its plates. It works from side to side, taking the surface down without cutting the strands through. The rasp comes closer; fine pieces sift into my passage. The animals on my back lift their mouths into the disturbed water. One leaves me to pick at the new lining, moving from strand to strand behind my tail. It may catch me again where the passage bends, or it may find another moving body in the felt.

The grazer passes, and I continue toward a place where the roots give more readily. The floor here has been pressed flat not long ago. I can work through it, but the stems lie tight together, and I must turn aside twice before my head finds a gap. Far above, the grinding has stopped. A pressure comes through the bed, broad enough that I cannot tell where it begins. My passage trembles; several newly fastened strands slip apart.

Then a call travels up from the flank. Another answers from somewhere beyond the terrace. The roots carry both, and for a time the animals on my back hold still, their hooked feet sunk deep in my skin.

I wait. The pressure shifts away. A little water begins to move through the loosened passage, bringing fragments from the surface. When I turn back to repair it, I find one of the small creatures feeding at the broken crossing. I fasten a fresh strand beside it. It crawls onto the strand and stays there as I leave.

The passage will bend inward as the surrounding roots grow. In time it may become a sealed chamber, or another burrower may find the soft line I have made and open it again. I keep pushing, carrying the others with me into the dense living floor, while above us the grazers return and begin their slow work across the stems.
