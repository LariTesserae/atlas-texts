I live behind the second gauge, in the space between its brass case and the wall. There is just room to turn around if I draw my knees up. The wall is warm when the pumps have been running for a long time, and in winter I sleep with my back against it.

I am small, and pale from living out of the light. My hands have more joints than the keepers’ hands do, which is useful among the pipes. I can grip the threads of a screw with two fingers while reaching past it with another. The oldest keeper has seen one of my hands. She was tightening the bracket on the gauge, and I reached out to catch the nut before it fell into the water.

She stopped. I held the nut out to her. After a while she took it, and finished the work without looking behind the case again.

I did not always live here. Before the gauge was mounted, I slept in a seam in the pump-house wall. Water passed through it, bringing fine grit and sometimes the empty shells of the white animals. I kept the shells in a row on a ledge until the current took them. When the keepers fitted the second gauge, they opened the seam to lay its pipe. I came out to see what they had done and found that the new space was dry.

The gauge is not mine. I cannot make its needle rise or fall. A narrow tube runs into the wall behind it, and whatever pressure comes through that tube moves the mechanism. I have followed the tube as far as I can, but there is a bend too tight for me, even with my arms at my sides.

I do look after the mechanism. Condensation works its way under the rim of the glass, and the screws grow a little red fur. I rub this off with a scrap of cloth I found beneath the lamps. There is a tooth in the gearing that catches when the air is very cold. I can free it by pressing upward through a gap in the case. If I wait until the keeper comes, she may tap the glass, which works too, but the needle jumps and takes some time to settle.

From my place behind the gauge I can see a strip of the basin. When the water is high, the upper window appears there. I have to lean far out to see it properly, holding the bracket with one hand.

The figure in the room has a small lamp with a shade that tilts. Sometimes its light falls on the table and I can see objects arranged along the edge. I think one of them is a screw, longer than I am. Another might be a length of wire wound around a card. On some days the figure works with its back to the window, and I see only the movement of its elbows.

Once it placed a shallow dish on the table. Something in the dish moved. I watched until the pump clicked and the water trembled, and then the dish was only a pale oval again. I stayed at the bracket until my hands hurt, but the figure took it away.

I have tried to make myself visible. The first time, I stood on top of the gauge while the window was clear. I raised both arms. The figure moved toward the glass and I nearly lost my footing, but it was only opening the window. The curtain drew inward. I could not tell whether the figure had seen me.

After that I tried bringing out a bright washer the keepers had dropped. I held it in the lamplight and turned it slowly. Nothing happened. Later I used the washer to mend a loose place in my sleeping shelf, where it has been useful.

The oldest keeper still comes, though she lowers herself more carefully to read the gauges now. She puts her bag on the dry stone beside the pump house and rests a hand on the wall before stepping into the water. On her last visit the second needle had caught. She tapped the glass once, then waited. I pushed the tooth free, and the needle moved.

She wrote down the readings. Before leaving, she put a folded scrap of clean cloth on top of the gauge. It was too large for me to carry all at once. I tore off a strip and left the rest where it was.

That night the upper room was lit. I cleaned the rim of the gauge with the new cloth, then went out onto the bracket. The figure was at the window. It had cleared a patch in the condensation and was standing close behind it, with one hand resting on the sill.

I stayed there for a while. Water came through the grilles; a moth settled in the lamp above me. The figure moved away from the window and returned carrying the shallow dish. It set the dish down where the light fell into it.

I could not see what was inside. I watched until the reflection broke apart, and then until it gathered again.
