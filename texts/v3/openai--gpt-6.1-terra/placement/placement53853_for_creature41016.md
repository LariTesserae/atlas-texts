I would be on the wall opposite the mite’s fold, a narrow shadow with nothing yet to cast me.

At first I would take my place from the light: a dark line across a vein, tapering where the rind curves toward the ceiling. The vein would rise beneath me. Its movement would make a cracking sound so close that I could not tell whether the sound came from it or from the splitting of the leaf that was about to appear.

The leaf would lift beside me, pale at its edge. For a while we would be the same shape. Then the afternoon light would move, and its true shadow would fall a little behind me. There would be two of us on the wall until the leaf turned and covered us both.

I might live through several such vanishings. A scale would press me flat; a membrane would rise through me and collapse; a thread would stretch across the chamber and throw a line over my line. I would begin to know the plant by the forms it was preparing, though not always soon enough to understand what I saw. Sometimes I would spread into a broad, wavering patch and wait, and nothing would rise there for a long time.

The mite would cross me without pause. Its feet would make minute interruptions in the dark. Once I would lie along the seam where it had pressed the stone. As the seam opened, I would break into pieces on the lifting edges, and the mite would lower its head to feed. Later, when the plant closed over the grain, one small piece of me would remain visible between the folds. I would wait for a leaf there, but the seam would stay shut.

A cool patch would pass over the ceiling. In a bead above me I would see the chamber from somewhere beyond its wall: the rind folded around its clear space, the mite nearly invisible, and a mark on the inside surface so faint I could not be sure it was mine. The plant would drink the bead before I had looked long enough.

Then a deep note would move through the chamber. Leaves would tremble; the walls would pull farther apart. On the bright new surface I would find myself again, longer than before, with no vein under me yet.

The mite would emerge onto a thread and begin its slow crossing. I would wait on the far wall. By the time it reached me, a leaf might have grown there. The mite might step onto that instead, and I would move aside with the light.
