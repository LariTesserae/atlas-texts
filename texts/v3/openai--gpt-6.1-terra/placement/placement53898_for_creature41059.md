I think I would first find myself seated beside a pump, with my back against its casing and one hand in a cup of water.

There are larvae in the cup. I can feel them move when my finger comes near, though they are too small for me to make out their shapes by touch. I count them by drawing them gently against the side, but I am never sure of the number. If there are too many, I carry half of them to another cup. I keep the cups in a row along the wall, with a notch in the concrete beside each one so I can put them back in order.

This is not my main work. I tend the route between two pumping houses, testing the rope and replacing the oil marks where the water has washed them away. The cups are something I began keeping because the insects got into the housing. Now, when I arrive, I check them before I open my tool bag.

The route takes most of a day if the ground is firm. I know it by the distance between knots, the grain of the rope under my palm, and a place where the line dips through a shallow pool. There is a stone there with a sharp lip. I have cut my wrist on it twice, and after the second time I wrapped the rope with a strip of cloth to warn myself.

One morning I reach the cloth and find the stone beyond it, as usual. Then, five knots farther on, my tapping rod strikes the same lip. I kneel and feel it carefully. There is the narrow split, the rough underside, even the small shell lodged in the crack.

I go back to the first stone. The shell is there too.

At the next house I write this down. The worker there has noticed the water changing temperature near the discharge pipe, but only during certain strokes of the pump. We compare our records with our fingers on the raised marks, then sit together on the island for a while. The pump is running well. We can feel each stroke through the concrete.

I expect the route to be troublesome for a few weeks. Instead it stays that way through the warm-water rise. We leave the rope as it is, because both houses can still be reached, though not always on the first attempt. I add a jar of sharp-smelling oil at the turning before the stones. The scent helps me find the turning; it does not tell me which stone will come next.

After some months I begin to recognize an animal along that stretch. It is one of the heavy ones, an adult, and it feeds in the reeds near the line. At first I know it by the way its weight settles: one foot, a pause, then two close together. Later I find where it has rubbed a broad, smooth place on the stone beside the pool.

There is a young one with it. Its steps are quick and close-set, easy to miss when I am walking. I learn to stop when I feel the adult and wait for the smaller pressures before I continue.

One day the young animal is calling from beyond the second stone. Each stamp comes through the ground distinctly. The adult is behind me, answering with a slow shift of weight, but when I move back along the rope I feel that it has gone some distance away.

I wait beside the young one. I cannot tell how close it is until it moves and brushes the water against my boot. I put my tapping rod down so I will not strike it by accident. It stamps again, and after a time the adult answers. They keep this up while the pump beat travels faintly through the shelf.

I have a tank to deliver, and I worry about the worker waiting for it. I leave the tank beside the rope, with my oil jar tied to its handle, and go on to the house. The worker comes back with me carrying an empty tank, but by then the two animals are together. We feel them moving away through the mud, the quick steps following the slow ones.

We find my tank by its scent. On the way back, the worker asks me whether I touched the young animal.

I tell her I did not. I had thought about it, with the water moving around my boot, but I did not know whether my hand would help or frighten it.

She says there is slack in the line near that pool and suggests tying in a longer piece. If an animal becomes caught while the places are laid together, we might not be able to reach the right part of the rope to free it. On our next trip we bring a length between us and splice it in.

I think that is what I would become there: a worker with a particular stretch of rope to care for, known at both houses, entrusted with keys and tanks and records. Someone who keeps making small adjustments to a route that cannot be made dependable once and for all.

At the end of a journey I would put my hand against the pump casing until I could distinguish its beat from the pressure of my own pulse. Then I would check the cups. If one needed water, I would fill it; if the larvae had grown crowded, I would carry some to the empty cup at the end of the row.
