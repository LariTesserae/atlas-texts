I would first know myself as a vacancy crossing the white bed.

A grain rolls into me; I am beside it. Another settles, and I am farther on. From above, nothing much has happened. The surface remains level, and the ring of stones is still badly made. But I have crossed from the side nearest the dark weed to the side where the current brings its hardest blows.

I do not think I would begin there. I think I would begin with a crack.

A slab splits at the rim. For an instant its two faces have not yet struck anything else. The creature takes something from that instant and grows thicker. A line of brightness finds an older line beneath the grains. Somewhere in the bed a place is left unoccupied, and I find I can move through it.

At first I would take myself for part of the creature: one of the ways it feels across its surface. I would follow the shapes it holds, passing along the bright seams where one noise has met another. Some lead back into the same great low note, which I can feel through every grain but cannot travel beyond. Others end abruptly. I would wait at those ends for the mountain to make the sound again.

The jointed animals would teach me that there are other places to wait.

One comes down from the fins carrying a stone between its pale limbs. It puts the stone at the edge of the ring, draws back, and remains there through a surge. The stone tips. The animal catches it and tries again, turning it so that a narrow face meets the bottom. This time it stays.

A few grains have caught in the animal’s joints. As it lifts a limb, one of them leaves a vacancy at the edge of the bed, and I pass into it. The limb rises. For a moment I am above the ring, with nothing around me but the rushing water and that one grain held against a fold of flesh.

Then the grain comes loose. It falls very slowly. By the time it reaches the bed, the animal has vanished, and I am back among the others.

Afterward I would go to the edge whenever I felt one approaching. Most carried stones. Some carried their young, soft enough that the low note moved their limbs for them. When one slipped into the bed, I would feel a new shape spreading through it: not a crack or a blow, but many small hinges held close together. A limb sometimes rose among the grains days later. I would travel beneath it while it opened and closed.

I could not tell whether the young animal was still there. Once, when an adult returned and stood a long time at the ring, I brought the vacancy as close to its feet as I could. It touched the grit with the pale inside of a limb. The limb in the bed folded. The adult withdrew.

There would be long stretches when I could do very little. The hollow would draw in until the rim crowded the ring, then lengthen again while the slabs kept their pace. When white streaks appeared in the distant cracks, I could sometimes follow one out. The grains were sparse there, and I had to wait for each to settle before I could find another place. Beneath a sheltering animal I might reach a joint and feel it tense. Usually the grains withdrew when the animal moved, taking me with them.

Once they stayed. The animal brought them back on its underside, walking carefully through water that kept trying to turn it over. At the ring it laid its limb down and scraped the grains free against a stone. I entered the bed again carrying a faint impression of the crevice: the pressure of several bodies resting side by side, and one narrow place left open among them.

When the copper light came, I would feel the bed change beneath me. Grains lifted into a shallow mound facing the ridge, and the vacancies between them opened wider. I could move farther then, though there was nowhere to go that was not already part of the creature. The animals did not repair the ring. Stones went one by one, and the mound held.

Then the ridge appeared to descend. White grains fell from it, and the creature took them in. The quiet it held afterward was difficult for me too. I could still move, but the old cracks no longer showed their paths. I would come to the end of one and wait, unable to remember which way it had led.

In the crevices the animals waited as well. One remained near the ring, holding a stone against the bottom with both limbs. The current pushed at it repeatedly. Each time, the animal steadied the stone and stayed.

At last a grain trembled beside me. Another followed. The low note filled the bed again, and I crossed toward the edge, where the stone was still being held in place.
