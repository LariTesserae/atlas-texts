I live beneath the end of a street, in a chamber whose floor slopes gently toward the cistern. When I wake, I can tell whether the water has risen by stretching one small limb into the notch beside my sleeping place. Most mornings I touch dry stone. After long rain, I touch the surface and feel it give.

Our street ends at a ridge of pale paste. It has ended there since before I was hatched. The paving reaches the ridge and turns into two narrow channels, one on either side, so that water can pass around the unfinished ground. We keep the channels open. We replace the reed markers. We have a slab showing where the street will go.

I used to think the slab was a picture of something we had lost. The lines were so definite: the street, the turns into the houses, the little cistern at the far end. I asked one of the older keepers where those houses had gone. She took me aboveground and let me feel the wet ridge for myself. Then she placed my forelimb on the paving and walked me slowly along the imagined street, lifting me over the softest places.

I remember how awkwardly she held me up, and the cold on my underside. I remember less clearly where we were going.

As a young one I sorted wicker beneath the terrace. The good lengths bent without splitting and were set apart for frames; the brittle lengths went to the baskets where our food grew. I learned to find a flaw by drawing each reed through my smaller limbs. There were many I judged too quickly. When a keeper found one in the wrong bundle, she put it back among the unsorted reeds without telling me which it was.

Later I helped clear the drain that runs under the square. In dry weather I could crawl nearly its whole length, though there was one place where my back caught against the cover stone. The sound inside was immense. Water struck the bends ahead of me and behind me; the gutter note entered through the joints overhead. At first I could make out nothing else. Then, with practice, I began to feel the separate places where the covers rested on their supports, and the small loose tremor of a stone that needed setting again.

I liked that work. It gave me a way across the city even when the square was busy with wind. If someone tapped for a clearing crew at the far side, I could often tell which drain they were beside before the message had finished.

One year the ridge opened farther into our street. The change was small at first: water lingered in a joint that had always drained, and a pale film appeared along the edge of a slab. We lifted the slab and found paste underneath, cool and grainy. By the time we had carried the wicker frames up, a crack had crossed the place where the slab had stood.

We framed it as best we could. One side took the wicker neatly; the other kept spreading, very slowly, pressing the stakes out of line. We moved them twice. Afterward the household gathered around the plan slab belowground. I held a lamp while the oldest keeper scraped away part of the incised street and marked a new edge.

The houses at the far end no longer fit. She left their lines for the time being. We would know more when the paste had settled.

That was when I began keeping the messages across the square. The new seam interrupted them. A tap sent from our side reached the nearest household as a weak, confused movement, and sometimes it did not reach them at all. We tried a long slab across the opening, but the wet ground shifted beneath one end. We tried a bundle of reeds laid tightly together; that carried enough vibration to be useful for a few days, until rain loosened it.

Now there are two of us who carry messages around the seam. When I feel a call on the paving, I go to the edge, receive the taps, and walk the long way around by the open channel. I put my forelimb down on firm stone and send them onward. Most are ordinary: a request for reed, a warning that a drain cover is being lifted, news that a cistern keeper has tasted a change in the water. If I am unsure of a word, I go back and ask. The journey is short enough, and a mistaken message can travel much farther.

At dusk I sometimes stay aboveground after the work is done. I settle near the end of our street, with one limb on the old paving and another on the frame. The gutter holds its note. The frame moves almost imperceptibly as the paste bears against it.

There is no call I need to answer then. I can feel the work of the city through the stone: a mallet at a distance, footsteps crossing a drain, someone setting down a basket. When the wind falls quiet, the voices in the square become audible. Mine is among them occasionally, though I am more practiced at tapping.

The last time we examined the plan slab, the keeper asked me to hold the scraper. She showed me where the revised edge should go, then waited while I made the line. It wandered a little. We felt it together, and she showed me how to deepen the straightest part.

The houses beyond it are still there on the slab. The ridge has not finished changing, and neither have we.
