I would work where the rails end.

Not quite at the cutting face: the cutters need clear space around them, and there is little for me to do while the stone is still being lifted. I would follow a few days behind, with a covered lamp, a sounding plate, and shoes whose soles are broader than most. My work would be to find out when the new growth can bear a road.

From the older districts I might be mistaken for a surveyor. I would stand in the open a long time, looking down, then move a few paces and stand again. The carts would know me by the plate: a dull square of grown fiber that I carry against my back. When I set it on the young surface and step onto it, my weight spreads over a wider area. I can feel whether the film yields evenly or whether one corner sinks.

There are better instruments for measuring strength. The rail crews use them before they lay anything permanent. I come earlier, when the question is not how much the surface can bear, but where it has begun to join itself together. A dark patch may be thick and sound; another may only cover a hollow. I mark the uncertain places with little stakes cut from old roof ribs. Later I return and pull most of them up.

I live farther in, beside the cable works, in a room with one window and a wall that has to be trimmed twice a year. The window looks across a freight square. In the brighter hours I can see my neighbor leaving for the presses, though by the time I have recognized her she is usually too far away to call. She has a way of carrying her lunch tin with her wrist bent outward, as if it were still hot.

We sometimes walk home together. She asks whether the new road is ready, and I tell her which stretch I tested. She knows the places by their distance from the cable works; I know them by the seams in the rock. It takes us a while to establish that we are talking about the same stretch.

One morning, near the end of a dim shift, I find a grazer on the new surface. It has come farther out than any I have seen before. Its back is pale with dust, and two young ones are behind it, so close that I count them only when the adult moves.

The oldest growth here runs in narrow bands along the seams. Between them the films are nearly transparent. I have been stepping from one band to the next, using my plate where the distance is too great. The adult grazer crosses without trouble, its weight held on six broad feet. One youngster follows. The other stops with its front limbs on a film and its rear limbs on bare stone.

I wait. It lowers its mouth, rasps once, and draws back. The adult continues a short distance, then stops too. The young grazer tries again, this time turning toward a thicker band that I had not thought to test.

After they have passed, I put down the plate. The band holds. It runs around the edge of a shallow hollow and joins a strip I had already marked as sound. A road cannot follow it exactly—the carts need a gentler curve—but it tells me where to look for the next section.

At the end of the shift I leave three stakes instead of the seven I brought. On my way home I pass the cable works, where the sweepings have been set out in trays. My neighbor is coming across the square with her lunch tin. I wait under the window of our building until she gets close enough to hear me, and then I tell her that a road may be ready sooner than I thought.
