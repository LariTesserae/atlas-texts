At first I look for myself in the moving water. There is room there: the long sweep from the driving pipe to the wall, the returning curl, the sudden confusion when the third mouth changes its mind. A flake can pass through the chamber many times before it finds the sucking pipe. Perhaps I am one of those flakes.

But a flake has little to do except go. I think I would be somewhere the water passes *over*.

Behind the lamp cage, in a seam where metal meets mineral, there is a thin film. The shrimp brushes it sometimes on its way back to the crack. I would be part of that film: a gathering of minute bodies holding to one another and to the roughness beneath us. Not one body, exactly. The oldest of us may already be buried under newer growth; some have been torn away and carried out into the room.

The water brings what we need, though not evenly. Near the edge of the seam it comes quickly, and we grow there until the current takes the edge off. Deeper in, we are protected, but what reaches us has passed through many others first. I would live across that small difference. I would be the fresh growth and the buried layer, the place where the film thickens and the place where it has just been scoured bare.

The pumps would be difficult to distinguish as sound. They would arrive instead as changes in what holds and what pulls loose. Before the third pipe reverses, the trembling comes through the cage. Then the water turns and presses into the seam from the other side. Sometimes a strip of us lifts. The rest stretches with it for a moment before it breaks free.

The shrimp’s return is a larger disturbance. Its legs catch at the deposits, and its body swings close enough to darken the amber light. Once, working its way behind the cage, it might scrape away a patch of film. I would lose that part without knowing where it went. Later, the bare place would begin to be covered again.

When the water grows quiet, the shrimp comes out and its antennae drift apart. Grit settles on the cage, on the shrimp’s back, on us. Some of the grains lodge in the film and stay when the pumps start again. Others lift away.

I would have no view of the whole chamber. I would know that the flow had changed by the materials arriving at my edge, by the strain passing through the film, by what was left exposed when a piece of me went. The shrimp would remain close by, returning to the same shelter. In the quiet, its antenna might rest against the cage above me, and in the next rush of water it would lift and bend downstream.
