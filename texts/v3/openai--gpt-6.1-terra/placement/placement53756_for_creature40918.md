At first I would think I was one of the damp lines.

I would find myself stretched across the floor, following a curve around a ridge that had not risen yet. Behind me, a walker would come on its many legs, touching down carefully at each turn. When I stopped, it would stop too.

But I would not make the floor damp. I would darken it. The line would continue ahead of the walker, and I would remain beneath it, attached at the feet. Only when a grain fell from the ceiling and the walker paused would I discover that I could keep moving.

I would be its shadow.

This would not mean that I knew where the walker was going. The water would gather, and its legs would begin their small adjustments: touch, turn, touch. I would lengthen and shorten beneath it as the light changed. If another walker crossed our path, its shadow might pass through mine without either body coming near the other. I would feel nothing from the crossing, though afterward I might have trouble telling which darkness belonged to which animal.

The walker I belonged to would carry young for a while. In the brightest light I would be able to see their shadows under mine: small crowded shapes, constantly loosening and taking hold again. The walker would move more slowly with them, but it would not hesitate at the ridges. Its damp line would run nearly straight to the water, and I would go with it.

At the edge, the young would hang low enough that their shadows touched the shadows of the larvae. The hunter’s shadow would lie among them. I would watch it open without seeing the crease in the animal itself. Later, when the hunter had moved away, a little dry shape would remain, and my walker might pick it up and carry it to a seam. Its legs would straddle the closing place until the shape was held fast. Then it would go on, leaving the wall smooth behind it.

When the water disappeared, the young would drop away. Each would take a little darkness with it. One might stay close beneath the parent for a time, so that I kept mistaking its shadow for part of my own. Then it would turn toward a hair-thin damp line and follow it across the dry patch.

I think I would pay particular attention to the grain that fell from the ceiling. There might be only one in a long while, and I would never see it begin to fall. I would hear the tick and find the walker motionless above me. Its legs would still be planted; the young, if it had any, would be still as well. I would slip out from under one foot, then another, and reach toward the shelter in the corner.

Through the gap between the chips I would see the distant animals. Their shadows would move over a floor that continued farther than I could follow with my walker held still. Once, perhaps, a long-legged animal would come close enough to darken the gap. I would stretch toward it. Then the walker above me would take a step, and I would be drawn back beneath its body.

The walker’s line would not always reach the water. Once it would stop in an open place, with no ridge ahead and nothing visible in its way. The walker would wait. I could move a little around its feet, but not far enough to see what had ended the line. Other animals would pass at their different times. A larva would press its mouth to the floor where the water had been. One of the winged things would cross overhead, visible to me only as a quick, wavering darkness.

After the water vanished, I might find myself moving again in another part of the chamber. I would not know whether the walker had crossed the floor or whether we had simply begun elsewhere. The unfinished line would still be visible from a distance, a fine damp mark with nobody following it.

Near the ceiling the moss would point upward. A drop would form at its tip and rise into the stone. Below it, the larvae would gather as they always did.

My walker would touch the floor and turn a fraction. Ahead, a new line would appear. I would travel underneath, keeping pace as best I could, until the next grain fell.
