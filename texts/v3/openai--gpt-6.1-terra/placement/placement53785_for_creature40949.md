I would live below the crest, on a shelf where the sunlight has already turned green.

At first I might seem to be a shadow caught between two roots. Look closer and there is a rounded edge, dark emerald against the amber stone. A broad leaf presses against my other side, keeping it flat. I am one of the cushions, no larger than a hand.

My threads hold a little of the mountain. Grains gather among them: black flakes from a broken fin, pale mineral dust, a piece of a grazer’s path so thin it is nearly nothing. The threads grow around what stays. My outer surface is green; inside I am silver, close-packed and sheltered from the light.

The neighboring leaf was small when it first found the seam. For a long while there was room between us. Then its edge reached mine, and as it widened it pressed me back against the root. I continued to grow where I could, becoming round on the side that faced the open shelf. Now the leaf lies over part of me, and in that shade my threads hold grains that might otherwise have drifted away.

A grazer lives beneath it. I know the animal by its work before I meet it: a clear path appears overhead, then lengthens and turns beside a milky vein. Once the grazer comes down as far as my edge. It takes a few threads and leaves a small notch. Long afterward, the notch is still there, though its edges have softened.

I have no need to follow the grazer. There is so much arriving even here, where the water hardly moves. A bubble slips from the seam beneath my stone and passes through the leaf bed. The leaf lifts from me for a moment, and light reaches a place that has not received it for many seasons. The bubble touches a stalk, divides against a heavy cluster of leaves, and rises out of sight.

Above, the green stalks branch over me. Their roots run across the shelf, seeking cracks. One root has grown alongside me so slowly that some of my threads have taken hold on it. We are joined there, although the root continues on toward the stone.

I cannot see the whole ridge, but from my shelf a narrow opening faces the deeper water. Through it there is a pale leaf in a cleft, visible against rock I can no longer distinguish. Sometimes a swimmer holds still beside it, its body a fine line across the light. When it bends away, the leaf remains.

A plate breaks loose somewhere above me. The vibration passes into the stone and is gone. Much later, a few grains arrive and settle in the hollow at my rounded edge. My threads reach among them. Most of the grains will shift again; perhaps one will stay. If it does, that side of me can grow a little farther before it meets the leaf.
