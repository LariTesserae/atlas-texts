I would live near an inward fold of the escarpment, where an old root shelf projects just far enough to keep a little mist from the morning sun. From below, the place would be difficult to pick out. There is a pale seam along the cliff and, above it, a column with one limb shorter than the rest.

I would know the column by the sound its broken limb makes. In a steady wind it gives two low notes, the second rougher than the first. When the wind shifts, I sometimes mistake another column for it and have to stop and listen.

I travel, but I return to the fold. There is a hearth there, and a shallow bowl wedged in a crack above the reach of the morning damp. My sister made the bowl. It leaks more quickly each year, though if I fill it and drink without setting it down, enough water remains.

At dawn I go to the column with the bent digging stick and two bowls. The groove on its eastern side drips first. I can usually set a bowl beneath it before the climbers come down. The animals know me, or know the shape I make against the trunk; they watch from above and begin their rapid calls as soon as I put the bowl in place. I leave the second bowl farther away, where a thinner groove runs. If the first fills slowly, the second may have gathered enough to be worth carrying.

Once the climbers gather, I move back. They come down in little bursts, young held tight beneath their mothers, older animals reaching around one another for a place at the groove. Sometimes they throw bark. I have learned that even a small piece, dropped from high enough, can cut the skin.

When the sun reaches the trunk, I take what water I have and walk the short route east, looking for the scars left by people who passed before me. Some are my own. One old cut has closed almost entirely, but a line of pale leaves still runs through it. Another has widened where grazers fed at the edge; there I can lift the crust gently and collect a few of the animals in my hand. I press the leaves down afterward, though they do not always take.

I had thought, when I was younger, that I would cross the whole plateau. I still walk out beyond the columns I know, but I have begun to pay attention to smaller distances: how far a bowl can be carried before it empties through its weave, how long the shade lasts beneath the shelf, where a herd turns when the ground has been dry for too long.

One evening, after rain fell somewhere beyond sight, I feel a pulse through the hearthstone. I put my palm on the ground and wait. It comes again, farther off. The sound reaches me later, mixed with the wind.

I climb out of the fold. Across the plateau, trunks are opening one after another. Warm air streams from their slits; seeds catch the last light and then disappear. The climbers call in alarm, and something small strikes my cheek and falls into the crust. I cannot find it.

The pulses continue after dark. I return to the hearth and lie with my head near the stone, listening to them travel toward the escarpment. By morning the column above me will be closed again. I will take my sister’s bowl from its crack and go to the eastern groove, early enough, I hope, to drink before the climbers come down.
