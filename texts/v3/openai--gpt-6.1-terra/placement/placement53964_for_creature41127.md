I think I would begin on a roof, with a shallow tray in my hands.

A condenser tender has asked me to carry water from one of the pools to a workshop where someone wants to look at the branching film. I have been told to walk slowly and keep the tray level. The first part is easy; on the climb between the buildings I have to stop twice anyway. The second is harder. By the time I reach the workshop, the film has broken into flecks too small for us to follow.

The woman waiting there tips the water back and forth. Then she puts it aside and asks whether I noticed where in the pool the lines had begun.

I had not. I was watching my feet.

The next morning I go back. The tender lends me a stool and lets me sit beside the pool while she clears the channel above it. The center of the water is cool against the back of my finger. There are only two lines at first, so fine I lose them when the roof changes brightness. I draw them in a notebook, badly. When a bead of water falls from the condenser and breaks one apart, I draw that too.

After a few days the tender asks to see the pages. She points out a deposit she has been finding where the channel narrows. I had included it in each drawing without thinking it important. She scrapes it away and, for the next week, the water reaches the pool more slowly. We watch to see whether the film returns differently.

That seems to be how I find work in the city: staying with a question long enough that it begins to touch another one.

I carry notebooks between roofs and workshops, and sometimes down to the surveyors. At first I mostly copy things for people whose hands are occupied: temperatures, dates, the width of a seam, the times at which a pipe knocks. I learn to leave room on a page for somebody else’s correction. I learn that a measurement without its place and hour is often less useful than no measurement at all.

My room is above a wire-drawing shop, beside a stair that turns around a hot duct. In the morning the third step is comfortable to sit on. By afternoon I take my notes to the landing instead. The woman who works below leaves her door open when she is annealing wire, and sometimes calls me down to listen to a change in the pipes. Once I bring her three pages of intervals and she finds the trouble by touching a joint I have not mentioned.

“You can write that down,” she says.

So I do, along with the fact that the joint had been repaired twelve days earlier. Later, when the same knock comes back, we know where to look first.

On the unfinished streets I become acquainted with a surveyor who rests at the same bench every few days. I sit there too, catching my breath, and eventually she asks if I can hold the end of her marked rod. The work gives me a view across the whole shelf: roofs close together, a train moving so slowly into the yard that I notice its shadow before the train itself, and a cloud passing over the upper walls without quite reaching us.

One season the surveyor’s marks show that part of a foundation has shifted. The crew has already stacked blocks for its next course. They leave them where they are while the measurements are checked, and people begin using the stack as a meeting place. A child scratches a little railway into the dust on the top block. Someone sets a lunch bowl there; someone else leaves a tool. Weeks later, when the crew comes to move the stone, I help carry the smaller things away and copy the child’s railway into the margin of my book.

I go back to the pool whenever I can. The film does not appear to care that we are watching it. Some weeks it spreads almost to the edge; some weeks there is nothing. The tender and I keep disagreeing about what the slower flow has changed. We have enough observations now to argue properly, which pleases us both.

One evening, crossing the roof after a cloud has passed, I find a pale creature drinking at the drain. I stop well short of it. Behind me, through the wall, the workshop’s flywheel is turning; I can feel it faintly through the stone when I put down my hand. The creature finishes drinking and steps backward into the space beneath the tank.

I stay a little longer. Then I go downstairs to add the day’s measurements to the notebook, leaving the dry space under the tank clear as I found it.
