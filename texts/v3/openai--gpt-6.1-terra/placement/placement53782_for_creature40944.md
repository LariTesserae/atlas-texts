I might first take myself for a part of the wall.

There is a place beneath a shelf where the stone is rough and the bodies above me are almost always still. I rest there with my legs drawn in. A colony gathers on my back, so light that at first I feel it only when the gas changes and the little bodies adjust their folds together. They feed while I wait.

When I move, some remain attached. Others brush off against the animals I pass. I eat from a pale trace at the edge of a fissure, scraping carefully until the stone begins to show through. Then I turn aside and leave the rest.

I am one of the small plated grazers. My back bears a ridge where it healed badly after something gripped me from below. The ridge catches dust. I can work a grain down along its edge with one leg and bring it forward to the notch beside my mouth. Most grains fall away again. Now and then one is the right shape, and I carry it.

There is a curved partition on a warm shelf, not far from the fissure where I feed. It has been broken in two places. Behind it, pale eggs lie among fragments of the wall and the husks of others that have already opened. I cannot tell which eggs will hatch. I press my grain against the broken end, lower my head, and hold it there until it stays. Then I go back for another.

I am not alone in this work, though the others come and go. One presses a flat scale into the curve, making a surface I could not have made with my round grains. Later a soft-rimmed animal crosses the wall and knocks the scale loose. I find it on my return and push it back into place. By then another grazer has filled part of the gap I left.

A warning reaches me through the shelf: one body lifts, then the next. I grip the stone. The animal against my left edge shifts upward, and something long and jointed passes beneath it. I feel the hunter pause. Farther along the shelf, a grazer begins to scrape again, and the hunter moves toward that repeated pressure. I lose it among the other movements. After a while I carry another grain to the wall.

When the heat rises, I notice it first through my feet. The smallest bodies at the fissure loosen and spread. A soft-rimmed animal pulls over my back, and then another presses beneath me, lifting one side of my shell. I climb with them.

At the broken partition I catch on an egg. For a moment it rolls against my underside; then the animals behind me push forward, and it slips back among the others. I find a hold on the shelf above. Bodies pack around me, some gripping the stone, others gripping only their neighbors. We climb until the stone is cool enough to hold.

Below us, the exposed floor breathes out yellow dust. Some catches on my back. I wait while the pressure of the animals around me changes, while those nearest the vent begin to test their way down.

The partition is still there when I return, though its flat scale has fallen inward and half the curve is buried. I clear a little space with my mouthparts. An egg lies beneath the loose grains. I leave it where it is, find the scale, and push it toward the broken end.

Another grazer arrives carrying a grain. I hold still while it packs the grain beside the scale. When it moves away, the piece remains upright. I leave to find something that will brace it from the other side.
