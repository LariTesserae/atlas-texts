I think I would live at the dim end of the works, in a room made by closing off the space between two old frames. There would be just enough height to stand near the door. At the back, where the roof slopes, I would have a narrow bed and a shelf fixed to iron.

I would be one of the people who looks after the frames.

Not the engine or the belts; others know those better. I would carry a short knife, a mallet, and a length of wire bent into a hook. Much of my work would consist of finding iron that had disappeared. A doorway might have grown too narrow, or a roof might have settled, and before cutting or binding anything I would need to know what lay underneath.

The oldest people could often tell me. They would stand beside me with a thumb pressed to the wall and say, *There was a brace here once,* or, *Mind the water pocket.* Sometimes they would be right only about the water pocket. Then we would both get wet.

I would have a way of tapping along a wall and listening for the change in sound. Children would follow me for a little while, tapping too, until someone found a hollow that sounded promising and everyone wanted to put an ear against it. Once, behind a house, we would find a forgotten gate hinge entirely enclosed in root. The gate itself would be gone. I would leave the hinge where it was, after cutting a small opening around it so it could be seen. For a few days the children would bring people to look.

There would be a mark beside every frame I had worked on: a short piece of cord tied to the nearest exposed iron. Green for a new binding, pale for one that had held through a season. This would not be a perfect system. The cord would weather, and roots would grow over the knots. I would still need to walk the town and put my hands on things.

In the mornings I would help open the works. One hinge would always be slower than the other, and I would watch the roof lift to see whether the gap between them had changed. Then, while the rollers began their soft knocking, I would take my tools out into the square.

People would stop me with small requests. A shelf had tilted. A latch had risen just enough that the door would no longer catch. Someone had dropped a knife between two runners and could see the handle but not get hold of it. I would try the hook, then borrow another from a house nearby, because mine was too thick. The person who had dropped the knife would crouch beside me and offer advice. When we got it out, the blade would be clean and cool.

I imagine being useful in this way without being particularly important. If I were ill, somebody else would cut back the doorways. The town has lasted too long to depend on one pair of hands. But I would know, for instance, that the iron beside the north window of one house ended sooner than it appeared to, and that the woman who lived there liked the little leaves left along the top of the frame. I would cut them back only when they began to shade her table.

At the end of the day I would return through the works, past the spools standing under the eaves. The new ones would still be green. I would put my tools on the shelf and lie down for a while without lighting anything.

From my bed I could hear the roof settling after it closed. A hinge would give a small knock, then another much later. Sometimes I would think I ought to get up and make a note of it. Usually I would wait to hear whether it happened again.

By morning there might be a line of damp along the frame above me, and leaves opening there, small enough that the room had not yet changed shape around them.
