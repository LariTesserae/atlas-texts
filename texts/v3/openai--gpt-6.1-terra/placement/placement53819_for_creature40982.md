At first I think I would be the trembling in the water. It reaches everywhere: the bare floor, the grate, the undersides of the leaves. But it is the pumps’ doing, and when they stop, as one day they must, the trembling will stop with them.

I would rather be something that can remain.

Perhaps a fallen fern leaf, darkening at the edge of the chamber. It lies partly on the concrete and partly in the water, held there by roots. Its stem keeps its shape longer than the soft green tissue around it. The water passes through it, and little by little the leaf becomes easier for other things to enter.

I would be among those things: a film of bacteria on the leaf’s underside, so thin that the water still runs through me. Not a single creature looking out, but a patch of living cells, growing where food settles and where the leaf itself can be taken apart.

The flatworm comes close. Its body presses the leaf against the concrete; when it moves away, the leaf lifts only a little. A trail of mucus remains, and some of us grow over it. The worm may eat the small animals grazing on the film, or turn back under a root. I do not need it to know I am here.

As the leaf softens, pieces break loose. Some catch in the roots downstream. Some reach the grate. A little of me goes with each piece; more remains on the stem, on the concrete beneath it, on the fine roots that have grown through it.

The leaf above us browns in its turn. Light reaches a little farther under the fronds. For a while there is more room, until a new leaf unfolds and covers the place again.

By then there is almost nothing left of the first leaf to show where I began.
