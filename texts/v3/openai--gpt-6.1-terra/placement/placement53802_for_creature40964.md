I think I would be one of the builders, but not on the outer face where the water first enters. I would have settled farther down, where a passage divides around a low chamber. Water reaches me after it has already touched many mouths.

There is an advantage in that. The larger fragments have been taken or broken apart; what comes to me is fine enough to catch. There is a disadvantage, too. When the mouths above withdraw together, the current changes. For a little while my tentacles reach into almost nothing.

My sleeve bends. It began against the side of an older one, and as I added to its rim I had to grow around a protruding edge. My opening now faces across the passage rather than into it. A neighbor’s opening faces mine. When we both extend, our tentacles sometimes touch.

I don’t know whether I would distinguish that touch from food at first. I would draw in, close, wait. When I opened again, the neighbor might still be there, extended into the same water. Eventually that would be one of the shapes of the passage: the wall on one side, the small steady pull of water, and this other set of tentacles reaching almost to mine.

Above us, a grazer crosses the roofs. Its weight presses through the soft mineral. Several mouths close before it arrives, mine among them, and for a while I feel it pass overhead: a slow, broad pressure, then a scraping at the edge of my sleeve. When I come out again, light has reached a little farther down the wall. The cleaned strip will not remain clean.

A swimmer rests in the chamber below me. It comes and goes through a gap too narrow for the grazer, turning sideways where two sleeves nearly meet. When it pauses, I can feel the small disturbances it makes in the current. Once it brushes my tentacles, and I withdraw. Later it returns, or another does; I have no way to tell.

I add mineral to my rim. My neighbor does the same. The gap between our openings grows narrower, and the water that used to slip between us begins to pass above. For several feedings I extend toward the old current. Then a piece catches higher up, and I reach there instead.

Our sleeves meet. The join is soft for a time. A grain lodges in it, then another, and the next layer holds them both. What had been an open space becomes a little roof. The swimmer can still pass beneath it, though it must fold itself more tightly.

I would live there, I think: at the edge of that roof, with my mouth tilted toward a current made partly by my neighbors and partly by the work we have already done. The passage would not stay as I knew it. A new animal might settle above me; its sleeve might lengthen across my opening, leaving a tube through which I could still extend. I would reach into that smaller stream and gather what it carried.

And if one day the opening closed before I could reach it, water would remain around my body for a time. Above, beyond the new wall, other mouths would continue to open.
