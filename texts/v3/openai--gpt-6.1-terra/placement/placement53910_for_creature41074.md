I live below the kelp, where a rounded shoulder of rock drops into a trough. There is a crack there, wider inside than it looks from the water. I can draw all my arms into it, though the tips sometimes trail outside when I sleep.

I am an octopus. My den has two entrances: the one I use, and a narrow gap at the back that I have only once needed to squeeze through. In front lies a heap of shells, pale where I have cleaned them out. They roll and knock together when the tide runs hard. I have moved them aside more than once, and they gather again.

From the entrance I can reach a patch of mussels on the rock. The larger ones are difficult to pull free. When the current slackens I go farther, feeling under stones for crabs. My suckers find the fine edges of shell, the grit lodged in a joint, a leg drawn tight against a body. Sometimes the stone comes away in my arms and there is nothing underneath. Sometimes the crab has already slipped out the other side.

The kelp begins just above my den. At slack water its stems hang apart, and I can climb among them, putting an arm around one holdfast and then another. I keep close to the rock when a seal passes. Its body moves through the forest quickly; afterward the stems sway and fish come back through the gaps in small, uneven groups.

There is iron farther along the trough. I have explored the still parts: the packed mussels, a seam under the housing, the cable lying against the seabed. The vibration reaches my den on some tides. Close by, it comes through the metal into my arms. Fish shelter there, and crabs climb over the housing, but the moving blades leave stretches of water I do not cross. A loose kelp stem once went through while I clung behind a rock. Its broken length came tumbling past me.

After a hard run of tide, sand covers the front of my den and shells lie scattered down the slope. I push the sand out with my arms. Sometimes a fish has been left in the lee of a boulder, or a torn frond has brought small animals down from the shallows. I take what I can find and carry it back.

One season the crack grows too tight. I can still force myself inside, but turning around is slow, and my arms spill over the shell heap. I leave at dusk, moving along the base of the ridge with one arm extended into each dark opening. A crevice under a ledge is deep enough, though the entrance faces the current. Farther down I find a chamber behind two stones. I pull one stone closer and settle into the space behind it.

The new den is quieter. Sand lies on its floor, and there are worms in it. From here I can no longer reach the mussel patch, but crabs pass along the ledge above. The turbine’s vibration is faint; the stones in the channel are louder when they begin to roll.

I leave the old shells where they are. In time, something else may shelter among them.
