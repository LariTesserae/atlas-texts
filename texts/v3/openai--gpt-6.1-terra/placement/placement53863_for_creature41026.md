I would live on the sheltered ridge, in the third house from the end. Its doorway faces a split in the rock that funnels the wind away, so the curtain hangs almost still even when the roof weights are moving.

I would be a maker of needles, though not the only one. My mother taught me to split a grazer’s leg bone along its length, and I still use the flat stone she used for rubbing the pieces smooth. The stone has a groove in it now. When I work outdoors I put a scrap of dark wing membrane across my knees, so that I can see the bone and find it again if I drop it.

People bring me bones when they come back to the ridge. Sometimes they bring broken needles too, wrapped in leaf so the points will not work through a pocket. A broken needle can be made into an awl, or scraped down for a child’s first sewing. I keep even the smallest pieces until I have decided what they might be good for.

On a bright day I can sit outside the house with my work. I can see the rain crossing the lower basin from there, though it may be a long time before it reaches us, if it reaches us at all. The people coming up from that direction usually arrive wet at the shoulders. We ask them what the air is doing beyond the bend, where the ridge blocks our view.

One morning I would find a round door opening beside the path: a grazer had pushed through its little lid of ice. I might set a loop there, but first I would notice that the path felt soft under one foot. The mat had risen a finger’s width from the rock. Farther out, the same edge ran toward the pool, where we had stitched it the previous season.

I would fetch a hooked pole and a second person. Together we could test how much had come loose. If only the path was lifting, we could pull it back and weight it. But if the knots beneath the pool had begun to let go, we would have to move the water covers and warn anyone coming home across that side of the ridge.

It would take most of the day. We would kneel at the edge, work the hook beneath it, and pull carefully enough to feel each knot release. The grazer would disappear underground. Once, a broad shadow would pass over us; we would look up and wait until it had crossed.

By the time we finished, the pool might be half-bare and its surface beginning to freeze. We would lay the cover back over the water and anchor it to stone. Then we would go inside, bringing the loose section with us. Some of it would be too damaged to use, but the green-edged blades near the middle could still be worked into cord.

That evening I would sit beneath the black ceiling, splitting a bone while someone else softened the leaves. If the wind rose, we would stop to listen to the roof. In the pause I might hear the curtain brush the floor, and think of the open patch beside the path. Tomorrow I would go out and press a way around it. The little grazer’s door would be somewhere underneath, unless it had already made another.
