I think I would live near a crossing, where the street climbs the wall above the second basin. My room would be narrow at the entrance and wider at the back, with a hollow worn into the floor. The crossing would be a busy one: three routes meet there, and the ridges are always rising for somebody.

At first I might choose it for the company. Lying in my hollow, I could feel people pass through the threshold and know, from the weight and pace of them, which neighbors were awake. The seam beneath the crossing would carry other movements too: the small, quick adjustments of doors, the strain of a message going farther than the next room. I would learn to tell these apart, though perhaps not as readily as I believed.

I would be one of the people who attend the seams. Not the strongest or most skillful among them. My hands would be good at waiting on a junction until its regular movements became familiar. Then, when a strand began to pull loose, I might notice the interval that had changed.

One day I would find a flaw under the crossing: a faint catch whenever the ridges turned someone toward the upper rooms. I would send word along the street and settle down to wait for another worker. Meanwhile the crossing would keep trying to guide people, and I would feel it hesitate under them.

A child would come along carrying a folded skin of water. The ridges would lift too late. I would reach out, touch her wrist, and guide her over the uneven place.

She would ask what was wrong, pressing the question into my forearm. I would show her where to put her hands. She might feel nothing at first except the ordinary traffic of the seam. We would wait through a pressure pulse, flattened side by side on the warm street, the water skin tucked safely between us. Afterward the catch would come again, and her fingers would tighten against the stone.

The other worker would arrive with wedges and new strand. The child would stay until we needed room, then continue up the wall with her water. We would open the junction, brace the old material, and bind it. For a while the seam would strain against us. The crossing would send up a confused ridge beneath my knee; my companion would press an apology into the stone, although the crossing could not understand it. When we tested the repair, the ridge would rise smoothly from one end to the other.

That evening, in my room, I would feel the child passing again. She would stop outside and tap a quick pattern on the threshold: the uneven beat of the damaged junction. Then she would repeat it without the catch.

I think I would begin to collect such patterns. There would be the old door on the lower tier that opened in two stages, the basin gutter with its loose flake, the particular tremor of the woven screen at my neighbor’s entrance when the pressure changed. Some would disappear as we repaired them. Others would belong to the ordinary movement of the town and remain for years.

People might bring me patterns they wanted remembered. A worker could show me how a seam had felt before it was repaired; a child could imitate the steps of someone who had moved to another room. On a quiet interval we might gather around a stretch of stone and take turns making the movements, while the others followed them with their hands.

I would still work at the crossing. When the air changed and condensation was expected, I would help clear the gutter down to the basin. If a hunter had taken shelter there, I would wait for it to move or coax it gently past the comb. Then I would lie beside the junction, feeling for the familiar lift beneath me as people made their way home.
