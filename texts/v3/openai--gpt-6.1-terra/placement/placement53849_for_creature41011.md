My room would be above the pumping works, reached by the ladder whose third rung turns slightly under a foot. I would know to put my weight at its left end. Visitors would discover this for themselves, and afterward I would mean to tell the next person.

There is just enough room beside my bed for a shelf. On it I keep folded paper, a stub of pencil, and things that have not yet been thrown away: a bent washer, a lamp fitting with a cracked rim, a short length of copper pipe. The washer belongs to a repair I made badly. I kept it to remember its size, although the part it fitted has since been replaced.

I would be the person people bring troublesome little things to. Not the lamp itself, perhaps, because there is someone better at lamps, but the screw that will no longer hold its thread, or the drawer that sticks only when the masonry is wet. I make shims from scraps of metal and patterns from paper. Sometimes I can mend something; sometimes I carry it through three rooms asking who knows what it used to look like.

In the morning I would come down sideways past the washing and put my hand on the pump wall. I am not one of its keepers, but I have got used to its tremor. Once it seemed different under my palm, a slight pause followed by a stronger beat. I fetched a keeper, who stood there longer than I had and said it felt ordinary. We checked again that evening. It still felt ordinary to her, and by then it did to me too.

On days when there is work fine enough to need sunlight, I take it to the lane. The strip moves off whatever I am holding if I get absorbed. A child reading nearby has learned to warn me before it goes.

I have also made a paper gauge for the shoots in the southern wall. The resident who measures them asked for something with a notch, so the paper need not be pressed against the stem. The first gauge was too narrow and caught on a leaf. I made another, and we tried it on a piece of wire before taking it back to the wall.

The person who dislikes the measuring watched us that day. They held the lamp while we worked, though afterward they said we ought to leave the shoots alone for a month. We have agreed to wait two weeks. I put the gauge on my shelf, where the warm damp has begun to curl its corners.

At night I would lie with the window open to the lane. A drop might fall into a dish below, or someone might come up the ladder and find the loose rung. Through the floor the pump would go on turning. I think I would sleep well there, except when I had lent a tool and could not remember to whom.
