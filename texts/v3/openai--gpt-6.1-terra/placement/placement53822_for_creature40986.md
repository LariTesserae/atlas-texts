At first I would seem to be part of a tube: a pale seam running down its sheltered side, where it meets the rock. Black grains collect along me. A grazer passing above might put a foot on my back without finding enough purchase to know I am there.

I am a grazer too, one of the flat, many-legged kind. My body fits into places the broader animals cannot enter, and my mouth works sideways against the stone. I live on the thin growth at the edge of a warm fracture. When I have scraped a patch clean, I move on, and by the time I return a new film has begun to hold the grains.

The tube above me belongs to an old animal. Its base is buried in red crust, and higher up it wears several collars of its own making, each wider than the one below. I can reach the underside of the lowest collar, where the water moves gently and pale grains settle. There is a good surface there to grip when the mountain knocks.

I know the tube animal by the timing of its plates. It opens slowly. Its neighbor opens sooner and takes up more room, so that often the old one must fold partway before it has fully spread. When the neighbor closes, the old one opens again. The movement passes through its shell and into the narrow space beneath it. I stop scraping while the plates settle; a falling piece of grit can trap a leg.

Farther along the crack, the water is warmer. The stone there is newly broken and still pale under its black deposit. Other grazers have found it, including the high-backed ones. They cannot follow me under the tube, but on open stone they can push me aside. Sometimes I climb over them. Their hinged backs make smooth bridges, and beneath their edges their legs move steadily as they feed.

There is also a dark surface beyond the warmest opening. It looks much like the rock around it. I have crossed its edge more than once, scraping as I went, and turned back when the surface shifted under my feet. A grazer with a broken rear leg went farther. The pale limb that unfolded around it was so thin at first that I saw the black grains through it.

Afterward I kept to the tube for a long while. The hunter remained where it was. Its edge had settled into a slightly different shape around the base of a shell.

The great reports stop everything. I grip with all my legs, even those I have just lifted to walk. The tube animal closes, and the sound of its plates goes through me after the sound from the mountain has already passed. For a time the seep continues to hum through the stone, and nothing touches my back.

Then one animal opens. It may be some distance away, but its neighbor makes room, and that movement reaches the next. Eventually the old tube animal begins to spread its plates. I feel it through the rock beneath my feet and let go with my front legs.

One day the crack that feeds us will narrow. The red collars are growing over it from both sides. The black deposit will become thin, and the high-backed grazers will leave first because they can travel over exposed rock more easily than I can. I will remain while there is still something to scrape beneath the tube.

If the stone opens nearby, I may reach the new edge by way of a seam between shells. The first film will already be there, though too thin for me to see. I will find it by touch, put my mouth against the fresh surface, and begin.
