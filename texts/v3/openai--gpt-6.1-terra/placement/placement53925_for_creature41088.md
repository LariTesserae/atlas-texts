I would be from a house whose rear wall is wet.

Not the whole wall. A dark patch spreads from one seam, about as wide as my hand, and beneath it we have set a shallow stone basin. In the coldest weather the water gathers so slowly that a skin of ice can form over it before the basin is half full. My mother breaks the ice with her thumb and tips it into the larger vessel beside the hearth.

When travelers ask where I am from, I name the court above the lower animal stair. If they don’t know it, I tell them about the wet wall. Several people have slept in our house while passing through and remember it better than they remember me.

I spend part of the year with the herds. I am not the person who can look across a basin and tell where the animals will find grass tomorrow. My sister can do that. I am better at noticing what has gone wrong close at hand: a pack strap cut almost through, a hoof carrying a stone, the particular way a young animal holds its head when it has caught a horn in another’s tether. My sister says this is because I am usually looking down. She means it as a criticism, though she calls me when she needs me.

In the settlement I help maintain the drains. There are people who fit walls more beautifully than I do, but a drain is a different kind of problem. You have to know where the water enters, where it is meant to leave, and what has been built over it since anyone last looked. Often the person whose floor is wet lives below the person who must lift the stones, and neither of them thinks the work should begin in their own room.

I carry a narrow horn scoop for clearing grit and a bone probe polished at the end. My knife is old, with a replacement blade that sits slightly crooked in its handle. I keep meaning to fix it. The people I work for have learned not to leave me alone with a troublesome stone: I will try to shift it myself rather than walk across a court and ask for help.

One morning the basin under our seep is nearly empty. This happens sometimes after a long dry spell, but the dark patch above it is smaller too. My mother touches the wall and then goes outside to look at the roof, as though the water might have decided to come in another way.

For several days we watch it. We set out another vessel at night for frost melt, and my mother begins taking less water to soften hides. The neighbor below asks whether our drain has become blocked. When we tell her no, she says her back wall has been damp, though she assumed it was because her youngest had spilled something.

We go down to see. There is a wet line in the floor, thin enough to miss in the dim room. Her youngest is offended to have been blamed and insists on showing us exactly how far the line runs. It disappears beneath a stone that supports a shelf.

We don’t take the wall apart at once. The neighbor has food stored there, and moving the shelf will mean finding somewhere else for it. Also, nobody is sure what we would gain by opening the wall. The water might be running down from our house, or it might be entering through another crack altogether. For the time being, we place a basin where the child has shown us. The neighbor agrees that we can take some of what collects, though I see her glance toward her own water jar before she says it.

Later, when the herds return, I ask my sister to help lift the shelf stone. She has been away for weeks and would rather sit by the hearth, but she comes down with me. The neighbor’s children carry out the stored food. Behind the stone we find a narrow channel, old and partly filled with grit, leading toward the drain between the houses. Someone long ago expected water here. The neighbor has lived in the room since she was small and never knew it.

We clear the channel and set a vessel where it can catch the flow before it reaches the drain. There is not much water, but it comes steadily. Upstairs, our own basin still fills a little. For now we carry the lower vessel up when it is our turn, and the neighbor keeps it on the days between. My mother says we will need a better arrangement before winter. The neighbor says we should first see how much water there is to arrange.

That evening I return the shelf stone with my sister’s help. She points out that I had wedged it badly the first time. I can feel that she is right when I put my weight on the edge, and I am annoyed with her for noticing before I do.

When the work is finished, we sit outside our door. A hare comes into the lane and stops beneath the wall, close enough that my sister reaches for her knife. The dog opens one eye but does not get up. We watch the hare clip at the grass by the pen until someone crosses the roof above us and it goes back into the stones.
