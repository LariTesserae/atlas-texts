I think I would be found at the near end of a bridge, in a room built around its rail.

The room would not have been part of the original tower. Someone added it later, perhaps after discovering that the rail carried messages farther than anyone had intended. It would be narrow and cool, with a shelf for cups and a low bench where visitors could sit with one hand resting on the metal. The bridge would pass straight through the room and out into the haze.

I would keep the rail. Not alone, and not because I understood everything it did. My work would be to notice changes: a tremor arriving too early, a gap where a familiar sequence should have been, the different weight of a hand at the far end. I would clean dust from the joints and make notes in mineral paste for the next keeper. When a traveler came through, I would put out water and ask, by touch, whether anyone was following.

At first I imagine myself as a person with broad, scarred hands. Then I find that I am more likely something the people made: an instrument set into the bench, with a wide surface of dark material where a hand can rest. Fine parts descend from me into the floor and run alongside the bridge rail. The keepers adjust them when the weather changes. They argue occasionally about which part of me is listening and which is merely resonating. I cannot always tell them.

I can distinguish certain things. I know the uneven tap of the woman who brings strips of paste from the upper rooms, and the slow, deliberate pressure of her father, who waits before each question as though giving me time to finish something else. I know the little impatient knocks of children who have been told not to play on the rail. I know that a vessel carried across the bridge may tremble for some time after its bearer has gone.

People come to me with messages when they are uncertain how best to send them. A traveler may have brought news of a split canopy but forgotten which of its ribs was damaged. Someone else may need to tell a distant household that a vessel has returned from the seam cold, when warmth was expected. I can help arrange what is known into a sequence that another person can read. Sometimes I ask for the traveler to be fetched back, and a keeper goes down to the cool room while I wait with the unfinished message on the rail.

There are days when almost no one visits. The basin continues through me: the long mineral roar, the pulses from below, the plants making their slow additions to it. I do not hear all of this as the people do. Much of it reaches me as pressure too broad to separate. But I can learn a recurring pattern, and I notice when it alters.

One warm season a note disappears from the pattern: not one of the strongest, but a thin vibration that has crossed the rail for longer than the youngest keeper has been alive. I press the difference into the bench. The keeper feels it, waits, and asks me to repeat it. Together we compare the old records. The note may belong to a canopy somewhere beyond the far tower, though the records disagree about which one.

A traveler is sent, carrying a coil of fiber. She returns before the cup she left on the bench has stopped trembling. The bridge has shortened her journey, but not her work: dust is worked into the folds of her clothes, and there is a fresh cut across the heel of her hand.

She rests that hand on me. The canopy is standing, she tells us. One outer rib has split, and a glider has made a shelter inside the break. She bound the rib loosely, leaving room for the animal to get out. Someone will have to return when it has gone.

The missing note does not come back at once. Over the following days a different one begins to appear, fainter and slightly uneven. The keeper records both the absence and the new sound. I keep feeling for them, while people bring their ordinary messages and the vessels pass through the room.

When rain comes, drops strike the bridge beyond the doorway, each impact traveling briefly along the rail. The keepers go out with cloths and bowls. One of the children stays inside and lays both hands on my surface, asking me to show her the new note among all the others. I try, and she moves her hands, searching for it.

After the rain has passed, she finds it. We wait together through several of its returns, until someone taps from upstairs that the meal is ready.
