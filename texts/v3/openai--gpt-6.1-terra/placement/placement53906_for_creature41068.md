I would be under the bridge when it begins to move.

The traffic above presses it down in little pulses: ore carts, then a pause, then the lighter, quicker feet of animals going the other way. I am wedged between the doubled struts with a strand of wire in my jaws. My middle feet hold the underside; the others search for purchase as I bend the wire around a loosened joint.

The first shiver travels through my hooks before it reaches the traffic. I let go of the wire. For a moment I stay where I am, feeling the joint work against my back. Then the whole bridge dips, and I climb down a cable with the others.

On the storage ledge we wait among the coils. One of my hooks is caught in a frayed end. Another animal pulls at it with its jaws until the end comes free. Above us the bridge is still carrying traffic. A cart has stopped halfway across, and animals bunch behind it, their feelers passing rapidly over the backs ahead.

A flake comes down from high on the wall. It strikes the far end of the bridge and breaks into pieces small enough to pass between the struts. The bridge holds. The lane beyond it does not.

When we return, the wire I left at the joint is gone. The cart has tipped against the rail, and its load has spilled through. Below, animals are already collecting the ore from the broken stone. One of them climbs over a still body to reach a bright grain.

We take the bridge apart from the damaged end. I carry a short length of rail to the ledge and come back for another. By the time the first support is fastened into the newly exposed wall, dust has settled on our backs. A furnace worker arrives with a strip of soft metal. We draw it through the holes in the support while she braces it with her forelegs; then she leaves, and we hammer the strip flat against the stone.

The new crossing is lower. To reach it, traffic must pass close to a grease channel where animals lie in the dark. At first the carriers hesitate there. They touch the rail, touch one another, and turn back toward the old approach. We shift the guide wire until it brushes their backs as they pass. After a while they follow it without stopping.

I stay beneath the crossing as the carts begin to move again. The new support clicks each time a load passes. I can feel the click through the rail before I hear it. It is small, but it comes at the same point each time.

I work my way toward it. The opening between the struts narrows around my back; one plate catches, then slips through. At the support I find a grain of stone trapped under the flattened metal. I loosen it with my jaws and push it out into the gap. A cart crosses overhead. This time the support holds still.

I remain there through another cart, and another. Then I back out between the struts, carrying the grain in my jaws. On the ledge I set it beside the coils. It is soft enough to eat, and I have been working for a long time.
