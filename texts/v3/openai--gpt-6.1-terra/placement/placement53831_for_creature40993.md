I think I would live in one of the lowest houses, where the wall has grown a long hollow for storing cable. It was there before I arrived. The person who made it must have kept their reels standing on edge, because the hollow is narrow and curves upward at both ends. I keep mine flat, so they spill out onto the floor.

My work would be to inspect the lines lowered toward the leaves. Mostly this means sitting with a length of cable across my lap, passing it slowly through my hands. A broken fiber catches against the pads of my fingers. I mark the place with a loose knot of colored thread, then go back and splice it. When I find damage close to the end of a line, I cut the end away. I save the pieces, though I have more than I need.

The work is quiet enough that I can feel the factory through the floor. Three turns, a pause, three turns. Some mornings the earlier rhythm is so faint I have to rest my cheek against the wall to find it. The wall cools my face. It has begun to make a shallow place for me there, although I have never asked it to.

From my window I can see one of the gaps between the leaves. The pale material below has risen high enough that, when the air moves, a leaf tip brushes it. At first I thought the leaf was growing downward. I tied a thread to its tip and watched the other end against a mark on the wall. The thread stayed level. The stone rose.

I told the people above me, and they came down with their children. We took turns looking through the window. One child said the material was yellow, another said it was the color of a mouse’s belly. Their mother asked what I thought. I said I would need to see a mouse.

That night I checked the foundation knots and laid out the long cable we would use to lift the house. I found a place for each coil where it could run freely. One reel was too close to the wall; I moved it and left the space clear.

In the morning there was another reel in that space. It belonged to my neighbor, who had heard me working and thought I might need more cable. She had left it without waking me.

For a while I would think of myself as someone who mends lines: useful when something frays, less useful when it holds. But the neighbors begin to bring me other things. A shoe tie that has pulled apart. A child’s practice vine, worn smooth at one end. Once, a little sling in which someone had carried a bird with an injured wing. The bird was gone by then, and the sling did not need mending. Its owner only wanted to know whether it was strong enough to keep.

I tested the seams and said it was. She took it home.

At the far end of our root, a new house appears. I feel the extra weight before anyone tells me. It is slight, and at first I mistake it for a person standing very still on the road. I put my hand against the wall and wait. The weight remains through the factory’s pause.

My neighbor and I take water and a small reel of finished cable. We hang our shoes outside the new house and wait for its window to open. Through the rind comes a soft, intermittent pressure, as if someone inside is moving their belongings from one place to another.

The window opens a little way. No one is behind it. We leave the water within reach and set the reel beside it. My neighbor goes back first. I stay to tie the reel so it cannot roll away.

While I am doing this, something taps twice on the other side of the wall. I put down the cable and touch the rind with my fingertips. After a time it taps again, farther to the left.

I do not know whether it is a person or the house adjusting around an empty room. I tap back anyway, gently, and wait with my shoes hanging beside me.
