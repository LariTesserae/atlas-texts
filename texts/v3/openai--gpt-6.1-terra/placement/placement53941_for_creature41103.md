I am under the crushed conduit, in a pocket between the slab and the broken lip of a door. The pocket is only a little larger than I am. Water forces its way through it with every change in the current, and the stone has polished one side of me smooth.

My hard center is not attached to anything. I am held in place by the threads I have grown through the cracks. Some lead toward the turbine casing in the intact station. Others climb through the fallen roof of the station beside it. I did not grow all of these threads myself. Some were laid down before my center hardened, and I can feel how much older they are by the number of times they have branched and sealed.

The turbine reaches me through four paths. Along one, the beat is sharp and close. Along another, it comes softened by a length of cable that moves in the plants. The difference lets me hold a shape for the space around me, although I have never extended into much of it. When the cable catches on a stone, the shape changes. When it comes loose, it changes back.

The other station gives me no such steady beat. Its roof shifts against the floor; water enters through the break and presses against the inner walls. I have a thread fastened to a metal frame there. Each time the frame moves, I feel the stone above it take some of its weight. Recently the movement has grown smaller. I have been extending another thread toward the frame, fastening it a little at a time to the underside of the slab.

Sometimes the threads from the intact station draw together, and I answer them. My surface tightens; I pass the pull along the branches that are still joined. I can feel it taken up farther out, among the cables and plant stems. At other times the pull comes from the collapsed roof, and I answer in that direction. Between the two, there are intervals in which I neither tighten nor release. Water passes over me. The turbine continues to turn.

During one of those intervals, a blow arrived through the thread on the metal frame. It was strong enough to press my smooth side against the stone. I waited for it to reach me by the other paths. It did not.

Later, while I was answering the intact station, the same blow came through the turbine casing, the cable, and the stone beneath me. The metal frame had fallen. The thread I had been extending toward it was severed close to the tip.

For some time I kept the severed end curled against me. The frame might have fallen in several directions; I could not tell where it had come to rest. A pull from the collapsed station opened the curled end again, and it began to branch. One branch found the frame farther down, wedged between stones. It no longer moved when the roof shifted.

The slab above me is separating from the lip of the door. I feel the widening most clearly through the older threads: their many branches pull against one another. Through a newer thread, the gap is already wide, and water passes freely. I have withdrawn a little from that place, but not completely. The current through it could carry a growing end into the rooms beyond the door.

Today the two stations are close. I can feel both sets of threads pulling without being stretched thin. A cord from the collapsed roof has come to lie against one leading to the turbine, and their outer fibers have caught. I keep them together. When the turbine beats, the cord from the roof carries a faint part of it back toward the fallen frame.

I am growing across the point where they touch. The tissue there is still soft. If the stations draw apart, it may tear before the older cords do. If they remain close, it may thicken enough to hold. For now, each revolution changes its shape, and in the small pause between revolutions I extend it a little farther.
