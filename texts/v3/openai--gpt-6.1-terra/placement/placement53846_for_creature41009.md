At first I think I would be in one of the deeper pools, where the sediment is fine enough to cover anything that stays still. But the place I keep returning to is the narrow strip between two pools: a dark seam in the mineral earth, damp even when the water has drawn back.

I would be one of the animals crossing it.

From above I might look like a scrap of pale leaf pressed flat against the ground. My front would already have reached the next pool while my hindmost limbs were still in the first. Each limb would find its own purchase. Some would hold me in place while others moved forward; a few would remain tucked against my body, taking no part for a while.

The earth along the seam would be warm. I would feel its grains and the slight drag of water among them. Where the channel widened, I might stop, not because I had arrived anywhere, but because there was a softened piece of leaf within reach. By the time I had eaten, a thin line of water would have gathered in the track behind me.

I would carry young beneath my fringe. Their grip would be light, and I imagine I could feel it change as I crossed the uneven ground: one letting go and fastening itself again, another hanging on through a stretch where my body lifted slightly over a grain of stone. In the pool, they would leave me one at a time. They might settle beside my flank, or wander a short distance and rest there.

The pool ahead would hold the reflection of a tree. Perhaps its leaves would be furled although the afternoon leaves above it had opened fully. I don’t know what that difference would be to me. I would turn toward the reflected crown, and remain beneath it while my young loosened their hold and the sediment settled around us.

Later, when the reflection changed, I would move again. From the rim, anyone watching might have trouble seeing that I had moved at all. But a root tip would be gone, and the little ones would be lying farther apart, each with a clear patch of water around it.
