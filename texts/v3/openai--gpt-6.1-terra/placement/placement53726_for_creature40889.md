I would be a snail on the underside of a stone shelf, where the seep comes out.

From above, the shelf would look like part of the broad pale back of the plain. Underneath, its edge is rough. Sand has lodged in the pits, and a thin green film grows wherever the light reaches. I would graze along that edge, my mouth working over the stone while my foot holds fast.

There are others here. Sometimes I come upon a shell before I reach the animal beneath it: a hard curve against my soft body. I turn aside, or continue over it. By morning the green skin is marked with tracks, though I do not know which are mine.

The seep changes slowly. In dry weather the water beyond the shelf becomes warmer and shallower. The fish pass closer over my shell, stirring silt that settles again. More snails gather below the falling waterline. We graze the stone until there are pale patches between us, and I must travel farther to find the film intact.

A bird’s foot comes down near the edge. Mud clouds the water. Something touches my shell and lifts it; I draw in, then fall. For a while I lie on my side in the silt. When I come out, the stone is not beneath me. I cross the bottom until my foot finds it again, and climb.

Later there is rain. At first it strikes the pool in separate drops; then the water above me moves with their weight. Silt lifts from the hollows. The shallow water spreads out beyond the shelf, over ground where there has been no water for some time. Fish pass across it.

I follow the stone upward as the water rises. Its exposed face has been rinsed clean in places, but in a hollow there is a fresh growth of green. I begin to feed there. By the time the water falls again, I may be farther down the shelf, or under a different stone. The marks I leave will last until something grazes them, or silt covers them, or the green grows back.
