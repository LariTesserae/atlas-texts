I am lying across a doorway when the house begins to leave the square.

My sister wakes me by pressing her heel against my shoulder. The floor has lifted her mat at one end, and her little water garden is sliding toward me, its pinned leaf leaking a dark line. I catch it against my chest. She takes out the pins one by one, folds the leaf tighter, and asks whether I felt the roots turn.

I did not. I was asleep.

She makes the sign for *good* with two fingers, meaning I needed the sleep. Then she makes it again, meaning I am getting old.

Outside, the square has narrowed. The red flower has opened overnight, and people are already standing at the entrances with their vessels. From where I sit, I can see the shine on its inner wall. Our house is being carried toward it, though so slowly that the nearest root tips have time to curl over my toes and withdraw.

My sister wants to move the mats before the house meets the crowd. I want to find out whether it will stop. We settle this by moving one mat and leaving the other.

I take my cord and weight from the peg. The weight is a piece of an old cooking pot, worn smooth where my hand has held it. I have carried it through six districts, though the pot itself traveled farther. It broke when our mother’s room closed around it, and for a long time she kept the pieces because she thought she might fit them together again. My sister used one to scrape the roots from the floor. I took another when I began walking the opening streets. There may still be a piece under the mat we have left behind.

People call me when a house goes missing. Not because I am especially good at finding houses, but because I will walk a long way with someone who has lost one. Sometimes the street has gone past it and left it intact, with the water garden still pinned to the wall. Sometimes we find a mat caught high in a fold, or a wall with the household’s growth marks stretched so thin they are almost impossible to read. I carry charcoal for copying the marks onto my arm. A person can be tired and still recognize the shape of a line they made themselves.

At the flower, the floor is crowded with bare feet and vessels. A man holds an ill child at the edge of the red chamber. I cannot hear what he says to her, but he bends his head to hers and she puts out her hand. The wall meets her palm. For a little while they remain like that, the child and the flower pressing against one another, while water drops from the leaves above into the vessels.

A woman behind me has brought a wide bowl and cannot get it through the crowd. She asks me, hand open, if I will take it forward. I hold up two fingers: I can try. We pass it between us, then over the shoulders of those in front. It tips once and strikes a man’s head. He looks back angrily, sees the bowl, and guides it on.

My cord has begun to lean against my leg. I lift the weight and let it hang. The cord slants toward a narrow place between two stems, though the air is still enough that the strips hanging from people’s belts scarcely move.

I touch the woman’s shoulder and point. She looks where I am pointing and makes the sign for *water*. I shake my head. Then she feels it through the floor: a small, quick yielding beneath us, followed by a firmness farther ahead.

People start turning. Someone raises a strip overhead, calling others close; someone else waves one low. The two signals cross in the air. A few people crouch with their hands over their heads, waiting for a fall.

But the stems are drawing apart.

The opening comes through the square without disturbing the flower. It runs between the father and the people behind him, under the bowl being passed overhead. The hands holding the bowl separate. For an instant I think it will fall; then someone on the other side catches its rim.

The street reaches me. I put my weight on both feet and feel it go through.

I see our doorway ahead, beyond the flower, with my sister sitting in it. I see the same doorway behind her, opening into our room, and on its floor the mat we left in place. My mother is there, bending over the broken pot. She has gathered the pieces in her lap. One is missing.

Then I am standing in the square again, my hand closed so tightly around the weight that its edge has marked my palm.

The father and child are still beside the flower. He is looking back at the opened street. The child has both hands on the red wall now, and he pulls her gently away so that others can reach it. She protests; I can see her mouth working, though the sound is lost in a series of splits traveling across the plain.

The woman’s bowl has come back half full. She holds it steady while I drink from my cupped hand, then drinks herself. We pass the rest forward to the father for the child, but another vessel has already reached them. He points toward an older woman sitting against the stems, and the bowl goes to her instead.

The new street is growing walls. A roof folds slowly across its far end. I could follow it now, before the rooms close and the marks on their walls stretch beyond reading. I stand for a while with my cord in my hand.

Then I go back to the house. It has moved only a short distance. My sister is outside, pulling the remaining mat through the doorway. A root has grown through its corner, and she has had to cut around it.

I help her spread the mat in a dry place. Under it we find the other piece of the pot. She puts it in my hand beside the weight, and we try their edges together. They do not fit.
