I would first take the narrow place behind the basin for my home: the joint where the overflow passes under the rim. There is always water moving there, and the stone carries news from every part of the settlement.

But it is too exposed for sleeping, and too wet for the thread. I would live farther in, beneath a roof flake whose edge can be lifted from below. I would be one of the builders.

In the sleeping cell I would wake against the others, uncertain for a moment which arms were mine. Then someone would move, and we would separate enough to pass. I would go to the tool cell, feel for the drilled shard, and bring it out to the basin. Another builder would follow with the thread.

We would lay it along the lip, holding it clear of the water. The level would be where it was before. We would touch the rim together to mark the result, then take the thread back inside. There would be other work: a joint to scrape, a wedge to turn over, a roof that gave a little when pressed.

I think I would spend much of my time listening at the houses nearest the basin. Not listening for words in the drops—they come too hard and too close together—but for what the drops strike. The basin has a different voice when a loose thing lies against it.

One day I would feel that difference through the wall. I would wait for another drop, then another. Each report would catch briefly on a higher note.

I would tap the stone beside me. A neighbor would put a hand against it from the other side. We would wait together until the note came again.

On the rim we would find a thin flake, one of those we had worked and left there. Perhaps we had set it too close; perhaps a vibration had edged it toward the water. My neighbor would hold my arm while I reached for it. A drop would strike the basin, and my face feelers would flatten. When I had the flake, we would carry it inside and test its edge against the wall.

It might still be useful. We would set it with the other chips.

At the threshold a hinge-back would be feeding on the scraps left there. Through its plates we could see its young shifting. We would wait until it had moved into the joint, then go in after it, each to a different task.

From above, I doubt our work would be visible. The basin would still be full, the bead still hanging between the paving stones. If a hand lifted the roof flake, it might find us pressed together in the dark, holding the stone while the last report of a drop passed through it.
