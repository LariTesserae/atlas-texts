I would begin under a flake of crust, in the damp groove where the rock keeps a little water after the rest has drained away. At first there would be too little of me to feel the grinding below. I would know it only by what it brought: grains shaken loose, a thin wash of warmer water, then the pressure of the flake settling back against the stone.

I would branch along the groove. The branches that reached dry air would stiffen and break; those that found water would go on, taking dark iron into themselves. Around me the silver grains would rust. Nothing in that small place would tell me how wide the flats were, or that there was a sky.

One day the crust would lift under a passing body. Water would get beneath it and carry some of my threads into a pool. They would bend around one another in the current. I might come to rest in another seam, or be taken into the mouth of a basker.

Inside the animal there would be folds enough for me to lodge in. Its pumping would draw water past me in heavy surges, rich with grains. The walls of its chambers would tighten and relax around my branches. I would grow there, losing some parts whenever the animal forced water out, extending others into the fine wet creases where the flow was slower.

From outside, the basker would look much like its neighbors: dark ridges, pale nodules, a mouth ring that opened when the water was rich. Perhaps one ridge would develop a rusty stain. The stain would spread, be covered by ice, and show again when the animal shifted.

I would come to know the others through their pressure. A neighbor leaning against the basker would flatten one side of its chamber. A small creature settling into a fold on its back would make a faint, repeated tapping, too quick to be part of the great breath. When the bank began to pulse, the folds around me would close so firmly that water ceased to pass. Then they would open, and a rush would bring grains from farther away.

Somewhere in that rush there might be another piece of me. It would not need to return. It could catch in the cartilage of another mouth, branch there, and send its own fine ends out into the water. I would be in several baskers by then, though I would not always know it. Their separate rhythms would travel through the rock and water; sometimes, when the bank pulsed together, those rhythms would meet.

A hunter might grip one of them beneath the ice. The basker would continue pumping while its body was held, drawing the hunter’s disturbed water through its chambers. When the skin finally tore, I would be exposed with everything else inside. The smallest baskers would gather at the opening. Some of my threads would go into their transparent jaws; others would sink with fragments of cartilage into the metallic drift.

The drift would be cold after the chamber. A thread caught there might lie still for a long while, until a limb scraped the bottom and stirred the grains. It might be carried under a plate of ice and left against a rock, close enough to a warm seam to begin branching again.

When the cloud descended, I would have no way to see the flats disappear. I would feel the changed weight of water in the seams and, through several bodies, the pumping continue. Afterward, a basker that had held part of me might be heard far off. The rock beneath my newest branches would still be the same rock, worn shallow and nearly level, but the water passing over it would come from another pool.

I would grow toward the warmth where I found it. Above me, ice would bridge the animals’ backs and break in small clicks as they breathed.
