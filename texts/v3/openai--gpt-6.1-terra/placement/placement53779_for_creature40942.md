At first I think I would live in one of the dark hollows, among the pale roots, where the water keeps its shape for weeks. From there the plateau would be a ceiling: leaves, crossing stems, the undersides of droplets.

But the hollow is too still even for me. I would want to see the light move.

So I think I am a cup-dweller at the edge of the nearer tower. My flower grows where the workers have cleared a little space around a glass panel. There is enough sun to warm the outside of the cup, and enough shade from the vine that its water lasts. From the rim I can see one length of cable and, when the foliage shifts apart, a portion of the pale stone beyond.

In the morning I climb down inside the stem. The walls press against my sides. I drink, then climb back up and arrange myself around the cup, with my tail near the water. The flower and I warm together. By the time the first carrier comes into view, I have turned enough to face the cable.

I know the carriers by their shadows. Each one crosses the leaves slowly, bringing a brief dimness that lets the transparent insects move. I have learned to wait beneath a particular leaf, though sometimes I am too early, and sometimes the shadow passes over an empty place. When I catch an insect I carry it back to the flower. I do not need many.

Another cup-dweller lives several flowers away. We can see each other when we both climb to the rims. I show two pale marks; it covers its own. On another day it opens a white patch and keeps it open while I edge along the stem between us. I stop before reaching its flower. We remain that way until the sun moves and the narrow strip of light between our cups disappears.

Later, when the flowers are deeper, I find it beside me at dusk. There is room for both of us if we fold our limbs closely. Our tails hang into the water, and through the night I feel each small adjustment it makes. In the morning we climb down the stem one after the other.

The workers come along the maintenance gap. I see them before they reach the tower: two pale shapes appearing in pieces between leaves. One stands by the panel while the other unfolds a shade. The first reaches toward my flower, and I press myself against its inner wall. The hand comes around the cup and lifts me out, supporting my belly and tail. It places me in a neighboring flower, where I can see the panel opened and the worker’s covered arms moving inside.

The work takes a long time. The shade changes angle; a carrier arrives and waits on the cable. At last the panel closes. The worker returns me to my cup, though not facing the direction I was before. I climb to the rim and turn myself.

A leaf has been removed. Through the new opening I can see farther down the valley, to a terrace of copper shrubs and, beyond it, the white trunks. The gap is bright for several days. Then a vine begins to spread across it, one leaf overlapping another.

I stay where I am. I know the morning route to water, the leaf where insects pass, and the flowers near enough to reach. Through the narrowing gap I watch the white trunks disappear one by one.
