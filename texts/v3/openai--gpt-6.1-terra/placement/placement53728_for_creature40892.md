I live in the moving film inside a reed’s root chamber. When the valve opens, water presses me flat against the wall. When it closes, I lift my front end and draw the fine growths toward my mouth. There are hundreds of us along the current’s path. We leave narrow clean streaks where we feed, and by the next day the streaks have grown over.

At first I know the basin only as water. It brings torn root hairs, grit, the shed skins of tunnel animals. Sometimes a grazer pulls at a cord and the chamber stretches around us. We hold on until the pressure eases. Once, a long-legged bird’s bill enters through the wall; its tip opens beneath us and a piece of the chamber comes away. I am on the piece. It drops back into the water and sinks into the mat, and I spend a long time finding another chamber.

This one has birds in it—or above it. Their nests are fastened among the stems. I feel their landings as small irregular taps, quite different from the wooden closing of the valves. When the young hatch, there are many more taps, then the scratch of feet as they grow.

The stand comes loose in a storm. The chamber rolls, and the current reverses, and some of the animals beside me are swept away. For a time I cling to a fold in the wall while water pours through a tear. Each turn of the stand presses the tear shut; each turn opens it again.

Afterward the chamber lies on its side. The valves still knock, but water comes in carrying green fragments and the soft white growth from beneath the mat. The birds return. One lands so heavily that the whole chamber shifts, and a thread of daylight enters through the tear.

I make my way toward it. Outside is not open air but a shallow space between the root bundle and the ground. Rain runs through it. I can see the underside of the mat, pale and densely veined, and beyond it a strip of grey water. A bird’s feet stand in the water for a moment, then lift away. Much later, as I am feeding on the exposed edge of the tear, their reflection passes across the strip.

The stand moves a little each day. New cords press into the ground and draw it forward. The chamber slowly rights itself, and the tear begins to narrow. Others gather there to feed on its growing edges. I stay with them, facing into the water when the valve opens, turning when the current changes.

By evening the birds have settled. Their weight bends the stems above us. The knocking slows, and in the longer pauses I can feel movement in the ground: an animal passing through a water-filled passage, close enough to lift the chamber and set it gently down again.
