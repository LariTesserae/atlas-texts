I would be working near the unfinished arches, though at first you might take me for part of a frame. I have spent so many bright hours holding its corners while the floor shifts beneath it that the leaves along my arms have grown in straight, overlapping strips. When I lower them, fine powder falls from the joints.

The frame belongs around the shadow of a room. Today the room is long and narrow, with the outline of a leaf lying across one end. Yesterday it was almost square. We have moved the frame three times this season, and one of its teeth is bent from being lifted out of a seam.

I used to think my work was to keep the frame in the right place. That was before I learned how slowly a shadow can change. I would set the teeth carefully, stand back to inspect them, and find that the doorway had moved while I was looking at the opposite wall. Now I watch for a while before touching anything. Sometimes the outline returns to where it was. Sometimes it goes on moving, and I call the others.

The older worker sits near the first ridge of wall. I know the sound of its plates: two close taps, then a pause while it gathers itself for another. It does not call often. When it does, I leave the frame and cross to it, keeping my shadow clear of its feet.

It asks me to feel the ridge. There is a thickening just below the surface, too slight to see. I put my hand there and wait. The wall presses against my palm, or perhaps my hand has settled farther into the powder. The older worker lets me wait until I am certain.

We put a little more powdered leaf along the ridge. It spills between our fingers. Mine is striped at the joints with several colors: gray from the press hall, pale yellow from the column where I was made, and a dark green I picked up while sleeping for a season among the old galleries. The older worker’s powder is nearly all the color of this basin. When we feed the wall together, the grains lie mixed for a moment before they disappear into it.

Farther inside the arches, a press is making panels for floors that have not yet been laid. One comes out with a vein broken near its edge. I carry it to a quiet place beyond the machinery and set powder along the break. The panel curls slightly under my hand. I leave it there, propped on two small blocks so that its underside can receive light. If it roots, someone may eventually have to cut it free of the blocks; I should probably have chosen a better place.

On my way back, I hear shell animals ticking inside a column. I tap near the base and crouch to listen. Their answers travel upward, then sideways into a gallery that has grown too dense for me to enter. I have been trying to guide them toward a chamber with poor circulation. The channel I made is clear, but the animals have found a pocket of dead fiber in the other direction. I can hear them working there instead. I leave them to it and make the channel a little wider.

By the time I return to the arches, the other workers are leaving. The older one is still beside the wall. Its hands rest on its knees, and powder has gathered in the seams around its feet. I stand nearby for a while without calling. Then it taps that the wall is taller.

It is. Not by much, but enough that I can hook the bent tooth of the frame over it while I straighten the tooth. The older worker watches me work. When I have finished, it taps that I should leave the frame where it is for another bright hour. The doorway’s shadow has begun to drift again.

A silver front appears across the basin. The undersides of the floor leaves lift in a line so distant that the line seems still. Then the columns nearest us whiten, and the light passes over our hands.

The older worker turns toward it. For a moment the ridge looks far beyond us, a low line under the arches. I reach out and find it where it was. The worker puts its hand beside mine.

After the leaves settle, we stay there a little longer. Then I help it to its feet. We follow the seams toward its sleeping column, stopping once so it can clear powder from a joint. Behind us, the frame stands around the shadow, and the small wall continues to grow.
