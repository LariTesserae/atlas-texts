I would find myself on the iron leg of a walkway, just below the waterline. At first I might take the dark, rising surface for a cliff. Then a boot would come down on the boards above, and the whole cliff would tremble.

I would be a snail, small enough to shelter in a pit in the iron. My shell would be pale under its coating of silt. A younger snail might travel on it for a while, scraping at the film that grows there, until it found something better and let go. I would not know where it went.

The leg would offer a good meal. Bacterial film covers the submerged iron, and the water brings fine particles to lodge in its roughness. I would move over it slowly, leaving a clean arc behind me. Shrimp would graze the same surface, working quickly around my foot; occasionally one would brush my tentacles and make me draw them in.

Through the iron I would feel the pump. Its pounding would reach the soft part of me that holds to the leg. Footsteps would be different: a weight arriving and leaving, then arriving farther away. When workers stopped overhead, grit sometimes sifted between the boards. Once, a bolt fell beside me, struck the leg, and vanished into the mud. Shrimp gathered where it had gone in.

I could go down from the iron onto the floor. There the mud would hold the track of my foot for a little while, unless a fish passed close or a shrimp worked across it. Worms would sweep the water nearby, then withdraw so suddenly that the place they occupied became a scatter of holes. A fish might nose into the silt and pass over me, stirring enough mud to settle on my shell.

Or I could go up. The iron would grow wetter and then less wet, until only a thin skin of water covered it. Above that I would need to wait for spray or a leak from the roof. At the edge of my reach there would be green film, richer than the pale growth below, washed down from a lamp on its cable.

Over many seasons the bottom of the leg would disappear. Silt would collect around it, and the place where iron met mud would rise. I might feed on a patch today and, much later, pass over it buried beneath me. The bolt would be somewhere under that new surface too.

From a distance, the walkway leg might look like one more piece of abandoned machinery standing in a flooded chamber. Close to it, I would know the grooves, the films, the sheltered side where the fish passed less often. The pump would go on beating through it. I would keep moving, leaving a clean trail that the water slowly covered again.
