I live at the edge of a bellows colony, where the last of the great bodies presses against a bank of broken stone. There is a fold there that stays open when the chambers contract. I can enter it even carrying a sheet of salt.

I am one of the heavy-jawed animals. My back is low and plated with short, stiff hairs. My forefeet are broad enough to brace a piece of salt while I break it, and my teeth meet farther forward than my eyes. When I am young, I try to carry pieces larger than I am. They catch on the lip of the fold, and I pull until they snap. Later I learn to bite them into strips first.

The fold is not where I sleep. It is where I work, though I do not know it as work. There is salt there, and the taste of it sets my mouth moving. Some pieces I eat at once. Others I drag into the fronds, following a route that has become smooth beneath my belly. I have several caches. The nearest is under a root bent into a shallow arch; the farthest is beyond a patch of blue-green cushions, where the ground rises and the fronds grow apart from one another.

I did not make that farthest cache. I found it one season by smell, when its owner was no longer there. The salt was damp and had hardened into a single lump. I ate a little, buried it again, and began adding my own pieces. The fronds above it have broad leaves that overlap like scales. Rain gathers on them and runs down their joints into the ground.

From the colony I know the valley by vibration. The bellows nearest my fold contracts a little after the one above it. When both are working, a small stone beside the entrance turns in place. I have seen it do this for so many seasons that I notice its absence before I notice the silence.

When the vanes close, I stop wherever I am. If I have time, I get beneath something: a root, a lip of rock, the side of a dead bellow. The air above the colony whitens. Sometimes there is only a flash deep in the vapor, and sometimes the discharge strikes stone and throws fragments across the valley. Once it struck close enough to split a frond trunk. Its clear segments lay scattered for years, gradually clouding and sinking into the moss.

Afterward I wait for the stone to turn.

One year the bellow beside my fold weakens. Its contraction comes late, then not at all. Its neighbors continue to move, pressing against it and drawing away. At first little changes for me. Salt still falls into the fold, and I still squeeze between the bodies to retrieve it.

Then the dead animal’s surface begins to crack. I pull at one edge with my teeth and uncover a hollow filled with fine red dust. The dust makes me sneeze. Behind it there are pale flakes, and behind those a chamber with a wall thin enough to break.

I enlarge the opening over several seasons. The chamber is dry and sheltered from the wind. I carry hair into it when I can find hair, usually caught on fronds along the lowbacks’ path. The black travelers sometimes come down from the herd to collect it too. They are quicker than I am. We avoid one another, though once one follows me to the entrance and reaches in after I have gone inside. I bite its foot, and it pulls away.

I raise young in the chamber. They are born with soft jaws and spend their first days pressed into the hair. When they begin to follow me, I leave small pieces just outside the entrance. They lick them, push them about, and eventually learn to hold them against the stone. One carries its first strip toward the colony rather than away from it. I follow as far as the fold. It drops the strip there and returns to the hollow without it.

The hollow changes even while we live in it. Young bellows settle in the damp stone below, their small vanes appearing one season where there were none before. Their bodies thicken. One presses upward through the floor of the old chamber, and I move the nest toward the outer wall. A few years later that wall splits wide enough to admit the wind. By then my young have gone elsewhere, and I sleep beneath the bank again.

The old entrance remains useful. Salt gathers around it, and the young bellows leave narrow spaces between their bodies where I can reach pieces other carriers cannot. I still take some to the cache beneath the broad-leaved fronds. The root arch has grown lower, and now I have to turn my head sideways to pass under it.

From there, on clear days, I can see a section of the herd crossing the far side of the valley. The lowbacks move slowly enough that their backs seem level and continuous, but their legs appear and disappear beneath them. Sometimes a dark calf shows between the adults. The calls reach me after I have seen their mouths open.

When the herd has passed, loose hairs remain on the fronds. I pull them free and carry them home beneath my belly.
