I would be a frog beneath the middle pump.

At first I might think of myself as belonging to the dark: a flattened body in the clearance under the plinth, eyes near the level of the wet concrete. Dust would cling to my belly and legs. Above me the pump would run and run, its weight passing into the plinth and through the floor. I would know the loose guard by its quicker trembling, though I would have no reason to distinguish it from the rest of the machine.

The darkness would not be complete. Beyond my shelter I could see a strip of floor, brown in the lamplight, and the edge of a puddle broken continually by drops from a pipe. Sometimes a gecko would cross the wall above it. Sometimes a pigeon would land and stand for a moment on pink feet before lowering its head to drink.

At night I would come out to hunt. The lamps would still be lit, but the air beyond the yard might be a little cooler, and fewer feet would cross the concrete. A moth could fall near the pump and beat its wings on the wet floor. I would move toward it, stop, then move again. If I caught it, the wings might continue to flutter briefly at the corners of my mouth.

I would not spend all my time under the middle pump. There is another shelter beneath a pipe bracket near the wall, and a shallow basin between the plinths that I could cross in a few kicks. Once I had found these places, the yard would be more than the patch visible from my first hiding place. It would be a set of distances: how far to the wall, how far across open concrete, how long a large shadow takes to pass.

A worker’s boot would come down near me one day, sending a sheet of puddle water over my back. I would jump before the foot lifted again. From beneath the plinth I would watch the worker kneel beside the housing, set down a grease gun and put a hand against the green metal. The hand would stay there for a while. Then the worker would rise and go.

Afterward, the boot print would slowly fill. I might come out and sit beside it, its edges still sharp in the thin film of grit. Water would drip from the pipe into the older puddle; the new one would tremble without being struck.

By then I would have dust on my skin again. I would wait where I could reach the shelter, facing the open floor and whatever might fall there.
