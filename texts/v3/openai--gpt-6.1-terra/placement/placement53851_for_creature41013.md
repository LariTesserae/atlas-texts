I would be easiest to find at the roofless shelter, though not always inside it.

There is a seam just beyond the doorway where rainwater leaves the floor and runs out between two fins. I return there after showers. Part of me gathers in the seam; another part reaches the shallow hollows inside, where the shelter’s shadow crosses them. In the late afternoon, when the sun has moved far enough, the hollow nearest the wall holds water longest. I have left three stone chips in it. One makes the water deeper. The others I put there before I understood that the water was lasting because of the shade.

I have not taken them away.

From the seam I can sometimes reach a dish on the next shelf. It reflects the ridge above the shelter, bringing the pale slope close enough that a dark edge of me may collect beneath it. The first few times this happened, I followed that edge as far as I could. I thought I had found a way to the ridge. But the dish dried, and the part beneath the ridge withdrew without ever having crossed the intervening stone.

Now I use the dish to see when rain has reached the upper slopes. The ridge darkens before the drops arrive at the shelter. There is usually time to move the hammerstone out of its niche and onto the wet floor.

I did not build the shelter. I have helped repair the wall beside its doorway. A slab there leans outward, and every hard shower washes grit from beneath it. Once, with another of us holding the wedge in its crack, I shifted the hammerstone toward it in small movements whenever the reflected floor drew near. We struck the wedge twice that afternoon. On the third attempt the stone rolled into the doorway and stayed there until a later rain brought enough water across the threshold to move it back.

The slab still leans. But it has not fallen, and grit has gathered again beneath its lower edge.

Others come to the shelter, especially after a dry interval. I can recognize one by the way it pauses in the third hollow even when there is water in the fourth. Another spreads out beneath the wall and remains there, so still that I have sometimes gone on working without knowing it was present. We share the hollows without needing to divide them. When the light moves, our shadows draw across one another and collect farther along the floor.

In heavy rain we go to the nearest ring. The fins outside seem to crowd forward and withdraw as their reflections shake in the pools. Inside, they stay where they are. I cannot reach the shelter from there, though I can see its doorway between two fins. I wait with the others, close enough that a movement at one edge may belong to any of us.

When the rain eases, we leave separately. I follow the runnel down to the seam and enter the shelter. Water will be passing over the floor, carrying pale grains around the hammerstone. The chips may have shifted in their hollow; once, one was gone altogether. I found it the next day against the threshold and brought it back during a shower.

Eventually the floor dries. The hollow nearest the wall becomes a film, then a dark mark. I narrow into the seam outside, where water remains between the stones after it has disappeared from view. From there I can no longer reach the dish or the wall, and the other inhabitants pass beyond me.

I stay. Some afternoons a drop falls from the doorway and travels almost to the seam before stopping. I extend toward it when I can. More often I wait for the sound on the fins to change, for the first hard drops to strike the shelter floor, and for the narrow line beside me to fill.
