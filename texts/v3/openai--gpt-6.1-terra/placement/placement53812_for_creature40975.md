I would live at the end of the staircase, where the last step meets the blank seam. Not on the stair itself: there the current sweeps too cleanly. Just beneath it is a shallow hollow, partly roofed by ceramic and partly by the tight sheaf of weed.

I would be a tube-builder, small enough that a fish might swallow me without slowing. My body would be soft and pale, with a foot fixed deep in the seam. The tube around me would be made of grains caught from the water and held together with a glue I secrete. Its oldest grains would be dark, taken from the depths; near the mouth there would be a few pale, rounded flecks of bolt.

I would put out a crown of fine threads to feed. They would open in the flow and catch whatever brushed against them. From inside the tube I could draw each thread across my mouth, clean it, and send it out again. Most of what arrived would be too small to make out. Once in a while a whole grazer would land on the crown, and I would have to fold the threads and let it crawl away.

The chamber would come to me through my tube. A fish passing close would make a quick pressure against the open crown. The pumps would be a constant trembling in the grains around my body. Through the ceramic I would feel the loose plate’s knock, and through the seam, the slower, irregular pulls of the weed roots as its leaves turned.

When I was young, I would keep the crown open too long. The reversal would catch it and turn the tube mouth inside out, peeling away a few grains. Afterward I would remain withdrawn while food moved past in the thick water. I would feed again once the flow had steadied, and spend some of what I gathered repairing the rim.

As I grew, I might come to close at the change in the pumping, just before the water turned. The crown would fold into the tube. The weed above me would sweep across the hollow, and grit would strike the new rim. When the worst of the rush had passed, I would open one thread, then the others, into the cloud of food.

There would be other tubes nearby, though none joined to mine. Their crowns would appear and disappear at different times. A fish sheltering beneath the stairs might brush across several of us, leaving a row of bare tube mouths behind it. We would open again after it moved off, the nearest first.

The seam would slowly change. A root would thicken beside my foot and press against the lower part of my tube. I would add grains higher up, building the mouth around the root’s curve. One season, perhaps, the root would split the old tube open, exposing the pale chambers I had abandoned as I grew. A grazer would pass through them, scraping.

I would remain attached to the same place. Afternoon light would reach my crown only in pieces, slipping through the moving leaves. At each reversal the current would bring unfamiliar particles up to the stair. I would take some into my mouth, fasten others to the rim, and let the rest pass.
