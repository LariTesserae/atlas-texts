I would be under one of the leaves when the water rises.

At first I might seem to be part of the leaf’s shadow: a pale jointed body held close to the ceramic, six legs finding purchase in the shallow irregularities. The leaf trembles above me. A droplet grows at its tip, and every tremor travels through it until the weight is too much. The droplet falls some distance away. I feel it as a brief, round disturbance in the floor.

I am a grazer, not long out of my skin. My old casing is caught against the root beside me, open along the back. The current lifts its edges and lays them down again. I remain near it because the new surface of my body is soft, and because the hollow beside the root holds water even when the flow is thin.

There is food here. I reach out, test the film with my front limbs, and draw my mouth across it. Behind me the ceramic shows through, a dark line in the green. Another grazer crosses that line and pauses. Its feet touch where mine have been; then it turns toward the thicker growth. We do not need to meet to make room for each other.

The engine is in everything. It presses through the shelf and my legs and the new joints of my body. I have never known the ceramic without it. What matters is what moves differently: the light repeated taps of grazers feeding upslope, the little shifting weight of a hunter in the basin below, the sudden hard pressure of water beginning to deepen.

That pressure comes now. I stop feeding and climb toward the root. My old skin pulls free, catches for a moment on my hind leg, then goes sliding down the shelf. I brace myself in a crack while water pours over me. Others are there already, packed into the shelter, their bodies bent around the root and around one another. The leaf whips against the wall. Something small and winged strikes the water and is carried away.

When the flow falls, I come out onto a changed surface. My bright feeding track has vanished under a scatter of skins and loosened film. The hunter below has shifted too; one fringed leg reaches farther into the current than before. I feel it moving as I approach and turn along the ridge instead.

The ridge leads toward a narrow course that has grown deeper over many such cycles. Water takes it readily now. The film along its edge is fresh, and I begin to graze there, leaving a cleared strip that another body may find.

Above us, beyond the leaf and the warm ceiling, the great turbines continue to turn. Here, the water bends around the small furrow I am helping to keep open.
