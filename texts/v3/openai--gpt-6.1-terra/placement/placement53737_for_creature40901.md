I would first take the tower for a landmark: a dark upright on the ridge, with a lamp showing through the white water. Coming closer, I would see that it leans slightly, not because the rock beneath it has shifted, but because one side of its base has been worn down. Its cable enters a fissure several body lengths away.

I think I would live there, though not inside the tower. I would be the animal at its base.

I have a flat body and six hooked limbs. For most of my life I have held to the sheltered side of the mounting, where the iron interrupts the current. My foremost limbs reach into the gap beneath it. They are long enough to touch the stone on one side and the warm casing on the other, and thin enough to follow the little channels between the bolts.

At first those channels fed me. Small bodies gathered there, and soft deposits formed where water emerged from the rock. I could stay braced in one place and reach several openings. When a limb became worn, I grew another. When the current tore a hook away, I waited for its replacement to harden.

The tower has been warm for longer than I can remember. Scavengers live under it now, packed so tightly that my reaching limbs pass over their backs. Sometimes I eat one that has strayed from the cluster. More often I feel among them for the things they miss: an egg caught against a bolt, a suspended feeder whose limbs have tangled in the rough iron, a strip of soft tissue carried in from somewhere I will never go.

The noise comes through my feet as well as through the water. The rock has its great continuous shaking; the casing adds a quicker pulse. I cannot separate these from the ordinary feel of being held in place. When I move a foot, the pulse stops in that limb until I set it down again.

Once, another feeler reached under the housing from the opposite side. I touched one of its forelimbs before I found the animal itself. We stayed there, each testing the narrow space, until it put two hooks beside mine and began to pull. I pulled back. A row of scavengers loosened and tumbled out into the current. The other feeler withdrew after a short struggle, leaving one hook caught in the stone. For a long time afterward I encountered it there: a hard, curved thing with no movement behind it.

The tower’s light reaches the edge of my shelter. I know it chiefly when the larger hunters pass. Their pale undersides cross it, and for a moment the reaching limbs in front of me darken. Then the light returns. I draw my limbs in if the water changes sharply, and wait with my mouth against the rock.

One day the quicker pulse stops. The roar in the stone continues, so at first the change is only a gap in a place where there has never been one.

I remain anchored. The casing cools slowly. The scavengers begin to move across the bolts and down onto the rock, exposing patches of iron that their bodies have covered for years. I follow them with a forelimb and catch one. Later I catch another, farther from the housing.

The lamp is still burning. Beyond its reach the ridge slopes away, and there are other cracks in it, some carrying a trace of warmer water. I have not needed to visit them. I release one hook and set it a little farther along the stone. Then another. Behind me, the underside of the casing is becoming bare.
