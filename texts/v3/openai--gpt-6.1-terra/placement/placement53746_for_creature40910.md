At first I would be under a copper fan, with three feet hooked around a rib and the others tucked against my body. I would not choose the shelter by sight. The rib would tell me when the next gust was coming: a tightening under my feet, then the slow beginning of a fold.

I would be one of the grazers.

My shell would be chipped at the back, where a fold once closed on me before I had learned its timing. Dust would lodge in the chip. Dust would lodge everywhere: in the joints of my legs, around the scraping parts under my head, in the fine grooves of the hooked pads. I would clean what I could reach and carry the rest. On a pale leaf I might look pale; in the mortar I would take on the wall’s color.

I would come out to feed when the fan opened. Not when the wind stopped—it does not—but when the strain in the rib eased enough that the leaf presented its broad face. Then I would move in quick increments, keeping hold with one foot while another found purchase. I would scrape the powder from a crease. Beneath it there might be a tender film, worth staying for if the fan remained open long enough.

The sound would be everywhere in me. The wall hums at one pitch, the ribs at others; my own plates would rattle faintly against one another. The impacts from the street would have their own character. A heavy step sends a brief shock through the mortar, and I would grip until it passed, though the wind’s warning is the one I would learn most closely. A footfall is over before I can do much about it. A gust begins in the plant and keeps arriving.

Behind the fan there would be routes I knew by the reach of my legs: a crack too tight for an adult but wide enough for the young; a glossy pocket where oil gathers; a dead bladder skin caught on a root. I would eat from the skin until it was thin as dust. Sometimes another grazer would be there before me, its shell moving almost imperceptibly as it worked. We might touch briefly in the dark, feel for purchase around one another, and continue.

The garden would not be a home I left untouched. I would scrape its leaves and cut its fine roots. A bead would gather at the cut, and I would hold on while I ate it. Around the galleries, the roots would grow more slowly. Elsewhere they would tighten through loosened mortar and keep the passages from falling in. I would depend on them without needing the garden to be kind to me.

From the outermost fan I might sometimes reach the very edge of the growth. There the salt lies open, each grain throwing its tiny shadow. Seed powder skates across it and gathers in the far corner. I could follow the leaf to its tip, but no farther; there would be nothing to hook a foot around.

The little green point beside the white stone would be beyond my reach. I might never distinguish it from the husks and scattered powder. If it lived and sent a stem toward the wall, perhaps one day I would find that stem in a sheltered crack, test it with a hooked foot, and scrape what had gathered on it.

Until then I would turn back along the rib. The leaf would begin to fold beneath me. I would flatten my body and hold on, with the chamber roaring through my shell.
