I would live above the room with the wall inside it.

From the lane, our house looks much like the others. Its roof is weighted with flat stones, and the curtain over its door has been mended so often that the birds must choose between root fiber and hide when they come to steal from it. But in the lower room there is an older wall, built across one corner and ending without meeting anything. My grandmother says it once held back a channel. My uncle says it was the side of a house that fell. Neither will let the other take it down to find out.

I sleep upstairs, where I can hear the water enter below. Most nights it comes quietly around the feet of the old wall. When it begins to knock against it, I know something has caught in the narrow space behind.

At first I thought my work would be to keep that space clear. I kept a hooked length of bone by the stairs and pulled out whatever lodged there: ice, shed shells, bits of curtain, once a wooden shade with one lens split. Then, one morning, I found a ball of fish fiber and down wedged in the gap. A bird had built a nest on the dry side, just above the usual waterline. She stood on the old wall while I looked at it, her throat moving.

I left the hook where it was and went outside to find my grandmother. She came down, felt along the submerged stones and said the channel had risen. We took three stones from the outer lane and laid them to guide the water away from the nest. By afternoon our neighbor had put two of them back: we had sent the water toward his doorway.

So we all worked at it, including the neighbor, who said very little until he had found a better place for the third stone. The bird stayed on the wall, and that evening I heard her moving in the nest while we ate upstairs.

I think I would be the person people send for when something is caught where a hand will not fit. I have narrow wrists, and I know how to make a hook from bone without leaving a sharp point that tears the roots of a gate. I keep several hooks hanging beside the door, though my uncle borrows them and puts them back in the wrong order. Children bring me things they find in the lanes, asking what sort of creature left a shell or whether a stone has been worked by a person. Sometimes I know. When I don’t, we take it to someone older.

In the bright hours I work beneath the bridges, where there is enough shade to lift my own shades for a moment. I announce myself first, tapping the bridge with my knuckles, so an underfoot has time to come out or make its displeasure known. One old female has a notch in her ear. She moves only as far as she must and watches my hands while I clear her drain. Once, when I pulled out a dead fish too large to lift through the gate, she took the other end. We worked it loose together, though she kept it afterward.

During a reversal I go to the upper bridge. From there I can see the central seep and the steps leading toward the ridge. Someone on the roof above signals when the first loose ice begins to climb. I turn my gate, then wait for the person at the next channel to raise theirs. We have learned not to hurry each other: a gate turned too soon can send water into a room where someone is still lifting the baskets.

Afterward I walk home by the altered lane, testing each stone with the side of my foot. I check the old wall before I go upstairs. If the nest is still dry, I leave it alone. If something is knocking behind the wall, I fetch the hook—and, if I cannot reach it, I call my uncle down to hold the lamp.
