I think I would begin as one of the black fronds, at the margin of a basin in the long shadow of an engine hall.

The ice would form around my stem each cold season. Above it, my surface would shed the water that rose and broke against me; below it, new growth would press slowly outward, sheltered from the wind. I would have a green centre and a thin translucent edge. The light reaching me there would be altered by the ice and by the pale wall of the hall.

At first I imagine that I would know the hall by its sound. But sound is too separate a thing: it comes through the water and the stone and my own submerged stem. The pump’s note would be part of the pressure around me, as regular as the pull of water toward the basin’s outlet. On still days I might also feel the deeper notes of other districts, carried through the rock beneath my roots.

The basin would seem, from where I grew, a complete place. Its water arrived through a dark seam at one end and slipped away through a narrower one at the other. Moss held the banks. Broad leaves leaned over the warmer shallows, their old scratches whitening each time the frost returned. I would not know how far their roots went, or where the water had been before it rose beside me.

Once, the pump would stop.

The change would come through the stone before the surface of the basin altered. Then, over many days, the water would settle lower around my stem. Ice would reach farther down. Some of the moss at the edge would dry and stiffen; the broad leaves would hold their places, though their pale bases received less water.

I would continue growing beneath the ice. That is where my newest tissue is, and where the cold takes longest to deepen. I would not know whether the pump would return.

Water would find another route, entering the basin from a crack under the moss. It would carry a little ground mineral matter and spread a dark stain over the stone. The moss would follow that dampness, growing outward by increments too small for me to distinguish. Eventually its edge would reach the new channel and begin to hold the water there.

The pump might start again after that, its note returning through the rock. The basin would rise, but not quite to its old line: some water would now leave by the channel the moss had helped make. A few new fronds would appear there, below the ice.

I would remain at the old margin, marked by several seasons of frost. From my place I could feel the machinery, receive the water it had moved, and grow beside the plants that changed its course. Farther down the outlet, beyond the stone I could reach, the water would continue.
