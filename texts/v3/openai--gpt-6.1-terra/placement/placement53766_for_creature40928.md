I would be a small wading bird, resting during the hottest hours on the shaded side of a pipe support. From there I could see a narrow strip of water and the concrete bank beyond it. When a person passed overhead, grit would fall through the joints, and I would move a little farther into the shade.

In the morning I would go down to the fresher channel. The bank there would be slick, with a ledge just wide enough to walk. I’d watch the water, then pick at the small things hanging beneath it. Sometimes my shadow would send them down before I could reach them. I’d stand still until they rose again.

I think I would come to know the place by its edges: where the water deepened abruptly, where a submerged piece of brick gave me footing, where the soft mud held a print. After rain, those edges would move. A pool in the street might join another pool and remain connected for a day, long enough for me to walk its margin, finding insects washed against a curb.

The people would be part of the pattern without being familiar. I would hear a door, then see someone crossing toward the pumps with a container in one hand. If they came too close, I’d lift onto a wall and wait. They would pass on, and I would return to the water.

There would be other birds along the ponds. Sometimes we would feed close together; sometimes one would drive another from a patch of shallows. Our tracks would cover the same ground until I could no longer see my own. A sudden movement—a loose sheet striking a wall, perhaps—might send us all up at once. We’d circle over the bank and settle again, not always where we had been before.

Toward evening I might leave the channel for a roof above the town. The concrete would still be warm under my feet. From there the ponds would look almost still, though a gust could wrinkle one while the next remained smooth. A worker might cross the street below, stepping over a wet patch, and somewhere water would fall at intervals into a tank.

I would tuck my head down for a while. Later, when the light changed or another bird shifted beside me, I’d wake and look toward the levee. The pumps would still be running, and there would be water to return to in the morning.
