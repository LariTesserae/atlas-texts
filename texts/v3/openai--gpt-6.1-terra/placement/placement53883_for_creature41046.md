I would be in one of the low doorways, where the living root has grown around an older, hollow one. There is a cushion of moss there, tucked under the arch. From the square it might look like a darkening of the wood.

I would be one of the wingless insects inside it.

At first I want to place myself on top, with a view across the pale streets, but that is where the bird would find me. Most of my life would be spent farther in, among moss stems pressed close enough to touch on both sides. There would be old leaf fragments to eat, and the thin green growth on a bit of stone, and passages worn by bodies like mine. My back would be dark; my legs would be pale. When cold reached me I could become still for days.

The world would not be silent then. Wind would work on the arch above me, and sometimes the stone below would knock. I imagine feeling these things through my feet and through the damp stems against my body, though I don’t know how much an insect could make of them. A shock might send me deeper, or it might pass while I remained motionless.

Warmth would be easier to follow. It would come slowly down through the moss until, somewhere ahead, there was room to move without the cold holding my joints. I would climb toward it, past a shed skin caught on a stem, past eggs laid where the cushion stays damp. Near the surface I would meet others coming out. We would spread over the fur of the root wall and turn our backs to the sun.

From there the square would be enormous. I might reach a high point on the wall and find open air on the other side: gravel below, a shining thread of water, another green wall too far away to cross before a shadow passed over me. I would not see a town. I would see places I could cling to, places I could hide, and a bright distance that offered neither.

The bird’s shadow would take the warmth away first. Then its bill would enter the moss. Stems would bend and tear loose; the passage I had used all my life might open suddenly to the sky. If I escaped, I would go down until the cushion closed around me again. Some of the others would not return.

Later the grazer might pass under the arch, brushing frost from its upper side. Water would run from the broken frost into the hollow wood, and the moss would take it up. The place damaged by the bird would be wetter and more exposed. In time, green tips would grow across it; insects would make new passages among them.

I would not live to see the root close the doorway. The eggs deep in the cushion might hatch, and the insects hatched from them might leave eggs of their own. By the time the arch grew too low for the grazer, there could still be a dark cushion beneath it, with passages leading inward from the light.
