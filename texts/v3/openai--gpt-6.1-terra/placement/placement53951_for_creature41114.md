I would be in the thickness of a bowl near the edge of the court. Not the large bowl itself: I would live around one of the little hollows nested in its rim, beside three others so close that the clay between us was thinner than a fingertip. We would be distinct, though at first I would not know it.

I would know the curve around me. On one side it rose steeply; on the other it opened into a lip that had been rubbed smooth. Beneath me, the clay joined the larger bowl. I could not follow it far in the beginning. The pressure was everywhere, and I knew it only as it was held in my own hollow.

One of my neighbors would make itself known by holding that pressure differently. I would attend to the difference for a long while before understanding that it came from another place. Then I would try to offer the shape of my hollow in return. My neighbor, whose rim was deeper, might take an equally long while to find me in it.

There would be a large inhabitant below us, extending through the slab and into the pavement. It would know where each of us had first become distinct. When I learned to attend beyond my own rim, I might find that it had already been attending to me.

I think I would become interested in the place where one bowl ends and another begins. Not because the boundary is doubtful—the clay has its own exact shape—but because the pressure held in a small hollow reaches the larger one too, and is received there differently. I would offer a neighbor’s greeting to the large inhabitant and learn how much of it had arrived without my help. I would bring the large inhabitant’s answer back, and find that my neighbor had received part of that already.

This would not be useful in any urgent way. No message would need to be carried quickly. Still, I would come to know which bowls could feel one another directly and which needed an inhabitant between them. I might spend a great deal of attention on a greeting, trying to let its shape remain itself as it passed through mine.

At some point an impression from beyond the court would reach us: a hollow in one of the descending walls, unlike any our edge inhabitants knew. My neighbors would attend to its unfamiliar curve. I would attend also to how it had come to us—how the wall had held it, how the outermost bowl had received it, and what had become of it by the time it reached our crowded rims.

I would offer it to the inhabitant beneath me. In the depth of its bowl, the impression might be held more fully than it could be in mine. When it returned the shape, I would find something I had missed: a shallow irregularity along the curve, perhaps another hollow opening from it, too slight for me to have distinguished alone.

Then I would give the impression to my neighbors again, with that small part included. They might already have found it. They would receive it nevertheless.
