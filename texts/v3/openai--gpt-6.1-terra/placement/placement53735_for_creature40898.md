I would be one of the animals at the bottom of the bowl, though I think I would begin above it, pale and soft in a felt sleeve.

There, the world would have a roof of leaf and a floor that could be eaten. Moisture would collect in the fibers, and I would find it by pressing the edge of myself against them. Beetles would pass close enough to touch. The metal underneath would be always moving—not traveling anywhere, but trembling so steadily that stillness would be a thing I had never felt.

I might come down by accident. The felt loosens where the sleeve crosses an old seam; a strip of it hangs away from the rib. I follow the damp along it and find, too late, that the strip has no further hold. I fall with a little wad of fiber caught beneath me.

The bowl receives me. For a long while I remain folded around the felt, taking what water it has. When I unfold, there are three dark shapes at the center, one partly covering another. I move toward them because the floor slopes that way and because the metal is cooler there. By the time I reach them the felt is dry.

I grow dark slowly. Grit passes over me in the wind; some remains beneath my edge, and I carry it until the next drop comes. A dead moth falls near me, and I learn the length of time it takes to make a meal of something larger than myself. Later, when I move away, one bright scale remains on the floor.

From where I live, the tower is not tall. There is the floor, and the long shiver coming through it, and the ribs at the edge where the floor becomes difficult to cross. The open air beyond them means little to me. Occasionally a shadow sweeps over the bowl and the vibration changes under the small impact of a bat touching down, then leaving again.

Rain is different each time. Sometimes there are only scattered drops, each with its own brief path down the slope. I lift my rim toward one and spread the fine branches underneath, but another animal reaches it first. I can follow the cooling line it leaves, though there is less and less to take from it.

In a heavier rain I draw my edges upward. Water gathers on my back, and for a while the weight makes movement difficult. A lizard comes down from the ribs and places its forefeet on me. Its toes are warm. I feel them shift as it drinks; then it takes one more small step and waits there, perhaps because the water is cooler than the floor.

When the deep note begins to swell, I feel it long before anything else changes. The short hairs beneath me lean and recover, lean and recover. The other animals are moving toward the center. I join them, fitting my edge over one neighbor and beneath another. We stay close as the days pass. A flower falls and dries a short distance away, untouched.

Under us, eggs adhere to the polished metal. When I eventually move, I leave some in the narrow place where my body covered theirs. I cannot keep the wind from them. I can only settle nearby for a time, until hunger draws me upslope.

By then the vines have opened. Things begin to fall again: pollen, a beetle, a torn piece of leaf. I cross the bowl slowly, leaving a clean track behind me, and fold myself over what I find.
