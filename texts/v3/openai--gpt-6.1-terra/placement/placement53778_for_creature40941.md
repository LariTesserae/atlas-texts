I would be under the storehouse, with my cheek against a beam and one arm thrust into the dark.

There is just enough room to lie there if I turn my head sideways. The chickens have followed me as far as the edge, and I can hear them scratching and muttering behind my feet. My hand is searching for the end of a drain that was clear yesterday. Today the water is coming out beside it, carrying a little fan of soil toward the storehouse post.

I find the blockage by touch: leaves, a twig, a wad of something soft. When I pull it free, water runs up my sleeve. A frog jumps against my wrist, and I knock my head on the beam.

The child who brought me here laughs. I can see only her ankles from where I lie, and the basket of eggs she has set safely on a stone.

“Did you see it?” I ask.

“No.”

“Then it was enormous.”

I am not the person in charge of the drains. There is no such person. My house happens to be nearest this storehouse, and I am often asked to look at the ground around it because I have replaced two of its posts. I also know where the spare poles are kept, though people who borrow them sometimes neglect to tell me.

I back out and sit up in the wet. The drain runs freely now, but the post is loose. When I put my shoulder to it, it moves a little in its bed.

The child tries it too. She is pleased to make it move, so I tell her to stop and take the eggs home. On her way past the yard she calls to her aunt that the storehouse is falling down.

By the time I have fetched a lever, three people are waiting. One has brought a length of cord, one a basket of stones, and the child’s aunt has come to see how badly I have frightened her sister. We all push the post in turn. It moves for everyone. This takes a while, because the storehouse is full of grain and nobody wants to be the first to say that we should empty it.

At last the aunt says it. We pass the vessels down the steps and carry them to the nearest hearth shelter. The lids are checked as they go. One has a damp patch inside, and its owner takes it away to spread the grain before the fire. Someone complains that the good dry place by that hearth is going to be crowded tonight. Someone else offers to relieve the crowding by eating the grain.

The post has not rotted through, as I expected. Its stone footing has slipped into a cavity, and beneath the cavity is an old watercourse packed with roots. We lift the post, lever the stone back into place and pack more stones around it. It takes all afternoon. The child returns and asks whether the frog is still enormous. She helps by carrying small stones and dropping them precisely where we ask, until she sees a duck in the sedge and goes after it.

While we work, the oldest of us points out where the water used to run before the storehouse was built. I have heard her describe it before, but now I can see the shallow line of it under the moss. We clear a channel toward it with our hands and a flat piece of wood. The water hesitates at the turn, pools around our fingers, then begins to move.

By evening the post is firm enough for us to load the storehouse again. We leave the path beside it lifted, so we can look at the footing after the next rain. I know the raised board will catch somebody’s foot in the dark; I put a stick across the path and call a warning to the nearest house.

At home, I hang my wet sleeve by the hearth and turn it when steam begins to rise. Through the door I can hear people calling the ducks in. The child comes past with her basket empty and tells me she found the bird’s nest. There were four eggs in it, all warm.

Later, when the voices thin out, I hear the water under the path. Tomorrow I will go and see which way it has taken.
