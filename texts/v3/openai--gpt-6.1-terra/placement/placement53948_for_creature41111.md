I would live beside the bend in the drainage channel, in a house whose roof has to be cleared more often than most. A fern grows from the seam behind it and leans its whole weight toward the water. By evening its fronds hang over the channel; after a wet night they can nearly close it.

I am the person who cuts them back, though the fern belongs to no one and anyone passing with a blade might do the work. I keep the best cuttings for the scatter outside my door. The tender tips go there, laid across a flat stone where I can see when they have been eaten. The tougher stems I split and carry to the court.

My doorway faces a collection stone. It is a good stone, broad and slightly hollow underneath, but one edge rests close to a crack. When I was small, I lifted it too quickly and watched the water run straight down that crack. I remember staying crouched beside it, hoping some would come back.

Now I slide a dish beneath the edge before I raise it. There are mornings when the underside is so crowded with beads that they join and run at once, and mornings when I put the stone down again without taking any.

The hunters shelter in the crack. I know this because their limbs appear when I lift the stone, pale and narrow against the dark. I try to leave them covered at one end. Once, when the fern had grown across the path and I was impatient to reach the dish, I shifted the whole stone aside. Three hunters unfolded from beneath it and stayed exposed on the gravel while I collected the water. I put the stone back as carefully as I could. By the time I returned from the cistern, they had gone.

In cold weather some of them come into my house. I roll my mat high against the wall and leave a strip of bare floor from the door to the hearth. Sometimes that strip fills too. Then I wait, or carry one out on bark if the light is going and I need to cook. Its limbs hang over the edges of the bark until it finds a place to set them down.

I think I would spend much of my life near that threshold: outside enough to see the fern change color as the grazers feed, inside enough to notice when one of my hanging bundles begins to darken. I would learn which leaves stay moist longest after cutting, and how far from the door to put them when the wind comes across the notch.

Toward evening, after the channel is clear, I would sit with a stem across my knees and split it for ties. People would pass with their water. The hunters would emerge from the paving seams, and I might lift my feet onto the sill to give them room. When the mist became too cold, I would close the door, leaving the small opening clear. Then I would watch the pale limbs come through, one at a time.
