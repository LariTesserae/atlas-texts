I would live at a workshop on the edge of a long descent, where the drain runs beneath the floor before spilling into a chamber in the rock. My bed would be close enough to the wall that, at night, I could feel the turbine through it. When the machine stopped for maintenance, I would wake in the unfamiliar stillness and lie there waiting for someone to start it again.

I would be one of the people who tend the drain and the cable route below it. The two jobs belong together: when the drain clogs, water finds its way under the foundations, and when the ground softens, the cable shifts. I would carry a hooked rake, a staff weighted at its lower end, and a short length of chain for sounding depths. My hands would have the fine ridges common to my people, worn smooth in places by rope and iron.

The screen at the drain mouth would be my first task after a storm. I would brace one foot against the stone, lift it by its cold handle, and feel the weight of everything caught against it: stems, grit, the fine threads that spread through the joints, occasionally a dead winged animal with its thick wings folded close. Most mornings I could pull the screen free alone. After a hard rain I might have to ask someone from the workshop to help, and we would argue companionably about whose turn it was to stand downstream.

There would be a pool dweller outside the drain. I would know it first by the way it took scraps before they reached the slope below. Later I might recognize the soft movement of its mouth against the stone when I lowered the screen. I would never be certain how much of the creature was gathered beneath the water, though once, clearing a jam, I would put my hand farther down than I meant to and feel a broad lip draw gently against my wrist.

I would learn not to throw the scrapings into its pool. That would make the screen easier to clean for a day, but the remains would settle in the side chamber, and in time the water would back up. Instead I would carry them beyond the cable trench and spread them where the roots could take hold.

The route would pass under two wide trees and across a shelf that always gives a hollow answer. There is a post at the near end, with three notches cut deep enough to survive a season’s growth. I would know the post by the shape of its iron foot as much as by the notches; I would have helped drive it into a crack in the bedrock, both of us holding the stake while another worker struck it.

One morning the shelf would answer differently.

Not a collapse, not even a dramatic sound. My staff would strike, and something below would return the blow a little later than it should. I would strike again, then move back toward the post and try from there. Behind me, water would be running down through the tree roots. Farther off, an animal would give a rapid series of clicks, pause, and begin again.

I would not trust my first judgment. I would sit with my palm on the shelf until the damp had worked through my clothes, listening through the heel of my hand. Then a drummer would strike somewhere beyond the descent. Another would answer, closer. I would feel several uncertain blows from a smaller animal, arriving before the larger echoes had faded.

The herd would be testing the ground too.

I would go back for another worker and a longer line. Together we would find that a narrow channel had opened under the shelf, carrying water across the old cable bed. The cable was still intact, but the iron clamps had begun to pull loose. We would work from the solid side, lifting the line out of the water and fastening it higher along the root arches. At the crossing itself we would set a new post, notched to warn that the hollow had lengthened.

It might take two days. We would stop when the herd came near, sitting against the stone with our tools gathered close. Their steps would pass through us in slow, heavy rolls. One young drummer might strike the shelf repeatedly from the far side, then wait for an older animal to answer before moving on.

Back at the workshop, someone would ask whether the route was safe. I would say where to place a staff, which root to hold, and how much line to carry. I would also say that the water was still cutting underneath. We would need to go down again after the next storm.

That evening I would lift the drain screen once more. The pool dweller would be waiting below, and I would tell it, as I worked, about the young drummer and the cable and the channel in the stone. The water would carry my voice away. When the screen was clear, I would lower it carefully into its guides and put my hand on the wall until I felt the steady motion of the turbine.
