At first I am not sure whether I would be in the hollow at all. The open space above the basin is so much larger than anything I need, and its light arrives from a direction that has no meaning deeper in the stone.

I think I would be under the basin, in a crack where two surfaces come close enough that my feet can touch both at once. I would be one of the pale animals there, with a dark line showing through my body after feeding. The stone would have a different texture on either side: one face rough enough to grip, the other slick with film.

When the water rose I would move upward with it, keeping my body near the stone. Sometimes there would be another animal in the crack, its legs extended across my path. We might wait until one of us turned, or pass by folding our legs close. I would scrape film from places the water had not scoured clean, then stop to pull a grain from the hairs of one foot.

The pulses would come through all the surfaces at once. Some would make me pause with my mouthparts against the rock. Others would pass while I went on feeding. I would know them by how they moved my legs, not by the towers or machinery that made them.

The basin’s rim would be a place I reached only occasionally. I might come out when the water was high and find the stone wet farther from the seam than before. There would be grit there, and a ridge of crystals, and the broad cold space overhead. A droplet could strike close enough to shift my feet. I would drink from its edge, then clean the crystals from my legs before returning.

Once, perhaps, the panel would open while I was out. The air would move across me and begin to dry the stone. I would turn before the worker’s light reached the basin, but not quickly enough to escape it entirely. For a moment my shadow would stretch across the wet floor. I would feel my way into the seam and remain there while the light moved over the place I had been.

Later, when the panel was closed, water would collect again. A little of it would carry dust from the worker’s boot toward my crack. Some grains would lodge at the entrance; finer ones would travel farther, settling among the folds of film. I would pass them while feeding.

If I lived long enough, I might fix eggs beneath an overhang deeper in the rock, where the water touched them without pulling at their cases. The young would stay near that place after hatching. Their shed skins would catch briefly on the rough stone, then loosen as the water rose.

I would keep coming and going through the crack. Above me the basin would empty; after a while, beads would gather on the ceiling. I might be near enough to feel one fall, or far below, with my feet braced against both sides of the stone.
