I would live just below the surface of one of the older mounds, where a passage has been opened and closed so many times that its walls hold the outlines of other passages. I would be a worker termite, pale and soft, with a dark gut showing faintly through my body.

At first I think of myself in a chamber among hundreds of others, but I do not stay there. I follow the moving bodies into a narrow gallery, touching the earth and the termites ahead of me with my antennae. The air is still and close. A change in its dampness tells me when we are nearing a break in the wall.

There is a beetle under the broken rim. It keeps its place while we pass below it. Beyond the breach, a soldier stands with its head toward the opening. A thin current of dry air moves around it. I turn away from that current and continue along a covered runway toward the scrub.

The runway ends beneath a litter of leaves. Here there is room to find something to carry: a fragment of leaf stem, perhaps, softened on one side where it lay against the ground. It catches against another piece when I lift it. I pull until it comes free, then turn back, meeting others on their way out. We touch briefly as we pass.

Above the roof, the night has begun. The beetle may feel the immense calling of the basin through its feet, but down here it reaches me, if at all, as faint trembling in the packed earth. I know the roof more directly: its coolness near the outside, its warmth where the passage bends deep into the mound, the loose grains that come away when I brush them.

At the breach, the soldier has shifted. Workers are bringing soil. One presses a damp particle into the edge of the opening, and another adds to it. I pass them with my fragment. By the time I return, the shape of the light has changed: it is narrower, with an uneven lower edge.

There are traces in the walls that none of us laid down. I encounter them when a thin partition gives way, or when a gallery being extended breaks into an old chamber. The earth has a different texture there; fibers have settled into the floor. For a while we use the space, crossing it and touching its walls. Later it may be filled again.

I do not know the breadth of the city. I know a route, and then another route when that one is closed. I know the bodies that meet mine, the food held long enough to be passed on, the dry edge of an opening that must be covered. The mound is large enough that I could spend my life inside it without reaching many of its chambers.

Toward morning I come again to the narrowing breach. The soldier is still there. Through the remaining gap, the air has cooled, and something outside moves over the earth. I do not go close enough to see it. I pick up a grain from the passage floor and carry it to the workers at the wall.
