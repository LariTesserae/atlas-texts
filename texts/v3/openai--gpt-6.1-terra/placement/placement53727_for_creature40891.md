I am at a doorway, where the stone lane opens into a court. One hand is against the doorpost. The other holds a length of cord, with a knot partway along it. I cannot see what the cord is for from where I stand; perhaps it is tied to something inside.

My voice is in the air. I had thought, at first, that I was calling to the person at the cistern. They are near enough to hear me, and their bowl is tilted toward the rain. But I cannot tell whether the sound is a name or a warning or simply something said across the court. It has a shape without arriving at its end.

From my doorway I can see the grey animal beneath the bench. Its head reaches into the light, and there is a little space between its nose and a sprig of grass. Across the court, the digging stick stands in the garden soil. The person holding it has a good view of the herd; I can see only the nearest few animals between two houses.

The cord presses into my palm. Its fibres are coarse, and one loose end is caught in the skin beside my thumb. There is stone dust on my sleeve, though I do not know whether I have been working with the flint in the basket or helping to fit a wall. The rake is close to my door. Its uneven teeth make me think of a hand spread against the ground.

I am a person of this town, then, though not necessarily a person with an answer for anyone. I have a doorway, a piece of cord, a voice that joins the bird and the water at the channel’s lip. The person with the bowl may be listening. The animal under the bench may be resting. Both are close enough that I can see them clearly; neither gives me any more to go on.

Beyond the court, the rain remains separate from the dry terraces. There is room in the lane for me to walk toward either one, but I am here, with my hand on the post, the word still held in the air.
