At first I think I would be a shelter: a clear wall growing slowly around a warm stone, holding a bowl of water for whoever came in. I would know the valley by the weight of rain and the footsteps at my door.

But a shelter cannot go down to the pools to see what made the clicking change. So perhaps I am one of the people who keeps returning to it.

The shelter stands above a long valley, not quite on the ridge. From its doorway I can see two of the three poles. The third is hidden behind a shoulder of rock, and I have to walk a little way to check that it is there. It always has been. Still, whenever I return after a season away, I go and look.

I travel with others when our paths happen to join. This year I have come back with a young person who has never seen this valley. On the way up we pass roofbacks feeding near the falls. Only their ears show until one lifts its head, a seed stem hanging from its mouth. The young person stops to watch it, and the rest of the herd becomes visible by degrees: copper backs among the grass, then a small one standing in the narrow shadow of its mother.

The doorway of the shelter has grown nearly shut. We pull its soft edges apart and clear the moss from the threshold. Inside, a seam-walker is asleep, its grey fur spread over the warm floor. We wait outside until it wakes. It leaves without hurrying, feeling its way around our feet before climbing the rock beside the entrance.

The bowl is half full. There is a dead leaf in it, and beneath that a little silt. I show my companion how to lift the leaf without spilling the water; then we carry the bowlful outside in our hands, wipe the hollow clean, and watch a new bead gather in the bottom. By night there will be enough to drink.

In the afternoon we walk to the poles with cutting stones. Plants have grown close to the bases, and we loosen their roots from the damp ground. My companion asks what would happen if the roots reached them. I tell them I don’t know. They ask why we cut the plants, then.

I say that my grandmother showed me, and that she had been shown in turn. It is an answer, though not a complete one. We finish the work together.

On the way back, a broad-winged animal passes overhead. Its shadow moves along the grass ahead of us, then turns toward a pool while the animal continues down the valley. We stop. The shadow rests at the water’s edge, darkening a patch of mud where the shelled creatures are calling. My companion wants to go closer, but waits beside me until a breeze crosses the pool and the shadow moves away.

That evening the arcs appear. We see the first one from the shelter doorway, a faint curve between a pole and the western ridge. We climb onto the rock above us for a better view. The cloud floor brightens, and every fern casts a sharp, separate shadow.

My companion asks if the light will come again tomorrow. I say it might.

We stay until the arcs fade. Then we climb down to the shelter, where the bowl has filled and the seam-walker has returned. It is asleep just inside the doorway. There is room to lie down on either side of it, so we do.
