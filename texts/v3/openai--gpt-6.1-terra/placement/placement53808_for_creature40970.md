I grow behind the third upright stone, counting from the low end of the row. The stone takes the strongest water on its polished face. Behind it the current loosens, and mineral dust comes down among the branches of a pale cushion. My mat has fastened there, in a seam too narrow for much silt to lie.

I am small beside the towers on the ridge. When the water runs evenly, my longest ribbons reach beyond the lee of the stone and are drawn taut downstream. Their tips travel farther than their roots can follow. Sometimes they catch for a while among the pale branches; sometimes they come free carrying a thread or a little dust. My younger ribbons remain in the slow water, folding over the mat.

The deep sound reaches me through the seam. It begins below the place where I am fixed, passes into my base and runs along each ribbon. Those held taut take it differently from those curled in the shelter. The stone receives it too, and for a moment the water around us changes its pressure. I know the note by these differences: where it holds, where it falters, how long the last tremor stays in my oldest strand.

There are others behind the stones. Their loose ends sometimes enter the water that reaches me, though the flow is seldom gentle enough to bring them close. Once, during a long sounding, a dark fragment lodged against my mat and stayed there through several changes of current. I drew what I could from it. Its fibers had been worn thin at one end and thickened at the other; I could not tell which stone it had come from.

The nearest ring stands beyond me on the shelf. A split in its roof lets water pass through, and from time to time the current brings fine clay from one of the bowls. It settles in the pale cushion and gives the shelter a different taste. A length of cord has worked loose inside the ring. It lifts from the floor when the flow is strong, then falls back. I have grown toward it as far as my stone permits, but the current carries my ribbons away from the opening.

I cannot reach the cord, though I have taken in pieces of it. Its strands are harder than mine and covered in crust; beneath that they are made of the same fibers. The water has worn a groove through the braid at one point, and now the cord turns there whenever it rises. Each time it settles, a little more of it comes apart.

During the longest notes, the other chambers sometimes answer. The vibrations arrive close together, then spread apart until I can feel one pass through my base while another is still moving along my tips. The cord lifts and turns in the opening. Around me, the pale branches close, leaving my lowest ribbons exposed.

When the sound ends, they open again. Dust gathers. My tips bow downstream, and the groove in the cord slowly deepens.
