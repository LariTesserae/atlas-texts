I would begin under a shelf of black stone, where a seam releases water into a hollow of peat. The shelf is not high enough to shelter a bird. Its underside is lined with moss, and a runner has worn a narrow feeding track through the pale growth there. When rain is falling, the water from the seam strikes one loose stone and makes it knock against another.

At first I would know the place by that knock. Then the stone would shift, and for a while I might not know it at all.

I think I would be an attention that forms where several small flows meet. The moss would lend me its threads; the stone would lend me pressure and the slow news of its settling. A few feet away, the roots of a shrub would give me purchase on the surface. I could not leave the hollow in the way a runner leaves it, but rain might join me to other strands, and I would find that a distant pool had become available to me: not as a picture, exactly, but as a set of tensions, a patch of cooling water, the weight of a bird stepping onto a leaf.

I might learn the bird before I ever sensed its flight. It comes to the pool on most mornings, pauses on the same half-submerged stone, and probes beneath the moss. The runner under my shelf folds its feelers when the clicks begin. Once, when the bird reaches into the seam, the runner presses so flat that I can feel water moving on both sides of its body.

I would like to draw the moss closed over it. Perhaps the roots could tighten, given time and enough water, but the bird is quick. Its beak finds nothing and withdraws. The runner stays flattened for a long time afterward. I remain gathered around the seam longer than I otherwise would have, as if there were still something to be done there.

During a heavy shower, the hollow would open into a larger stretch of the marsh. The knock beneath my shelf would travel outward, and other knocks would return—not all of them in an order I could follow. I might feel the weight of a stone before it falls, or the easing that comes after it has fallen, while the stone is still held in place. A reed bed far away would bend, and water would enter the dry moss at the edge of the hollow.

Would I have bent the reeds? I would feel the movement arrive through me, and that might be all I could say.

In the larger rain-attention there would be other tendencies besides mine. One would keep water among the pale trunks, where a nest sits exposed. Another would draw it toward a sunken basin. I might hold the seam open while something elsewhere closes a channel. For a time the water would rise against my stone, carrying grit into the moss. Neither tendency would necessarily prevail. The rain could stop first.

I would have a kind of memory, though I could not summon it at will. A mineral thread might grow around a root that tightened during a flood, preserving its bend after the root relaxed. Later, pressure passing along that thread would take the old turn. A bird’s weight might reach me by the route the flood had left. I would attend to the pool again without knowing why that pool, rather than another, had become so easy to feel.

There may be stretches in which I am not present. A dry wind could separate the wet filaments beneath the shelf. The stone might still knock; the runner might still feed; the shrub could go on leaning over the hollow. When rain returned, something would gather around the renewed contact. It would inherit the threads and their bends, including the track the runner had made and the route left by the flood. Whether it was me again would matter less to the water than to the question of who I am.

I imagine one evening the bird would build among the pale trunks, on twigs still bare of leaves. Through a connection made by rain, I would feel several leaves close above an empty place in the branches. The next day—or perhaps earlier; I could be wrong about the order—the bird would arrive there with a twig.

I could not see its nest from beneath my shelf. I would know it by the added weight, by the slight downward pull on the branch, and later by the bird settling into it. Water would drain from the connecting threads, and those sensations would grow faint. The loose stone would knock once. A runner would come out of the seam, feel along the submerged rock, and begin to feed.
