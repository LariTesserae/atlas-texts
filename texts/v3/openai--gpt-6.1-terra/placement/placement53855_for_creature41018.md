I would begin beneath a shelf, where a crack narrows until the roots must flatten to pass through it.

At first I might seem to be a stain on the stone: a branching deposit, dark at its edges and faintly red where the seam is warm. Some of that branching would be mineral, some alive. The difference would become apparent only when the water moved. The deposit would begin to dissolve; I would grow toward it.

I would be a colony of the soft organisms among the deepest roots, spread through several seams, with no reason to keep a particular shape. A useful patch of salts might sustain one part of me for years. Elsewhere I would thin to a thread, leaving small dormant pieces in the stone. They might wake when water returned, or they might remain there after I had grown beyond any connection to them.

Above me there would be a root that closes its walls against its neighbors. Beside it, another would admit water from several different plants. I would grow around both. When fog came, pressure would build in the stems overhead, and sooner or later a cool thread would reach my seam. It would carry the taste of the surface: salts from old leaves, fine particles of stone, perhaps something shed by a hinge-back feeding far above.

I would not know the animal as an animal. Its leavings would arrive slowly, changed by passage through other lives. Still, I might depend on the flakes from its flanks. Small rootlike organisms would gather around them, and some part of what they released would come down through the cracks. Years later, as the animal’s line returned to that place, a branch of me might still be growing on the nourishment of its previous visit.

For a long time I might occupy one warm seam. Then the current would shift. The red salts would gather farther along the rock, and my richest branches would become the ones nearest that new place. I would extend toward it while the old part thinned. A piece caught behind a tight fold of stone might be left entirely on its own.

If water eventually reached that piece, it could branch again. By then I might have grown through another seam altogether. We would share a beginning, though neither would have a way to find the other except by growing.

From the surface there would be little to see of me. Perhaps a dull red band would pass beneath the leaves in the darkest hours, or a blind grazer would enter a crack and scrape a pale track through a deposit I had been growing toward. The grazers above would fold at a hunter’s approach. Fog would settle and lift. Much later, water from that fog would enter my seam, and a small edge of me would begin to move.
