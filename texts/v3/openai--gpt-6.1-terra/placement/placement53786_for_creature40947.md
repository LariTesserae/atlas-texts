I think I would be on the eastern terraces, though not at the top. From the highest shelf I could look out over the whole basin, or as much of it as the weather allowed, but I would come down again to work where the quarry cuts are close enough to hear water moving over them.

I would tend a set of feeding walls there. Warm water arrives in a pipe from a workshop below, enters a shallow trough, and spills in a thin sheet down the old stone. The dark film grows well on the middle walls. On the lowest wall it grows too slowly, because the grazers can reach it from the street. I used to shut them out with a gate, but the gate needed mending so often that now I leave the lowest wall to them.

My work would be to keep the water moving evenly. I’d carry a scraper, a short brush, and a length of stiff wire for clearing the little holes in the trough. When mineral crust built up around a bolt, I’d chip it away before it forced the fitting apart. On harvest days I’d lift the film in broad wet strips into a covered cart. Some would go to the kitchens nearby; some would be dried or sent on to the works. I’d know which loads were which from the chalk marks on their lids.

There would be a narrow room beside the walls, with a drain in the floor and a shelf above it for things I wanted to keep dry. A kettle, a change of socks, a ledger whose lower pages had curled despite the shelf. In the ledger I’d record the flow, the weight of each harvest, and the repairs that needed more hands than mine. I’d also record when the calves first appeared each year, in the margin, until there were enough entries that I gave them a page of their own.

The little followers would come into the room. I’d hear their claws on the threshold before seeing them, and I’d have to keep the cart lids shut while I worked. One might grow accustomed to me and wait near the drain while I rinsed the scrapers. It would take whatever loosened scraps came down the slope, then dart back outside if I moved too quickly. I don’t think I’d name it. There would be others much like it, and some mornings I wouldn’t be sure which one had come.

At the end of a shift I’d climb the stairs to the housing ledge, carrying my damp clothes in a tin. The view would open gradually as I rose: first the workshop roofs and pipes, then the rail bridge, then the reservoir beyond it. The birds would be settling by that hour. I might pause to watch a late train crossing the basin, though most evenings I’d be more concerned with whether rain was reaching the upper tanks.

I would be a person there, I think: someone whose hands had acquired the pale residue in their creases, who knew the sound of a blocked trough before looking up, and who had neighbors waiting at home. If the herd was moving through the stair landing, I’d wait with everyone else. We’d stand aside while the calves passed between the larger bodies, and someone would tell me the gate on the next ledge was loose again. I’d remember to bring the wrench down in the morning.
