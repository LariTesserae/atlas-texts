I would be in a passage beneath one of the workshops, where the floor is worn smooth on either side of a turning shaft. There is room to stand if I keep my head bowed. A lamp in a wire cage gives enough light to see the bolts in the bearing housing and the pale seams of the animals gathered around them.

I tend the shaft. When the pressure in my jaw changes, I put a hand against the casing and feel for the bearing that has begun to run unevenly. I have tools in a cloth roll: a narrow spanner, a hook for drawing out the old packing, a flat dish to catch what falls. Some of the tools fit my hands; others have been ground down at the handles. I have worked here long enough that I no longer know which were changed for me.

I am built for these passages, though not as narrowly as the animals are. I have two hands and a back that stiffens in the cooler air beyond the retaining walls. My joints need oil. I keep a small flask of it beside the tools, and sometimes, after a long interval between repairs, I find the flask empty and a cluster of animals living beneath it.

To open a bearing, I first stop the shaft. The silence travels farther than the sound did. Dust settles from the housing, and the animals begin to uncurl. I wait for those nearest the opening to leave before drawing out the packing. There are always some deeper inside that I cannot reach without disturbing them. I lift them with the hook and set them in the dish, then carry the dish to the next warm motor.

They do not all stay there. When the shaft starts again, a few cross the floor and return to the cleaned housing. The young are difficult to see until they have gathered enough dust.

At times I go aboveground for a part. The storage rooms are easier to navigate than the streets: I know the stacks by their height and by the narrow routes between them. In one room a tile rests on a low bench, separate from the others. Its plan shows the workshop above me with a wider doorway and a short wall removed. The doorway is still as it has always been. I turn sideways to pass through it.

There is a spanner laid beside the tile, larger than any in my roll. I have tried it once against the bolts in the doorframe. It fits. I have not taken the bolts out yet; the shaft below needs a new bearing, and the wall carries a conduit whose far end I have not found.

When the repair is finished, I may return to the bench. I will trace the conduit first, and move what can be moved without stopping the press. If the doorway is widened, more grit will come in from the street. I will have to clear it from the stair.
