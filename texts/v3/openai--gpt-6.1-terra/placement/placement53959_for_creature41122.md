I would live near the edge of a broad court, in a house whose roof has been reset twice in my lifetime. One corner of the slab still projects farther than the others. When I was small, I thought the house was pointing.

In the mornings I would walk the channel with my older sister. She would carry the scraper and I would carry a narrow strip of limestone, smooth on one side, with notches along its edge. We would stop at the same walls each day. She would put her fingernail into a seam, or lay the strip across it, and tell me where to make a new mark.

At first I would be impatient with the marks. A seam could remain unchanged through many mornings, while animals crossed it and dust collected in it and light came and went. I would want to report something: that a crack had widened, that a block had tilted. My sister would ask me to put the strip back and look again.

Later, when she trusted me to inspect one side of the street alone, I would miss her corrections. I would find myself calling her over for things I could have measured. Sometimes she would come. Sometimes she would hold up her scraper from the far end, telling me to clear what was in front of me.

I would have a grazer at home, one too old to keep with the others in the pen. Its muzzle would be worn pale from pushing through grit. It would sleep beside the wall where the stone stayed cool, and when I sat to repair a basket it would come and investigate the loose ends. I would pretend to be annoyed, then move the basket so it could lie against my leg.

After a rain, I would go with the others to the bright court below the wider opening. We would sort what had washed down: dark material for the pens, pieces of root, grit to be carried away. Once I might find a short piece of wood with bark still on it. I would turn it over and smell the damp side before taking it to the household that kept the poles. They would set it apart to dry, and for years afterward I would look for it whenever I passed their doorway.

One afternoon, while my sister rested, I would go back to check a seam that had troubled me. A band of light had crossed it that morning, making a thin shadow where I had not seen one before. Now the wall was shaded. I could barely make the shadow out, but the measuring strip caught against the stone at one end.

I would fetch her. We would look together, then signal to the people at the house beyond the wall. They would come out with their heads wrapped, one still holding a bowl. While we stood there, a little dust would slip from beneath the roof slab and settle on the wall top.

That would be enough to bring the neighbors. We would take the jars out of the house first. Someone would lead the children across the court; someone else would go for poles. I would be given the task of watching the wall while the others lifted the slab.

I would stand back where I could see the whole seam. The people at the poles would move slowly, waiting for one another. When the slab rose, my sister would brush the grit from beneath it. A cluster of pale many-legged animals would hurry from the joint and vanish under the next block.

I think I would be frightened then, more than I had been when I saw the dust fall. The slab would be overhead, the wall exposed, and everyone would be waiting for me to say if it moved. I would keep my eyes on the seam even when the others began to speak. Once, I would raise my hand because I thought I saw the upper block shift. They would stop. We would wait, and my sister would come to look. She would touch the block, then tell them to continue.

When the slab was lowered and the poles were out, we would stay where we were for a while. The household would bring water. We would drink it in small turns, passing the bowl carefully around the court.

The next morning I would return with the measuring strip. The seam would still be there, though the wall above it had been cleaned and the roof sat a little differently. I would make a fresh mark and leave the old ones visible.

At home, the grazer would nose at the dust on my feet. I would sit beside it under our projecting roof, and before settling down I would tilt my head to listen.
