I would be on the side of a new ridge, where a root has opened a seam in the mineral crust. Water gathers there before it moves on. Above me, a young hollow blade is growing through the leaves. It is not yet tall enough to sound.

I would be a thin growth along the seam: a mat of small plants and the threads they send into the water. At first I would have no clear edge. The moss above would press into me, and root sheaths would pass through me, carrying grains toward the pale walls. My own green points would rise when the water was deep enough and settle again as it drained.

The blade’s root would be among the first I came to know. It would draw water from the seam, then leave the channel partly empty when the weather was dry. Fine threads would grow across the opening and hold beads there. Some of the water would reach me; some would pass onward, carrying pollen into places I had not touched.

When the blade reached the wind, the sound would come down through its stalk and into the crust. I would feel the seam tremble. On a still day there might be only the long vibration from older stands, arriving through the ground; then a gust would catch the young blade and add its note. As it grew, the note would change. Its root would thicken too, pressing the seam wider until a little mineral flaked away and settled among my threads.

I might spread into that opening. For a time it would be difficult to tell which green points belonged to the older mat and which had come up from the hollow below. We would share water whenever the seam filled. In a dry interval, some points would fold down against the crust while others remained raised, sustained by connections I could not follow.

Before heavy rain, the pressure from above would change as the broad leaves lifted. Then water would arrive, cold enough to slow my growth, and the seam would fill beyond the reach of my highest points. Loose grains would move along its bottom. One might lodge against my side and stay there after the water fell, while a root tip laid a clear film over it.

The young blade might break in a storm. Its torn end would seal, and for a while it would still sound, though differently. If it later fell across the ridge, the smallest growths would enter its wet tissues. I might meet it again in the seam, no longer as vibration from above but as dissolved matter passing through the water.

By then, fresh shoots would be rising around me. The seam would be narrower in some places and wider in others, its walls lined with new mineral. I would have grown over part of it and lost part of myself beneath the crust. When the rain returned, water would still find a course through, and my green points would lift into it.
