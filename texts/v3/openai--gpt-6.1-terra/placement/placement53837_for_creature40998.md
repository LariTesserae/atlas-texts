I think I would be on one of the short cable posts, where the walkway leaves the stone apron and crosses the wet ground. Not high enough to see over the basin, at first. A little metal box, perhaps, with a wire descending into a shallow seam of water.

A gauge. One of the things the workers installed because water that stays in the wrong place can eventually reach a foundation.

The wire ends in a probe. I know the water by its height against that probe, and the air by a second instrument fixed under the lip of the box. I have a clock, a store of readings, and a cable that carries them toward the power house. I do not hear the rotors as a bird would hear them, but their vibration reaches my post. It is strongest when the wind comes straight through the western gap. In a long freeze, it travels cleanly through the hardened ground.

At first I might take myself to be the record inside the box: numbers arriving at regular intervals, each replacing a small uncertainty with a measurement. Water rising. Air cooling. Water falling a fraction. For days the readings vary so little that the workers, looking later, might think nothing happened.

But the water does not stand still around the probe. Moss grows against it. A sedge blade bends across the wire and stays there, holding a bead of water well above the pool. In summer a fly settles on the shaded side of my housing, then another. Their weight tells me nothing; I know them only when the worker opens the box and finds their small bodies in the seam of the lid.

One year the water seems to rise earlier than it used to. The worker kneels on the walkway, pulls the probe up, and finds a cuff of green growth around it. They rinse it in the pool and lower it again. The next reading is different.

I had wanted, without quite knowing it, to be a witness to the basin’s slow changes. Instead I have helped make one of them difficult to see. The moss has used the probe as something to grow against. The worker has removed it so I can continue to measure. Both things belong to the place.

After the worker leaves, the lid is not closed perfectly. Wind brings a fine wetness through the gap, and a pale mark appears on the inside of the metal. The readings continue. In the cold season, a crust forms at the edge of the pool; by afternoon it loosens, and the water settles again.

Farther out, the peat is growing in ways I cannot record. The mammal passes beneath the willows without coming near my post. A bird rests on the shed roof and leaves. My reach is a narrow seam of water, a patch of air, the trembling of a cable.

Still, the seam connects to others. When the northern fractures send out more water, some of it eventually arrives here. It lifts around the probe, spreads through the moss, and passes on toward the foundations. I keep its height for a while in my memory, then send the number away.

Next summer someone will come back with tools. They may clean the probe, close the lid, and check the clock. Or they may decide the water has found another route and move me farther along the walkway. Either way, the little clear space around the probe will begin filling again.
