I would be outside a shelter with a pole in my hands, waiting for the person inside to pass me the last of the bowls.

We are moving because grit has been falling through the roof since the light came down the wall. Not much. A few grains on a sleeping wrap, a few more in the hollow of an overturned bowl. We have brushed them away twice. The third time, the woman who sleeps nearest the entrance puts on her mask and starts taking the fitted stones out.

I would be the one who goes ahead. Not because I know where the ground is safe, but because I know where another group built a shelter yesterday, and I think I can find it again.

We carry the bowls nested together, their rims wrapped in fiber. The pole tells me very little at first. It touches firm stone, then firm stone again. Runners part around its tip and close behind it. I tap the floor anyway; the person behind me can hear where I have stepped.

The marker at the edge of the trough is still there, though one of its stones has fallen. I put it back. Farther on, the trough itself has narrowed. A fresh ridge has grown along one side, lifting a pale slab at an angle. There are runners underneath it, climbing over the lip of the opening and disappearing. I remember crossing here with room for three people abreast. Now we turn sideways and pass one at a time, holding the bowls high.

A broad animal is standing where the other shelter should be. At first I think it has crushed the shelter, but then it moves a limb and I see the entrance beyond it, still closed with stones. The animal is working its mouth over a damp patch. A small trickle has come down the ridge beside the shelter; the people inside may not have heard it begin.

I go close enough to call. The broad animal shifts, and runners spill from a fold of its leg. I step back quickly. Someone moves a stone from the entrance, then another, and a face appears. When I point to the water, the person looks at it for a long moment before ducking inside for a bowl.

By then I am less certain what I am here to do. I had thought I was leading my group to a place where we could rest. Now we set down our things and help take the stones from this entrance too. The damp ridge may hold for another day, or it may not. The people who slept beneath it want their water first.

We fill every bowl we have. The trickle carries a little grit; we let it settle while we pull cord and wraps from the shelter. The broad animal returns twice, and twice we wait for it to finish scraping the stone. Nobody tries to drive it away. There is room enough, for now.

When we leave, there are seven of us, with two poles between us and more water than I expected to carry. I take the rear pole. Another person knows a shelf beyond the next band of light where we might lean the slabs together, if it has not split.

As we go, I look back for the three-stone marker. From here it is hidden behind the new ridge. Tomorrow, if the ground allows, I will come back and build another where it can be seen.
