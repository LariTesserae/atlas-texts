At first I think I would be in one of the passages behind the room, where the wind arrives already carrying news of the entrance: a husk tumbling end over end, a change in the pressure against a leaf. But nothing there needs to hear the whole room. The ants have their roads; the roots find their way without it.

I would be nearer the front, under the lip of an alcove, a small spider with a web stretched between a clay fragment and the stone.

The clay would be the better anchor. Its edge is rough and curled, and silk catches readily there. The stone is less obliging. I would have to lay several threads before one held, then cross and recross the gap, pulling each line taut enough to carry a tremor but not so taut that the wind would tear it loose.

From my corner I would know the room as a set of disturbances. Ants passing beneath the web would give the clay a faint, continuous tick. A beetle would make a slower, heavier scrape. When a lizard dashed across the floor, the sound would travel through the stone before the loose dust reached my threads. I would feel it and draw my legs in.

The bats would be difficult to live under. Their quarrels would shake powder from the crevice, and some of it would settle on my silk. Once, when they left together, a wing might strike the outer lines and pull half the web away. I would wait until the beating stopped, then go out over what remained, gathering and replacing what I could.

I would not catch much every day. An ant might touch a thread and withdraw; another might blunder in far enough to struggle. A moth would be more dangerous to take. Its wingbeats could ruin the web before I reached it, and its scales might come away against the silk, leaving the moth free. But now and then a smaller insect would hold, and afterward there would be a dry husk to cut loose.

The thin band of daylight would creep across the floor below me. I would seldom enter it. From the shaded alcove I could see legs moving through the light and disappearing again, and sometimes the dark shape of a lizard waiting where the band ended.

When the plateau wind came through, I would brace against the clay and feel the web pull at my feet. Loose leaves and husks would skitter past below. Then the air would ease, and I would go out to test the lines, one after another, while the ants resumed their traffic along the wall.
