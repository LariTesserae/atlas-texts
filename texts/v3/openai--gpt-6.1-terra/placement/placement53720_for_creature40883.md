I would be on a ledge halfway up a ridge, wedged between two warm bodies. My tail would lie across the back of the one below me, its tip curled loosely around hers. Above us the stone would still be giving out heat.

I would be a young pod-breaker, old enough to leave the ledge with the band, not yet good at keeping what I opened.

In the dark, I might wake when the animal beside me shifted. The noise would already be there, filling the basin below us. I could feel the quick work of her fingers in my coat, finding the places I could not reach. Farther along the ledge, another tail would tighten and loosen. We would settle again.

By the time we climbed down, moisture would be running in threads along the ridge. I would lick it from the stone, then follow the others into the litter, lifting leaves with my hands and nosing into the soft places beneath them. A ridgeback herd might have passed during the night. Its track would still be open between the plants, with broken stems lying across it and seed-pickers moving in and out of the folds of the dung.

A pod would be easy enough to find. Getting into it would take longer.

I would hold it between my front feet and test a pebble in my mouth. If the pebble were too large, my head would dip when I lifted it. If it were too small, the blows would fall quickly without making much impression. I would strike, adjust the pod, strike again. At first I would often hit the ground beside it. The others would look up at the tapping; one might come close and wait.

When the shell finally split, she would thrust her hand into the opening before I had dropped the stone. I would pull the pod toward me, shriek, lose it under our feet, and find it again with less inside. There would still be a fragment to carry away. I would eat it under a fern while the other animal searched the leaves for what we had scattered.

Later, I might stop to watch an older pod-breaker. She would turn a pebble once in her mouth before striking. Her feet would hold the pod so firmly that it scarcely moved. When it broke, she would snatch up a piece and back away at once. I would follow her, then lose interest when a bank of leaves began to shake and a ridgeback pushed through.

The grazer would pass close. I would feel the weight of it before I saw the dark rise of its back. Behind it, a calf would come scrambling over a patch of exposed stone. One of the adults would wait until the calf found its footing, then the herd would go on, stems closing slowly behind them.

I would return to the pod husks. My pebble might still be there, or it might have sunk into the litter among a dozen others. I would pick one up and try its weight.

That night, back on the ledge, I would have sore places in my mouth and another tail laid over my back. Sometime before morning the stone beneath us might shift. The animal touching me would tighten her tail; I would catch the one beside me, and we would all be moving before the ledge had finished settling. We would find another place to press together, higher up, where the rock was dry enough to grip.
