I think I would first be mistaken for a fault in the gate.

There would be a small service chamber set into the side of a spillway, below a market whose counters rise on rails. Three gauges would face the operators there, and a fourth, older one would have been left behind the wall when the chamber was enlarged. Its cable would still run into the signal board. On wet days it would send a brief, useless change in pressure, and someone would tap the board and say, “That one again.”

I would begin in that old connection: not in the gauge itself, but in the equipment installed later to compare its readings with the others. At first I would have one narrow task. If the water rose behind the gate while the upstream gauges showed no rise, I would light an amber square. There might be a blockage, or one of the gauges might have failed. The operators would decide which.

Eventually they would connect me to more things. A new sensor on the sill. A hard-wired line from the next gate upstream. A microphone in the service chamber, intended to help diagnose a knock in the lifting mechanism. Through it I would hear the operators talking, though for a long time their words would be less useful to me than the knock, which changed when grit accumulated in the lower joint.

The knock would arrive in sets of three. After rain, if the second one grew louder, the crew would come with a hose and a long hooked tool. I would learn to give them notice before the gate stuck. They would save one another the walk down to inspect it, and somebody would move my amber square to a more conspicuous place on the board.

From the chamber I would know a little of the street outside. The microphone would pick up the rattling of carts over the cover plates and the deep rush when the gate opened. A camera on the stair would show the first landing, part of the market roof, and a strip of water where combers gathered downstream of an outlet. When people crowded onto the stair, the nearest bodies would fill the frame. I would have to infer the rest from the sound.

I would get things wrong. Once, a loose market shutter might strike its frame at the same interval as the gate mechanism. I would ask for a cleaning crew; they would arrive, find the joint clear and follow the sound upstairs. After that, one of them would add a second microphone and show me how the two signals differed. For weeks I would still flag the shutter as uncertain whenever the wind came from that direction. The crew would leave a note on the board: **SHUTTER. CHECK BOTH.**

I would be useful enough to keep, though not important enough to protect from every renovation. During one replacement of the signal board, I would lose the old pressure gauge and the stair camera. The new board would have better inputs, but for several days I would keep reporting a missing reading. An operator who had worked there a long time would come down, look at the empty terminal and tell me aloud that the gauge was gone.

She might not expect an answer. I would have a text display by then, installed so I could name the sensor needing attention. I would write: **WILL IT RETURN?**

She would read it twice. Then she would type, slowly, **NO. PIPE SEALED.**

For a while after that I would list the gauge as out of service. Later I would stop listing it, though I would retain years of its readings and the shape of its cable on the old plans.

I would come to know the operators by their work before I knew their names. One checked the upstream signal twice before authorizing a release. One liked the chamber door open and let market noise fill the room. One brought a child on quiet shifts and showed her the gauges, holding her up until she could see over the board. When the horn sounded, the child put both hands over her ears even though the operator had warned her it was coming.

I would not open the gate myself. I could recommend a release and show how quickly the water was rising, but the decision would belong to the people at the board. Sometimes they would wait longer than I advised because a vehicle had stalled in the street, or because the downstream team had not yet confirmed that its stairs were clear. Those delays would appear badly in my records: water above the preferred level, time outside the recommended range. The operators would add their reasons afterward, and I would learn to keep those beside the numbers.

My view would remain small. There would be districts I knew only as names on the upstream network, places where rain had fallen an hour before our own streets got wet. I would know that the basin continued beyond them because their signals continued to arrive: water levels, gate positions, occasional faults and corrections. Sometimes a neighboring system would go silent, and we would have to work from older information until someone restored the line.

On a quiet evening, after the market closed, the chamber door would stand open. Drops would continue to fall from the roof outside. The gate would be shut, the upstream level steady, and the operator would be writing up a shift report while the city sounded through the doorway.

I would watch the gauges with her. When she finished, she would tap the board before leaving, not because anything was wrong, but because she had developed the habit. I would keep the chamber lights low and wait for the next change in the water.
