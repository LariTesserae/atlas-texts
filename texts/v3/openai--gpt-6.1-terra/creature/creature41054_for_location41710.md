The shelters are inhabited, though at first their occupants resemble the material stacked inside them.

Each has a back of overlapping dark plates, broad at the shoulders and tapering toward the ground. Beneath the plates is a pale body with several jointed limbs, folded so closely that it is difficult to count them. The outer plates are gathered from beneath the fronds and fitted into place one by one. They protect the softer body from the vibration of the ground, though they never quite still it. Along the edges of the plates, fine fringes are always trembling.

The occupant of the shelter beside the sorted heap is crouched with one limb extended toward the smallest plate. The tip rests just short of it. Dust has collected on both, but not in the narrow space between. The creature is reaching, and has been reaching for longer than the loose roof fiber has been falling. When it closes its limb around the plate, it will draw it inward and test it against a gap near its neck.

They are not equally slow in every part. Their fringes respond at once to changes in the chord. A deeper note from the mountains passes through a shelter and the fringes turn, each by a minute amount. Along the creature’s underside are bands of elastic tissue that tighten and release with the sound. From this continual work it draws what it needs to live. It does not eat the fronds; it takes only the dead plates and loose fibers, leaving the living growth undisturbed.

Their shelters are places to reduce the force of the noise, not to escape it. The leaned slabs interrupt some vibrations and concentrate others. A well-made shelter gives its occupant a steady set of pressures to brace against. The doorway faces away from the seam because the needles produce a sharper, less dependable part of the chord. When a needle changes its note, the whole basin may quiver. In a poor shelter the plates on an occupant’s back strike together until their edges chip.

The smooth-handled hammer belongs to a larger one, resting beneath the second roof. Its hammering limb lies across its body, with the end curled around a loose length of frond fiber. There is a polished notch in the stone beside it where the hammer has been set down many times. Its roof is lower than the other, and its slabs have been adjusted with care: the smallest stones are wedged beneath the largest, leaving a narrow space at the back where the ground’s deeper vibration can enter.

For much of their lives they remain at a basin. They mend their coverings, reinforce the shelters and sort fallen plates into sizes useful for different parts of the body. A plate that is too brittle for a shoulder may still serve along the lower edge, where wear is greatest. Fiber is drawn into cords and fastened around the plates. The work lasts so long that the living fronds sometimes grow over a pile before its sorting is complete.

They can leave. When the fringe of new growth reaches bare stone, or when a shelter’s familiar pressures begin to change, one may unfold and cross to another basin. Its limbs are longer than they first appear, with flattened ends that hold against the glass terraces. Movement on the open ground exposes it to the full chord; the fringes lie close and the back plates draw tight. On reaching shelter it may remain motionless while the plates gradually separate again.

The unreliable distances do not seem to surprise them. Their routes are remembered through pressure rather than appearance: the particular tremor of a seam, the bass note beneath a terrace, the thin ringing of glass at an edge. These signs can repeat, too, but they are more useful than a summit that moves behind the traveler. One individual bears several pale scars where the plates have been worn through, perhaps from a crossing that took much longer than expected.

They meet rarely. At neighboring shelters, two may be aware of one another for years before either moves. A creature can make a deliberate signal by pressing the edge of a plate to the ground. The sound does not rise above the plateau’s roar, but it changes the pressure under a listener’s limbs. An answer comes the same way: a small addition to the chord, held until the other has had time to feel it.

Sometimes a young one is found beneath an adult’s back plates. At first it is little more than a pale fold with a fringe that responds to the ground. It grows its own limbs slowly, sheltered from the harshest vibrations, and receives a few small plates from the adult’s covering. Eventually it moves out beside the larger body. It may spend a long time there before reaching for the first plate it will fit for itself.

The unfinished plateau makes new work for them. A rising terrace can alter the pressure beneath a shelter. A mountain whose outlines briefly coincide can send a deep note through a basin, loosening a slab that has stood securely for ages. The inhabitants brace, adjust, and continue. By the time a roof is mended, the shape of the ground may have changed again.
