The grazers are soft beneath their pale armor. Each carries a row of overlapping plates, chalk-colored where the surface has dried and almost translucent underneath. Their many legs are short and crowded together. A single grazer can move with surprising speed, but most spend their lives pressed among others, advancing a fraction at a time as their mouthparts scour the stone.

They recognize food by its chemistry and by the minute unevenness of the bacterial film. Where the film is thick, their scraping leaves pale tracks. Where it is thin, they rasp patiently at the rock itself. Their droppings collect in the hollows and feed other films, darker and finer than those growing at the mineral seeps. Young grazers live among these, small enough to pass through spaces that an adult’s legs cannot enter.

Their bodies bear marks of the crowd: worn plates, missing limbs, patches where the outer covering has healed around a bite. Some have broad backs that shed the press of animals above them. Others are narrower and slip down between their neighbors to reach damp stone. During a flood, their grip is strong but not always strong enough. The sheets they form on the rising channel walls continually come apart and reassemble, animals swept loose and caught again by the legs of those holding fast.

The long-legged hunters are darker, though darkness matters little here. Their bodies are narrow enough to pass between two overlapping grazers, and their legs end in hooked pads that can take hold of a moving back. They pause often, feeling through their feet for the rhythm of the animals beneath them. When one strikes, the motion is quick and local: a few grazers recoil, then close over the gap.

Not all of these hunters eat grazers. Some carry thin sensory limbs stretched ahead of them and wait for the characteristic jolt of another hunter’s strike. They approach across the shifting backs, lifting each foot clear before setting it down. A caught hunter may shed a leg and escape; the abandoned limb twitches among the grazers until something eats it.

The heavy diggers follow the channels slowly. Their folded skins retain moisture, and grit gathers in the creases. Around the blunt head the sensory pits are especially dense, each holding a fine structure that responds to vibrations in the ground and water. A digger may rest with its face against a fissure for a long time, breathing in shallow pulses, before pushing its broad forelimbs into the stone’s softer seams.

It eats what it exposes: grazers, their eggs, the hunters caught among them, and wet scrapings from the fissure walls. Its digging also leaves fresh surfaces for the bacterial films. Old diggings can be recognized by the congregations around them, long after the animal has moved on. The diggers sometimes return, feeling along the same broken edges. Their young stay close to these places, sheltering in cuts too narrow for an adult and feeding where the smaller animals gather.

The callers cling to the channel walls by flattened undersides. Their bodies are no larger than a thumb, but each contains a stiffened plate that can be flexed against a ridged edge. The resulting rattle travels through the animal’s grip into the limestone. Their softer undersides are protected from drying by the close fit against the rock and by the bodies packed around them.

A caller feeds with delicate filaments extended from beneath its body, collecting bacteria and fine debris from the damp surface. It can hold its place for days without shifting. When it does move, it releases one edge at a time, keeping the rest anchored. The smallest live in the spaces between adults, where their calls are thin and high.

The noise is not uniform, even when it seems to fill everything. Near a moving digger, the rattles break off in a widening patch. Behind it, they start again unevenly: one pulse, then several, then a hard rushing chorus. A hunter crossing the wall produces a narrower silence. Callers beyond it increase their rate, and the change travels farther than the hunter itself. Some predators use that disturbance to find their way; others arrive at the edge of a silence and wait for whatever caused it.

When rain comes, the first water carries dust, fragments of film and loose eggs into the channels. As the flow rises, the inhabitants of the retained pools emerge. The flat swimmers unfold from beneath stones, their translucent bodies edged with short paddling fringes. Their gripping mouths attach to grazers, callers and one another. In the turbulent flood they can release and catch again, moving from body to body through water thick with displaced life.

The larger blind swimmers have spent the dry weeks in narrow chambers where a little water remains. Their skins are dark and slick, their mouths wide. They leave shelter only when water fills the passages above them, following the current into the temporary pools. There they feed heavily, taking stranded grazers and flat swimmers. As the water falls, they turn back toward the cracks. Those that remain too long in an open hollow must cross wet stone with slow, powerful contractions, vulnerable to the hunters waiting along the rim.

Eggs and young are left in many places by the flood. Some cling beneath the pebbled folds of a digger’s skin until they are rubbed off against rock. Others lodge in old burrows, in packed grazer droppings, or in the damp seams beneath silent callers. The pools shrink around them. Flat swimmers gather at the last connected edges; blind swimmers disappear one after another below the stone.

Then the callers exposed by the falling water begin to sound. At first their rattles come separately from the black walls. More join them as their plates dry enough to move. Grazers return to the fresh films, and hunters step out across their backs. By the time the basin is full of noise again, a hand on the limestone can feel the bodies still hidden within it.
