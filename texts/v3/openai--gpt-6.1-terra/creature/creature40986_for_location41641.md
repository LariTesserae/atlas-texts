The first to occupy a new crack are almost invisible. Colorless microbes gather where the warm water meets the sea, making thin, slippery layers over the fresh stone. They live on what rises from inside the mountain. Their growth catches black grains, and soon the crack has a dark border that can be found by the little jointed grazers.

The grazers come in several kinds. Some are flat and many-legged, able to pass between a tube and the rock it stands on; others have high, hinged backs that protect their softer undersides. They scrape the deposits with mouthparts that work sideways. When a feeding plate snaps shut nearby, they stop and grip the stone until it opens again. Pale grains pass from their bodies and collect in the sheltered corners of the fissures.

The white tubes belong to animals anchored deep in the crown. Each animal extends a cluster of stiff, feather-edged plates into the water, then folds them away when a neighbor expands or a piece of grit strikes. Those at the hottest openings have short, thick plates; farther out, where the seep has mixed with seawater, the plates grow longer and finer. Their tubes accumulate one layer at a time. An old animal may sit within several abandoned collars of its own shell, raised higher each year as the rock around it becomes crowded.

The translucent bladders are animals too, though it is difficult to see where one ends and another begins. They swell slowly as water enters them, showing branching threads and darker knots inside. Some remain inflated for days. Others collapse with a report that passes through the rock and startle every feeding crown nearby. Afterward they lie in folds, nearly invisible, until water gathers in them again. Small grazers pick at their surfaces; the bladders seem not to notice.

Among the tubes lie the broad hunters. Their backs carry the same black dust as the stone, and their edges fit themselves around whatever shell or fracture lies beneath them. A grazer crossing one may walk almost to its center before a pale limb unfolds and brings it toward a mouth hidden underneath. The hunters move rarely but are not permanently fixed. When a seep cools and its grazers thin out, a hunter may work itself a body-length toward a warmer opening over the course of many days.

The rust-red crust grows over old tubes and bare ridges alike. Up close it is not a single skin but a multitude of small, layered animals, each with a narrow opening through which it draws water. Their edges grow together into raised collars. They can eventually seal a fissure, leaving the last inhabitants of the crack surrounded by their own empty tubes. Where the stone breaks again, a fresh white surface appears among the red, and the slow work begins there instead.

Above all of them hang the silver animals. They are not attached to the crown, but they remain over it so steadily that their ranks seem another part of its growth. Each holds itself head-down and draws water through a series of fine openings along its sides. From below, only their flashing bellies and the occasional movement of their tails are visible. When one turns, its neighbors follow, not quite together; the change travels along the rank and is gone.

They descend closer when the water carries many pale grains. At other times they keep above the sharpest ridges, where old strips of skin still cling. After a deep report from the mountain they hold their places, scarcely moving at all. Below them the hunters remain folded, the grazers cling to the rock, and the white feeding plates stay closed. Then a plate opens, another makes room for it, and the crowded crown resumes its work.
