The city’s inhabitants measure the day partly by the heat of surfaces. In the morning a wall may still be warm enough to work against; by afternoon the same wall can make a narrow passage uncomfortable. People learn which stairs have rails that can be held barehanded, which yards are crossed most easily in the shadow of a chimney, and where a pipe under the paving makes an otherwise cool threshold warm.

They move deliberately on the climbs. New arrivals sometimes mistake the slow pace for leisure until they try to keep it on the unfinished streets. Loads are divided among more carriers than their weight alone would seem to require, and benches are placed at the turns of steep passages. People sit on them without apology, talking or waiting for their breathing to settle.

Many households share a wall with a workshop, and the distinction between home and work is not always clear. A wire drawer may live above the room where the wire is annealed; a family may keep its tools in a courtyard used by three neighboring shops. The quiet machinery permits conversation, though workers still develop the habit of touching a wall or pipe when a sound changes. A knock repeated at the wrong interval can bring someone out into the passage to listen.

There are people whose work is to follow the water: clearing mineral deposits from condenser channels, testing the insulation around the hot pipes, and collecting what the morning cloud leaves on the roofs. Children are often sent up after a cloud has passed to check that the gutters are open. They know the roofs as a second set of streets, though adults warn them away from the edges and the hot ducts.

Others tend the city’s allowance for movement. They inspect the black material in the seams, press fresh compound into places where it has pulled loose, and record cracks that cross a block rather than follow a joint. Surveyors make their rounds with marked rods and notebooks. Their measurements are discussed in workshops and at meal tables: not because every change means danger, but because the uneven rising is a matter everyone lives with. A door that has begun to stick may be mentioned alongside a cliff mark that has shifted.

The crews on the upper streets have an especially patient trade. They lay a course of stone, check it, and sometimes leave it through a season before building higher. Blocks stacked beside the foundations become familiar landmarks. People passing them may know which are intended for a stairwell, which for a shared wall, and which have been set aside because a new survey suggests the foundation needs another seam.

The pale jointed creatures are known to the tank workers, who sometimes find them folded into narrow spaces behind supports. Their bodies are light in color even when the dust around them is dark. They drink at the drains after nightfall, placing their feet carefully before drawing themselves forward. A worker returning across a roof may see several together, but a dropped tool or a heavy step sends them into separate cracks. People generally leave them alone; some keep a small section beneath a tank clear of stored equipment, so the creatures can retreat there when the roof is busy.

The branching films in the pools have their own quiet observers. Condenser tenders watch them return after a basin has been cleaned, first as fine lines near the cooler center, then as a delicate network visible from the edge. The films come apart under a finger or a falling drop. Whether they are alive, and what they take from the water, are questions people argue over; samples carried into the warmer workshops often lose their shape before anyone can agree on what to look for.

The winged thing above the peaks belongs less to daily life than to the long intervals between tasks. A person resting on an upper stair may point it out to a companion, and both will watch until one sees a movement the other has missed. It is sometimes gone for weeks. Then it appears again over a different peak, holding its place while the cloud passes below.

For all the city’s exposed position, much of its life is close and domestic. Voices cross the yards; meals are carried between adjoining rooms; neighbors know when a workshop has stopped by the absence of its faint vibration. In the evening, people come out onto the roofs or the unfinished streets, where the stone is still warm and the lamps have begun to show. They look down over the pipes and chimneys, or up toward foundations that will eventually hold another row of rooms, and make plans that can wait until the next survey.
