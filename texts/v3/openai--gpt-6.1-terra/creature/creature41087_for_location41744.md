In the packing around the drain, a rotifer is feeding.

It is smaller than the bead of water that holds it. Its body is nearly transparent, and at its head a ring of cilia turns in rippling succession, drawing bacteria and loose particles toward its mouth. When the floor shudders, the bead changes shape. The rotifer folds in on itself and grips a moss thread with its forked foot. Once the water settles, it extends again.

There are others among the threads: nematodes moving through films of water, single-celled grazers, bacteria gathered on the rough fibers and in the seams beneath them. The green patches on the mortar hold a thinner community, continually wetted by mist. Some of these organisms arrived in reservoir water; others may have come on the moss itself, or in dust before the surfaces became wet. The dam is new, and their footholds are small.

They live within the changing water rather than alongside it. A discharge can lift loose cells from the trough and carry them across the plate. Most pass through the grate or are drawn down with the pumps. A few catch in a seam, or settle into the packing when the flow slackens. There they feed, divide, and are eventually disturbed again.

The rotifer has no need to hear the machinery. The tremor reaches it through the water and the moss; the current moves it when its grip fails. Between one opening of the valve and the next, it feeds in the brief, unsteady shelter of the drain.
