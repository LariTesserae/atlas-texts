The most numerous inhabitants are the clay-colored walkers. At rest they resemble crumbs of the wall; moving, they lift themselves on a surprising number of fine, hinged legs. They have no eyes that can be distinguished from the pale knots along their heads. They find the water by touching the floor, turning their bodies a fraction at each touch. A walker with young beneath it moves more slowly but takes a straighter course. The young cling to its underside until the water is gone, then drop away one by one and begin feeling for paths of their own.

Some of the walkers leave damp lines in front of themselves. A line may turn around a ridge that has not yet risen, or skirt a patch where another animal’s shadow will later fall. The walker follows it closely, stopping whenever the line stops. If the line runs out before the animal reaches the water, the animal waits there. It may be seen again after the water has vanished, moving elsewhere in the chamber; or only its line may remain.

The larvae at the water’s edge belong to more than one kind. The smallest are almost clear, with a dark thread visible inside each body. Others are thicker and chalk-white, and leave a momentary hollow wherever they press against the film. They drink by laying their mouths flat. When the water is too thin for this, they roll partly onto their sides and remain there until another larva pushes them upright.

Among them lies the soft hunter. Its color is much the same as theirs, but it does not bend to drink. It has a crease along its length that opens when prey comes close enough. After feeding, it contracts around what it has caught and becomes difficult to distinguish from an empty skin. Later it moves away, leaving a small dry shape on the floor. The walkers sometimes carry these shapes toward the wall and place them in seams that are beginning to close.

The winged animals gather where the ceiling is lowest. Their bodies are slender and their wings are so clear that a flight is easiest to follow by its shadow. They touch down briefly on the moss, though it is not clear whether they eat it. When the moss is wet and pointing upward, they settle facing upward too, their heads close to its tips. A drop may rise past them into the stone without disturbing a wing.

Beneath the moss, the larvae continue to gather even when no drop falls. Their bodies make a pale, restless patch on the floor. By the time the next skin of water appears, some have grown too large to cross the chamber quickly. They arrive at the edge after the others and drink from places that already seem dry.

There are animals visible through the shelter as well: long-legged ones, and low round ones that move without any legs showing. They pass at a distance behind the chips. Occasionally one approaches until its outline nearly fills the gap, then moves away again. No animal in the chamber follows it. The walkers feel along the outside of the shelter as they do the walls, sometimes pausing at the gap, their forelegs lifted.

The inhabitants have no common resting time. Even when their bodies pause at the fall of a grain, their shadows continue to shift among the ridges. Afterward, the larvae resume drinking or waiting, the hunter settles around its prey, and the walkers pick up their careful paths. In the brightest period, newly hatched young can be found scattered over the dry patch, each casting a small, sharp shadow longer than itself.
