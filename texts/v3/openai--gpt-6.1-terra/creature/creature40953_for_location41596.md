The animals in the doorways are called hinge-backs, though they have no names for themselves. Their pale plates overlap like the chips of the drain. They spend much of their time pressed into the joints between stones, grazing on the soft edge of the mineral crust. When the water rises beneath a house, they feel the pressure through their feet and retreat upward before a bead appears in the paving.

They do not enter the basin. Its surface is too treacherous, and the falling drops strike with enough force to break them. They gather instead where the overflow passes under the rim, reaching into the current with fine, jointed mouthparts. Their young are almost transparent. For a while they travel beneath the adults’ plates, where they can be seen shifting like pale grit.

The builders live deeper in the masonry. A few of their passages open onto the streets, but most mouths are hidden under roof flakes or behind stones that look fixed until they are pushed from within. A builder can flatten itself to pass through a joint, then unfold in a hollow on the other side. Its body is soft and gray as wet slate, with several short arms and a fringe of feelers around the face. The feelers taste the stone and lie flat when a drop strikes the basin.

They are rarely still, although their movements do not amount to much distance. One may spend an hour turning a flake against another flake, testing its edge, setting it aside, taking it up again. They keep pebbles for weights and wedges, and thin chips for scraping crust from the joints. The shard on the basin rim is a gauge: a thread drawn through its hole can be laid against the lip to see whether the water has risen. The thread is kept inside, where the current cannot take it.

The houses are not all dwellings. Some hold tools; others protect openings into the lower rock. In the broadest cell, several builders sleep pressed together, their arms tucked close. Elsewhere there are small chambers containing heaps of scraped mineral and the cast plates of hinge-backs. The builders eat both, breaking them down with their hands and mouths. They leave scraps at certain thresholds, and the animals return to those places.

They work by touch and by the sounds carried through the stone. A flake tapped against a wall tells a neighbor which joint needs clearing; a hand held on a roof can feel the answer. The basin’s reports are so loud that they cannot be used for ordinary signals, but the builders notice changes in them. A drop striking a flake left too near the water has a different voice from one striking bare rock. Someone will come out, collect the flake, and carry it home.

The settlement is old enough for every threshold to have been worn smooth, but the builders do not count its age. They know which roofs have been lifted and replaced, which stones tend to shift when the drain is cleared, and which passages become difficult after a new growth of crust. They remember these things together, hands resting on the same wall.

From above, there is often nothing to see. A hinge-back waits in a doorway. The bead remains between the paving stones. The basin is full to its familiar lip. Under the roofs, hands continue their small adjustments, and each falling drop sends its report through the homes and bodies of the people below.
