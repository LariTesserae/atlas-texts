The moss lives in three capped sockets, each tuft confined to the shallow space left around the monitoring contacts. Its roots—or the fine structures made to serve as roots—hold to a mesh beneath the caps. They draw what they can from the salts that the water brings down: little enough that a new tip takes days to show.

When the valve opens, the exposed tips stiffen under ice. The moss does not wake so much as endure. Some of its cells lose water before it can freeze inside them; others hold their shape through the cold by means designed into them. After the rain, a drop caught deep in a tuft may remain liquid longer than the frost on the tiles. The moss uses it slowly. A change in its chemistry travels through the contacts and becomes a reading somewhere above the warm panel.

The three tufts are not quite alike anymore. One grows nearest the trough and receives more of the white mineral dust. Its lower threads have become crusted, and new growth leans away from the deposit. Another sits under the edge of a pipe, where a bead sometimes hangs for hours before falling. Its tips are flattened in a small, repeating pattern. The third is the least disturbed. It has spread a thin green film over one side of its socket, so slowly that the film might be mistaken for a stain.

Around them, the room continues to acquire its parts. The mechanism behind the plates brings each one forward; sealant rises; a new edge cools in the air. The moss nearest the moving wall receives a trace of that sealant’s vapor, and its reading changes briefly. When the vapor clears, the reading settles, though not quite where it was before.

There are shapes on the floor for things that will come later. One lies close to the third socket. Frost gathers there first, outlining a narrow base with two feet. If the fitting occupies that place, it may shade part of the moss and leave another part exposed to the water running off its edge. Until then, the tuft grows across the faint line, its newest threads too small to interrupt the frost.
