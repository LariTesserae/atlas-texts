The termites are easiest to find where rain has exposed them. Break open a damp wall and pale workers move through the torn chambers, carrying bits of earth in their jaws. Soldiers gather at the opening, their darker heads held outward, while workers begin to close it around them. By the following day the breach may be sealed, leaving only a rough seam on the slope.

There are many colonies in the ridges, some occupying old chambers built over and under by others. They follow buried wood, dead roots and layers of fallen leaves, extending galleries as food becomes available. Some tend beds of fungus on the plant material they collect. Winged reproductives emerge after certain rains and rise through the trees in great numbers; most fall prey to birds, frogs and ants, but a few shed their wings and disappear into cracks in pairs.

Ants have their own routes through the country. Their files cross roots, skirt pools and climb fallen branches, narrowing wherever a twig bridges a channel. Some carry fragments of prey or soft white termite bodies. Others bring seeds and scraps of fruit to nests beneath the litter. A line can persist for hours, though a falling leaf or a hunting lizard may scatter it briefly.

The damp ground supports animals smaller than the ants: mites among the husks, springtails in rotting wood, pale grubs feeding inside fallen branches. Fungi spread through the debris in fine threads and send up short-lived caps after rain. Snails graze over these growths and leave silvery trails on the roots. Beetles spend the day under bark or in hollow stems; at dusk some fly clumsily toward openings in the canopy, striking leaves as they go.

Frogs occupy different parts of the hollows. The larger ones sit at the edges of persistent pools, partly hidden under leaves, while smaller frogs call from wet stems above them. Some leave eggs in the water; others seek pockets of moisture that will last through a dry spell. When a pool contracts, tadpoles crowd into its deepest part and leaf litter collects around them. A long gap between rains can empty it entirely.

Lizards make use of the warmer mound faces, coming out where sunlight reaches a wall or fallen trunk. They hunt the insects moving over those surfaces, then retreat into the cracks. Skinks nose through loose litter lower down. Snakes follow both, moving quietly along roots and through the spaces beneath them; some also visit the hollows where frogs gather.

Birds work through the layers of the forest. Small insect-eaters pick along branches and investigate curled dead leaves, while larger birds turn over litter or probe into softened wood. Fruit-eaters move between trees as crops ripen, dropping seeds and pulp onto the slopes. Some of the seeds lodge in old nest walls, where a new root can enter the porous earth and widen it as it grows.

Bats emerge from deep cavities under the canopy, passing close to trunks and hanging vines. Some catch insects in the dim passages; others visit flowers or carry fruit to sheltered perches. At dawn, scraps beneath a roost mark where they have fed. Monkeys begin moving through the crowns above them, breaking twigs and dislodging leaves. What falls becomes food or shelter below, worked into the litter by many small animals and, eventually, into the ridges by termites.
