The dark mat on the roof is alive, though it grows so slowly that its growth is hard to distinguish from the settling of dust. It holds to the mineral grains with threads finer than the silver ladders in the cracks. When a flake shifts, the threads stretch. Most break; a few remain attached, and the mat begins again from those.

It has no need to find food in the street. It takes what it can from the water and the stone, making use of the small differences between them. Beneath the staircase, where the current enters the bright joint, the water carries one balance of dissolved minerals. A finger’s width away, beside a fastening in the roof, it carries another. The mat grows most densely across the boundary. Its cells pass charged particles from one to the next, drawing a little energy from the difference.

The green filaments under the balcony do something similar, but they are not the same kind of life. They grow toward the seams in the paving and make use of the light. Their tips divide whenever they are damaged. When the flow reverses, some tear loose and lodge among the glass vanes, where they remain until a stronger pulse draws them into a pipe. Most do not survive that journey. A few return through the windows of the opposite house, pale and shortened, and adhere wherever the current lets them rest.

Between the two growths there are others, too small to be visible as growth. They occupy the wet faces of the silver threads and the inner surfaces of the pipes. Some build thin cases from minerals; some consume those cases after their makers have died. They have settled in layers according to the water each layer can bear. A reversal may bring one colony the substance it needs and poison the next. The survivors spread over the empty places before the direction changes again.

The street’s machinery does not know them individually. It registers the effects of their presence. A film thickens inside a pipe, and the pressure changes; a vane turns a fraction farther; somewhere in the rock, a valve adjusts. On the roof, the mat grows over a flake that covers part of a lit seam. The seam brightens. The extra light reaches the balcony, and several filaments begin to lengthen.

This is as close as the inhabitants come to keeping house together. None of them tends the street. They live in the adjustments it makes, and their living adds small errors to its measurements. The machinery corrects for these, while the growths continue wherever its corrections leave them room.

After a pressure door opens and the metallic chord has passed through the street, the mat remains pressed against the roof. Its outermost cells have been damaged by the sudden force. For a time the charged particles move unevenly across it, stopping at the torn places. Then the living edges begin to close around the tears. Above them the staircase carries its current into the rock; below them the seam shines through the mineral dust.
