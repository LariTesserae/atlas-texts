The largest animals in the chamber are called, by no one who has seen them, the keepers. They are soft-bodied beneath their armored backs, with four pairs of limbs folded so close that a resting animal looks like a piece of paving lifted slightly at one edge. Their mouths are broad and delicate. When a keeper moves, it tastes the stone as it goes, drawing in the film, the silt, and whatever small bodies have failed to get out of its way.

A keeper may spend weeks in one hollow. Its back gathers a pale coat; snails cross it, grazing as readily there as on the wall. At intervals it rises on its limbs and the water flows beneath it, carrying away waste and stirring loose the crumbs accumulated around its mouth. Then it settles again, sometimes on the same patch, sometimes a little farther along a ridge. The clean shapes left behind are the surest evidence of its journeys.

The crustaceans are most numerous near the passage. They gather where its slope meets the chamber floor, climbing over one another to intercept the particles that come down. Some have narrow claws for pulling food from a current; others have blunt ones for breaking it apart once it has lodged. Their young shelter under the adults and pick through what falls from their mouths. When a keeper approaches, the crowd opens reluctantly around its front edge and closes again behind it.

The worms live deeper in the joints, anchored in tubes of packed grit. Each feeds within reach of its neighbors. Their crowns overlap when the water is generous, and a disturbance in one passes along the seam as they withdraw in succession. A little later the crowns emerge again, not all at once: one translucent fan, then another, until the joint has resumed its quiet work.

The snails travel farther than any of these, though slowly enough that their paths are visible only in the film they have scraped away. They climb the curved walls, cross the old gate sockets, and follow the mineral-rich mortar wherever it shows. Some enter the upward passage. Empty shells occasionally come back down with the fine particles, their chalky surfaces worn thin.

The keepers lay their eggs in the shelter of their folded limbs. There the young remain after hatching, grazing on particles caught against the parent’s underside. When they are large enough to leave, they do so gradually, returning several times to the warm hollow before finding hollows of their own. A young keeper is almost transparent at first. Its back thickens as it feeds, and silt begins to settle on it just as it settles on the others.

There are luminous animals here too, small enough to go unnoticed among the crowded seams until something brushes them. The brief glimmer they make does not reveal much at once: perhaps a worm’s crown folding inward, or the lifted margin of a keeper passing over a ridge. Afterward the chamber is dark again. The particles continue to come down the passage, and mouths throughout the chamber continue to find them.
