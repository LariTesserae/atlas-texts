The most numerous inhabitants are almost invisible. A pale film lives where warm water emerges from the rock, thickest at the mouths of the cracks and thinning to nothing a few inches downstream. It traps mineral grains as it grows. When a thread of water finds a new route, the old film dries to a brittle skin, while another begins in the wet line below.

Moss occupies the cooler margins. Its stems are crowded together so tightly that each patch holds water long after a pool has spilled away. Tiny mites move through it, and pale springtails gather beneath the surface. When a drop strikes the moss, a few may jump clear, landing among the fern roots or on the steel grating. The grating is useful ground: grit collects around its bolts, and the trembling metal sheds enough moisture to keep the first small cushions alive.

The ferns began as spores lodged in wet seams. Their older fronds shelter the small, heart-shaped growths from which new ferns rise, though a heavy spill from a pool can wash these away. Where a fern has found a deeper fissure, it can lift its leaves high enough to catch the rain directly. Elsewhere it remains small and close to the stone, with roots following the water into places the fronds cannot reach.

The thick-leaved shrubs grow slowly. Their leaves last through many seasons, accumulating scars from grit and falling droplets. Under them, fine roots share the narrow cracks with the pale, tougher roots that are beginning to widen the stone. Fungi live among these roots, sending threads into pockets of mineral dust and dying plant matter. Much of the garden’s soil is made there, in amounts too small to see until a handful has gathered behind a rock.

The dark-winged insect on the warm stone is one of several that use the notch in passing. Small flies rise from the wet leaves when disturbed; moths come at night and settle under the overhang. A spider has fastened a loose web between two fern stems, positioned where the warm air from a crack carries insects upward. The web needs frequent repair. Water beads on its strands and sometimes pulls the whole structure down.

The bird visits more than it inhabits the garden. It comes up from lower on the mountain, lands briefly on the rim, and calls before dropping among the leaves. It searches the undersides for insects, probes the moss, then leaves by the open side. It may return several times in a day, particularly after rain, when small creatures have been driven out of their shelter.

The person who tends the pump is another intermittent inhabitant. Their boots press a temporary dark path through the moss on the grating. They open the shed, listen to the motor from close by, check the fittings and clear leaves from the water intake. They may cut back a fern that has grown across the door, leaving its fronds on the rock, where the mites and fungi can reach them. Between visits, the station runs on, and everything that can grow around it does so.
