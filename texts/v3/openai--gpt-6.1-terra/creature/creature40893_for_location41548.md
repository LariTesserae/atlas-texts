The gliders are easier to see than anything else on the plateau. They rise on the afternoon wind with their wings crooked and their tails spread, appearing for a moment to be held motionless above the ridges. Their bodies are small beneath all that wing. When they fold the long outer feathers and drop into a basin, they can vanish into a band of trees without so much as shaking a leaf.

They nest among the fern roots and in pockets along the cliff faces. Their young are covered in a dense, pale down that mats readily with dust. For most of the day the adults hunt by making short flights from ridge to ridge, listening and watching for movement on the pavement. After rain, they walk awkwardly around the stone cups, taking insects and the small pale animals that emerge to drink.

Those animals live in colonies beneath the green bands. They have broad feet, soft fur and blunt faces, and they seldom venture far from cover while the stone is dry. They feed on fallen leaves, tender roots and the thick stems of ferns, carrying pieces back into chambers excavated where soil has collected in the cracks. Several entrances may open around a single tree. In hot weather, air passes through the chambers, and the animals sleep heaped together in the coolest one. Their tracks after rain look at first like scattered handprints; a closer look shows the drag of a low belly between them.

There are people on the plateau too, though the empty shelters can give the impression that they have gone. Most live below the surface, in fissures widened over generations and roofed where necessary with stone. From the open ground, a dwelling may show itself only as a dark slit under a tree band, or as a line of carefully placed rocks that turns a little runoff toward an entrance. Inside, the passages are low but often surprisingly long. Families sleep in the deepest rooms, where the air remains damp and the sound of the wind is dulled to a steady rush.

They move between basins according to the season and the rain. A shelter that stands empty for years may be repaired in an afternoon when water returns to its hollow. The bowls outside it are seldom carried away: they are heavy, and a good one becomes more useful as its surface weathers and its grooves deepen. People coming upon an occupied shelter leave the water alone unless invited to share it. At an empty one, they may drink, but will clear dust from the grooves before going on.

They gather fern shoots, vine fruit and the seeds of the low trees. They dig for the pale burrowing animals, and collect eggs from nests they can reach without disturbing the colony. Their fibre work is made close and tight. Loose ends are tucked away, and carrying nets are fastened at several points to the body, because anything that flaps in the wind soon works itself free.

In the basins, people speak at close quarters, often with a hand resting on the other person’s shoulder. Across a distance they use movements of the arms and strips of pale bark, held firmly at one end. The signals are plain enough to be recognized through blowing dust: a warning, a direction, a request to wait. Children learn them before they are allowed onto the open pavement.

The deep evening note is known to everyone, though it is not equally strong in every place or every year. It comes through certain ridges and the floors of certain hollows when the wind turns toward the cliff cuts. In some underground rooms it can make a bowl creep slowly across a shelf; elsewhere it is no more than a tremor felt through a hand laid on the rock. People listen to it for what it tells them about the wind beyond the basin. A change in its pitch may mean that a route across the pavement is becoming dangerous, even while the air at the shelter entrance still feels mild.

Some of the oldest passages have small alcoves cut into their walls where the vibration is especially clear. People sit there in the evening with their backs against the stone. They may talk when the wind permits, or pass a bowl from hand to hand. Above them the trees bend and recover, dust skims across the basin, and the gliders settle one by one into their sheltered nests.
