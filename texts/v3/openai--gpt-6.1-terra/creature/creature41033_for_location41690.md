The inhabitants are small enough to stand beneath the lower balconies. An adult, uncurled, is about as long as a human hand. They have low, flexible bodies and six limbs, the foremost pair finer than the others, with pads that can feel the grain of stone and the faint mechanical movement beneath it. Along their flanks are folds that open and close with the pressure of the air. In a sudden shift they flatten themselves, tuck their limbs close, and let the pulse pass over them.

They have eyes, or the remnants of eyes: two dark, sealed patches beneath the skin. Young ones press these patches against warm surfaces. Adults rarely do. They know a doorway by its temperature, the worn lip of its threshold, and the particular hesitation with which it opens. They know one another by touch: a pattern of ridges along the wrist, a scar, the speed and sequence of a greeting.

Most live in rooms cut behind the streets. A room may hold one inhabitant or several related households, with sleeping hollows at the back and tools kept in grooves near the entrance. The oldest hollows have been deepened and joined until their occupants sleep in a close cluster, each within reach of another. When the air grows cold, they cover the opening with a woven screen that lets the pressure through but catches some of the dust.

Their food comes partly from the pale plants, whose leaves they take sparingly, and partly from soft growths cultivated in damp cracks behind the walls. They tend the basins carefully. When condensation is expected, they clear the gutters with narrow combs and set flakes of stone beneath the balcony drips to lead water toward the roots. A full basin is not emptied at once. A little is carried away in folded skins; the rest is left until the plants have turned and drunk.

The flat insects are eaten too. Children learn to catch them by waiting beside a threshold with both hands still. The many-jointed hunters are usually left alone, though a hunter that settles too near a sleeping room may be coaxed into a jar and released farther along the gutter.

The town’s seams are attended by people who spend much of their time lying against the street, feeling for irregularities through their palms and ribs. They insert thin wedges where the old material has separated from stone and bind weakened junctions with new strands. The brief red glow means work is being done somewhere, or that something has gone wrong; the inhabitants do not turn toward it. They feel the strain arrive through the floor.

They speak by tapping, stroking, and pressing against one another. Across a room, they can send a message through a wall, but the great pulses interrupt it, so long conversations are patient affairs. Some of their stories are performed with several people touching the same stretch of stone: one makes the movements, while the others feel them arrive at different places. A familiar story can be recognized from only a few beats.

They leave the chamber open for the air to move. A winged animal crossing it is an event, felt first as a faint disturbance on an exposed face or flank. If the machinery quiets enough, people pause in doorways to hear it land. Then the pressure builds again, the doors adjust, and they go on along the remembered streets, guided at the crossings by ridges rising gently underfoot.
