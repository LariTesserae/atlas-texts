The people who live here are easy to miss from a distance. They wear the colors of the dry leaves, and when a tower begins to split they press themselves into its folds until the falling has finished. Then they come out in twos and threes, shake the pale growth-skins from their shoulders, and look for the places they had been going.

They make homes in chambers that are already forming. A family will brace an opening with cut stems, lay sleeping mats on the rising floor, and score the walls with shallow marks so that, when the room stretches overnight, they can see which way it is growing. A house may carry its occupants slowly across a square. It may close around them and have to be coaxed open with heat. When a wall sends roots through a sleeping mat, the mat is moved. When it sends roots through a pot, someone breaks the pot and saves what can be saved.

People keep little water gardens inside their houses: folded leaves held shut with pins, their interiors cool and beaded. Each garden belongs to a household, but a person passing through may ask for a mouthful. The request is made with an open hand. Refusing is permitted, and remembered; so is giving more than was asked.

Children learn the sound of a vein under pressure. They put their ears to the floor and listen through the tremendous noise for its thin, continuous hiss. When a vein breaks, everyone nearby runs toward it with cups and hollow stems, then kneels around the darkening patch to drink before the roots gather the water in. The youngest are lifted over the others’ shoulders. Some come away with green marks around their mouths where the pavement has briefly closed against their faces.

There are people who travel the opening streets. They carry no map, because a map can be outgrown in the time it takes to draw it. Instead they carry small weights on cords and watch which way the cords hang when an avenue begins to make room. They can feel its approach through the soles of their feet. Some follow it in hope of reaching a new district before its rooms close; some walk beside it, searching for a person or a house it has passed around.

Those who have been passed through are asked what they saw. Often they disagree. One may have seen an empty street extending toward a distant red light; another, standing in the same place, saw their own doorway at both ends. A woman in one district says she saw her brother walking away from her in two directions. She keeps a place for him at her water garden, though he has not appeared in either.

The people have no need to be silent, and little opportunity. They speak close to one another, cupping hands around ears, or press their foreheads together so the bones carry the lower sounds. Across a square they signal with strips peeled from young blades. A strip held overhead means *come here*; waved low, it means *the floor is opening*. There are signs for water, for a child found alone, for a wall about to fall. Arguments can be seen a long way off, both parties snapping their strips sharply in the air.

At night, when the light in the thin roofs fades, people feel their way along walls they know may have changed. They sleep in groups, partly for warmth and partly so that someone will wake if the floor begins to move. An old person might lie across the doorway, feeling each small adjustment of the roots beneath them. By morning the doorway may be on the other side of the room, and the old person still asleep where it used to be.

The red bloom in the square draws people from several districts. They arrive with vessels and stand at the narrowing entrances, watching the moisture collect. A few go close enough to touch its wet inner wall. It presses gently against their palms and then withdraws.

One man brings a child who has been ill. He holds her at the edge of the chamber while someone else gathers the water falling from the leaves. The child drinks, then reaches out toward the pulsing red wall. Her father lets her touch it. Around them the roots continue to turn toward the flower, and the people farther back begin calling for those in front to make room.
