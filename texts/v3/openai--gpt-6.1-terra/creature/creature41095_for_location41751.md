The people who live here know the district by its intervals: the time between one pump striking and the next, the length of the hush when all of them stop, the few minutes when the paving shows and a drain can be reached without going under.

They sleep in the upper rooms of the workshops, where the walls stay warm through the night. Beds stand on low platforms, and clothes hang from lines beneath the ceilings. Even there, above the highest mineral band, blankets are damp by morning. A few rooms are fitted with fans scavenged from the cooling tower; in others, people sleep with their doors open to the walkway and wake when a neighbor’s lamp passes.

The work is shared among shifts. One person watches the gauges while another listens at the pump housings with a metal rod pressed to the ear. The readings do not always agree with the sounds. A pump can hold its pressure and still develop a new knock, faint enough to disappear under the rain. The people who tend it learn to wait through several cycles before deciding what they have heard.

They wear gloves against the hot pipes and soft-soled shoes on the wet grating. Their tools are tied to their belts or to the railings with lengths of cord; anything dropped through the walkway may be difficult to find even when the water is low. Children are taught to stand still when a pump stops, and to keep their hands off the substation fence until the suspended drops have fallen. They learn the drains too: where the paving dips toward them, which ones clear readily, which must be opened from the workshop side.

Food comes in along a raised service passage beyond the rim. In dry corners above the workshops, people keep sacks on shelves and grow a few herbs in trays beneath lamps. Much of what is cut from the district’s vegetation is carried out in bundles. Some becomes compost in lidded bins; the tougher vines are stripped and braided into ties for doors, cables and loose sheets of metal.

The trees need little tending, though people sometimes remove a branch that presses against a roof or shades a gauge. Their roots have found the flooded streets and gone on widening there, lifting the edges of the paving. The ferns collect grit in the gutters until whole strips can be peeled away, roots and all. Moss grows over the marks left by boots on the lower brick, then grows over the fresh mortar used to repair it.

No animal calls answer the machinery. The people are accustomed to hearing one another at a distance: a shout from a doorway, a wrench tapped against a rail, a familiar footfall crossing the grating overhead. When the pumps stop, they often pause in what they are doing. Conversation carries farther in the sudden quiet, under the transformer’s hum. Then the drops come down around the substation, the pumps take hold again, and work resumes.
