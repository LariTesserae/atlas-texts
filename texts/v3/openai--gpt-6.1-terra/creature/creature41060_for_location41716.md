What lives there is spread so thinly that it is easy to mistake for the wetness.

Most of it lies along the seep, a translucent skin fitted to the flutes of the rock. Finer strands extend into the groove and down over the lip of the bowl. In the deepest seams it gathers into soft folds, pale as the underside of the snail. When a drop falls, the folds close around the tremor and slowly open again.

It has no single place where its body begins. A strand can be drawn away by water and settle elsewhere, growing finer until it joins another. Some parts die when the water changes; others endure beneath the mineral crust and return through it. The whole creature has been present a long time, though very little of it is old.

It takes what it needs from the seep and from the minute living things carried in with it. It also tastes the sea through the lowest seam. When a wave reaches the entrance, the change travels inward through the water, and the creature gathers a little more tightly against the stone. By the time the next wave comes, it has loosened again.

The bowl is its favored place. The clay holds a different warmth from the rock, and the water entering it has passed over nearly the whole length of the creature. A delicate membrane spans part of the bottom, too clear to see until silt settles on it. There, the animal keeps traces of disturbances: a snail’s slow passage, a crab’s brief visit, the impact of drops. These marks fade as the membrane grows.

The person who placed the bowl is gone. Perhaps the creature was already living along the wall when the groove was cut; perhaps a fragment arrived afterward, in water or on a hand. It cannot remember in the way a person does. Still, it has grown into the channel and around the chipped lip, and if the bowl were lifted away, it would cling to it for a while before the strands parted.

Most days it does not leave any mark that can be seen. Water enters, passes over it, and goes on toward the sea. The creature remains in the bowl, in the groove, in the narrow places where the rock stays wet.
