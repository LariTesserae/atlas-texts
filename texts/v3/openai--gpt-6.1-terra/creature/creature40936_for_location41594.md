The most abundant inhabitants are the films. They live in the seams between blocks, on the shaded faces of fallen slabs, and inside the tissues of the larger growths. A scraped stone looks bare until it has been left in the water a while; then a faint bloom appears on it, following the shallow marks of the tool that shaped it.

The films are made of many kinds of microbes. Some take what they need from the mineral-rich water that seeps through the masonry. Others live on the substances those microbes release, or on the slow remains of the fronds above them. In places the film is pale and branching; in others it is dark, almost the colour of the grit. It thickens over exposed surfaces, bridges narrow gaps and holds fine grains together. A settling block may tear it open, leaving two ragged edges that go on growing from their separate sides.

The broad fronds lying over the pavement have no need to hold themselves upright. Their surfaces are tough and slightly ridged, and water passes through them along a network of fine channels. Microbes live in the channels, supplied by the seepage that reaches them from below. When the current runs, a frond lifts and opens along its folds. When the water is still, it settles again over the stone, sometimes covering the same patch for years.

The groves near the ridges grow from thickened bases wedged into the joints. Each stem bears flattened blades, crowded with fine internal passages where its microbial partners live. New blades appear very slowly among the old ones. A stem may remain standing for centuries, its lower length gradually enclosed by sediment and dead growth. When it folds, the living part can persist along the fallen length. Other stems rise through its remains, and the bed grows deeper.

Where a channel cuts through a grove, its exposed side shows layers of old blades pressed together. Some are still recognizable; others have become a dark, yielding material threaded with living filaments. The current takes a little of it away. Much more stays where it fell, and the microbes work through it over a time too long to be marked by any visible change.

Higher on the plain, the ribbon growths meet the light. Their tips are green and thin enough to move in the weakest water. Far below the tips, the older parts are darker and heavier. When a season of blue thinning reaches them, the upper ribbons grow; when it fades, they persist on what they have gathered and on the work of the life in their tissues. Detached ribbons drift down and catch around the stems, where they soften into the thick leaf beds.

The few animals live among these folds. Tiny crustaceans pick at the films on the leaves; translucent worms pass slowly through the grit and leaf litter. They are scattered so thinly that a long search may find nothing, and then several together in a sheltered pocket beneath one frond.

All of them live on a surface made by others. The ridges guide the seepage and the currents. The joints give stems a place to hold and films a sheltered edge from which to spread. As the blocks loosen, living growth enters the new spaces. As the growth dies and settles, it covers more of the masonry. The old work remains underneath, carrying the beds and groves while they slowly bury it.
