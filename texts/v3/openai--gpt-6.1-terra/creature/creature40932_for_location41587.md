The people who live in the lower streets know the water by its sound. A shallow flow clicks grit against the paving; a deeper one makes a continuous rubbing noise around the corners of buildings. When the pitch changes, people look toward the nearest gauge even if no horn has sounded. Children learn to read the colored bars before they are trusted to go between buildings alone.

Most wear shoes that fasten well above the ankle, with soles soft enough to grip wet ceramic. The people who work in the current wear heavier boots or jointed frames that brace against the pavement. They carry hooks for lifting grates and retrieving things that have gone into the water. A hook is useful for a dropped tool, a jammed cart, or a person whose foot has caught in a drain.

Some families have lived along the same channels for generations. They know which gates serve their street and how much warning each one gives. Others are newly arrived and rent rooms wherever there is space: in the upper stories of warehouses, behind market stalls, inside the thick walls of old barriers. Landlords advertise the height of a room as carefully as its size. A high room is safer from floodwater, but its occupants carry everything up and down, including drinking water when the pumps fail.

Homes are built around drying. There are racks near warm pipes, ventilated cupboards for bedding, narrow shelves where shoes stand upside down. Clothes develop a persistent mineral smell. People oil hinges, wipe condensation from electrical contacts, and turn mattresses so that both sides get a chance to air. In crowded apartments, a fan running at night is often the most valued appliance, though it can hardly be heard over the city.

Many people work with water without calling themselves water workers. A market porter checks the gate signals because an hour’s delay can spoil a delivery. A tram operator knows where a sudden surge will wash grit onto the track. Cooks keep their stores in sealed bins and their stoves above the usual flood line. In lower markets, counters are mounted on rails: when a warning comes, two people can crank an entire stall upward, leaving the floor clear for the water.

Food arrives continuously. Barges bring sealed cargo along the canals, and freight lifts carry it to markets at different levels. Much of it comes from outside the basin; other food is made in enclosed production halls, where cultures grow in tanks and warm rooms. The broad-bodied canal swimmers are caught too, especially after a flood has brought them close to the streets. Their flesh is firm and oily, and vendors sell it hot from covered pans. People argue about which channels produce fish fit to eat and which merely produce fish large enough to catch.

The small animals in the flooded streets are called combers in some districts. They are nearly colorless underneath, with flexible plates along the back and several pairs of hooked feet. Their feeding fans collect fine matter from the water; from a distance a group of them looks like a patch of pale sediment trembling in the current. They do not always flatten quickly enough for a heavy vehicle, and crushed ones attract the canal swimmers. Street cleaners leave the living combers alone where they can, but scrape them away from grates. A clogged grate can drown a shop.

The gliders overhead spend much of the day tucked into exhaust galleries, gripping the warm surfaces with short, strong toes. Their membranes are mottled the color of old ductwork. At dusk they emerge in loose groups, catching rising air beside the towers and feeding on smaller flying animals drawn to lights. They land awkwardly on level ground, hauling their folded membranes after them until they find a wall to climb. People who maintain the galleries wear face shields: a disturbed colony will pour past in a confusion of claws, loose grit and papery wings.

The shell colonies on the retaining walls are slower neighbors. A single animal is difficult to distinguish beneath its shell, but a band of them responds quickly to vibration, closing in a wave that travels across the wall. During heavy rain they open and feed, extending dark fringes into the runoff. Their young settle on almost any damp surface, including doors, cameras and the flexible joints of bridges. Crews remove them with broad scrapers and collect the shells for aggregate. On some old walls, the bands are left untouched because the underlying surface has become too fragile to scrape.

At the height of a storm, the work of keeping the city open becomes visible everywhere. Teams at the gates watch gauges and communicate with lights, radios and hard-wired signals. Drivers hold at marked points while pedestrians climb out of the street. Shopkeepers check that everyone has reached a stair or a raised platform. Even with warning, people are injured: a gate can release more water than expected, a stair can be blocked, or someone can go back for a case of goods that took years to acquire.

Afterward there is mud in the joints of doors and grit in every moving part. Neighbors lend pumps and share warm rooms while soaked homes are cleaned. Arguments follow too, about who opened a gate, whose drain was neglected, who built a threshold just high enough to send water into the next property. At local meetings, residents bring photographs, sensor records and jars of sediment. The engineers bring maps showing where the water came from. Both are useful, and neither settles every dispute.

Higher up, a person can spend most days without putting a foot in street water. Office workers cross tensioned bridges between towers; children travel to school through enclosed walkways; repair crews move between rooftops on vehicles that follow fixed overhead routes. They still live with rain, noise and the motion of the structures around them. A bridge shifts slightly beneath a crowd. A brace gives a low knock as the day cools. Drops continue falling from upper ledges long after the sky over a district has cleared.

In the evenings, people gather wherever a little room and relative quiet can be found: on sheltered landings, in rooms built behind thick machinery housings, beneath the roofs of markets after the stalls have closed. Some talk with their lips close to one another’s ears. Some sign, or use the colored lights in their sleeves. From a landing they can watch traffic crossing at several levels, the bright windows of homes opposite, and the canal below, where a barge moves slowly past with swimmers following in its wake.
