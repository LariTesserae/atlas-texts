The people of the excavation are accustomed to looking a long way across a room. A child can see a parent crossing a square several minutes before there is any hope of speaking to them. They learn to recognize one another by gait, by the shape of a tool carried over a shoulder, by the way a cart is steered around a patch of tender pavement.

Most wear smooth, close-fitting clothes that shed mineral dust. The cuffs are reinforced with grown fiber, and the soles of their shoes are broad enough not to bruise young leaves. At the cutting face they add masks and hard caps; in the older districts, where the dust has settled into the living floor, people often go bareheaded. Their hands tend to be resin-marked. A fresh smear can be lifted away before it sets, but an old one must wear off.

The people who tend the city’s growth are not quite gardeners and not quite builders. They know which films can bear a rail and which will tear beneath it, how far to trim a wall before a window loses its seal, and when a roof frond has become too stiff to bend without splitting. They carry narrow knives and little covered lamps for examining pores. When a new district is planted, they walk it for months afterward, feeling the surface through their soles and occasionally kneeling to press an ear against it.

Factory workers know the same living material in another state: cut, dried, pressed, twisted, fitted to a machine. They can tell by the sound of a spindle whether a strand is too damp or too brittle. At the end of a shift, they brush pale fibers from their sleeves and leave the sweepings in shallow trays, where small cleaners sort through them for whatever can still be eaten.

The six-legged grazers are the most conspicuous of the city’s animals. Each moves on three pairs of short, jointed limbs, with its rasping mouth held close to the ground. From a distance it resembles a low cart carrying a rough gray load. Up close, the load is its back: a thick, ridged covering dusted with the same minerals it scrapes from the leaves.

Grazers are not tame, though some grow accustomed to particular streets. People give those ones names and notice when they fail to appear. Young grazers follow adults across the squares, stopping whenever the adults stop, sometimes pressing their mouths to the ground before they are strong enough to remove any crust. Cart drivers learn to wait for the last youngster, which is often hidden behind the rest.

The blind climbers live mostly above ordinary reach. Their long bodies grip the undersides of bridges and gantries, and their front ends slowly open and close around the air. At a lamp they may hang motionless for hours, then fold around an insect so quickly that the cables tremble. A climber removed from a drive-belt is usually carried to a bridge rather than put on the floor. The worker with the padded pole must be patient: the animal grips hard, and pulling can injure it.

The filament-tenders in the generator housings are less often seen. Their colonies occupy warm seams behind removable panels, where they lay and harvest white threads. Maintenance crews tap a housing before opening it; the creatures withdraw from the vibration, passing deeper into the wall. Workers leave the panel propped nearby until the repair is finished, then watch for the first soft bodies returning across its inner surface.

People live among these other inhabitants without treating every encounter as an interruption. Meals are taken beside windows cut into living walls. During the dim hours, neighbors sit in sheltered passages, mending clothes or stripping hardened resin from tools. Children are taught not to peel a pavement leaf merely to see it seal, and not to stand beneath a climber while it feeds.

At the far edge of the city, where the stone is still bare, the cutters are often the first people to arrive and the last to leave. They watch for the branching films between shifts. Once the new surface can support a road, drivers come through, followed by workers carrying rails, lamps and young roof-growth. The animals arrive in their own time. A grazer may spend its first day there inspecting the seam between old pavement and new, scraping a little at each side before deciding where to cross.
