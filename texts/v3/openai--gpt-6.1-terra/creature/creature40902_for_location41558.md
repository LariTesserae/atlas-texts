The long-legged creature has four joints in each leg, though two are held so close together that, at rest, they look like one. Its feet spread on the wet glassy stone. When it drinks, it lowers its body rather than its head, folding until its belly nearly touches the water. It can stay like that for a long time, taking occasional mouthfuls and watching the beads gather on the wall.

There are others, but rarely more than two or three visible at once. Their young are darker and keep to the warm runnel, where the ferns hide them. They eat the soft growth on the undersides of leaves and the thin films that form in the pools. An adult will sometimes lift a folded leaf with its narrow muzzle, wait, and then put it down without biting. Once, when a pool emptied suddenly, one stood over a stranded young creature until water began to bead out of the rock again.

The ticking in the fern bed comes from small shelled animals no larger than seeds. Each has a clear lid over its back and a pale body that can flatten against the stone. They move in the damp undergrowth, grazing where the water has recently passed. When a fern is disturbed, they draw in beneath their lids and make a sharp sound, perhaps by snapping the shell shut. A whole patch may tick in succession as something walks past.

The broad-leaved plants are rooted shallowly, with fine, colorless roots that wander through the warm surface of the stone. Their leaves fold most often when the structure clouds, though not all at once. The solitary plant against the wall is usually first; the stand by the seep follows, leaf by leaf. Water held in the folds drains slowly toward their centers. The small shelled animals sometimes shelter there until the leaves open again.

The structure is inhabited, or is itself an inhabitant. Its filaments seek water, but they also pause beside roots and remain there while the roots grow around them. In the mornings, faint branching shapes can be seen moving through its pearly walls. They gather on the side facing the pool, then spread apart as the surface clears. Nothing is visible moving inside in the ordinary way.

The long-legged creatures do not approach it closely when it is cloudy. They wait among the ferns or at the edge of the basin, facing inward. When the water begins to climb again, they return to the pools, placing their feet carefully between the closing tracks.
