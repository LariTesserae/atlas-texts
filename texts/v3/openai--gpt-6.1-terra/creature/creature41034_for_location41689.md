The most numerous are the swimmers, though they are difficult to count. They are nearly transparent except for a dark line of gut and a pair of cloudy paddles that beat beneath their ribs. They live between the ceramic teeth, turning sideways to pass through the narrowest spaces. Mineral condensate gathers on the teeth in a film, and the swimmers graze it away, leaving pale tracks that disappear as soon as the film thickens again.

The wall animals are larger, jointed and blind. Their flattened bodies overlap like scales, each animal holding to the metal with six hooked feet. When the compressor drives the wall inward, they lean with it; when it releases, they brace. Their legs do this even while they feed. The outermost animals catch swimmers that rise too close, passing scraps inward through the layers. Those nearest the wall scrape at deposits around the seams. A dead one is dismantled where it clings, and its neighbors spread into the space while smaller animals climb up from below.

Around the bolt heads, the snails make low white rings. They seal their shells to the wet metal with a clear adhesive that trembles but does not come loose. Their mouths reach out along the rims to rasp the mineral crust and the soft growth beneath it. Young snails are small enough to fit under the adults’ shell edges; for a while they feed there, sheltered from the wall animals, before moving outward to find metal of their own.

The moss is sparse and almost colorless. Its submerged tips collect a little of what the water brings, while its upper threads cling to the grout near the orange indicator. Swimmers shelter among the tips, and some are caught there by fine-limbed juveniles from the wall. The moss is cropped continually but grows back from the points where it is held fast.

There are eggs in the chamber, fixed singly to the undersides of ledges and in the seams between bolts. They are no larger than grains of grit. The vibration rocks them against their stalks, and the air pulse holds beads of water around them. When they open, the young enter a place already occupied: a swimmer presses into the water between two teeth, a snail finds the edge of a shell, a jointed animal works its way beneath the moving feet of others.

Their bodies are worn by constant contact. Shells ring and acquire polished patches; hooks break and are replaced at the next molt. The empty shells and shed skins are eaten, ground against the ceramic, or lodged briefly among the teeth. Nothing lies loose for long.

All of them move with the machinery, though each does something different with its motion. The swimmers paddle against it, the wall animals brace, the snails remain sealed in place. Together they cover nearly every available surface: a living layer of clear paddles, hooked limbs and small white shells, fitted tightly around the factory’s warm pinhole and its cold iron bolts.
