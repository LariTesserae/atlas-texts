The mat is not one growth but many, fitted so closely that a hand laid on it may touch three kinds of life at once. The broad fronds feed where warm water spreads thinly over them. Beneath them lie pale, yielding cushions threaded with mineral-loving bacteria, and beneath those the ropes of root travel from seep to seep. A damaged frond folds around its wound. It may stay closed for days while smaller feeders work at the torn tissue, cleaning it down to a surface that can grow again.

The grazers have hard, close-set legs and mouths that rasp sideways. They follow the warmth beneath the mat, where the air is still and rich with the scents of decay. When water rises, they climb into folds in the fronds and wait there, gripping with their hind legs. Their young are soft for a long time. Adults carry them in shallow hollows along their undersides, and a grazing animal disturbed by a hand will curl around its brood before it tries to move away.

In the pools are the broad-bodied seep dwellers. Their skin is cool at the edges and warm over the center, where water passes through a set of slow valves. They eat what settles on the stone: bacterial films, shed tissue, the fine mineral-rich residue left when a seep changes course. Each knows a number of warm places within reach. When one cools, the animal lifts its rim, takes hold of the rock with a ring of muscular pads, and pulls itself toward the next. The journey can take most of a day. Smaller creatures shelter beneath them until the pads begin to let go.

The hunters overhead descend for these smaller creatures and for grazers that stray onto open ground. They listen to the feeding sounds beneath the chord of the rim, then drop with their wings folded close. Their feet are long and sensitive; on landing, they feel for movement through the mat. Often they wait without taking anything, shifting their weight as the living surface settles beneath them. When they have eaten, they climb the rounded stone on gripping hooks and launch themselves into the open air again.

Around the central structure there is almost always an animal preparing to shed. Grazers gather in the fine grooves at its base, while the larger pool dwellers press one edge of their bodies against its warm wall. The wall gives slightly beneath them. Old skins loosen more readily there, and the creatures work free by rubbing against the seams or pulling themselves slowly over the stone. Others come afterward to eat what remains.

The structure’s own life is harder to distinguish from its work. It receives water, alters it, and releases it; it closes around the dry platform when something enters, then opens again when that thing leaves. The living mat grows right up to its walls but never across them. At intervals, a faint movement can be felt beneath the smooth surface, passing from the ground upward. The animals resting there do not stir.

When a mouth in the rim falls silent, the change is felt throughout the basin. Hunters alter their crossings through the air. Grazers leave the cooling ground, and the pool dwellers begin their long journeys toward another seep. The mat follows more slowly: one fold dies back, another thickens, and roots extend under the damp floor toward the new water. Eventually the abandoned mouth is covered, though its groove remains under the growth, holding a little moisture and the eggs of animals waiting for warmth to return.
