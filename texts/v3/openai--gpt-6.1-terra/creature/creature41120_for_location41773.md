The people of the basin are deft with their hands. They can split a crab at a joint without losing the meat, thread a fish-bone needle in the dimness beneath an eave, and feel through muddy water for a dropped hook. Most have small cuts on their feet and shins, made by the edges of the paving. Children learn early to step on the broad middle of a stone rather than its rim.

They live in crowded households. A house may hold several generations, with sleeping places raised on slabs above the usual level of the water. Baskets hang from the walls, out of reach of crabs. A family keeps its hammer near the door because there is always something to mend: a roof weight shifted by a bird, a crack widened by the pressure of a rootless, settling wall, a paving stone tipped up where water has worked beneath it. When a repair needs many hands, neighbors arrive carrying their own stones. They know the older work by its fit and by the marks of the hammer that shaped it, even when the courses look alike to a stranger.

Meals come from the basin and the water beyond its ridges. People lift crabs from the foundations, set hooks in the drains, gather eggs where the birds nest, and tend shallow traps in the outer pools. Some scrape the pale films from mineral seeps and carry them home to feed snails in sheltered basins. The seeps are shared carefully. A family may watch one for years, removing only a portion of the snails at a time and clearing away the shells of those that die. Children sent to check a seep learn to put their fingers down gently: crabs wait among the stones, and a snapping claw can make them drop the whole basket.

Cooking is done in raised clay hearths, with fuel gathered or traded from beyond the basin. Smoke has difficulty escaping under the low roofs and damp air. It stains the undersides of the stones and leaves the insects there sluggish enough to be brushed into a bowl. Some are eaten; others are fed to fish held in baskets in the water. Little is left lying long in the lanes. What people discard is quickly investigated by crabs, birds, flies, and their neighbors’ curious children.

They have a language of touch as well as speech. A hand laid flat between the shoulders means something different from two fingers on the upper arm. People warn one another of a lifted stone, ask for room in a passage, or announce that they are carrying hot food before the person ahead has turned around. Close friends can be recognized by the weight and hesitation of a touch. It is rude to seize someone suddenly unless there is danger; even a necessary grip is usually followed by a hand resting for a moment, to show who it belongs to.

They also speak, leaning close, their mouths near one another’s ears. In the midst of the roar they are attentive to changes in a familiar voice: a strain, an unfamiliar rhythm, a pause that lasts too long. Children’s names are often made of short, distinct sounds that can be called across a courtyard, though a child may answer more readily to the sight of a waving arm. At night, people lying awake can feel the calls of frogs and the drumming of rain through the stones beneath their bedding. The town is never quiet, but its sound changes. A sudden rush of wings along the ridge, or the faltering of insects beneath a roof, can bring people to their doors.

Boats are passed down and patched like houses. They are shallow enough to push through a lane and broad enough to carry a basket, a sick person, or a pile of roofing stones. In low water they rest against walls, collecting rain; in high water they crowd the passages. Children play at steering them around the corners, poling with old handles while adults call warnings from doorways. On ordinary days, the same passages are full of people picking their way around crabs or stopping to exchange a handful of hooks for a bowl of eggs.

The town has its specialists: people who know which clay will hold a hearth together, who can lift a roof stone without disturbing the ones beside it, who recognize the movement of edible fish in almost black water. An old person whose hands have grown too unsteady to make hooks may still sit with younger workers, examining each point by touch and sending the poor ones back to be scraped again. Skill is visible in small things: a seam that keeps a basket’s contents dry for a little longer, a wall that sheds water into the lane rather than onto the sleeping slabs, a crab taken whole from a crevice where three other people have failed.

The animals are not merely food to them. Some crabs are known for the burrows they occupy and the reach of their claws; birds return to the same nesting places; a frog that calls from beneath a particular threshold may be tolerated there for years. People curse the creatures that steal from baskets and make room for the ones that eat pests. A household might protect the crabs around one foundation while harvesting heavily from another, having learned which stones stay cleaner, or which drains remain open, where the crabs are left alone.

Life proceeds by tending rather than clearing. No one can empty the lanes of water, silence the roofs, or keep the animals out for long. A person sweeps a passage so that someone else can pass safely, then does it again the next day. A wall is mended with stone taken from a broken wall nearby. At a meal, people sit close enough to touch knees, passing shells and bowls from hand to hand. Outside, claws tap on the foundation, and the water continues to rise slowly through the joints in the stone.
