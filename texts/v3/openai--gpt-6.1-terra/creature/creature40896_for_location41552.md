What lives here is made of the water passing through.

It has no fixed shape. At the start of a surge it gathers along the row of holes, thin as a row of fingers. Then it comes through in a rush, spreads over the tiles and curls around the pots. For a moment it is broad and bright and full of sand. By the time the channel has drawn most of it away, it is narrow enough to fit between two grains.

It knows the chamber by resistance: the clean corners of the blocks, the drag of the grass, the little knock each pot makes against the ledge. The empty pot gives the deepest note. On each surge it fills; as the water eases, a small whirl remains inside it longer than anywhere else. The inhabitant lingers there, turning slowly, until the next rush finds it.

Outside, the lagoon is much larger, but the water moves differently there. Here the walls and channel give its movements a returning shape. Each surge carries part of it away, and each brings something new in. It remembers the chamber not as a picture but as a sequence: the holes, the ledge, the grass pressed flat, the pot’s low rattle, the pull toward the floor.

Sometimes a strand works loose from one of the ties. The inhabitant worries it back and forth, lifting it in the current and letting it settle. It cannot retie the grass. It can only keep the strand moving until it catches on a rough place in the clay, or slips away down the channel.

At the softest point between surges, it is difficult to say where the inhabitant is. The water lies nearly still; the pots rest on their ledge. Then the next swell thuds through the wall, and it gathers itself again.
