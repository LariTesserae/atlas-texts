The basin’s most numerous inhabitants are the small ones that can live in a film of water. Some have flattened bodies edged with fine gripping hairs; others travel on dozens of legs too small to distinguish unless they are lifted from the stone. They graze on the transparent growth that coats the hollows. When a surface begins to dry, they gather in the last connected threads of moisture, following them downhill or pressing themselves into the rock’s shallow pits. A few remain exposed, curled around a pocket of water until the films return.

The pale crowds that converge on falling drops are quick by the standards of the basin. Each animal feels the impact through the stone and turns toward it. They drink from the new puddle and pick at whatever the drop has brought down: grains of pollen, shed skin, fragments of leaves. They are often accompanied by smaller, dark-bodied creatures that take nothing from the water but feed on the crowd itself.

Along the fern stems, the rice-sized clingers face in alternating directions. Their bodies are clear enough that a line of green food can be seen moving through each one. They pierce the soft surface of the plant, but rarely remain in one place long enough to leave an obvious mark. When disturbed, they fold their legs beneath themselves and slide into the water below. Some will climb back up the same stem; others settle on a different fern and begin feeding among strangers.

The moss shelters a slower population. Jointed animals push between the fronds with their heads down, opening temporary passages that close behind them. The largest are about the length of a fingernail and carry a fringe of young beneath their bodies. Their young leave the parent for short excursions, returning by touch. In a dry spell, several families may occupy the same deep pocket of moss, their limbs intertwined without apparent trouble.

Beneath the round leaves of the creeping plant, the translucent swimmers feed on suspended matter and on one another. From above they are difficult to see except when they turn together: then the leaf’s shadow seems briefly filled with silver scratches. Their eggs adhere to the undersides of the leaves in loose spirals. The grazers eat many of these eggs along with the tender growth, though enough remain, tucked close to the leaf veins, to keep the water full.

The grazers are the most conspicuous inhabitants when they venture into the open. Their legs are long and narrow, with spreading feet that rest lightly on the bottom. Each has a small eye set high on either side of its head; when its mouth is underwater, the eyes remain above the surface on short, flexible stalks. It can watch a hunter pass while continuing to feed.

They do not form a close herd. A grazer entering a pool usually leaves a stretch of open water between itself and the next animal. They know one another nevertheless. When two meet in a narrow channel, they pause and touch the water with the tips of their mouths. One then steps onto the stone and waits while the other passes.

The broad-winged hunters take many of the animals stirred up by those careful feet. They also hunt among the trees, settling lengthwise along a limb where their mottled backs resemble patches of lichen. Their wings are wide enough to carry them across the basin on a falling glide, but they climb awkwardly, gripping bark with hooked toes. After rain they spend a long time drawing the fringes of their wings through their mouths, cleaning away water and the small creatures caught there.

The smooth-backed animal at the rim is older than most of the visible life around it. Its shell grows in low overlapping ridges, worn almost level at the top, and supports a thin garden of film, moss threads and grazing animals. It feeds at the basin’s edge, pulling up soft plants with a blunt mouth, then returns to warm stone. When it moves, some of its passengers descend to the ground; others hold fast and are carried to a fresh patch of water. The shallow impressions it leaves behind fill with rain and become pools of their own.

The silver structure has inhabitants suited to its steadier water. The swimmers there are longer and finer than those outside, and many bear a dark spot near the head. During the bright hours they turn in dense clouds under the roof, rising and sinking together. As the light changes, the clouds draw out into ribbons along the walls. Individuals leave through the opening when the water connects with the channels outside, but most remain within. Small clinging animals live on the inner surface above the waterline, where condensation gathers. Their trails make faint patterns visible only when the wall is viewed from the darker side.

Under the fallen arches, animals arrive throughout the day. The trough offers cool water even when the exposed hollows have grown warm. Minute drinkers gather at its lip; larger animals lower their heads between them. The hunters sometimes wait nearby, though they cannot glide easily through the arches and must approach on foot. In the cooler spaces, eggs adhere to the stone and to the broken edges of the structure. Some belong to familiar basin animals. Others produce soft, blind young that spend their whole lives there, feeding on material carried inward with the rain.

At night the basin is less still than it appears by day. The moss dwellers emerge onto the shelves, and the flattened grazers spread across broad wet surfaces. The larger grazers settle among the fern channels, their folded necks resting on their backs. A hunter may remain awake in each tree, changing its perch as the shadows move.

By morning, the exposed stone bears the evidence of their passage: cleared tracks through the film, small empty skins, eggs laid in the shelter of a hollow. The first warmth draws beads from the rock. As they join and begin to run, the small animals follow, and the day’s feeding starts again.
