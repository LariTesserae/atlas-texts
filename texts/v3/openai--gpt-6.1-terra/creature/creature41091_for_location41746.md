The most permanent residents are the cliff birds. They sleep and nest in the holes, returning year after year to the same small darkness in the stone. Their nests are untidy cups of grass, moss and feathers, tucked well beyond the reach of rain. A bird arriving with food may cling at the entrance for a moment, calling into the hole before disappearing inside. Soon it comes out again and drops into the open air, where its wings seem to catch it at the last possible instant.

They know the hollow by its currents. What looks from the ground like a sudden turn is often a practiced movement: out through the insects, down along the falling water, then up on the air that presses against the cliff. The young must learn this. For several days after leaving their nests, they make short, noisy flights between the wall and the sheltered shelves, while adults call from the rock.

Among the stones at the meadow’s edge live the thick-furred mammals. Their passages are low and narrow, with several openings, and their bedding is made from sedge dragged underground in dry weather. They feed most readily when the birds are making their ordinary noise. A change in the calls sends them beneath the stones, though one may remain at an entrance with only its nose showing. They eat the soft parts of leaves and flowers, and gnaw the pale shells that fall from the nests. In the brief warm season, they carry stems into their chambers to dry.

The smallest inhabitants are the ones most closely tied to the water: flies whose larvae cling in the shallow grooves, tiny creatures moving through the moss, and beetles sheltering beneath wet flakes of stone. When sunlight reaches the hollow, insects rise from the meadow in a loose cloud. The birds feed on them, and some drift over the lip and are carried far down the mountain.

The hunter that comes on the rising air does not belong to the hollow in quite the same way. It knows the ledge and checks it, passing slowly enough to look into the meadow and along the wall. Its shadow can empty the open ground before the bird itself is visible. After it has gone, the first animal to emerge is often a cliff bird, followed by others calling from their holes. The mammals take longer.

The person who tends the shelter comes and goes. The path through the moss is narrow, and it grows less distinct between visits; close to the hearth, though, the stones have been shifted and set back too often to be mistaken for a fall. Whoever comes knows where dry grass can be kept, which seams give water after the clouds have passed, and how to make a small fire without sending sparks into the meadow.

On a clear day, that person may sit at the shelter’s entrance to work a piece of dark stone, with the basin filling slowly nearby. The birds continue to fly, keeping a little farther from that part of the wall. When cloud enters the hollow, the person and shelter disappear from the cliff edge just as the distant country does. Only the sounds remain close: the sharper drops from the roof slabs, the wind against stone, and the birds calling to one another from their holes.
