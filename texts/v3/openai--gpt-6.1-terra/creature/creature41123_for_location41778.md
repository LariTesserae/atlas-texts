The largest grazers are dark, thick-bodied animals with narrow heads and splayed feet. They move through the fern beds in small groups, cropping the tender centers and leaving the tough outer fronds standing. Their coats are short along the back but long around the belly, where the hair stays wet most of the day. A calf will follow so closely behind its mother that, in the cover, the two seem to be one animal with too many legs. When startled, the adults lift their heads together and listen before moving off, pushing through the ferns with a long, diminishing rush.

On the scree slopes live smaller animals, nimble and gray-brown, with tails they hold stiffly behind them for balance. They browse the cliff shrubs and lick minerals from exposed stone. At midday they sometimes lie flat on the warm rock, but they keep clear of the vents: the places that steam most heavily are too hot even for their feet.

Birds are easier to hear than to find. One makes a hollow, double note from within the trees; another answers with a thin whistle farther up the valley wall. Among the moss and hanging roots, tiny birds search for insects, turning sideways to probe the wet folds where their prey shelters from rain. A larger bird follows the grazing animals at a distance, dropping to the ground after they have passed to take the beetles and soft-bodied grubs they uncover.

The warm pools have their own inhabitants. Dark films coat the gravel where the water first emerges, and fine green growth spreads farther out, where it has cooled. Larvae cling to the undersides of stones in the moving water. Above them, midges rise in loose columns, scattering when rain strikes the surface and gathering again as soon as it eases. The frogs wait along the cooler margins. Some are leaf-green; others are nearly black, with pale specks that make them difficult to distinguish from wet mineral grit. At night their calls overlap so closely that the separate voices are hard to pick out.

The ground is busy too. Fungi thread through fallen wood and ash, raising small translucent caps after heavy rain. Worms and pale, many-legged animals work beneath the root mat, drawing dead leaves into its damp interior. When a tree loosens from a cliff, it brings down a curtain of moss, soil and roots. For a time, light reaches the place where it stood. Ferns spread into the opening, and new shoots take hold among the fallen branches.

Near the hottest springs, life is sparse but not absent. The pale crusts bear patches of rust-red and cream, soft when wet and brittle when the water shifts elsewhere. They grow, dry, break and are covered by fresh deposits. Around them the valley keeps moving: water rising, leaves falling, roots finding purchase in what the water and the mountains leave behind.
