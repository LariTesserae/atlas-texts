The broad-footed grazers are called shelfbacks by anyone who has watched them from above. Their shoulders rise in a long, level ridge, furred with a coarse gray coat that darkens almost black in rain. Each foot spreads under their weight, pressing the fern beds flat without breaking through the wet turf. They feed slowly, taking the soft centers of plants and leaving the tougher outer fronds standing. A herd’s passage can be traced by those empty green cups long after the animals themselves have vanished into cloud.

They are not defenseless, though the mist makes them seem so. Their ears turn independently, and their nostrils close against water when they push through flooded growth. Calves stay close beneath the adults’ bellies, learning the firmer routes between basins. In heavy rain the herd moves toward the stony ridges. The animals begin to go before the blue light appears, lifting their feet sharply from ground that looks no different to an observer.

The solitary hunters use the grazers’ paths, but rarely follow a herd directly. They are low-bodied, with long forelimbs and a coat that carries the colors of wet roots. Their paws have thick pads split into many small lobes, useful on slick stone and quiet among the ferns. They take birds, small mammals, and occasionally a young grazer caught away from its mother. Much of their hunting is waiting: a hunter can lie under a fallen bough with water dripping through its fur for so long that moss insects venture onto its back.

Above them, the suspended gardens have inhabitants of their own. The small climbing mammals sleep in dry pockets against the trunks, where a roof of living moss sheds the worst of the rain. They have flexible toes and blunt faces, and their fur holds the hooked seeds of dozens of plants. Some seeds are groomed out and eaten; others remain until the animal squeezes through a narrow fork or brushes past a damp branch, where they may germinate. A few of the oldest moss rafts are thick enough to contain branching runs, generations of nests, and little stores of husks that have begun to sprout.

After rain these climbers descend in numbers. They gather around exposed mineral nodules, licking them and sometimes gnawing loose grains from cracks in the rock. They jostle without much ceremony, though an animal with a favored hollow may defend it for a few minutes. Once satisfied, they carry their wet fur and mineral-stained mouths back into the branches, where their droppings feed the plants overhead.

The ground birds spend most of their lives nearly hidden. They have rounded wings, strong legs, and plumage patterned like the undersides of fern fronds. They scratch through the fallen leaves of the current-feeding plants, turning up larvae and small snails. When disturbed, a flock bursts upward with a startling rush, flies a short distance, and drops again into cover. Their nests are shallow hollows lined with dry fibers gathered from the interiors of moss banks. The chicks leave the nest soon after hatching and follow the adults through tunnels in the vegetation.

The pale insects in the tree crowns are seldom seen from the ground. Some graze the thin films growing on wet bark; others bore into dead wood softened by fungi. There are moths whose translucent wings fold flat as leaves, and small beetles that spend their larval lives inside mineral-rich nodules washed into the moss. Snails move between these gardens and the soil on wet nights, rasping at fungi and carrying spores in the folds of their bodies.

Beneath them all, fungi spread through the root layers in fine, branching threads. Some live against the roots of the metallic-threaded plants, taking sugars and helping release minerals from the rust-colored grains that fall from their leaves. Others consume dead roots, drowned insects, and the compacted remains of old moss. Their fruiting bodies appear only briefly: pale knobs under a log, thin fans along a root, a scatter of red-brown cups in a hollow after the water has receded.

No single animal seems to command the plateau. The grazers open it, the birds scratch through it, the climbers carry pieces of it upward, and the fungi work through what falls. Even the hunters leave something behind: a stripped bone in the ferns, a place where the plants have been flattened, a meal for smaller lives. By the next season the hollow may be full of seedlings, with water slipping through their roots on its long way downhill.
