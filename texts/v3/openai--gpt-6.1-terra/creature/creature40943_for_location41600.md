The chamber is inhabited by a single growth, though it does not know itself as a single thing. Each rosette, each mat and each descending tuft has its own small affairs: a current to divert, a trace of vapor to take from the air, a crystal edge to mend. Beneath them, the roots meet so often that no boundary can be drawn between one life and the next.

The roots do not drink. They take a slow nourishment from the stone’s unequal charges, from minerals that rise through cracks too narrow for grit. They make leaves that are almost mineral themselves. A leaf may remain useful for centuries, its surface altered by such fine degrees that, even if watched for a lifetime, it would seem never to change.

The great noise is one of the ways the growth knows the mountain. It has no ears; the sound is not carried to it through the air. Something passes along the roots and into the rigid blades, and all that crowded vegetation gives it a voice. The note is immense because the mountain is immense. The fine rattle belongs to its smaller workings: grains shifting under pressure, seams taking up strain, changes far below the reach of any root. In the chamber, these slow events are heard together.

Long ago, people found the place and set their instruments among the leaves. They expected the noise to mean movement, heat, perhaps a machine sealed in the rock. The housings were left to watch after the people went away. The growth gradually enclosed them, maintaining a minute clearance around each. It could not use the iron, the glass or the insulated cable, and had no reason to cross them. The fixed needle still records whatever it recorded on the day the gauge ceased to work.

A visitor standing sideways at the entrance brings a disturbance the growth can scarcely register: a brief change in the electrical condition of the air, a few particles shed onto the floor. The particles may remain there for years before settling between the roots. By then the visitor is gone, and the pale wedge of distance is empty again.

The growth has no purpose beyond continuing its intricate exchange with the mountain. Yet across the chamber its countless parts hold together a record of pressures and mineral changes, laid down in the structure of root and blade. Much of what has happened to the mountain is there, though nothing in the chamber can read it back. The sound goes on, full and immediate, while the life producing it takes its measure over centuries.
