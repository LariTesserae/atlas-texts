The ribbon weed holds the chamber together as a place to live. Its roots have entered the joints between the cast pieces and grown thick there, gripping cracks that the current cannot widen. Each tuft has a different shape: one spreads low along the floor, one reaches toward the shaft, and the third grows in a tight sheaf beneath the old stairs. Their leaves are scarred by grazers and edged with the pale tracks of eggs.

The grazers are scarcely larger than the bolt fragments in the corners. Through their clear bodies, the green of a meal can be seen passing slowly from end to end. They move in crowds when they feed, scraping a fine film from the leaves, but at a disturbance each draws its legs close and lies flat. Some are still taken. The silver fish have learned to wait at the edges of the gardens, facing into the flow, with only their fins moving. When a grazer lets go, the nearest fish turns sideways and catches it before it can find another leaf.

The fish shelter among the weeds too, though not for long. There is room in a tuft for a few at a time, and they chase one another out with sudden flashes of their flanks. The smallest stay closest to the roots. Older fish cross the open water more freely, keeping above the smooth fins of the grate.

Under that grate the jointed swimmer passes much of its life. Its body is too long to turn easily in the chamber, but below the floor there are passages between the ribs. It travels through them with slow strokes of its rear limbs, feeling ahead with the finer arms near its mouth. Sometimes it catches a fish; more often it gathers what the flow leaves against the underside of the metal: dead grazers, torn weed, and soft things carried up from farther down. The tips of its arms are worn almost white.

When the intake is about to begin, the swimmer retreats deep into the machinery. The fish gather in the lee of the walls, and the grazers grip the leaves. Then the gardens bend, loose grit rises, and a cloud of food enters the room. For a little while all three gardens are hidden. As the water clears, the fish come out first, snapping at what remains suspended. The grazers unfold more slowly, among fresh grains caught in the folds of the leaves.

The swimmer returns last. An arm appears between two ribs and rests there, its small joints opening and closing in the current. Above it, afternoon light travels down the shaft and breaks across the fish. They scatter when the arm lifts, then settle again in the spaces between the gardens.
