The people of the basin have broad, flexible feet, with toes that spread when they stand on the living avenues. Children learn early not to grip the leaves. The mat can bear a person’s weight more easily than it can bear being pulled, and a torn place admits water and hungry things from below.

They are long-limbed and generally slight, though age and occupation alter them as much as any inherited shape. Those who work in the towers develop strong hands and shoulders. Court tenders often have one side of the body more deeply browned than the other, from years of kneeling with the same shoulder toward the sun. Their ears are small and close to the head; their voices are clear, and they call across astonishing distances without seeming to raise them.

They wear little against the warmth: wraps of soft fiber, short jackets with many ties, hats woven wide enough to shed a brief rain. Most carry a narrow roll of dry cloth in a waterproof leaf. It is used for everything from wrapping food to binding a cut root, and people are particular about whose cloth they borrow.

A person in the basin may belong to several towers in a day. Rooms are allotted by use and affection rather than by permanent address. An old woman who needs morning light may sleep in one tower, receive visitors in another, and keep her medicines in a chamber whose door has not opened in the same place twice that month. People remember where a room *feels* as well as where its current doorway stands: the coolness at its threshold, the bend of the stem beside it, the particular way its leaves brush the back of the neck. Still, strangers sometimes walk in on one another. Apologies are ordinary; a screen can be grown across a doorway if greater privacy is needed.

The resin used to mark passages is prepared by a few households from the sap of an especially dark-stemmed plant. They keep their recipes private, but not their work. When a doorway has gone uncertain—opening onto the wrong chamber, or only a little way—they are called to inspect the frame. They taste a trace of the resin, feel the tension of the leaves, and ask everyone who has recently passed through what they were carrying. Their remedies can be surprisingly domestic: remove a heavy chest from the room, let the frame rest, bring the person who planted that tower to speak beside it.

The cropped courts are tended by people and animals together. The long-legged flocks eat larvae that would otherwise hollow the roots, though they also steal tender shoots. They are quick, fussy creatures, with narrow bills and loose feathers that lift in the crossing winds. At a warning call they stop so abruptly that a whole court seems to have been painted with them. Children can imitate that call, and are forbidden to do so for amusement.

The slick-backed browsers are less easily managed. They come close to houses when the ground is wet and press their translucent ears against walls, listening, perhaps, to water rising inside. Their young sleep with their legs tucked under them and their ears folded over their eyes. People leave them alone unless they begin stripping a tree; then several neighbors gather with leafy branches and gently insist that they move.

The reedlike predators take the flock animals and occasionally a browser’s young. They are not welcomed, exactly, but their presence keeps the flocks from exhausting a court. Their own young are pale and conspicuous until the first dry season, when their coats stiffen and acquire the broken browns of dead stems. A predator lying beneath a tree can be identified by the small clear patch around it: no insect settles there for long.

Below the avenues live fish, water beetles, threadlike worms and soft-bodied grazers that rasp the older roots. After a heavy rain, silver fish may push almost to the surface. People part the leaves with a practiced hand and lift out a few in shallow baskets, taking care to close the mat again. The catch is shared quickly; in the warm basin, little stays fresh for long.

After storms, families go out together to inspect their usual routes. The youngest carry markers: small colored knots to leave where a surface has weakened or a passage has shifted. They learn the town by these walks, and by listening to the older people disagree. One will remember a drowned court as it was before the last change; another will point out that the water has already begun to drain. Someone kneels, lays a palm on the avenue, and waits for the leaves to loosen enough to show what is happening underneath. Then the others come close to look.
