The people of the basin are practiced at noticing small departures from habit. A woman carrying bread home from the bakery sees that the third lamp on her street is visible from the corner where only two could be seen yesterday. She turns back for the longer route. By morning the lamp has disappeared behind the boiler house again, and someone has rubbed the warning off the ward board with a sleeve.

The boards are tended by volunteers, usually older residents who have time to watch. They do not agree on every report. A man may insist that a passage has opened beside the freight depot, while the woman who walked with him remembers only that they were talking and arrived sooner than expected. Both accounts may be chalked up, with a question mark between them. Children are sent to look, but never alone, and never with instructions to go through.

In the workers’ flats, families mark the heights of their children on doorframes. When a frame is replaced or a building is lifted, the marks are copied onto the new wood, including the dates. Some families also keep a mark for the mountain wall. This is made on a windowpane, with grease pencil, at the height where the wall meets the cloud. It is an unreliable measurement: the cloud moves, and the glass is washed, and the wall itself is not always at the same apparent distance. Still, people like to look.

There are families whose members work on foundations for several generations. They know the noises a building makes when its plates need cleaning, and can distinguish these from the ordinary creaks of pipes cooling at night. An apprentice learns to place a hand on the brick before going down to inspect the runners. Sometimes the brick is warm from a boiler or wet with condensation; sometimes it seems to press more firmly against the palm than it did the day before.

The factory crews have their own knowledge. Before a shift begins, someone walks the empty floor clapping or rattling a length of chain. Birds lift from the beams and settle again in the high windows. Those that do not leave are coaxed out with a broom, except during nesting season, when a section of machinery may have to be screened or a nest moved carefully, with its whole shelf, to another wall. The workers complain about the droppings. They also bring scraps to the birds in winter and know which of them can open the latches on the ventilators.

The birds are narrow-winged and almost black, with a dull blue sheen visible only when light catches their backs. They feed over the ponds at dusk, turning together above the reeds. A flock crossing the railway cut may vanish behind the embankment and reappear in pieces: a few birds over the engine, a few farther down the line, the rest pouring out above the workshops. They collect in the roofs afterward and call to one another until the flock is whole.

The little antlered grazers are less patient with one another. They quarrel over the silver shrubs and strip the bark from young trees, leaving gardeners to wrap the trunks in wire. Their antlers are short and uneven, often broken on one side from pushing through fences. Newborns can stand quickly, but take longer to learn the herd’s routes. In spring, a late train may find three or four adults waiting beside the track while a spotted youngster searches for them from a patch of ground that seems to be both near and inconveniently far away. The adults call softly until it comes.

Under the sidewalks, the long-bodied animals make nests from grass, feathers and the fibrous insulation stolen from pipes. They are bold at night and elusive by day. Residents know them chiefly by their tracks and by the neat piles of beetle wings they leave beside the support columns. They will also take eggs, which puts them in conflict with the birds. At dawn a person passing beneath a factory window may hear a clamor overhead and see one of the animals retreating along a pipe, carrying an egg so large it has to hold its head sideways.

The ponds have quieter inhabitants. The pale fish gather in the warm currents below the grates, moving out into the cold only when disturbed. Their young shelter among the reed roots, where their nearly transparent bodies are difficult to see until they turn. Frogs sit on submerged stones with their noses just above water. On wet nights they leave the ponds and spread across the open ground; by morning some have reached the cistern channels, where children catch sight of them through the covers and beg the attendants to lift the lids.

Near the warm seams, the pale shoots grow without leaves for their first few days. Then they open into small, dark fans that fold at the touch of rain. The grazers eat them when they can reach them, and people sometimes gather them too, though the taste varies from seam to seam. In one ward they are cooked with onions; in another they are considered fit only for goats. A seam that has opened beneath a fence may produce a bed of shoots on both sides, and neighbors who have scarcely spoken all year will find themselves kneeling together to harvest it.

At night the flats grow quiet unevenly. A late shift returns along the elevated walkways, stopping to listen for the second echo of a train horn. Someone in an upstairs kitchen sets a bowl beneath a drip from the ceiling. In the vacant ground, the grazers move between the rails, and the animals under the sidewalks come out to investigate what the day has left behind.

From a lit window, a person may see a small animal crossing the dark toward the next cluster of buildings. For a moment it is hidden by a pipe. Then it appears much farther away, still walking at the same unhurried pace. By morning there may be an arrow on the ward board, or no sign that anything has changed.
