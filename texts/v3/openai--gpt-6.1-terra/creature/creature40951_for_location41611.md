The largest living things on the plateau are difficult to count. A moss bed may have several green centers separated by bare stone, yet a sheet of pale runners beneath the surface joins them. Elsewhere, two beds touch along an irregular seam without mingling. Their edges rise against one another, making a low ridge that holds water after the rest of the ground has dried.

The moss begins as a film in a crack. It thickens where the stone sweats, sending fine threads into the mineral seams and holding each drop until the wind has passed. Given water, it builds upward in folds. The lower folds darken and soften; the upper ones remain springy and green. A bed can preserve the shape of a fallen wall for a long time after the wall itself has disappeared. Some of the straight ridges visible from the causeways are old property lines, still standing in moss.

The black-leaved trees grow more slowly. Their trunks are often wider than they are tall, and many have divided so close to the ground that a grove appears to be a single thicket. In winter their leaves press together around the branch tips. In the brighter season they loosen enough to let damp air circulate within the canopy. Water condenses on the inner leaves and travels down the branches to the roots, so that a tree may keep a patch of ground wet through a long dry interval.

Young trees establish themselves under moss, then gradually lift it. In some places a whole grove wears a green skirt, torn and raised by the widening trunks. In others the moss has overgrown the trees entirely. Black leaves remain alive in pockets beneath it, flattened against the stone; when a fold of moss dies back, a branch may emerge and open again.

There are smaller plants among them. Gray rosettes grow on exposed paving, their leaves held tight around a depression that collects frost. They open only when the frost melts, absorbing the water before it can run away. A translucent growth spreads inside the condensation screens, following the weave of the reeds. It is almost invisible when dry. At dawn it swells into fine clear cords, and the screens become heavier and more efficient at catching moisture. Eventually the weight pulls them down, and the growth dries with them. New screens, where they survive upright, have often been reached by runners from the old.

No creatures tend these plants or carry them from place to place. Their spores travel in the wind, and pieces of moss sometimes loosen and roll across the bare ground. More often they proceed by patient contact: a runner reaches a wet stone, thickens, and sends out another. A drainage channel gives it a sheltered route across a district. A leaking cistern lets it cross a courtyard. A door fallen flat makes a bridge over a threshold that had kept the green outside.

The abandoned houses contain some of the deepest beds. Their roofs slow evaporation, and the floors are lower than the ground outside. Moss covers benches and fills hearths, leaving the mouths of the hearthstones visible as dark hollows. Where condensation gathers on a lintel, a narrow curtain of growth hangs from it and trails across the floor. In a few houses the interior changes from one morning to the next, but the curtain remains attached to the lintel. It lies one day in a snowy courtyard, the next along a descending stair, wetting whichever stones are beneath it.

The builders appear to have understood that a wet place might not always remain beside the same dry one. Around some cisterns they laid broad aprons of fitted stone, directing overflow into several channels. One channel might lead to a garden wall on a clear day and to an empty stretch of pavement under cloud. The others would still carry water somewhere. The channels have since filled with growth, but many continue to work.

The route bowls support their own communities. A thin green skin forms on the inner surfaces of some, leaving a clear patch at the center where the reflected sky can still be seen. In others, a fringe of tiny rosettes has rooted in the cracks left by old repairs. They drink from water that appears without rain. When a bowl fills with the reflection of a dark sky, the fringe sometimes closes before the first cloud reaches that part of the plateau; the leaves have grown sensitive to the water’s cold, whatever road the water has come from.

There is no fixed border between city and vegetation. The plants depend on gutters, screens, walls and jars, while their roots hold some of those structures together. A moss-packed wall may stand for another age because the moss keeps wind and frost from its joints. When the moisture shifts, the packing dries, the stones settle, and the opening gives a new runner access to the rooms within.

At dusk, water gathers on leaves and woven reeds alike. It falls into cisterns, onto floors, down the trunks of the low trees. The beds swell almost too slowly to see. By morning a few runners will have entered a crack that was dry the day before, and somewhere a channel will carry water to a patch of ground the builders never meant to plant.
