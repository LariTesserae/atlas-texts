The pale crust is not one thing. It is a close association of several lives, layered so finely that a broken piece looks solid until it is held up to the light. The upper film gathers what little light reaches the pools. Beneath it, threads draw minerals from the stone and pass them sideways through the colony. The lowest layer grips the rock so tightly that it often comes away with grains of the surface attached.

When a pool drains, the upper film folds inward. Its edges curl over the softer layers, and the whole patch darkens. It can remain this way through a long exposure, swollen by rain and flattened by wind, until water returns. Then it opens slowly from the middle. Bubbles collect under it and escape along the seams.

The transparent animals graze mostly at those seams. They have many small jointed limbs, though the number is difficult to count: some are held against the body and brought out only when the animal needs to brace itself. Their mouths can rasp a path through the crust without tearing it from the rock. Behind each grazer is a narrow, clean track that gradually clouds over again.

They are most visible when they are carrying young. The eggs are pale and opaque, packed in a shallow hollow beneath the body. A laden animal cannot flatten itself as completely as the others, and its shelter must be chosen with care. It tests a crack by passing one limb into it, then another, before squeezing in. If the water rises too fast, it may leave several eggs caught at the entrance. Other grazers sometimes take these into their own hollows, where the hatchlings emerge among unrelated young.

The animals react to doubled ripples before the water becomes dangerous. They also respond to vibrations in the stone, pressing their undersides against it and remaining still. A wave of this stillness can pass across a basin: one animal stops feeding, then another, until the whole crust is dotted with motionless bodies. Sometimes the disturbance follows. Sometimes the animals resume grazing and a distant roll of thunder passes beneath them afterward.

In deeper seams live soft-bodied creatures that seldom come entirely into the open. They extend a fan of fine arms into the current and collect fragments of crust, mineral grains, and the shed skins of grazers. Each arm can break away without killing the animal. A broken arm may continue to close around particles for some time, and a few that lodge in sheltered cracks grow into new individuals. They enlarge their dwellings by slowly dissolving the stone around them. When a seam closes under a walker’s weight, they contract into pockets just beneath its narrowest part.

The gray blades have roots far out of proportion to what shows above the ground. A tuft no wider than a hand may grip several long cracks, sending thin roots down into the warm water and tougher cords sideways beneath the crust. The visible leaves are expendable. When a storm strips them away, buds protected in the cracks produce more.

Around the stand behind the wall, the roots catch material washed through the gaps. Soft-bodied animals attach themselves to the roots, and their loose remains enrich the pockets of mud. The plants flower close to the ground, beneath stiff sheaths. Their seeds are heavy enough to sink and ridged enough to catch on rough stone. Most are carried away when the channels reverse; a few lodge against the wall and begin roots of their own.

There are larger grazers, though they are easily mistaken for loose pieces of the plain. They are low, gray-brown bodies with a flexible rim and a back that takes on the color of whatever sediment has settled on it. They move by lifting one edge at a time, drawing themselves over the crust in slow folds. In still water, a row of small openings along the back rises and closes. In a strong current, the entire body clings flat.

A large grazer leaves a broad scraped patch, exposing stone that the crust may take years to cover again. Smaller animals gather at the edge of that patch. They feed on the torn film and shelter beneath its lifted margins; later, when a new coating begins to spread across the bare stone, they graze it too. The large animals are uncommon enough that a single one can provide feeding ground for many generations of the smaller.

They gather in shallow basins at certain intervals, though the gathering is difficult to distinguish from an accumulation of stones. Their bodies touch along their rims. When they separate, small dark capsules remain stuck to the rock. These open after repeated wetting and drying, releasing young no larger than grains of gravel. A young grazer may spend its first season on the back of a larger one, feeding on the film that grows there.

Something lives in some of the stone rings as well. In the larger ones, fine tubes run between the stones and disappear into the mud. Their openings face inward, toward the calmer water. During heavy rain the tubes fill and stiffen, lifting the surrounding pebbles by minute amounts. When the water recedes, they slacken and draw the pebbles closer.

A ring with these tubes changes slowly. Gaps narrow; stones that have fallen inward are eventually nudged upright. Whether the animals began the ring or merely occupy it is hard to tell. Empty rings also retain their shape for a long time, and living tubes are sometimes found beneath a short stretch of wall. The animals feed by filtering water through their bodies, trapping what the current carries between the stones. Young tubes grow outward from the older ones, following the shaded sides of pebbles until they meet a neighboring branch.

The nearly bare rock inside some rings supports a thinner kind of growth, visible only when the surface dries. It appears as a network of dark points connected by lines finer than scratches. Water beads on it and rolls toward the stones rather than spreading evenly. Tiny grazers enter these rings, but they do not remain long; they cross the bare center quickly and collect in the wetter margins.

Beneath the plain, where the seams join, there are communities that never see the sky. They grow around warm flows and feed on substances carried up through the stone. Some form pale branching mats; others live inside translucent sacs that swell when fresh water passes over them. The grinding below can tear these colonies apart, but fragments travel in the upward flow. Now and then a warm pool develops a milky cloud, and by the next day a delicate white growth has appeared under its rim.

Many inhabitants spend much of their lives waiting. Eggs adhere beneath stones. Contracted crusts remain dark on the exposed pavement. The jointed grazers rest deep in cracks with their limbs folded, while the larger animals settle under a skin of silt. When the water returns, it brings motion before it brings food: a tremor through the rock, a change in pressure, ripples spreading from a place where no drop has yet fallen. The creatures emerge at different times. By the time a basin is full, its floor is already being crossed, rasped, filtered, and held.
