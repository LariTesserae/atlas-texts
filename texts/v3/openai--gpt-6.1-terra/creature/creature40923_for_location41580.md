The people of the shelf are broad-footed and short-bodied, with long, flexible hands for finding holds among the plants. Their skin is mottled olive and gray. Thin folds open along their ribs when they breathe, and when they are resting these move so gently that, among the fronds, a person can be mistaken for a piece of old wall.

They walk more often than they swim. Their feet fit the shallow hollows in the steps, and their toes spread on the springy ground. In the open lanes they push off from posts or doorframes and travel in slow bounds, catching a handrail when the current takes them sideways. Children practice crossing the channels without touching the bottom. They begin by hauling themselves along the polished rails, then learn to let go and glide from one post to the next.

Each household tends a patch of the plant mat. They lift away dead fronds, make room for young growth and guide the pale stems so their crowns do not shade everything beneath them. The clippings are food for people and grazing snails alike. Some leaves are folded around eggs or soft shoots and secured with a root fiber; others are laid across roofs and weighted with carefully chosen stones.

The houses need constant attention, though rarely the kind that requires taking them apart. A loosened pin can be driven back with a rounded stone. A wall bowed by roots may be eased upright over many days, a little at a time. The grove on the roof has its own keeper, an older woman who clears the finest leaves from the doorway and checks that the thicker roots are not pulling the wickerwork free. Fish shelter in the curtain she leaves behind, and her visitors part it gently.

People speak with clicks and low pulses made in their throats. A close conversation is quiet enough that the movement of a companion’s hands carries much of it. Across the town, a call can be tapped on a rail: a few notes that pass through the wood to anyone holding it. At the central basin, where several channels meet, the rails are crowded with hands when news arrives.

The children know the animals individually before they know their names. They watch the pale fish under the steps and learn not to put their hands too far into the drains. Crayfish are caught there in woven traps, though some are left alone because they keep the channels open. Snails are gathered from the walls and moved to places where growth has become too thick. When the little fish rise from beneath the fronds in a single cloud, everyone nearby pauses to see what disturbed them.

At night the town grows quieter, but not silent. Families settle in sheltered courtyards, beneath leaf roofs or among the stems around their houses. They tie loose loops of cord to a wrist or ankle so they will not drift far in sleep. The fish go on drumming beneath the shelf. Now and then a sleeper reaches out and feels the faint vibration of a neighbor turning, or the steady pull of water moving through a channel.
