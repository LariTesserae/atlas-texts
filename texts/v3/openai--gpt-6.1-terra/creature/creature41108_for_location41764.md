The shell animals are difficult to find while alive. They are the color of the gypsum seams, and their shells are almost transparent until the creatures die. They graze with a soft, square mouth, following the lines where roots have loosened the packing. A living one may take most of a day to cross a tile, though a tile is not always the same distance across.

The shells on the platform do not all come from animals that have died. Now and then a live animal is found with a pale, unfinished patch on its back, precisely the shape of a chip in a shell newly arrived on the stone. The patch hardens in a day or two. The animal does not appear injured, and goes on rasping at the seams.

The flat gray insects eat the trees while the plump grazers eat what the trees have shed. Each insect holds to a leaf rib with a row of tiny hooks, drawing resin through a beak tucked beneath its body. When the leaf begins to dry, it moves, lifting its hooks one after another so that a ripple passes down its length. Several can inhabit one ribbon without touching. Their young are narrower still, and when they gather along the edge of a leaf they look like the leaf’s own fine serration.

The grazers are less particular. They take dead fronds, fallen seed cases, the green nap from shaded brick, and occasionally a shell animal too slow to draw away from a seam. Their stone-colored teeth grow throughout their lives. Each grazer has a small bare patch under its chin where the teeth meet when its mouth is closed; older animals polish this patch against the tiles after feeding. They travel in loose groups, seldom quite together. If one hears another chewing ahead of it in a thicket, it may stop and wait, even when the animal making the sound is still behind.

The furred creatures in the crowns have broad, delicate ears and hands suited to clasping the leaf ribbons without breaking them. Their fur is dense along the back but thin at the throat, where the skin shows through in a warm pink fold. They click into it on waking because the fold carries the vibration well. Others nearby answer through their own fur, so quietly that a person standing below might hear only the first click and see several small bodies raise their heads.

They eat the flat insects, picking them from the ribs with patient fingers. They also eat the soft kernels inside the seed cases, but leave most of the cases themselves hanging on the trees. At dusk they move down into the undergrowth to drink the beads of condensed moisture that collect on the coolest tiles. They return by different routes, sometimes reaching a sleeping branch before another animal has finished climbing it. Such arrivals cause brief disputes: a ruffling of fur, a few sharp clicks, then one creature edges farther along the branch.

There are animals that tend the seams. They are pale, jointed things about the length of a thumb, with blunt heads and two stiff brushes beneath them. They gather loose gypsum against exposed roots and pack it down by walking over it. Wax clings to their feet; as they move from seam to seam, they carry some of the trees’ food with them. The roots grow toward their work. A seam that has been visited for several days becomes slightly raised, then breaks open in a line of new green tips.

The enclosure was planted too completely for any one kind of life to remain in its assigned place. Roots enter old planting sockets and press against the clay. Fronds shade young trees the builders meant to receive light. Grazers open bare patches that fill first with green nap, then with rosettes. By morning a path cleared the previous evening may hold a line of shell animals, each working steadily across it.

Someone still cares for the passages. Fresh fiber ties appear around the fronds, and broken tiles are occasionally set aside in careful stacks. The work is done sparingly: a leaf is bent back rather than cut, a root is given gypsum rather than removed. On the platform, the shells are sometimes gathered to one side, leaving room for more.
