The most populous parts of the basin are invisible from its rim. They lie on the undersides of stones, along submerged seams and in the mouths of cavities where water passes from darkness into light. Here microbes grow in films so thin that a wet surface may look merely slick. They gather energy from minerals dissolved in the water, some using substances that rise from within the rock, others living where that water meets oxygen near the surface. A film can contain several kinds together, each occupying a slightly different depth or fold.

The crustaceans live among these films. They are almost colorless, their bodies translucent enough that a dark gut or a grain caught inside can show through. They cling to stone with many small limbs, keeping low against the current. Seen in a dish of clear water they would be quick, but in their channels most of their movement is careful: a short advance, a pause while they hold against the flow, then the repeated working of mouthparts over the surface beneath them.

A sheltered stone may support a few of them, or a small cluster scattered along its downstream edge. They do not need a cave entirely sealed from light; they need water that will not sweep them away, and a surface on which food has had time to grow. A crack, a shallow depression, the narrow lee beneath an upright flake of shale: these are substantial places to them. The open water a few inches away may be too swift to cross.

The roar reaches them through the rock and water as continual vibration. Their bodies and the films they graze are always being touched by the current. Fine particles arrive and lodge in the microbial coating; others are carried away. Where a stone shifts even slightly, the sheltered space beneath it can narrow or open, and the flow through it changes. Some animals may find another hold. Others are swept into the channels, where most of the pale surfaces offer nothing to cling to and little to eat.

There are young crustaceans in some of the patches, smaller and harder to distinguish from the clear water around them. Between one inhabited seam and the next there can be a great distance, measured against their bodies: long reaches of clean stone, fast water and empty pools. Now and then the flow carries an animal into a suitable crevice, or moves a fragment of living film onto a new surface. Most such journeys may end without establishing anything. The few that succeed are enough to make another patch possible.

Lift a stone from one of these places and the life beneath it is easy to overlook. The film shines like the rest of the wet rock. The crustaceans hold fast until the water and the stone part around them; then their limbs move in the air, minute and rapid, before they are lowered back into the current.
