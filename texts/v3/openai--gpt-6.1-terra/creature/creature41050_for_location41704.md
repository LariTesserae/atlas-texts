The grazers have low, many-jointed bodies and shells grown in overlapping plates. Their undersides are crowded with short gripping limbs, so that even on the steep terraces they can pull themselves along the yielding root-felt. Beneath the front of each shell is a rasp, hard-edged and continually renewed. A grazer presses it into the floor and works its whole body from side to side. The sound travels much farther than the animal itself: a slow, rough grinding, interrupted when a plate knocks against another grazer’s shell.

They seldom eat all the way through a strand. What they leave behind branches and thickens, and the places most heavily grazed become dense with new growth. Loose fibers catch on their limbs and are carried along until they tangle in a hollow or snag against a terrace wall. Smaller animals follow in the disturbed water, taking fragments too fine for the grazers to hold.

Inside the bed live the burrowers. They are soft-sided, with blunt heads and powerful rings of muscle that pass down their bodies as they push between roots. They do not simply make holes: they draw strands across the spaces behind them, fastening each to the next with a secretion that hardens slowly in the cold. A newly made passage remains open for a while, lined with smooth leaves that bend inward as the roots grow. Old passages become seams and chambers buried deep in the felt.

The blind creatures on the burrowers’ backs are hardly larger than knots in the roots. Each has a ring of hooked feet and a mouth that opens into the current stirred up by its host. They feed on the loosened threads, the films growing over them, and one another’s discarded skins. When a burrower pauses, they creep around its body and pick at the lining of its passage. Their young leave in the water, then cling to the first moving surface they meet; many are scraped away, but a few find another burrower and remain there for years.

Between the fronds travel the narrow swimmers. They hold themselves in loose ranks not by sight but by a stream of faint clicks, each animal answering the ones it can hear. A rank spreads where the water is open and draws close when it passes under a canopy. There, the swimmers turn their long bodies sideways to comb suspended fibers from the water. Their clicks change when they feed, becoming slower and uneven as neighbors drift apart.

Some of the fronds’ inhabitants never venture into open water. They hang beneath the dark sheets by adhesive pads, reaching upward to graze the films that gather on the surface. Others live in the folds near the anchor, where torn fibers collect. When an edge glimmers green, it briefly shows pale limbs, translucent bodies and the small swaying shapes of young attached among the fibers. The light fades before they have moved far.

The great animals below the plateau are heard more often than seen. Their calls rise through the root-bed as well as the water, making the burrow walls tremble and the hanging fibers stir. Sometimes a call is answered from another flank, then from farther away, until the sounds overlap and the animals’ positions are difficult to separate. They come up to the terraces to feed where the grazers have exposed tender growth. A passing body can flatten a wide stretch of floor, but the roots remain joined beneath it; after the animal has gone, the pressed stems slowly lift, and grazers return to work across them.
