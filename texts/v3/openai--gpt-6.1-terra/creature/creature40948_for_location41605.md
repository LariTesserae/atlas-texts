The people who live here are used to doing several things at once. A mechanic walking to the pumping station may carry a spanner, a basket of washing and a message for someone in the workshops. She can tell from the vibration underfoot whether the pump is running as it should, though she still goes inside to look. Her boots leave pale dust on the station floor; in wet weather they bring in seeds that later sprout beside the threshold.

The workers’ houses hold families, solitary people, and people who have come for a season and stayed longer. Doors stand open in the warmer part of the day. Someone is usually mending a coat or sorting bolts at a kitchen table, within earshot of the yard. Children know the numbers on the workshop doors before they know what happens behind all of them. They race the carts along the rails until a driver makes them stand clear, then walk beside him asking for a ride.

Those who work the kiln learn where a wall will be warm hours after the fire has been banked. In the cold evenings they sometimes lean there to talk. The fox knows the place too. It sleeps higher up, ears turning at voices below, and occasionally opens one eye when a cart passes. The workers have become accustomed to seeing its tail hanging over the brickwork. They leave it alone, although someone will always point it out to a visitor.

Rabbits come out from under the unused kilns when the yard quiets. They crop the plants nearest their entrances first, leaving little round clearings in the green. The lizards are bolder in sunlight, flattening themselves against the warm walls and vanishing into cracks when a shadow crosses them. At the sumps, beetles pick their way over wet stone; sparrows descend in a crowd to drink and scatter again at the approach of a person.

The sparrows have many places to hide. They quarrel in the pumping station ivy, inspect the eaves for loose nesting material and fill the roofs with noise each morning. The swallows keep to the open air, passing so close to the yard that a child reaching upward might believe for an instant that one could be caught. Their nests mark the sheltered ledges of the houses, and people watch for their return.

After dark, moths gather around the lamps and bats turn among them. Workshop light shows through the misted windows. Someone crossing home can hear a radio faintly through a wall, then the stronger, steady beat of the pump. The people here notice changes in that beat, in the weather, in which rail holds water longest after rain.

They notice the vine as well. When the mechanics grease the crane bearings in spring, they have to work around its stems and reach through the new leaves. Some say the old arm moves more freely afterward; others say it would turn in a strong wind anyway. They continue to grease it. Later, as the flowers come out, people look up from the yard to see how far the arm has traveled.
