The beavers know the basin by water level. A submerged entrance that was deep enough yesterday may be exposed by morning; a familiar channel may have become too shallow to carry a branch through. They travel it again, pushing through sedge or under a shelf of ice, and work where the current has begun to hurry. Mud goes between the sticks, then more sticks. Sometimes the repair holds. Sometimes the water opens another seam a few yards away.

Several generations shelter in the lodges. The young come out into pools already crossed by the paths of other beavers, where cut willow floats against the banks and shoots have begun to grow from the dams themselves. They learn to dive, to strip bark, to haul a branch awkwardly broadside before turning it to fit a channel. In the cold months the lodges are close and damp with breath. In the growing season, green leaves gather around the roofs until a dome can be difficult to distinguish from the thicket beside it.

Water voles make smaller routes through the same country. Their tunnels open just above the water beneath the grass, and their narrow runs pass from one patch of sedge to another. They eat where there is cover, leaving short pieces of stem and neat heaps of cut vegetation. When a shadow moves overhead, a vole slips into a bank or enters the water with scarcely a ripple.

Fish hold in the clear channels, facing the flow, moving out of the current to rest behind stones or among roots. Insects cling to the undersides of gravel and browse the films growing there; others rise through the pools and emerge on the sedges. Frogs wait in the warmer mud and feed where the shallows open around the hummocks. Their eggs, when the season comes, lie in sheltered water that the dams have slowed.

Birds carry nesting material into the willows: fine grass, loose fibers, bits of moss. They move between the thickets and the open water, taking insects from stems or dipping briefly to the surface. An otter passes through less often. It can follow the channels from pool to pool, disappearing beneath the water and reappearing farther along, sometimes with a fish held crosswise in its mouth. At the pond edge it rests among the reeds, close enough to the water to enter without crossing open ground.

The mosses do not wait for settled soil. They spread over wet stone and dam wood, holding fine silt among their stems. Fern roots enter cracks in the exposed ridges; willow roots reach into the soft banks and bind them as they grow. Where a beaver has cut a stem, new shoots may rise around the stump. Where a dam overtops, sedge catches what the water carries. The inhabitants continually alter one another’s ground, and each new piece of shelter or shallow water is soon occupied.
