The copper-backed grazers are called roofbacks by the people who share the plateau with them. Their backs are not armored: each copper scale is a stiff, overlapping leaf of skin, warm to the touch and able to lift at its edge. When rain comes, the animals raise their scales and stand with their heads lowered, collecting water in the channels between them. Their young drink there before they are old enough to follow the herd to a pool.

A roofback spends much of its life with its face hidden in grass. It has four broad, soft feet and a narrow mouth that can strip a seed head without disturbing the stem. Its ears, which show above the grass, turn independently. A herd can lie so still that only the turning ears give it away. When danger approaches, one animal stamps, and the others stamp in succession, sending a small thunder through the ground. Then they move off together, usually without running.

The grey animals on the cliffs are known as seam-walkers. They have six limbs, each ending in a dark pad fringed with hairs. The hairs sense water moving inside the stone. A seam-walker follows a seep until it finds one of the pale cliff plants, then rests beside it and drinks what gathers in the folds of its leaves. It also eats the tiny soft-bodied things that feed on the plant’s roots. The plants grow more thickly along routes that seam-walkers use, though whether this is because of their grooming or something left behind in their fur is not certain.

Their young begin life in the water. They are almost transparent, no longer than a finger, and live under stones in the warmest pools. As their limbs lengthen they climb out at night, returning to the water before dawn. For several weeks they practice on fallen branches and the low walls of basins. A young seam-walker that slips is likely to fall only into moss; the adults wait on the cliffs until it can reach them.

The clicking around the pools comes from several kinds of animal, but the lowest notes belong to round, shelled creatures that live half-buried in the mud. Each has a pair of hard plates beneath its throat. It draws water over them and snaps them together, making a sound that travels through the pool and into the rock. The calls are not made all at once. One begins, its neighbors answer, and the rhythm travels from basin to basin. After rain, when new channels have opened between pools, the chorus can be heard from much farther away.

The broad-winged animals feed on things lifted by the warm air: seed fluff, winged insects, and sometimes a small fish caught up in the spray above a fall. They have wide mouths and fine sieves behind their jaws. At dusk they land among the highest ferns, folding their wings around the fronds without crushing them. A fern may hold one sleeper for years, its stem growing thicker beneath the familiar weight.

Their shadows are less dependable. On some days each shadow keeps faithfully beneath its owner. On others it lags, crosses a neighboring shadow, or travels up a slope while the animal glides down the valley. Roofbacks watch these moving patches of darkness, but do not always flee them. The people of the plateau watch them too. They say that a shadow which stops at a pool is looking for its wing, and they leave it undisturbed until the wind changes.

The people themselves are not numerous, and they do not live together in a permanent town. They move between the valleys in small groups, following the seasons of fruit and the slow changes in the water. An individual may return to the same ridge after many years and find that a shelter made by a grandparent has grown another clear layer over the stone.

They are long-limbed, with dense, dark hair over their shoulders and backs. The hair traps a little moisture, keeping their skin cool when they leave the shade. Their hands have broad palms and short, strong fingers suited to gripping wet rock. They can walk upright through the grass, though on steep ground they often use all four limbs. Their voices carry well in the thick air: a low call from one ridge can be answered from another without either caller being seen.

A shelter begins as a scrap cut from an older one. The people press it into a warm seam in the rock and keep it damp. Over the years it spreads, forming a roof and walls wherever its edge finds enough water and mineral salts. Doorways must be kept open by hand. The bowl inside grows from the same material and draws a little water through the wall; it fills slowly and is never large enough to empty a nearby seep.

When a family leaves a shelter, they clean the bowl and clear the moss from the entrance. Anyone may use it afterward. Animals use the shelters too, especially during heavy rain, and the people sometimes arrive to find a seam-walker asleep across the warm floor.

The three poles are older than any shelter. The people tend the ground around them, cutting back plants whose roots might reach the bases, though no one remembers who first taught this work. When the evening arcs appear, they pause if they are nearby. Children are lifted high enough to see the light in the cloud below; older people check the poles for any change.

Afterward there is supper to gather, water to carry, and a place to sleep. By morning the arcs are gone, the poles stand straight, and the roofbacks are feeding in the grass around them.
