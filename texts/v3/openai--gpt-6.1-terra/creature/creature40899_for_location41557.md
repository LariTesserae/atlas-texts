The animals are not all of one kind, although at first their stillness makes them seem like parts of a single covering. Some have six legs, some many more. In the narrow spaces between them are thread-bodied creatures with no shells of their own, their amber organs showing through a skin as clear and dry as mica. A few cling to the undersides of the larger animals. Their feet are sunk into seams in the shell, where a line of pale powder has collected.

The grazers have wide, ridged plates inside their mouths. Beneath each one is a shallow depression, its floor patterned with the marks of those plates. The hooked hunters are rarer. Most face a grazer at close quarters, with the points of their mouths resting against its softer edges. Their own shells are often occupied: little animals sit in the grooves along their backs, and smaller ones still are tucked between the segments of those animals’ legs.

In one place a hunter has been split open, perhaps by a falling stone. Its empty shell remains upright among its neighbors. The cavity holds a fine sorting of grit and several living creatures, the largest no bigger than a fingernail. Their lights are visible deep inside, where daylight cannot reach them.

The columns are made by animals of several shapes. At the bottom there is usually a broad one, its legs spread against the weight. Above it stand narrower bodies, sometimes from different species. Their feet fit into sockets or grooves on the heads below. Some columns lean so far that the uppermost animal hangs beyond the base, yet each joint remains precisely seated. There are broken columns too, their members lying separately among the grit. The animals in them are alive.

The plants make a second, less visible pattern. A root exposed beside a shelter is wide and flat as a strap. Its mineral sheath has been worn through at one edge, revealing a dense gray interior, folded over and over. Flint cuts cross it there. The same root, or another like it, appears at a distance beneath a black stone, then again between two mats of animals. Where its leaves open to the light, the small creatures gathered around them are arranged head inward. Their mouths are too small to distinguish without a lens. The leaves bear no obvious wounds.

People have lived here, though the shelters give little indication of how long they stayed. The lowest stones in several rings are polished on their inner faces, where bodies or clothing must have rubbed them. A bowl has been repaired with a cord passed through pairs of carefully drilled holes. In another shelter, a blade has been laid beside a row of shells, each shell set with its opening toward the doorway.

The pictures are more crowded than the view outside. At first they seem to show animals piled upon animals, but some of the lines have been scratched over older lines. The makers returned to the same subjects: the locked jaws, the columns, the dark fringes around the leaves. One shelter contains a long sequence of marks showing a single grooved shell from above. The grooves are the same in every picture. Beside them, a human hand has been drawn again and again, approaching from different directions, its fingers sometimes spread and sometimes closed.

There are no human remains in the shelters. A length of filament cord lies just outside one doorway, with a knot at either end and a little pink grit caught in its twist. Inside, three animals sit in a bowl. Their lights can be seen through the sides when the bowl is lifted. Beneath them, the clay is marked by three pale patches, exactly the shapes of their feet.
