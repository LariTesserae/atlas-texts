They live inside the fitted stone, in spaces too small to be seen from the passages. A few are beneath the basins on the towers; many more occupy the damp clay between blocks. The largest lives under the central water, spread through a network of joints across several terraces.

They are almost entirely water themselves. Each has a skin so thin that it clings to the walls of its chamber, with fine folds that extend into the wet clay. In a dry joint the folds withdraw and the skin tightens into a pale, brittle film. It can remain that way for years. When water returns, the film takes it in, loosens, and begins again.

They draw what they need from the water and the stone: dissolved minerals, traces carried in on the wind, and energy from slow chemical changes along the wet faces of certain blocks. It is a meager living. One beneath a tower may spend a whole season drawing nourishment from a single drop as it passes. Those in the central basin receive more water but less of its movement; they grow wide and very thin, divided by the corners of the paving tiles.

A body has no fixed center. Its active parts gather where conditions are best, then diminish there as another part thickens somewhere else. In this way a resident may shift the greater part of itself from one joint to the next without ever leaving the stone. The large one under the central water has occupied some of its joints for so long that its oldest folds are trapped beneath mineral deposits. New folds reach around them. The trapped ones still take in water when the basin rises.

The inhabitants are sensitive to pressure. A drop striking a tower basin sends a small change through the clay beneath it. A thread of water entering a groove, a stone cooling in the evening, the gradual weight of the central pool rising by a fraction: all press upon their skins. Each body responds slowly, admitting water to one fold and closing another. A change in a shared joint can pass from one inhabitant to its neighbor. Along a well-connected run, the effect of a drop may travel several blocks before it fades.

This is not speech, but neither is it meaningless to them. A body that repeatedly feels water arriving from one direction thickens its folds there. Its neighbor feels that thickening and may adjust in turn. Over a long time, a group comes to fit the particular habits of its stones: the hour at which a shaded face begins to bead, the delay imposed by seven cups, the occasional filling of an unfinished socket. When a channel is blocked by a fallen chip, the bodies below it become still. Those above continue to swell with each arrival of water, pressing farther into their chambers.

New individuals begin when a fold is pinched off in clay or sealed behind a mineral crust. Most such fragments dry out. Some remain folded for a long time before a wet season reaches them. Others are carried a short distance in water, too small and clear to be noticed, and lodge against a seam. If the seam stays damp, the fragment opens along it.

They did not build the basin. The tool marks were already on the stone when the first of them entered it, and they have no way to lift a block or finish a wall. They make use of what was left: every delay in a groove gives them longer access to a little water; every tightly sealed joint offers a chamber sheltered from the wind. The unfinished places serve them as well. In the sockets at the end of the wall, water stands for days after the neighboring surfaces have dried, and the clay beneath remains inhabited longer still.

From the rim, nothing of them is visible. At the last step, a shallow pool rests beneath the white sky. Within the sealed joint under it, a fold takes in water and slowly presses against the stone. By the time it has filled, the light has moved on.
