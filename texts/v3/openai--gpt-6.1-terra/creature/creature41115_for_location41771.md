The cleft’s inhabitant has no name for itself, and no single place where it can be found whole.

Its oldest parts lie under the moss on the western ledges: a mesh of pale strands running through peat and dead root, fastening one cushion to the next. The strands enter cracks in the stone and emerge yards away beneath the willow. In the sheltered dark they thicken into soft, knotted cords. When exposed, they dry to a brittle gray that resembles the roots around them, though they have no leaves of their own.

The fall severed many of these cords. Some now hang from the broken wall, their ends sealed with a clear, tacky substance that catches grit. Others remain buried beneath the sharp blocks. The buried parts are still alive. Water reaches them through the fractures, and they draw what they can from the crushed plants pressed against the stone.

On the ledges above, the loss has changed the pattern of growth. Moss nearest the break has gone dull and loose; farther back it is unusually thick, mounded over the strands as though covering a wound. Where a willow root was torn free, fine white threads have begun to gather around it. They will not pull it back into place, but if the root holds through the winter, they may help it take hold again.

The inhabitant grows toward wetness and away from open air. It makes use of the plants without entirely belonging to them, and takes shelter in the mountain without being stone. Its reach has changed many times as ledges have broken and new seams opened. Some of the dark cords deep in the cracks are dead, left behind when the living mesh moved elsewhere.

Wind is useful to it. Leaves, spores and fine soil collect behind the same rocks that give its strands shelter. But the wind also dries the exposed scars, and each fresh fall can tear away years of slow growth. The grinding from below has been coming for several seasons. It travels through the stone as well as the air; whether the inhabitant can distinguish it from other disturbances is impossible to tell.

After rain, a bead hangs from the underside of a fern. It falls into the moss and disappears. Beneath it, the pale strands swell slightly, so slowly that nothing in the cleft seems to move at all.
