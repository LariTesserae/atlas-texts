The most numerous inhabitants are the thin pale films at the edges of pools. A film begins where warm water rests against a new surface, and for a while it is indistinguishable from the bloom that sometimes forms on wet clay. Then its edge divides into minute, unequal branches. Each branch follows a seam or the faint groove left by a tool, staining the clay beneath it a little darker.

A film can survive the loss of its pool. When the water slips elsewhere, it remains on the paving as a dry-looking lace, although the clay under it is still wet. Rain brings it back. The branches thicken, join one another, and sometimes form a closed figure no larger than a thumbnail. Such figures are common on the floors of cisterns, where generations of films have been stranded and revived. In an old cistern the stains may overlap so densely that the paving appears to have been written on.

The films take nothing from roots or soil. They live on differences between surfaces: the newly pinched bank of a channel and the older plate beneath it, a brick’s smooth molded face and its rough broken end. A film grows across such a boundary and leaves the two sides more alike. In time, the edge of the brick becomes soft enough to accept the fine branching impression. Whether the film feeds upon the difference or merely uses it to find its way is difficult to say. It does not travel through water. Water must come to it, or the paving beneath it must be carried somewhere else.

A second kind of inhabitant occupies spaces rather than surfaces. These are the dwellers in the unfinished chambers: small at first, no larger than the hollow a cupped hand might leave in mud. They are easiest to find in walls that have just risen. A brick is lifted or slumps out of place, exposing a cavity with a smooth interior, still warm and faintly luminous. The cavity has no opening until the brick is disturbed.

Left alone, it may enlarge. The wall around it thickens by a little each dark interval, and adjacent bricks settle into an arch. A chamber can grow to the size of a room without ever acquiring a door. If water enters through a seam, the chamber often makes a narrow passage for it, pressing the surrounding pellets apart. The long hiss heard beneath the plain sometimes ends at one of these sealed rooms, newly full.

An occupied chamber can be distinguished from an empty one by the way it bears pressure. An ordinary cavity collapses when the soft wall above it becomes too heavy. An occupied chamber yields, becoming flatter and wider; when the weight shifts, it slowly returns. It may also keep a small object in a particular place. A fallen brick at its center will be found there again after the chamber has changed shape, though the surrounding walls have moved several feet.

The brick need not be special. A mold, a lump of packed pellets, or a piece of paving will serve. Once a chamber has held an object long enough, the object begins to appear in other chambers. Its copies may be far away and differently situated: one lying flat, another set upright in a wall, another half submerged. They retain the original marks at first. Later each acquires marks of its own, and some of these pass between them.

The chambers are not all alike. Some remain shallow and endure for a long time under the open plain. Others bring walls up around themselves, adding court after court until they are enclosed by a district. A few appear to divide: a partition forms across a room, the partition thickens, and after several dark intervals each side begins to keep a different object. When a district is displaced, its chambers are often displaced with it, although not always to the same place. A wall may arrive intact while the space it contained is found beneath another wall at a considerable distance.

There are also inhabitants associated with the marks of hands. They have never been seen as bodies standing over the work. Their presence is known by sequences of impressions.

A first impression may be found on the top course of an unfinished wall: four narrow fingers and part of a palm, pressed deeply enough to raise a rim of clay. By the next light interval there may be another print on the inner face of the wall, and a third beneath the coping. The fingers bend in different places from one print to the next. Sometimes there are more fingers; sometimes one is very short, as though still being made.

The impressions are not simply left behind. Their ridges soften, deepen, and exchange position while the surrounding clay remains still. A print can close around the edge of a brick without pulling it loose. Later the brick may be found a short distance away, set accurately into a course, with the print flattened beneath it. No track connects the two positions.

These inhabitants seem to require work in progress. They are commonest at the abrupt ends of walls, beside empty molds, and under roofs that have not yet become upright. When a structure is finished, its impressions gradually lose definition. Sometimes they reappear at the nearest unfinished edge. Sometimes a large, careful thumbprint remains for many intervals, holding the end of a wall in its shape.

During the disturbances, the prints are among the first things to change. Fingers lengthen across the clay; palms overlap; a mold may turn inside out around an impression that was on its outer face. Afterward there are usually more prints than before, but they are scattered among the new structures, and no sequence can be followed with certainty.

The films, the chambers, and the handprints meet in the larger enclosures. A film may spread across the floor until it reaches the threshold, then continue in branching stains along the inside of the gate. Beneath the paving, chambers form and fill, leaving the surface level. On the walls, impressions gather at places where another course could be added. Each kind makes use of what the others leave: a chamber brings water near the surface; water revives a film; the softened edge of the film takes and holds a new print.

The immense enclosure stands quiet for some time after it appears. Its paving is bare except for shallow water and the slight irregularities where the plates meet. Then, along one seam, a pale film begins to branch. Under a plate near the center, a small space gives way and holds. By the low gate, on the inner side where the clay is still especially soft, the first narrow finger joint presses into it.
