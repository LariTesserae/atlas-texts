The living ground is made of many plants, though it is difficult to tell where one ends and another begins. Their roots have grown through one another for so long that a broken piece may contain the tissues of a hundred individuals. Some send up tall stalks; others never rise above the surface at all. The buried plants spread slowly through the old layers, taking nourishment from mineral seams and from the dust of their neighbors.

A seed that catches in a furrow sends down a pale, flattened root before it puts out a leaf. The root presses into the mat and holds fast. For years the young plant may be visible only as a seam that opens and closes with the wind. If it survives, a stalk emerges, furled tight against abrasion. The silver leaves come later, and the great turning seed head later still.

Old stalks bear the marks of many repairs. Their flutes twist where splits have sealed, and their crowns may grow lopsided after a vane has been torn away. The cords inside continue to work as long as the head can turn. Beneath the plant, chambers close around mineral nodules, grinding them into a fine powder that the roots take up. Other chambers open the seed cases. When the wind changes, a head that has been turning all morning may stop abruptly, hold against its cords, and release a burst of seeds as it swings around.

Small winged creatures live in the cavities of the heads. They are pale, flat-bodied animals with folding wings and hooked feet, able to cling to the ribs while the vanes revolve. They feed on the soft lining of unopened seed cases and on the seeds themselves. When a case splits, they crowd to the opening. A gust can take hundreds at once; some are carried to another plant with seed dust caught under their scales. Many never find another head.

The grazers are called rim-eaters for the narrow strips of new growth they crop from cracks in the ground. They have high shoulders, long legs and blunt, flexible mouths, with which they can pull tender tissue from between hard root layers. They obtain most of their water from that tissue. Their droppings are dry pellets that break apart quickly in the wind.

A herd moves with the opening of the ground. Each animal travels well apart from its neighbors, grazing for a few moments before walking on. The young keep closer to their mothers, sheltering behind their legs when the wind rises. Grazers call to one another in short pulses low enough to carry through the root mat. An animal standing alone may stop with one forefoot lifted, feeling for the movement of the herd.

The burrowers are shorter and heavier. Their overlapping plates are scored by grit, and their broad snouts have a hard cutting edge. They push through the newer layers of the mat, eating roots and packing the loosened fibers behind them. At intervals they turn downward into older ground, where a hollow may remain sheltered for many seasons. Several generations may use the same chamber.

Hunters wait above their runs. These are small, long-bodied animals with padded feet and narrow heads. They crouch beside a moving ridge, following it at a distance until the burrower stops to feed. Then they dig rapidly, pulling away the loosened root fibers before the animal can turn. In places where hunting is good, the ground is pocked with shallow excavations, each rimmed with pale shavings.

After a fire or a stripping storm, the exposed old layers support a different life. Tiny animals shelter in the curled growth bands, eating the first soft root tips that push through. Grazers come later, when the cracks widen. Burrowers follow the new growth across the damaged ground. Years may pass before a stalk rises there, and longer still before its seed head begins to turn. By then the furrows around it are likely to be old again, worked deeper by wind and crossed by many paths.
