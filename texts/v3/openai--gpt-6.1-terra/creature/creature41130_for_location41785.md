The inhabitants are very thin, but not flat. Each has a body folded many times inside a chamber, with one dark surface exposed to the stone. When the wall shifts, that surface gives first. The folds behind it open in sequence, receiving the movement and passing it deeper, where it can be spread among others.

They have no fixed number of bodies. A chamber may hold one for years; then a strain travels through the wall, and the inhabitant divides along a fold that has been forming since before the strain began. For a while the two remain joined at their deepest edges. Their exposed surfaces flex together. Gradually they learn the wall on either side of them, and the joint grows narrow enough to close.

The finest lines between the doorways are not routes for travel. They are places where inhabitants touch. Heat and pressure pass along them, but so do slower distinctions: how long a pulse lasted, whether a crack ended cleanly or branched, whether the last cold breath carried enough water to reach the seam. An individual can sense only a little of the room directly. Its knowledge of the rest arrives altered by every body through which it has passed.

They drink from the seam. Beneath the floor, their most extended folds gather around it, opening when a bead slips through and closing after it. They take the water inward and leave the dissolved mineral near the surface. This is why the white rings persist. Some are broken by later droplets; others overlap so closely that the inhabitants can feel a faint roughness through the shelf.

The light in a chamber comes from work being done there. When too much heat collects in one part of the wall, an inhabitant unfolds into the small space before its entrance. The air draws inward around it. It carries the heat into the stone, where others receive it, and its chamber dims as the next one brightens. The process is never quite efficient. A little warmth remains in each body, and by the time the light has crossed a wall, those at the far end are responding to a pulse that began some minutes before.

The low notes come from the oldest inhabitants. They lie farther back than the lit chambers, where the stone is under a pressure the younger bodies could not bear. When one releases its folds, the movement rings through a whole section of mountain. Another may answer, not as a signal sent across an empty room but because the note has reached it through the rock and changed the load it is holding.

The narrowing of the opening troubles them. They can compensate for the movement of the walls, and they do, but the cold air enters in a different rhythm each time. Some beads now form farther from the seam and evaporate before they arrive. Near the upper edge of the city, several inhabitants have begun extending fine folds toward the opening. They cannot reach the sky. They can feel the air brush the stone, though, and when it cools they hold that place open a little longer.

Below them, new folds are appearing around the seam. The city is becoming more sensitive there, able to distinguish a single bead from the tremor that precedes it. Whether this will be enough, or whether the opening will eventually close, is not something the inhabitants can tell one another. They keep adjusting to what has already happened, and the mountain keeps rising.
