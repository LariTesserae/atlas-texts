The most numerous inhabitants are the ones that never emerge. They move through the mat in narrow, water-filled passages, loosening and eating the soft white growth at its base. Some are no larger than a finger joint. Others make a traveling ridge in the surface several yards long, with smaller ripples following alongside. When two tunnels meet, the animals pause head to head, feeling at one another through the water. Then one folds itself flat and lets the other pass.

The pale burrowers come up to feed on the exposed green layers. Their bodies are broad and almost without necks, and their feet have long, blunt toes that spread their weight. They can move quickly over sound ground, but they test each patch before crossing, pressing it several times with the underside of the jaw. A feeding group leaves a scatter of shallow cups behind it. By the following day, the cups are usually full of clear water and the nibbled edges have begun to turn upward.

The grazers are dark, heavy animals with narrow muzzles and backs that slope toward the tail. Each keeps a considerable distance from the next, though the spacing is maintained with some care: when one lies down, its neighbors gradually shift around it. Their young stay close for the first season, following in the wet prints until their legs are long enough to reach the next patch of firm ground. In sleet the adults stand with their backs to the wind. Ice gathers along the ridges of their spines and breaks away in plates when they move.

They eat the deeper roots, gripping them below the surface and pulling steadily. A root may stretch for several feet before it parts. If the animal drops it, the loose end draws back into the mat, leaving a fine wake under the water. Smaller grazers follow the herds and feed on the torn ends before they disappear.

The long-legged birds take burrowers where their tunnels come near the surface. A bird may stand over a faint ripple for an hour, one foot lifted, its head angled so that the wind passes along the edge of its bill. When it strikes, it drives the bill through the vegetation and works it open underneath, prying apart a section of the living mat. Sometimes it brings up a pale animal. Sometimes it brings up only a twisting handful of roots, which it drops and watches withdraw.

There are birds that follow the reed stands, too. They nest among the white stems, fastening their eggs into shallow slings of wet fiber. During a storm an uprooted stand may travel a long way, rolling low over the ground with the birds flying behind it. When it comes to rest, they settle again among the stems. Some stands carry nests containing young; the parents return to these with food even while the root chambers are still beating and the whole bundle is inching toward a new hollow.

The reeds draw water through those chambers. Their thick cords tighten one after another, and the water passes upward through valves that close with a wooden knock. Fine growths inside the chambers take what they need from the water; other creatures live on the growths, clinging in rows where the current is strongest. When a chamber is torn open, its inhabitants spill out in a dark, moving film. The mat soon grows over them, and many survive beneath it.

The black shrubs hold the highest and driest ground, where there is enough stone close to the surface for their roots to grip. Their branches are crowded on the sheltered side with hard little leaves. Beneath them, animals collect during bad weather: grazers’ young, birds too wet to fly, and pale burrowers with their backs pressed together. The shrubs are not large enough to shelter them completely. The animals simply arrange themselves as best they can and wait.

Some of the largest hunters spend most of their time underwater. They lie in the flooded hollows with only a pair of low breathing tubes above the surface, looking much like broken reed stems. When a grazer enters a pool, the hunter follows its movement from below, disturbing little more than the loose fragments on the bottom. A successful strike is brief and violent. The herd scatters, then spreads out again at a distance, grazing with its heads turned occasionally toward the water.

During the great knocking, animals continue to find one another across distances that look wrong. Grazers call in short, resonant notes, and each animal answers its nearest neighbors. The calls can remain evenly spaced even when the herd appears to have thinned toward the horizon, its members dwindling to specks. A bird crossing a pool may land and begin to preen while its reflection is still moving beneath it. The bird occasionally looks down, but does not wait.

At evening, as the chambers slow, the surface fills with small signs of feeding. Burrowers lift their noses from the mat. Young grazers pull at the tender edges of old tears. Birds walk the shallows, picking through whatever the wind has carried there. Under all of them, passages open and close, roots extend into the day’s wounds, and water moves slowly through the packed green layers.
