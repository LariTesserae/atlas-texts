The pale chamber-dwellers are called *foldbacks* by anyone who has watched them sleep. Each draws its long body into three soft bends, tucking its head beneath the last, so that a sleeping colony resembles a stack of folded leaves. They have six short legs and wide, sensitive feet. Their skin is almost colorless except along the spine, where a line of dark branching marks shows through. Young foldbacks are transparent enough that, after a meal, the green in their stomachs can be seen.

They leave the chambers in the hours when the light is strongest. A colony travels in a loose procession under the foliage, testing the ground with its forefeet and feeding on the tender seams where one layer of the mat grows over another. They also eat small shelled animals, turning them over until they find the opening. Their mouths are padded; even a large group makes little sound beyond the occasional tearing of a stem.

The chambers are not nests in the usual sense. A foldback begins one by catching a young stem in the notch between its shoulder and neck and leaning on it until the stem keeps the bend. Others add stems, passing beneath them repeatedly as they enter and leave. When the arch is established, they bring strips of living root in their mouths and press them into the damp joints. The roots take hold, and the structure thickens on its own.

A chamber may serve many generations, though not always continuously. If the food around it becomes too coarse, or water gathers too deeply in its floor, the colony moves. An abandoned chamber remains hospitable for some time: small grazers shelter there, and the leaf-strainers fasten their eggs along its inner walls. Eventually the entrance closes. The compressed floor persists longest, a patch of unusually firm ground beneath the moss.

Foldbacks are attentive to one another but not especially quick. When danger approaches, the first to notice presses its side against a neighbor. The pressure passes through the group as each animal turns and leans. A colony can become motionless in this way long before the threat reaches it, every pale body hidden beneath a leaf or sunk into a shallow depression. If they must flee, they go in different directions and reassemble later by following the faint scent left by the glands in their feet.

The moss-backed grazers are smaller and more solitary. Their gardens begin while they are young, when spores settle in the damp grooves of their backs. A grazer keeps the growth cropped by passing beneath low leaves and rubbing against stems, but it cannot reach the center. There the moss rises into a soft mound, sometimes with tiny cups or a single pale frond growing from it. Older grazers carry whole communities of mites and minute crawling things. When two grazers rest together, these passengers cross from one back to the other.

The jointed leaf-strainers spend most of their lives hanging upside down. Their limbs end in hooked pads that leave no visible mark, and their bodies are so narrow that a leaf may hold several without sagging. They comb the air with fans of sticky hairs, drawing them through their mouths whenever enough spores have collected. In dry intervals, rare as those are, they drink from droplets on their own legs. They lay their eggs in threads, each egg separated from the next by a length of elastic fiber; if a leaf tears, the thread can stretch across the opening until the young hatch.

At dusk the rising animals emerge. They have no single pair of wings, but many clear fins arranged around a body shaped like a seed. They climb through the air by making small changes to the curves of the fins, and once among the trunks they remain almost motionless, taking suspended particles from the air. They are eaten by larger drifting animals, whose bodies are nearly as transparent. A hunter can sometimes be detected only by the sudden absence of the smaller forms in a patch of air, followed by the slow closing of a pale mouth.

The great animals that make the distant calls are mat-walkers. Even close by, they are difficult to see: their legs are low and set wide, and their backs rise gradually from the surrounding growth. The skin of the back is ridged and dark, with patches of leaf litter held in its folds. They move slowly not because they are slow to notice, but because they test each place before putting their weight upon it. Their feet spread against the surface, and fine feelers along their undersides register the tension of the roots. Over hollow ground they change course.

A mat-walker feeds by lifting a section of the living surface with its broad lower jaw and drawing out the soft growth beneath. The lifted mat usually settles back, though a long shallow fold may remain. Rain gathers there, and for a few seasons the fold favors plants with deeper cups. Smaller animals follow a feeding mat-walker at a distance, taking the exposed grubs and pale roots it leaves behind.

Its call begins as a vibration in the chest and passes through both air and ground. Other mat-walkers seem to feel it before it can be heard: a feeding animal will stop, lower its head, and stand with all its feet firmly planted. Calls pass between them across great distances, especially when the weather is changing. When two eventually meet, they touch the sides of their heads together and remain that way for a long time. Their young stay beneath the adults, emerging to feed in the sheltered spaces between their feet.

Below the floating floors live animals of another sort. Some graze on the pale undersides of roots, moving through the water with slow sweeps of fringed limbs. Others wait among the hanging fibers for fragments to fall. They have little use for eyes; many find their way by the slight currents made when water seeps down through the mat. A few come close to the surface to release their young, which attach themselves to wet roots and grow downward. If the floor above becomes too thick for enough water to pass through, the colony dwindles there and persists nearer the open edge.

The inhabitants rarely encounter the plateau as a single landscape. To a leaf-strainer it is the underside of one leaf and the air that passes beneath it. To a foldback it is a succession of feeding places and remembered chambers. To a mat-walker it is a map of firm ground, hidden water, and distant calls. Each carries part of the place along: moss on a back, spores on a leg, root fibers caught in the grooves of a foot. Where they stop, something else is often able to begin growing.
