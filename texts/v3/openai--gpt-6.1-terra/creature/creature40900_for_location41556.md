The ring is tended by the jointed animals. There are more of them than a glimpse suggests: they live in the cracks beneath the fins, pressed side by side in places where the water cannot quite reach. When a stone is carried away, one of them will eventually come out and replace it. The replacement need not be the same shape or even the same size. A shard will do, or a rounded pebble brought from somewhere beyond the rim. They set each stone down with the pale inner surfaces of their limbs, withdraw, and wait through several surges to see whether it stays.

They do not go inside the ring.

What lives there is difficult to distinguish from the grit. A single grain, lifted on the tip of a stone, looks like a crumb of white mineral until it is returned to the bottom. Then it moves, not toward the other grains, but into whatever small vacancy the others have left for it. The vacancy travels across the surface. The grains themselves scarcely appear to shift.

The creature has no fixed outline beneath them. Sometimes the grit covers a body as thin as a sheet, stretched between the stones; sometimes there is a depth to it, and a fin pushed into the white surface meets resistance for a long way down. The animal appears to feel with the whole of that surface. Each crack of stone makes a pattern in it, though not one visible as movement. A slab can split at the rim, and the white bed will hold the shape of the sound after the sound itself has gone.

It keeps these shapes for a while. Some fade quickly. Others persist through the overlapping blows, and when a similar crack occurs the two seem to find one another. The grit becomes very bright along a line no broader than a hair. A moment later, one of the unsettled slabs lifts higher than usual, or a distant point of the rim comes suddenly close. The creature is not necessarily causing these things. It may be learning the mountain by the noises it makes, and the mountain, still breaking into its own form, may be learning the creature in return.

It feeds when a slab splits. Not on the stone, though new grains do reach it from the ridge, falling slowly into the ring. It takes something from the instant of separation. The jointed animals feel that instant too: they draw their limbs tight beneath them, and sometimes one is caught in the open, motionless as the white surface. Afterward the creature in the ring is thicker and less still. Individual grains rise from it on fine clear stalks, turn in the current, and sink again.

The animals bring their young to the edge of the ring when they are newly shed and soft. An adult holds each one there until the low note has passed through it several times. Some are taken back into the crevices. Others slip free and enter the white bed. They do not emerge, but later a small articulated limb may appear among the grains, folding and unfolding without hurry. Whether the creature has eaten the young or kept some part of them, the adults continue to bring more.

The creature has a way of reaching beyond its ring. When the hollow lengthens, thin white streaks appear along the bottoms of distant cracks. A jointed animal sheltering in one may find the grains under its body, though it has not seen them arrive. Usually they withdraw when the animal moves. Once in a while they remain, adhering to a joint or the underside of a limb, and the animal returns with an awkward, careful gait to lay them at the ring’s edge.

There are times when the ridge beyond the rim points upward for days, and copper light reaches the hollow again and again. The creature responds slowly. Its grains loosen from their level bed and make a shallow mound facing the light. The jointed animals leave it alone then, even when the current has taken half the ring apart.

When the ridge next appears to descend, the mound subsides. A few grains may be falling from the far stone, each following its own unhurried course through water violent enough to fling a loose slab across the hollow. They settle among the others without a splash or a gap. For some time afterward, the creature does not take the shapes of the cracking stone. It holds something quieter, which the animals cannot teach their young to bear: they wait in their crevices, and the ring goes untended, until the first grain begins to tremble again.
