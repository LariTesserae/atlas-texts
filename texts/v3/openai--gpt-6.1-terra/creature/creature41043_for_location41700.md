The largest inhabitants are about the length of a hand. They have six limbs, each ending in a soft, broad pad, and a narrow body that can flatten against a frond. Their color is close to that of the basin floor: pale along the back, darker underneath, with a red dusting caught in the folds of their joints. When one pauses on a wall, its outline becomes difficult to distinguish from a shed skin.

A pair of thin membranes lies folded along its sides. To descend, it spreads these between its middle and hind limbs, pushes away from the growth, and drifts. The warm air carries it farther than a fall would; a small turn of the body is enough to bring it against another trunk. It lands pads first, folds itself, and continues on foot.

They eat several things. With a comb of fine mouthparts they strip the red threads from fronds, taking little from any one surface. They also break open seeds, hold the halves beneath their bodies, and pick out the dense pale kernel. The discarded husks collect on ledges and eventually find their way to the central chamber. Young creatures eat the softer threads and spend much of their time close to their birth hollows, where the fronds grow thick enough to hide them.

An occupied hollow is usually recognizable by its smooth entrance. Passing bodies wear the inner surface, and the plant responds by laying down firmer tissue around it. The creatures do not cut doors or clear paths, but they favor certain routes, returning again and again to the same sheltered turns. A passage that is used for several seasons may become a tube with a waxy lining, wide at one end and scarcely wider than a body at the other.

They are not solitary, though they seldom gather into a tight mass except when very young. Several may share a hollow, resting with their limbs tucked close, while others pass outside. On meeting in a narrow way, one presses itself flat until the other has gone by. Touch seems to matter: a returning adult often places a pad briefly against each of the young before settling among them. In the open chamber, where there is room to pass without contact, they sometimes cross the floor together and touch only at the far wall.

Their shed skins remain nearly complete. At first these cling to the undersides of leaves, translucent and delicately jointed. Later they loosen and fall. The floor of the central chamber is thick with them; when a creature walks there, its pads lift faint prints in the dust.

The smaller grazers live nearer the frond surfaces. Some are round-backed and slow, with mouths suited to the red threads; others shelter under the jointed cushions and emerge when seeds fall. Their movement keeps the cushions trimmed around the edges and carries seeds into cracks the larger inhabitants cannot enter. A few seeds germinate there, sending pale tips through the greenery toward the light.

After the basin darkens, the larger creatures become easier to see. They leave the hollows while the stone is still warm and move along the pale trunks, stopping where vapor seeps from a crack or where a frond has unfolded fresh growth. Above them the opening to the sky stays clear. Below, the settlement holds its warmth, and the youngest sleep in chambers that are still slowly thickening around them.
