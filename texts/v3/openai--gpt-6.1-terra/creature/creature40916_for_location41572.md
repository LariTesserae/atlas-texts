The most numerous inhabitants are the small pale animals under the water cups. Each has a flattened body and six limbs, though the rear pair are so broad and thin that they resemble flaps of skin. Their feet lie against the leaf without gripping it. A dark eye shows on either side of the head; between the eyes is a narrow mouth pressed to the leaf’s surface.

Their bodies are nearly transparent at the edges. Looking through one, it is possible to see a vein in the leaf continue beneath its abdomen. A fine white branching passes through the animal itself, from its mouth toward the folded limbs. It is not clear whether this is a part of its body or a pattern of light from below.

They are found singly beneath the smallest cups and in little groups beneath the larger ones. In a group, no two touch. They lie with their heads toward the place where the stems meet, as if facing the water above. Some have a round swelling along the back; in others the back is flat, and the pale skin is creased where the swelling might have been. A few smaller individuals rest farther down the stems, their limbs still tucked against their sides.

There are also animals among the scales of the trunks, harder to distinguish. Their bodies fit into the narrow spaces between the overlapping plates. Only the ends protrude: a pair of blunt feelers, sometimes a dark bead like an eye, sometimes a row of tiny hooks curved over a scale’s green edge. The hooks occur at regular intervals up certain trunks, all the way to the arches. On an adjoining trunk there may be none.

A scale lifted carefully reveals a creature scarcely thicker than the leaf-mat on the ground. Its underside is covered with short, clear bristles, each bearing a droplet too small to fall. Its upper surface carries the same green fringe as the scale itself. When the scale is lowered, animal and trunk fit together so closely that the seam disappears.

The foliage has other occupants whose outlines are less settled. Deep in a folded leaf there may be a soft mass the color of the warm air around it, held in place by a web of almost invisible strands. The mass has a surface, and at one end a shallow opening, but no feature that can confidently be called a head. Several leaves contain such forms. Their strands run into the veins, and the veins around them are unusually broad and white.

The hum is strongest near these folds. It can also be heard beside the pale root columns and at the mouths of the leaf-roofed chambers. In the open avenues it seems to come from many directions at once. Nothing in the folded leaves can be seen vibrating, but the small hairs along their inner surfaces lean toward the soft masses, and some hairs end in clear knobs that resemble those on the trunk animals.

The chambers are not quite empty. The green covering inside them is finer than the mat outside, and beneath it are shallow depressions in the substrate. Some are round; others are long and divided into several lobes. The covering continues unbroken across them. In one chamber, a pale limb lies in a depression, its tip resting against the wall. Following it inward is difficult: it becomes thinner and greener until it is indistinguishable from a stem.

The chambers stand apart, but their roots meet below the floor. Where the living mat has drawn back around a mineral nodule, pale fibers can be seen crossing one another in layers. Some are roots. Others have jointed sections and minute fringes along their length. They pass through the same soft substrate, sometimes lying together so closely that it is impossible to tell where one ends.

The large plants, too, shelter life within themselves. The furled buds are packed with pale grains, each enclosed in a delicate membrane. A grain opened along its seam contains a folded green blade and, beside it, a white form no larger than the dark eye of a cup animal. Some white forms have a crease down the middle; others have several. The buds remain closed around them.

Everywhere, the inhabitants fit the places that hold them: the cup animal beneath its reservoir, the scale animal inside its narrow seam, the soft body in its warm leaf. Even the tiny bristles bear their water in separate beads. None can be seen feeding or moving, and the bare-looking chambers give no certain sign of who might occupy them. Yet the longer one looks, the more of the country appears to consist of bodies resting against other bodies, their edges fitted together under the steady light.
