The room is crowded, though much of its life is small enough to vanish into the stone.

Ants use the old joints as roads. Their trails run around the bases of the walls, over fallen grains and beneath the skirts of leaves; in places the workers pass so close together that the line seems to flow. Beetles move more slowly below them, shouldering through the powder. Some leave fine furrows that remain until another foot crosses them or the wind smooths them away.

Among the clay fragments, lizards wait with their bodies flattened and their eyes open. Their skins are close to the color of the floor, but the quick turn of a head gives them away. When one strikes, it crosses a patch of open stone almost too quickly to follow. Then it is still again, a small throat moving.

Moths shelter beneath the leaves and in the deeper alcoves. Spiders have placed their webs where the wall’s traffic narrows, drawing threads from a clay edge to a bit of rock, or across a crack that insects must pass. Dust gathers on the silk. A struggling wing shakes it loose.

The bats keep to the crevice above. They are packed together, their folded wings pressed against their neighbors, and now and then one wriggles into a better position while the others scold and shift. When they leave, the room briefly fills with the sound of them: claws releasing stone, wings striking the confined air, bodies finding the opening.

The plants live at another pace. Succulents hold their thick leaves out from the wall; narrow-leaved mats spread over the shelves and grow through their own older, dry layers. Lichens make pale maps on the stone. Beneath them all, roots enter the seams, and dead stems remain standing long enough for insects to feed inside them. Every shelf has become a little different: one shaded by leaves, another bare except for lichen, another deep in dust and husks.

The settlement’s builders left useful shapes behind them, though not for these inhabitants. An alcove gives shelter; a passage carries air; a shard of clay makes cover for a waiting lizard. The room is dry enough that each disturbance can be heard. Even after the wind has gone, feet continue to patter, stems to rasp, and the bats to settle themselves against the stone.
