The dark animals in the flower cups are called cup-dwellers by the people who tend the towers. They are about the length of a finger, with narrow shoulders and soft, jointed limbs tucked so close to their bodies that a resting animal looks like part of the flower. In the morning each one climbs down into the stem to drink from the water gathered there. It spends the brightest hours curled around the flower’s central cup, turning slowly as the sun moves.

A cup-dweller has no use for a call. Its skin carries a row of pale marks that can be covered or exposed by the overlapping edges of its scales. When another comes near, the marks appear in quick arrangements: a broken line, a pair of dots, a white patch held open for several breaths. Most meetings end with both animals settling in separate flowers. In the season when the cups are deepest, two may share one, pressed together above the water, their tails hanging into it.

They eat the transparent insects that live beneath the leaves, though not many at a time. The insects taste the air with long, clear legs. In direct sun they become almost impossible to find; even their shadows are thin. A passing cloud gives them their chance to move from leaf to leaf, drawing up the droplets that collect along the veins. Their young live under the damp mats, where their bodies are milky rather than clear. They help keep the fine channels through the moss open, and in turn depend on those channels for the water that remains after rain.

Farther down, among the white trunks, there are larger animals that seldom come onto the exposed plateau. They have broad feet and gray coats, and their backs are often sprinkled with moss spores. They browse on the low branches and leave passages just wide enough for one of their bodies to pass. A gap may remain open for years if a family continues to use it. Once abandoned, it fills from the sides: fern first, then vines, then the slow black-barked trees.

These animals watch one another closely. A raised head passes through a group like a change in weather; one notices, then another, until all of them stand looking in the same direction. Mothers keep their young near by touching them with a long, flexible muzzle. At night, beneath the trees, the animals lie with their flanks together and can feel one another shift and breathe.

The people who come to the plateau are harder to see than the traces they leave. They visit the pumping house and the towers along narrow maintenance gaps, usually in pairs. At a distance their pale coverings resemble patches of exposed stone. They carry folding shades for the glass panels, water in sealed vessels, and tools wrapped against loss. A dropped tool makes no useful sound, and in the vegetation it can disappear almost at once.

They speak face to face with their hands and with small movements of the head. For work that takes them out of sight of one another, they use strips of polished material to catch the light. A brief flash from the pumping house can reach the nearer tower; from there another can be sent along the valley. On foggy mornings the workers wait until they can see one another or walk the message themselves.

The fibrous material in the carriers comes from the inner sheaths of a vine that grows through the older fractures. The vine keeps living after the sheath is taken, although a harvested stem grows slowly for a season or two. The towers draw the loosened fibers through their sealed housings, wash them with water from below the rock, and send them onward in shallow baskets. When a carrier stops in sunlight, the fibers spread slightly as they dry. Later the basket closes and continues.

Around each tower the workers leave some growth standing. Its shade keeps moisture in the soil and its roots hold the loosened stone. They clear the glass panels carefully, cutting away individual leaves rather than stripping the vine. Cup-dwellers sometimes settle in the cups that form along the cleared edge; the workers lift them out before opening a panel and return them afterward.

At the eastern cliffs, where even a shout travels only a few steps, a person can put a palm against the rock and feel a faint, irregular movement. Some of it comes from roots entering the cracks. Some comes from water passing through the stone toward the pump. The workers check the cliff this way when they visit, holding their hands still for a long time before making a note and moving on.
