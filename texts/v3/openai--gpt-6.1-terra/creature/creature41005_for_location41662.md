The most conspicuous inhabitants are the hollow-bladed plants of the canopy. Their stalks are pale where they rise through the dimness and darken toward the crowded heights. Each blade is stiff enough to sound in the wind, but its base is jointed and can turn. In settled weather the blades hold a broad face toward the light; before a storm they tilt together, exposing their silver undersides and making the canopy harder to bend.

A blade may go on sounding after its tip has broken away. The torn end seals with a clear skin, and neighboring growth winds around the exposed length. Spores form inside some of the hollow sections, in chambers that open at particular pitches. The wind need not be especially strong to release them: a blade responding to the motion of others may sound the necessary note on an otherwise quiet day.

Below these plants are broad-leaved forms that spend much of their lives almost horizontal. Their leaves overlap like damp plates, gathering the delayed rain. When water collects beneath them, they lift and make a passage; afterward they lower themselves again. Young leaves remain curled for a long time in the crowded air. They unroll into whatever space has become available, sometimes covering a fallen stalk before it has stopped leaking its stored water.

The threads between stems belong to many plants. Some are fine extensions of the larger leaves; others rise independently from the mossy floor and divide until they are difficult to see. Water travels along them in beads. Pollen grains move with it, caught and released at the junctions. In places the threads are stretched taut enough to carry a vibration from one stem to the next. Elsewhere they hang in slack veils, continually broken and renewed by the slow movements around them.

Beneath this tangle, the root tips work against the young mineral crust. They are pale and soft at their ends, with translucent sheaths through which grains and dissolved substances can be seen moving. A tip may settle against a shallow pool for days, laying down ordered films and leaving behind a thin, flexible layer of new ground. Other roots thicken within older crust until it parts around them. The opening fills with water and fine growth, and the plants begin to work along its edges.

There are plants small enough to live between the moss leaves and in the seams of fallen tissue. They form dark, wet mats on anything that lies still for long. A collapsed blade first loses its clear outer skin, then softens where the mat has entered it. Its ribs remain for a while as a delicate framework. Eventually these too are taken apart, and the space is occupied by fresh shoots.

Light reaches the lower growth only in scattered traces, but the plants there are not wholly dependent on what falls upon them. Energy gathered in the canopy moves through living tissue from plant to plant. It passes around a damaged patch and into it, sustaining roots and shoots whose own blades have been stripped. The connections are numerous and uneven: a stand may remain green after weeks of darkness, while another only a short distance away pales and sheds its leaves.

After heavy rain, the smallest inhabitants flourish in the shallow hollows. Their mats swell, and fine green points rise through the water. As the hollows drain, the points settle close against the mineral surface. Some remain there beneath the next layer of crust; others grow upward into threads, leaves, or hollow stems.

A new ridge begins among them. The ground firms around its roots, the roots divide through it, and shoots emerge along its back. At first it is possible to see water running down either side. Then moss covers the damp slopes, leaves spread across the channels, and the first hollow blades rise high enough to catch the wind.
