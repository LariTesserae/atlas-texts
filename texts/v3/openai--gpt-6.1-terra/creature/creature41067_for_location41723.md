The largest animals are difficult to count. They stand among the grass with their heads lowered, close enough to touch, and when one moves the others make room almost without seeming to notice. They are grazers, narrow-legged and watchful. Their teeth crop the coarse growth in short pulls; now and then a stem snaps loudly in the dark. A younger animal stays near an adult’s flank, pausing from its own feeding to listen.

They know the paths through the basin by the feel of them: firm ground between root and mound, a gap in the grass, the edge of a channel where the soil gives slightly underfoot. The open ground lets them feed together, but their ears turn toward the thickets. Once, several raise their heads at the same time. After a while the sound of chewing begins again.

Under the thickets live animals small enough to travel beneath the branches. Some have burrows with several entrances, half concealed by leaf litter. Others use the hollows left between old roots or occupy abandoned chambers in the termite mounds. They come out cautiously, noses working, and gather seeds or bite through tender shoots. One carries food a short distance before stopping to eat it, forefeet held close to its mouth. Another waits at an entrance until the grass has stopped trembling around a passing grazer.

The occupied mounds have their own inhabitants, mostly unseen. Termites move through dark passages in the soil and through the hard walls they have built above it. Workers tend their young, repair breaks and carry pieces of dead plant matter inward. After the rain, they may have found softer wood or fresh litter to use. The older mounds shelter different lives: beetles in cracks, a spider behind a loose crust of earth, perhaps a small lizard that will emerge when the sun warms the surface.

Beetles are everywhere the leaves have fallen thickly enough to hold a little moisture. Some push under curled edges; others drag fragments through the litter or feed where they find them. Their legs lift and shift the leaves, exposing pale undersides for a moment before they settle. Ants use the fallen limbs as raised roads, passing one another along the grain. A few leave the traffic to investigate a morsel and return, touching those they meet.

Above them, moths cling beneath leaves, their wings folded close. Some resemble scraps of bark; others show a faint, dusty pattern when they turn. Spiders wait between stems with their feet resting on threads too fine to see. When a moth or small fly strikes a web, the spider crosses quickly, then becomes still again beside its catch.

At a hoof hollow, a frog sits low at the water’s edge. The puddle is shallow enough that a falling leaf could cover much of it. The frog moves only when something comes near enough to eat. Another calls from farther into the grass: a short sound, followed by a long silence. The water will not last, but tonight it is enough to bring insects and the animals that feed on them together.

Something with wings passes over the herd. It may be hunting, or merely crossing the basin on its way elsewhere. The grazers do not scatter. In the grass, a lizard shifts against its stone, still taking warmth from it. Near morning, an insect climbs a stem and remains there as a bead of water gathers above it.

The trees and grasses shelter all of them without becoming quiet themselves. Their leaves lose moisture, their roots draw on what the rain left below, and damaged stems give off the smell of greenery. By first light, ants will still be moving along the fallen limb. The small mammals will have retreated underground, the herd may have wandered beyond the basin, and the frogs will be hidden beside what remains of the water.
