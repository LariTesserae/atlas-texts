The people of the high country learn to walk before they learn to stand still. A woven floor gives a little under every step, and a child who plants both feet rigidly is soon sitting down. Babies are carried in slings of plaited fiber, with one hand left free to grasp the carrier’s hair or collar. Later they are given short lengths of root to hold while they practice crossing the rooms where they live. Nobody hurries them onto an open bridge.

Most settlements occupy places where several terraces meet. Their houses are screens and roofs fitted between living trunks, with floors renewed a strip at a time. A family may have slept in roughly the same place for generations, although the doorway has migrated around the trunk and the hearth has twice been moved to accommodate a swelling root. Beds hang low in nets; shelves are pegged into deadwood panels rather than the living walls. At night the panels tick softly as they take up moisture.

People know the growth habits of the structures they inhabit much as they know the habits of relatives. One pillar drinks quickly and must be watched in a wet season, because it will thicken against the roof. Another sheds its bark in long, useful sheets but cannot be cut when its leaves are silver-side up. The knowledge belongs partly to households and partly to the terrace as a whole. When a brace begins to fail, everyone who uses the path has reason to help replace it.

The work can take years. A young trunk is bent a little, padded where the binding presses it, and left to grow. Children who first help carry its wedges may be grown before it bears a roof. Some repairs never quite succeed. There are abandoned platforms tilted among the leaves, their old lashings swallowed by bark, and passages narrowed to a slit through which only animals can travel.

Gardens are small and scattered. People grow tender shoots in baskets of rotted leaf, train edible vines along railings, and gather fruit from trees whose branches are too widely spread to call an orchard. They keep frond-grazers in enclosures woven loosely enough for new growth to pass through. The animals are valued for their milk and warm coats, but also because they crop back leaves that would otherwise close the paths. A grazer that develops a taste for fresh bindings is traded away quickly.

Water is collected from dependable drips in deep wooden bowls. Pools are treated more cautiously: a person approaching one brushes aside the floating leaves with a stick before putting a foot near the edge. Pool insects are trapped for food, and the pale wood-borers are dug from fallen limbs when they are fat. In a living support, their small round openings are plugged and watched. If powder continues to fall from them, someone listens with an ear against the bark, trying to judge how far the passages have spread.

The hunters beneath the paths are harder to manage. People can often feel one moving before they see it, a patient irregular pressure against the soles. On busy routes, strips of dry bark are hung below the floor so that anything climbing through will disturb them. Travelers carry hooked poles for lifting the bark and looking underneath. Children are taught not to trail their fingers through the weave, even on the floors at home.

A journey begins with questions. Has the white-scented flower opened beside the third fork? Are there still seven leaves in the long pool, or has one folded under? Someone returning from a neighboring terrace may be asked to describe the scars on the last arch, and then to describe them again after eating and sleeping. A changed landmark is not necessarily a disaster, but it is news that must be carried both ways.

Those who tend routes travel in pairs when they can. They bring cuttings of the flowers used as markers, spare ties, and flat stones for weighting leaves into their familiar positions. Where a path has become uncertain, they may sit for hours, feeling its slope and waiting to see which direction the mist travels. Sometimes they come home quickly from a place that should have taken days to reach. Sometimes the food runs low before a familiar railing appears. People leave portions of dried fruit and wrapped shoots at sheltered points along the longer bridges, and replace what they take.

There are settlements that see one another’s lights more often than they see one another’s people. Marriages and visits take place between them, but arrangements are made loosely. A guest expected in the warm season may arrive after the new leaves have hardened, bringing a child who was not yet born when the invitation was sent.

In the evenings, resin bowls are lit on platforms where their flames will not catch the hanging growth. Someone stays beside each one, trimming the wick and feeding it small pieces of resin. The lights help travelers find a landing, though not always the landing they intended. They also tell the people across the hollow that this terrace is still occupied, that its paths are being kept open, and that someone is awake to receive a stranger.
