The people of the city are in its sound. Each is a sustained arrangement of tones, too closely packed to be separated by an ear standing in the street. Near a wall, where the sound deepens, one of them may be felt as a pressure against the ribs. At a pool the same person is present as a fine, high band within the hiss. Neither is a voice in the ordinary sense. The tones have no need to begin or end.

They are not all alike. Some occupy long passages, their notes distributed along the living walls and gathered more densely beneath the arches. Others are small and intricate, held between the roots of a single building. The broadest extend into the rock below the basin and can be felt, faintly, on neighboring shelves. Where two meet, their tones remain distinct, though each is shaped by the presence of the other. The city’s immense sound is the sum of these lives, with very little silence between them.

The chambers were grown for meeting. The root-banks define a space around which several people can gather without any of them having to be confined to one place. The bare floor at the center is not a stage or an altar. It is where their tones overlap most clearly. The banks themselves carry the deeper parts of the sound; the pale growth above them carries finer harmonics. Moss stops at their edges because that is where the conditions for its growth change.

The trunks and roofs are alive in their own right. They draw water, hold leaves to the heat, and have grown into courses that suit the people who live among them. The relationship is old enough that the shape of a passage and the sound within it belong equally to both. No hand guided the roots. Their growth and the lives sounding through them developed together, until an arch became the familiar form in which several neighbors could be present at once.

The flowers facing inward are part of this life too. Their open throats hold small chambers of air, each tuned by its shape and wetness. They carry delicate portions of the sound into rooms where the great leaves would otherwise muffle it. Nothing visits them; their abundance serves the city as it is.

The people have differences that might once have changed: a tone leaning toward another, a close pattern opening into a wider one, a meeting held at its most intricate point. Now every such inclination remains exactly where it is. No one crosses a room, and no conversation proceeds from one note to the next. Even so, the sound is crowded with relation. The chambers are full of presences attending to one another, sustained in an hour that does not pass.
