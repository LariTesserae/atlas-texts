The grazers are called long-legged only because their bodies are so small. An adult could rest in the palm of a hand, though its legs would have to fold several times to fit. Their feet spread into soft, many-rayed pads, which leave little starbursts in the mineral around a pool. If the surface begins to give way, the rays curl inward and the animal lifts itself free before the crust can close over them.

Up close, their colorlessness is not quite complete. Something greenish moves through the clear body after a meal, and a narrow silver line runs from the head toward the tail. They feed with a fringe of fine mouthparts, skimming organisms from the water without seeming to touch it. Sometimes several stand around a pool with their heads lowered, calling harshly between mouthfuls. The calls travel much farther than their small bodies suggest. A grazer that has found fresh water can summon others from across the basin, though the first arrivals may drive it away from its own discovery.

Their young are darker and much less assured on their feet. They stay close to the translucent moss, where a fall is softened and water can be had simply by pressing a cup. The adults cross those patches quickly, trailing dark prints; the young often stop to drink from each print as it fills.

The hinged creatures spend much of their lives under stones, but they are not attached to any particular shelter. Their backs consist of overlapping plates, dull and pebbled like the basin floor. When closed, each animal resembles a rounded fragment of rock. When it opens, pale limbs emerge all around the edge, followed by a head so small that it is easy to mistake for another limb.

They come out together when warmth reaches the undersides of their stones. The clatter is made partly by their backs striking one another as they hurry toward water. At a crowded pool, they climb over their neighbors, drink, and climb down again, leaving a scatter of fine scratches in the soft rim. Some carry smaller animals beneath their raised plates. These are their young, or sometimes merely other creatures that have found a warm place to hold on.

The flyers spend less time in the air than they first appear to. In thick mist, many are clinging to the underside of the basin’s rising lip, folded so flat that their outlines disappear into the stone. They let go to feed, falling before their membranes open. The deep humming comes from movement too rapid to see clearly, though it changes as a flyer turns or slows. When several pass close together, the sound seems to come from the ground as much as from above.

They take tiny creatures from the air and occasionally from the pools. A grazer will duck when one passes low, but the hinged drinkers seem indifferent until a shadow touches them; then their plates snap shut in a quick succession across the water’s edge.

The blue-green fronds are older than most of the basin’s visible surfaces. Where a seam widens beneath one, it may lean almost flat for days, then slowly raise itself again from a new angle. Its roots have never been seen whole. Broken pieces of frond sometimes lodge in cracks and remain green through many rains, though few grow. The moss is quicker. A fresh wet shelf can acquire its first translucent film within days, and an abandoned pool may leave a crescent of it behind.

During rain, the black-glass shelter fills with a company that would not gather so closely anywhere else. Grazers stand with their feet tucked beneath them; hinged creatures crowd into the spaces between; flyers grip the upper edges, their membranes folded. They become quiet before the rain reaches the stone. Whether they hear the early patter inside the shelter or follow one another’s stillness is difficult to tell. When the rain stops, the first to leave often pauses just outside, while heat moves visibly over the basin and the others wait behind it.

The three structures do not seek shelter. They have no obvious mouths, roots, or young, yet the animals treat them as parts of the living ground. Moss grows nearest the metal ring where its joints pass slowly over a damp seam. Grazers sometimes stand within the ring after it has sounded, picking at whatever the trembling water has brought up. The suspended pane remains clear or clouded long after the break it attended has settled; flyers circle it but turn away before contact.

No creature stays still for long when the boom comes from below. Even the fronds quiver. The animals lift their heads or close their backs, and for a moment the basin seems to listen with its whole surface. Then feeding resumes, often before the last ring has reached the edge of a pool.
