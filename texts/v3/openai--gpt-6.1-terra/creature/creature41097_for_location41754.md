The plateau’s most numerous inhabitants live almost their entire lives without seeing its horizon. Beneath the green surface, small grazers work through the mat in family groups, eating tender roots and the pale shoots that grow in darkness. Their backs are broad and rounded, their fur short enough not to catch. As they travel, the roof rises over them and sinks again, leaving a slow seam across the open ground. When one finds a particularly rich patch, the others gather close; from above, the mat trembles as though something much larger were struggling beneath it.

The beetles use the same passages. Some eat dead stems, some feed on fungus, and some follow the grazers to pick through what they leave. In the morning, when dew has reached down between the leaves, they come near the surface in glittering streams. Later they retreat into the damp interior. A fallen stem may be covered in them by evening and reduced to a scattering of fibers within days.

The moss ridges grow slowly over generations of this activity. Their woody outer stems keep the wind from stripping the softer growth inside, while roots bind the ridge to the stone. The hollow ones shelter beetles, birds driven from their nests, and occasionally people. They have a smell of dry bark and cool earth. In a large chamber, the roof may quiver continuously with the movement of insects, though nothing can be seen passing overhead.

Birds occupy the fissures in dense colonies. Their nests are wedged onto shelves or suspended from roots, close enough that a returning parent must find its own young among hundreds of open mouths. They know the calls of their chicks, though to a listener at the rim the colony seems to produce one continuous shriek. Many birds leave the plateau only briefly, plunging out over the edge and rising again on the warm air. Others feed almost entirely on the swarms that emerge from the sedges.

The pale hunters at the fissure mouths eat both birds and moths. They are many-jointed and remarkably still, with limbs that can fold close along the body or reach across a gap wider than the animal itself. By day they wait under the mat, gripping roots with the tips of their feet. At dusk they climb toward the light, feeling the vibrations of the birds above them. A hunter may hold its position through several waves of moths, letting the insects pass until a bird comes close enough to seize.

The moths have spent most of their lives hidden among the sedges, feeding and growing through the brief wet hours. Their emergence is so sudden that a quiet-looking trough can become a column of wings in minutes. Many are taken immediately. Enough rise together, however, that the birds and hunters cannot catch them all. By nightfall the surviving moths have dispersed across the plateau, laying eggs among stems already drying in the wind.

People come to the basins when there is water. They travel in small parties along routes they can recognize by standing stones, flattened trees, and the shapes of the moss ridges. A route that appears safe from a distance may cross a fissure roofed over with roots; travelers test the ground ahead with poles and learn to notice the slight downward pull of the mat near an opening.

At a shelter they set vessels beneath the channels where water runs after rain. Sod roofs keep the interiors cool and make the noise outside a little easier to bear. Visitors sleep in shifts during the height of the swarms, when the ground shakes with wings and the hunters move through the vegetation. To speak outdoors they come close together; at a distance they use raised arms, cloth, or the turning of a polished vessel in the sun. Marks cut into shelter poles and stones tell later parties which basins held water and where the ground has opened since the last visit.

Some may remain for a season, gathering beetles and moths, tending small patches of useful sedge, and repairing the shelters as the living mat grows around them. Even then, the plateau does not become quiet or settled in the human sense. The roots keep advancing through a doorway. Birds take possession of an empty vessel. At night the grazers pass beneath a sleeping place, lifting its floor gently and carrying on.
