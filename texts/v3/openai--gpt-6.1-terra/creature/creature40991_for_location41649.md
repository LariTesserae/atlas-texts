The settlement lives as a crowd of growths so closely fitted that it is difficult to say where one life ends and another begins. The trees are its largest bodies. Their trunks carry old scars where branches have pressed together, and their roots grip the stone and one another. In some places, two trunks have grown around a gap that once admitted light; now their bark meets above it, and the gap has become a doorway.

Each tree keeps its own reach of needles in the canopy. The outermost branches take the snow and wind; those beneath receive less light but lose less warmth. Dead needles fall only a short distance before lodging in moss or in the forks of branches. There they soften and collect, making pockets in which other things can grow. A leathery plant may spend its whole life above the ground, rooted in what the trees have shed.

The moss is everywhere the trees can hold water for it. It thickens along the sheltered sides of roots, climbs the trunks in damp cushions and spreads over the stones in the pools. When it is wet, it swells into the smallest openings. In a dry or bitter spell it draws close and darkens, leaving the fine shapes of needles impressed on its surface. Beneath it, even during the cold, there are places where water remains liquid and an insect can move.

Lichens are slower inhabitants of the exposed edges. They cling to the blue rock where little else has yet accumulated, and to old bark above the dampest reaches of moss. Some are pale and crusted, almost indistinguishable from a stain in the stone; others lift tiny cups or branching tips into the cloud. They endure where the canopy offers little shelter, while the plants in the cracks behind them hold enough water to work the rock apart.

The occasional bird is a visitor rather than a resident of the lanes. It searches the upper branches, finding shelter and perhaps an insect in the folds of the growth, then leaves a small disturbance behind: loosened snow, a broken spray of needles, a dark mark on a branch. Below, the living mass continues its quieter traffic. Roots take in water; fungi thread through the accumulated litter; new shoots press toward whatever light reaches them.

The oldest parts bear the weight of many seasons. A fallen limb can remain caught overhead for years, slowly hidden by moss, while roots and stems grow around it. In a low chamber, pale growth spreads over wood that can no longer be seen. Elsewhere a young tree rises through a narrow opening, widening it above and narrowing it below. No single plant preserves the streets, but their competing shapes leave room for them, for a time.

When a root dies, the hollow it leaves may carry water. When a branch breaks, more light reaches the growth beneath it. What lives here is neither one creature nor a gathering of independent neighbours. It is a dense, enduring company, fed in part by its own remains, whose many small changes give the settlement its shape.
