The people of the city learn the plateau first by its water. Children are taught which clear pools have stone beneath them and which conceal soft mud, how to read the thin white lines of submerged ice, and how to give a herd room without scattering it. A basin can change its shape between seasons, so the old routes are remembered alongside the new. People still use the names of pools that have drained away.

Many work at a distance from the city. Surveyors travel in pairs or small groups, staying in low shelters anchored to broad plates that can move with the ground. They visit instruments when the reports become uncertain, clear ice from their housings, and sometimes find a grazer sleeping beside one for the slight warmth it gives off. They return with specimens of mud and water, measurements from fresh seams, and accounts of things the instruments did not record: a bird nesting unusually far upslope, or a herd lingering where there had been no grass.

Within the city, water workers tend the treatment gardens under the walkways. The gardens are beds of reeds, mosses, and fine-rooted plants in shallow channels. They catch sediment and take up dissolved minerals before the water goes into storage. Their keepers know the beds by smell and color as much as by the readings displayed on the channel walls. In cold weather, steam rises through the grates around their boots.

The city’s open spaces are used lightly. Gardens have low, rounded edges rather than high walls, and young shrubs are protected by loose stone collars that can be moved when a passage opens. In the northern districts, people sometimes wait at their doors while grazers file past. The animals are small enough that a large one barely reaches an adult’s waist, but a herd can take a long time to cross. Neighbors stand together on the warm walks, watching the pale backs pass between the buildings.

At close range the grazers look less delicate than they do from a terrace. Their coats are dense and slightly oily, divided along the back so rain runs off in narrow tracks. Their long legs end in splayed hooves that grip the smooth rock and spread their weight over wet ground. They feed almost continuously when forage is good, cropping sedge and the tender centers of moss cushions. A herd moves in a loose pattern: animals drift toward a promising hollow, spread out to feed, then gather again as the patch is exhausted. The youngest stay near several adults, not always the same ones.

The burrowers are among the few animals that make the ground visibly busy. They have blunt noses and short forelimbs, and carry mouthfuls of dry grass into chambers beneath moss and fractured stone. On a mild afternoon they may appear everywhere in a colony at once, pausing upright beside their entrances before darting out to feed. Their old tunnels admit water during heavy rain; afterward, dark birds walk over the cushions and pull exposed insects from the softened soil.

Those birds are patient in the shallows. Some have narrow bills for picking small animals from cracks; others turn pebbles and probe the cloudy water stirred by hooves. They arrive quickly at newly filled basins, although it is difficult to tell where they have come from. Their nests are shallow cups tucked against stones, lined with grass and scraps of moss. When a grazer approaches too closely, a nesting bird slips away on foot and watches from several paces off.

The heavy-bodied hunters are seldom seen near the city. Their dark coats hold little snow, and their broad feet let them follow a herd across stone, mud, and thin ice. They travel alone or in small groups, resting for long periods in the folds of the ground. People monitoring migrations sometimes recognize an individual from a pale scar or a damaged ear and follow its progress across successive basins.

Evening is a quiet time in the city. Some people eat beside windows facing the plateau; others walk the raised paths after the rain, when the lights come on beneath their feet. Beyond the last buildings, grazing continues in the dark. The city’s listeners count the herds where they can, but much of the plateau remains beyond their reach, occupied by animals moving from one wet hollow to the next.
