The people of the city tend to know the weight of things. A rail can be lifted by six workers if the ground beneath them is firm; a workshop can be moved by one driver, provided someone has checked the joints in its cable and the path is clear of roof birds. Children learn early to notice a change in the spacing between paving blocks. They put chalk marks across the seams near their homes and compare them on the way to school.

Most households have someone who works with plants, even if that is not their trade. They keep cuttings in jars beneath lamps, shake condensation from window frames into covered pots, and know which vines should be pulled away from a vent before they obstruct it. Some leaves are grown to eat, some for fiber, some because they root readily in the dust and hold it in place. The glasshouse workers exchange varieties among themselves, carrying shoots wrapped in damp cloth through the cold streets.

The workers who travel with buildings have a less settled idea of an address than most. They know their destinations by the people and machines inside them: the place with the green lathe, the repair shop where a woman keeps beetles in a tin, the canteen whose chimney leans a little north. Before a move, someone walks through each room fastening cupboards and lifting pots from shelves. Someone else goes ahead with a pole to raise the trailing cables over obstructions. It is slow work, and there is usually time for a person inside to finish a cup of tea while the view at the window changes.

At the edge of the city, surveyors work in pairs. One watches the other’s markers as well as their own, because it helps to have a witness when a numbered stake comes back in the wrong place. They keep careful records but leave room in their notebooks for things they cannot yet measure: the shape of a shallow fold, a bowl found empty, a patch that stayed soft after a bright day. The bowls are cleaned and filled with the same attention given to instruments. Some crews consider them a courtesy; others call them equipment. Both remember to bring them.

The pale mice are at home in the cable troughs, where warmth persists after a machine has stopped. They make nests from plant fiber teased out of damaged insulation and carry seeds from the roof gardens into places the gardeners would not think to look. Electricians occasionally open a trough to find a small patch of moss growing around a nest, or a litter of mice pressed together behind a warm junction. They move the animals before making repairs, though the mice often return.

The roof birds follow the workshops because a moving building disturbs insects and exposes seams that have been covered for months. They step delicately along the platforms, lifting their long feet over cables, and wait near workers who are prying up old panels. Several may gather around a single opened joint. They are accustomed to people but quick to leave a hand’s reach, rising only as far as the next roof before settling again.

Foxes come farther into the city during cold stretches, taking mice near the vents and sleeping under the edges of stationary platforms. Their tracks appear in the thin dust on walkways before the first shift. In the outer districts, residents sometimes leave a gate unlatched so a fox can pass through a yard without tearing the vines. By morning it may have gone several streets farther in, leaving a few white hairs on a hinge.

The moths spend most of their lives out of sight. In daylight they are folded into cracks, under leaves, or behind the warm housings of lamps. When a vent opens, they emerge into the rising air and gather around the light; when it closes, they settle wherever the warmth lasts longest. Workers learn to pause before opening certain doors at night, giving the moths time to lift from the threshold.

Something at the advancing edge may also be among the city’s inhabitants. No one has seen it arrive or found a body in the new ground. Its apparent preferences are known chiefly through what happens after people leave: a stake laid with its number showing, water missing from a bowl, a machine’s wheels finding purchase where the surface had been soft. Surveyors argue over whether these events belong together. Meanwhile they set their markers, leave water, and return in the morning to see what has held.
