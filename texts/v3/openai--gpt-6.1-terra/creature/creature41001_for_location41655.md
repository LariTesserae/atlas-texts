The pale cushions are not quite as flat as they look. Between their tightly pressed leaves are chambers full of damp air, and in those chambers live creatures fine enough to pass through the seam of a fingernail. They have jointed white bodies and dark mouths. They feed on the undersides of the leaves, moving so slowly that the damage is visible before they are.

When a cushion is lifted, the creatures draw together into its wet center. They do not scatter. Away from the rock they become transparent, and the black tips of their mouths are all that can be seen. Put the cushion down again, even on a different plate of stone, and they begin to separate.

The closed buds are their other shelter. A bud does not open, but its scales fit loosely enough for the smallest bodies to enter. Some buds contain generations: the living packed around the thin husks of the dead, with pale eggs held between the scales. Others contain only frost, though the stems that bear them show no sign of having died.

Something larger comes to the plants. Its bones lie on the dry ground inside one of the enclosures: little ribs, narrow jaws, feet with long curved toes. They are not all old. Among them are bones still joined by a strip of gray tissue, and scraps of a hide so fine it looks like dust until it is touched.

The living animals are difficult to follow. They move over a short stretch of rock on four low legs, stopping often to press their mouths against a seam. Then, without any visible hurry, one reaches the edge of a plate and is gone. It may be found later inside a wall, looking out between two stones, or beneath a cushion it could not apparently have reached.

Their mouths open and close against the wet rock. Whether they drink, or feed on something in the water, is uncertain. The higher note continues while they are there. If they hear it, they give no sign.

The enclosures offer them dry ground, though little else that can be recognized as shelter. Several animals may occupy the same one, lying separately among the bones. They do not appear to have built the walls; their feet are too small to shift the stones. Yet they carry smaller fragments in their mouths and leave them beside the older piles. Some fragments bear the marks of teeth. Others are bits of bone.

The white animal on the ledge is larger than anything found among those remains. Seen from the valley floor, it has the proportions of a hare, though its ears seem too broad where they lie along its back. It has never been seen approaching a plant or lowering its head to drink. Once, after a long watch, its flank was noticed to have a hollow that had not been there before. There was no telling whether it had drawn a breath or whether the light on the fur had altered.

From certain places the animal is visible on two ledges at once. Both ledges have a notch beneath them and a dark stone resting above. The animals, if there are two, face inward, toward the valley. Neither turns its head when one is watched.

The little bone-collectors emerge most often beneath those ledges. They keep close to the cracks and pause where the moisture gathers. One can sometimes be seen carrying a pale object, working its way over a plate toward an enclosure. It advances a few body lengths at a time. At each pause its sides move quickly, and its dark eyes remain open.

If the observer looks away, the animal may have reached the wall. Or it may still be on the plate, holding the same pale object, with the wall no nearer than before. In either case it puts the object down eventually and rests beside it, while the roar goes on around the stones.
