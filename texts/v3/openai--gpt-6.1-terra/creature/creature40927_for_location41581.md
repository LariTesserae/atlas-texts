The ring-makers spend most of their lives below the surface. They come up through cracks too narrow to look useful, pressing themselves between the warm slabs until their backs are level with the ground. At rest they resemble fragments of the plateau: dark, blunt-edged, dusted with mineral powder. A ridge that seemed continuous may have a small gap in it the next time the stars are visible.

An adult is roughly the length of a person’s arm, though it can spread itself much wider. Its upper surface is divided into overlapping plates, glossy where they have rubbed together and dull elsewhere. Beneath them is a softer, pale body that must be kept warm. It has no eyes that would be recognizable as eyes. Along its underside run fine branching channels, sensitive to heat and pressure; with these it feels the difference between a steady seep and a fresh fissure, between a stone settling and another ring-maker approaching.

It moves slowly when the ground is still, drawing one edge forward and letting the rest follow. During a tremor it can travel farther, using the brief separations between slabs. Often it disappears as soon as the movement stops. This makes it difficult to tell how many live beneath a given stretch of plateau. A single animal may visit several rings, and a ring may stand for a long time after its maker has gone.

The stones are gathered from nearby breaks. A ring-maker tests each one against its underside before lifting or rolling it: some hold warmth, some lose it quickly, and some become dangerously hot without warning. The largest go on the side facing a fissure. Smaller stones are fitted around them, leaving a gap through which the animal can enter. Inside, the temperature changes more slowly than it does on the bare ground. Dust collects there too, and in the dust are minute mineral grains and the remains of pale crusts, which the ring-maker draws into a shallow mouth beneath its plates.

It also tends the crusts. It carries flakes of them to sheltered stone, presses them down, and sometimes returns to shift a pebble that has fallen across a colony. The crusts grow slowly in the steady warmth and are easily lost. Whether they benefit from the animal’s visits or merely survive them is uncertain; the ring-maker leaves portions uneaten, but it may simply be unable to reach them.

When two meet at a ring, they usually remain motionless for a long time. Then one may withdraw, or both may begin turning the stones. Occasionally they settle side by side within the enclosure, their plates making a dry clicking sound as they touch. Later, one of them leaves behind several soft, translucent bodies no larger than the sorted pebbles. These are pushed into a crack under the ring, where they darken and harden over many weeks.

A growing one is most often lost when its shelter breaks apart. The adults sometimes retrieve the young from a fresh fracture, feeling along the exposed edges and carrying them beneath their bodies to another warm place. They do not always find them. After a large heave, an animal may return repeatedly to a scattered ring, moving stones that no longer fit together.

On the quiet reaches, the heaps beside the rings are its stores: stones selected for a repair that may be needed suddenly. If a low thud passes through the plateau and the ground begins its long trembling, the ring-maker withdraws beneath the nearest slab. When the motion ends, it comes out again, feels for the familiar warmth, and starts to put the stones back.
