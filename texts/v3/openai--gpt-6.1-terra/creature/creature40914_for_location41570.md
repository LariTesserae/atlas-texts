The gray animals are called plates only because, when they are still, the name seems sufficient. Each one has a soft underside held close to the stone and a back of six or seven overlapping scales. The scales are not attached in a single row. They can slide past one another, so that the animal narrows itself to enter a crack, then spreads again inside. A full-grown one is about as broad as a hand. The young are scarcely distinguishable from flakes of the wall.

They graze with a translucent edge beneath the foremost plate. As it passes over a seam, it lifts the dark film in narrow strips and leaves the mineral pale behind it. The animal stops often, pressing its whole underside down, then moves on in a slightly different direction. It appears to find the richest film by touch: it has no visible eyes, and individuals feeding in a shadow do not turn when the light shifts.

The film is the city’s most abundant living thing. In sheltered seams it makes a coating finer than dust, dark enough to show a fingerprint or the passage of a plate. It grows slowly into the mineral, following fractures too small to see. Where a wall splits, the freshly exposed faces are pale; after a long still interval, a faint darkening begins along their edges. Grazed patches darken from their margins in the same way. In some chambers the film climbs high above the floor, beyond the reach of any animal that has been seen, and there it becomes thick enough to peel and curl.

Plates shelter in groups within the walls. A narrow opening may lead to a pocket holding dozens, their bodies fitted together with little space between them. They do not seem to compete for these refuges. When the ground begins to change, the animals nearest an entrance draw back and make room for others returning from the streets. Sometimes a late arrival remains outside, pressed flat against the wall until the dust has settled. If the wall has moved too far, it goes searching along the new seam.

The young spend their earliest lives inside these pockets. An adult leaves a small cluster of pale disks against the stone, held in place by a clear substance that hardens at the edges. The disks take on the wall’s color over time. When the young emerge, they remain close together, feeding on the film that grows between the adults’ resting places. A group may use the same pocket through many shifts of the ground. Eventually its entrance is pinched shut or pulled wide open, and the animals disperse into neighboring cracks.

Their passages through the city are easier to see than the animals themselves. A track starts at the base of one wall and ends a short distance away at another; elsewhere, a matching track may begin at a doorway across an enormous square. There is not always an animal at either end. When one does appear, it may pause at the threshold, its plates slightly raised, before venturing onto the open stone. Plates seem willing to enter some seams and unwilling to enter others that look much the same. Those that emerge from distant doorways are not visibly harmed, though they often remain still for a while before feeding.

Other inhabitants are less readily separated from the walls. In old chambers, a fine branching crust grows beneath the folded edges, cream-colored at first and darkening as it ages. Its branches are hollow. Loose powder catches in them, and plates sometimes climb among them to graze. Where the crust has been disturbed, its broken tips slowly close. Whether it draws anything from the passing grit or simply uses it as shelter is difficult to tell.

The largest concentrations of life are hidden. Beneath an empty street, a fused passage may still contain a thin sheet of film and a population of plates moving through spaces no wider than their bodies. As the passage narrows, they follow the remaining cracks upward and outward. Some reach a newly opened chamber; others remain in the old wall, grazing where the stone has folded darkened surface against darkened surface.

At the outer district, plates are scarce. The grit scours the open floors, and the seams facing the drop hold little film. A few animals live on the inner sides of the chambers, venturing out only when the air is still. From those doorways they can reach the smooth floors, but their tracks rarely extend far across them. The distant streets remain visible beyond the edge, and there may be grazing animals there as well: from time to time, a patch of dark wall appears pale when it returns to view.
