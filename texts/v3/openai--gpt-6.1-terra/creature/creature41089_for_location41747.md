The people of the basin are good at noticing when someone is trying to be seen. A raised hand at the far end of a yard, a figure waiting beside a marked door, a cloth laid over a railing: each may be a request for attention. People arrange themselves where they can be found. They leave a clear space around workshop entrances and keep the windows facing the main paths clean.

Most residents know some of the common signs used across the works. There are signs for a vehicle approaching, a line under pressure, a gate being opened, and a person needed at once. Crews have their own additions, developed for jobs in which a wrong guess can injure someone. At the end of a shift, two workers may stand face to face and sign with the ease of long practice, then step into a sheltered room and continue aloud.

Children acquire these habits before they can read the painted notices. They learn to look both ways at a rail crossing and to make sure a driver has seen them. They also learn games from the pumps: where to stand for a clean pulse through the paving, how to count the strokes of a particular engine, how long it takes a friend to run between two junctions. Children from different blocks argue about which junction gives the clearest reading. Their parents sometimes use the same places to check that the engines are running as expected.

The mechanics who tend the buried mains develop a more exact touch. Some carry a short metal rod to place against a pipe or the ground; others prefer a bare hand. They know that a loose fitting can send its knock a considerable distance and that water, stone and an empty conduit each carry a different version of it. When they find a fault, they mark the paving for the excavation crew, then return later to see whether the repair has quieted that particular part of the line.

The city’s older residents remember yards busier than they are now. Some live in houses built for large households and use only the shaded lower rooms. They keep chairs by thick walls where visitors can sit close enough for conversation. In the hotter months, relatives bring food or help with repairs early in the day; a visit often lasts through the afternoon, when the exposed paths are brightest. People who have moved away return by the incline, bringing news that is told several times over in the old rooms.

Shift workers have other hours. They eat from covered tins in the lee of the factories, wash mineral dust from their arms at outdoor taps and leave boots to dry on racks above the damp floors. Some grow herbs and greens in boxes against the backs of buildings, where an overhanging pipe or a patched roof breaks the sun. A few keep stunted trees alive in residential courtyards, carrying water to them even in weeks when water beads on every cool surface. The roots need fresher water than many of the drains can provide.

The fish in the deeper channels are plain, tough-bodied creatures, pale along the belly and darker on top. They gather where a little shade crosses the water or where the current brings food toward a gate. Workers clearing the screens have learned to lift slowly and give trapped fish time to turn back. Someone new to the job may find a small crowd looking down beside them, ready to point out a flicker in the brown water.

Birds come and go with the weather. After rain they probe the seams of the highest roofs and drink from shallow hollows before the sun reaches them. Moths spend the day pressed into cracks or beneath ledges; at night they appear around lamps over the workshop doors. By morning, a few have settled on the painted symbols, their pale wings almost indistinguishable from old flakes of paint.

People here differ in how much of the city they want around them. Some like the open yards and the long sightlines; others seek the enclosed rooms of the old quarter whenever their work allows. A few sleep through the pumps without waking. Others fit shutters more tightly, move their beds away from trembling walls, or spend their days off walking the paths above the basin, where the air is clearer and the pulse underfoot is slow enough to count.
