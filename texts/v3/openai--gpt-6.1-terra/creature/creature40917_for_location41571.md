The smallest animals are called glassbacks, though their shells are not glass and are not quite transparent. Each shell has six overlapping plates, faintly clouded at the seams. Through them one can see a dark thread of gut and, on either side, little chambers that open and close as the animal works. Their legs are thin enough to vanish against the stone until they move. Packed beneath a ledge, hundreds appear to share a single surface of trembling hairs.

A glassback spends much of its life upside down. It scrapes powder from the ceiling with a mouth like a pair of hinged spoons, then passes the coarser grains backward along its body. A neighbor may take these grains from its feet and eat them again. The animals do not defend their places, but neither do they readily leave them. When one lets go, the others feel the gap through the stone. A glassback at the edge of the colony turns and walks into it, and the small vacancy travels outward until it reaches a place where a new animal has already unfolded from its shell.

Their cast shells are delicate while fresh. Later they harden to a chalky crispness, and the mat on the wall grows over them. The mat is made of many separate bodies, each shaped rather like a flattened cup, with its rim pressed against the rims of its neighbors. It swells slowly in places and sinks in others. Powder and shell fragments disappear beneath its surface; fine, bright granules emerge at its margins, where glassbacks collect them. The mat has no visible mouths. When a larger animal crosses it, the cups draw close around its feet and let them go one at a time.

The upright creatures are more difficult to approach. They are longer than a hand, with low, jointed bodies that can fold almost flat, although they spend their quiet hours raised in the cracks. Their faces are pale and finely ridged. They have no obvious eyes on the front; a pair of glossy patches farther back may serve that purpose, or may sense the warmth of the stone. Their attention seems to be directed upward regardless. Even an animal tucked beneath an overhang holds its face toward the dark surface above it.

The whine comes from a row of slits along the back. Thin plates lie beneath the slits, and the animal draws them against one another with muscles that never appear to tire. Each animal has its own pitch, fixed by the size and shape of its plates. Younger ones begin with a faint, unsteady sound, then settle into a note as their backs harden. If an adult falls silent, a younger animal elsewhere may lengthen or shorten its plates over the next few hours until it takes up the abandoned pitch.

The animals nearest the turning wheel also click. They do this with small projections at the ends of their legs, placing them in the path of the teeth and withdrawing them before they can be caught. They seem neither injured nor alarmed by the motion. A few climb onto the wheel itself and ride it around, holding their backs clear of the belt. At the bottom they step off into the powder; at the top they step onto the frame. The marked tooth passes them again and again, and they make no special response to it.

The pale stalks have no flowers. Their metallic leaves grow in pairs from each joint, one leaf broad and one narrow, with a raised seam running down the middle. The smallest grazers gather along that seam and file away a little of the surface. A leaf thins where they feed, then thickens again from beneath. The grazers seldom move to the tender tip, although it would be easier to bite; there they are exposed, and the upright animals occasionally take them.

Around each stalk the exposed roots make a dense circle of gripping fingers. Some have grown into cracks so narrow that only a silver line remains visible. Others lift clear of the rock before curving down to grasp it again. Young glassbacks shelter under these arches. The stalk benefits from their presence: the grains they carry and discard collect among the roots, where fine filaments draw out what the plant can use.

There are other inhabitants seen less often. Small white animals live inside the open pipe mouths, facing inward. They come to the rims when the machinery warms and retreat when a shadow crosses the bulbs. In the deepest seams, narrow-bodied scavengers move between the ridges without touching the powder, bracing themselves against both walls. Sometimes one emerges carrying the plate of a dead upright animal. It may drag the plate a long way before settling to rasp it down, leaving the smaller pieces for the glassbacks.

Death is not uncommon here, but little of an animal remains where it fell. Legs separate at their joints; shells are carried or eaten; the body beneath them is taken by the mat or by scavengers from the seams. A dead upright animal’s note stops at once. For a time its absence can be felt in the chord, especially close to the place where it stood. Then another body begins to sound, perhaps on the far side of the chamber, and the note fills the space again.

None of these inhabitants knows the whole chamber as a visitor would. A glassback knows a stretch of underside, the path to a stalk, the pressure of its neighbors. An upright animal knows the heat of its crack and the vibration arriving through the stone. The cups of the mat touch only the bodies immediately around them, yet a disturbance at one edge passes slowly across the colony.

Together they occupy almost every available surface. They leave the sharp ridges sharp, the stalks standing, the machinery turning. Beneath their constant noise are countless smaller contacts: a foot finding a hold, a shell settling over a soft new body, a root tightening in a seam.
