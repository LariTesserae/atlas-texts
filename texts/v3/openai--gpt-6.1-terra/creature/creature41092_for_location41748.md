The thing that lives in the hollow is easiest to see when the thumping stops.

Along the underside of the lowest stone, a fringe comes loose and drifts in the water. At first it looks like moss pulled free by the current, but the strands are nearly colorless, and each is tipped with a dark, moving point. They reach into the quiet water, bend toward one another, then withdraw as the surge begins again.

Most of it lives deeper in the crack, where the stones press together. There it holds on with a soft, many-branched foot, spread so thinly over the rock that it follows every ridge. The exposed threads gather what the water brings: grains of silt, fragments of wing, the bodies of the black flies that fall from the warmer stones. A fly caught against the fringe is drawn inward little by little, while the threads around it continue to open and close.

The pulses do not belong to the creature. They buffet it, flattening its fringe against the stone and then allowing it to lift again. It has grown in the intervals between them. Where the current is strongest, its body is short and tough; farther back, in sheltered seams, it spreads into pale folds as delicate as wet paper.

When the basin rises after rain, it extends itself over the stone’s newly drowned edge. By morning, as the water drains, those outer portions curl back beneath it. Sometimes a thread is left in the open and freezes there. In a thaw it may stir again; if it does not, beetles find it.

It is not alone. Tiny clear larvae graze on its surface, and something smaller still lives among the folds of its foot, leaving winding trails that close behind it. A frog resting on the stone will occasionally snatch at an exposed thread, though the creature retreats so slowly that the frog seems to lose interest.

New colonies have begun along the fresh breaks in the rock. They are no bigger than fingernails, each one a translucent patch with a few dark-tipped strands. In the long pauses between the thumps, all their fringes rise into the water together.
