The most numerous inhabitants are rooted so closely together that it is difficult to say where one ends and another begins. The broad leaves along the walls belong to several kinds of plant. Some draw their nourishment from the warm water; others send pale threads into the sediment caught behind the stones. Their roots cross and press together without joining, making a mesh that holds fallen leaves, empty shells, and the finer things carried through the pipes.

The copper-colored mat in the basin is a colony of small, soft-bodied animals. Each has a round opening edged with short tentacles. When warm water rises through the paving, they spread themselves to feed; as it cools, they draw inward. The movement passes slowly across the basin, though not as a single wave. A patch over a buried pipe may remain open while the animals beside it have already closed.

They grow by dividing. For a time, a new animal shares a narrow bridge of tissue with the one it came from, and food passes between them. The bridge eventually thins and parts. Even then the two may remain pressed side by side for years, their edges bent into matching curves.

The white fish lives chiefly beneath the pump outlet. It has little color because there is little light to show it by, and its eyes are large but not especially sharp. It finds its way through the streets by the movement of water against a fine line of pores along each side. When the pump sends out a pulse, the fish turns slightly and holds still until the current settles.

It feeds on tiny drifting animals and on the pale scraps that come loose from the root beds. Occasionally it enters the workshop and rests among the hanging tool heads, where the current is weak. It has worn a small clear patch on the rack by returning to the same place. There may be another fish beyond the bank, but none has entered the settlement for a long time.

The shelled animal on the valve wheel is older than it appears. Its shell has grown around one spoke, leaving the wheel fixed in place. Its feeding threads spread over the iron in a delicate fan. They close on particles too small to see, then pass them inward toward a mouth hidden beneath the shell. A few threads have found their way into a crack in the valve housing. When the warm water changes pressure, they withdraw before anything emerges.

Inside the pipes, where the mineral paste moves most slowly, live thin colonies that cannot bear much light. They form films on the inner walls and leave minute ridges in the silt. Some of the warm-water pipes carry their offspring out into the streets, where most settle and die. A few lodge beneath overlapping leaves or in the dark seams of the stone. They grow there until a change in the current carries them away again.

There are places where the vegetation seems arranged. Fronds hang on either side of certain passages rather than across them, and the root beds near the pump have open channels through which water can pass. The openings change gradually. A blocked channel fills with sediment; roots near it loosen and decay; water finds another way through. No single plant makes the adjustment. The settlement’s inhabitants grow into the conditions the pump provides, and in growing they alter those conditions for whatever comes next.

When a leaf loosens and comes to rest in the roots, small animals begin feeding on its surface. Its clear tissue clouds, then breaks into strands. The strands catch silt. A new shoot takes hold in the little mound, and its roots spread through what remains of the leaf. For a while the old veins are still visible beneath the new growth.
