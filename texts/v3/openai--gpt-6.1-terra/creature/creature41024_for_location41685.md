The smallest residents are easily mistaken for movement in the water itself. Pale mites pick their way around the pool rims, stopping where a droplet has made a fresh dark patch in the moss. Springtails gather there too, minute enough to stand on the skin of the water. When disturbed, they flick themselves away and vanish among the green threads.

Underwater, the beetles carry their air with them. A silver bubble clings beneath each dark body as it works among the submerged roots. Now and then one rises to the surface, touches it briefly, and goes down again. The pools are shallow, but their bottoms are complicated: fine roots, leaf fragments, pockets of silt, and narrow spaces where larvae can lie out of sight.

The frogs have their separate stations. One sits on a raised fold of moss, another beside a stalk, another near the entrance where the mist passes. Their colors are close to the surfaces they occupy, though their eyes catch the daylight. Each keeps watch over a small patch. A throat swells and relaxes; a head turns toward a click. When an insect comes within reach, the frog is suddenly elsewhere on its ledge, and then still again.

Most of the singers remain hidden. They live in the walls and in the loose weave beneath the floor, where the roots leave passages just wide enough for them. Their calls are not quite alike: some are dry and rapid, some have a ticking edge, and some carry a thin rising note. At close range an individual can be heard starting and stopping. From the entrance they make one continuous rasp. Eggs and shed skins lie deep in the damp vegetation, alongside the remains of insects eaten by frogs, beetles, and other insects.

The green chamber lives as its animals do: by taking in what arrives and giving something up. Moss grows over a dead leaf until only its veins show. Fern roots follow water into a fold; older roots soften and open gaps for small bodies to pass through. The warm pools shelter young creatures through nights when the air outside is cooler, though the warmth can change with the flow from farther out. A leaf that has rested flat for weeks eventually yellows at its edge and sinks toward the floor.

Beyond the entrance, the pumps go on. Here their thud reaches the frogs through the ground and makes a fine tremor in the hanging drops. The insects keep calling. Water falls, and a beetle turns beneath the place where it lands.
