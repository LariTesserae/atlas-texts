The wall-makers live packed together, each in a chalky chamber fixed to the chambers around it. Their soft bodies rarely come entirely out. Two fringed openings extend into the current: through one they draw water, and through the other they send it back, having taken the fine drifting food from it. When a shadow passes close, the fringes vanish almost at once. A colony that had seemed covered in tiny moving flowers becomes a wall of closed holes.

Young wall-makers drift for a time before settling. Many are swept away over the lip of the plateau or buried in dust. Those that reach an occupied wall attach themselves in the shelter of its uneven surface. They grow, lay down shell, and narrow the gaps between older chambers. In places where the current is strong, the living edge of a wall points downstream, while its upstream side is worn smooth and exposed.

The abandoned walls shelter other animals. A small pale fish may keep to one opening for months, backing deep inside when larger fish pass. Its head faces outward, and its fins work constantly to hold it in place. It darts out for drifting animals and returns to the same hole, though sometimes another fish has entered it in the meantime. Then there is a brief struggle in the dust.

The translucent grazers move over the open shell, so slowly that their progress is easiest to see by the clean tracks behind them. Their undersides grip the surface; their mouths work along it, scraping up the film that grows even in this faint light, together with particles caught in its slime. Where a basin collects dust, some leave the hard ground and plough shallow furrows through the deposit. Others stay on steep faces, where fresh food is delivered by the flow and the sediment cannot settle deeply enough to cover them.

Small animals live on the grazers, too. Tiny crustaceans pick along the folds of their bodies and take shelter beneath their edges. When two grazers meet, they may remain pressed together for a long while before separating, each leaving by a different path. Later, clear coils of eggs can be found fixed under the lip of a shell or deep in a groove, where the current passes over them without tearing them loose.

Above the flats, the silver schools spend much of their time facing into the water. They snap at specks too small to be seen from the bottom, advancing a little between strokes and then falling back together. Their flanks flash when they turn, though in the dim water the flash is often no more than a sudden change from pale to dark. A school crossing a narrow channel compresses in the faster flow. The fish at its edge turn first; the rest follow, and for an instant the whole school has the shape of a curved blade.

The long predators below watch for the fish that fail to turn. They lie against the shell in hollows where dust settles over their backs, their mouths directed into the current. Some take fish; others seize grazers or the small creatures that come out of the walls after a disturbance. A predator can remain almost motionless through the passing of several schools, moving only its gills and the tips of its fins. When it does strike, the settling dust may be visible longer than the chase.

On the oldest open surfaces, where little loose material remains, life is sparse but not absent. Fine threads and low patches cling to the shell, trapping particles. Minute scavengers pick through the trapped material. Drillers work into dead chambers, making narrow tunnels that will weaken the shell long after they have gone. When a piece eventually breaks free, it may roll across the floor, strike another fragment, and come to rest against a living wall. There it collects dust and gives a young wall-maker somewhere to settle.

The plateau’s inhabitants do not use all of it in the same way. The schools travel over great stretches of ground; a grazer may spend its life on a single slope; a wall-maker remains where it first attached. Around them, chambers fill, surfaces wear down, and new openings appear. In a quiet basin, a fine layer of dust can settle over everything overnight. When the current turns back into it, the grazers emerge first as clear shapes moving beneath the lifting cloud, and the fringes of the colony begin to open again.
