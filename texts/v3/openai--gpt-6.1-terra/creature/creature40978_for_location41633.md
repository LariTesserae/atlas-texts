The most numerous inhabitants are scarcely distinguishable from the mineral growths among which they live. Some of the pale films are sheets of tiny animals, each fixed beside its neighbors and extending a fringe into the passing water. They draw in suspended grains and smaller living things. When a fresh trickle reaches them, the whole sheet contracts unevenly, as if the stone were drawing back from the cold.

Other films have no life in them at all. The swimmers scrape both kinds. They are clear-jointed creatures, scarcely longer than a grain of rice, with working mouthparts and dark specks inside their bodies where their last meal is being digested. In the shallowest pools they can be seen moving in short, industrious bursts, stopping to brace themselves against a knob of mineral. A swimmer that loses its grip may tumble a considerable distance before finding another surface.

The dark animals under the shelves catch them. They are flat enough to occupy the narrow space between stone and water, and their edges adhere so closely to the rock that a passing swimmer may cross one without appearing to notice. When prey comes within reach, part of the dark body lifts and folds over it. Afterwards the hunter settles again, often with several swimmers feeding undisturbed only a little way from its edge.

The little shells that close at a shadow are anchored in clusters along the quieter pool margins. Open, they show delicate tissues that wave in the current; closed, they resemble the mineral beads around them. Some hold grit between their valves and cannot shut completely. Swimmers pick at those gaps, though whether they are feeding on the shell animals or on material caught there is difficult to see.

On the exposed shelves, the pale many-legged creatures follow the wettest routes. Their feet leave temporary clear tracks through the films. They pause at dry-looking ridges until a bead of condensation swells enough to bridge the surface, then cross beneath it, their bodies flattening further as they move. Young ones shelter in the same narrow seams as the adults. At times several gather under a single overhang, layered together in a thin pocket of water.

The broken pipe has made a separate little community. Its regular spray keeps one face of the iron frame wet, and a dense coating has formed there: crust at the top, living film farther down, and shell animals clustered around the places where droplets run together. The turning shaft shakes loose flakes of rust, which settle among the mineral grains below. Swimmers gather in the trough pool, where the spray and the slow flow bring them food; dark hunters occupy the underside of the collapsed wall.

The light at dusk belongs chiefly to soft-bodied animals deep in the crevices. They are difficult to see until disturbed, when brief points of light appear within their bodies. One animal may flash as a swimmer brushes past, and its movement sends a ripple into the next crevice. Then another brightens, and another, until a faint line of gleams has traveled along the shelf.

For a moment the light shows more animals than the surface suggests: transparent bodies crowded into gaps, shells tucked beneath shells, a hunter folded around its catch. Then the gleams fade, leaving the pools dark again while the movement underneath continues.
