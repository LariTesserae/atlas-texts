Something lives beneath the three stones, though it is difficult at first to separate it from the cup.

When the current drives inward, the cup fills with pale filaments. They lift and stream over its rim, so fine that the water seems to pass through them without resistance. When the current turns, they gather again into a small, shivering knot. One end of the knot remains fixed to the bottom of the cup. The other opens into a fringe of little flattened surfaces, each turned a slightly different way.

The fringe is always moving. It bends with the water, but not only with the water: certain edges quiver faster than the surge can account for, answering the high ringing in the hollow. At those moments, a seam of new stone begins at one of the edges and runs upward through the water. The creature releases it as it lengthens. Most are torn away at once. A few reach the ceiling.

The canopy is its work, although it does not build in the way the stones were balanced. It makes a line, loses it, makes another. Where a line holds, the creature sometimes climbs it, drawing itself out of the cup in a slow succession of grips. Up close its body is not one thread but many, loosely bound, with clear water showing between them. It can spread almost invisibly along a strand, or contract into something no larger than a fingertip.

It feeds there. Tiny particles carried in by the surge lodge among its filaments; a silver animal may pause at the canopy and pick at them too. The two sometimes remain together through several reversals, the animal holding position while the creature’s fringe brushes its fins. Then the water changes and both are gone—the animal into its crack, the creature drawn down into the cup.

The three upright stones give the cup a little protection from the strongest sweep along the floor. The creature does not seem troubled by their trembling. At times it reaches out and touches one, laying several filaments against the pale surface. New folds rise around their tips. One stone has a narrow ridge grown across its base, and another is beginning to acquire one; perhaps, in time, the shelter will become part of the hollow itself.

There may be others. Some of the ridges on the floor are hollow, and in one or two, at the height of the ringing, a dark thread can be seen retreating. A broken seam caught against the wall may continue to thicken for a while after the current has pulled it loose. Whether something remains alive inside it is hard to tell.

The one in the cup persists. In the twisting light it is sometimes silver, sometimes the color of the new stone. Between surges it rises just above the rim, its fringe spread toward the roof, and the finest lines begin again.
