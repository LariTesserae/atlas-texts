The first animals to reach a new pool are the long-legged grazers called seam walkers. Their feet have four narrow toes, each tipped with a pad that can spread around an edge or grip a blade of stone. A herd seems almost weightless from a distance. Close up, the animals are immense, their bellies hanging low between legs jointed like folded stalks.

They drink by lowering flexible mouths to the surface and drawing water up through the folds of their lips. They are reluctant to put a foot into a pool. Beneath the thin water there may be a crack, and beneath the crack a chamber breathing vapor. Young seam walkers must learn this. When one steps in, the adults crowd around it, pressing their plated flanks together until it backs onto dry ground.

The plates grow throughout an animal’s life, laid down in overlapping patches from minerals gathered in its food. They are pale on the shaded belly and nearly black along the spine. Between them, the skin stays soft and warm. Moss takes hold there, then insects, then the fine roots of vines. An old animal may carry a garden heavy enough to sway when it walks. Its companions browse these gardens, carefully stripping off shoots without disturbing the skin beneath. At rest, the animals stand close enough for vines to pass from one back to another.

The small lights seen in their shadows belong mostly to scavengers. They cling to the undersides of the larger animals, where they feed on shed skin, parasites and the soft remains of damaged plants. Each has a translucent body no longer than a finger, with a dark knot of organs visible inside. When the herd moves, the scavengers keep their light covered. At a pool they drop off and spread beneath the animals’ legs, uncovering dim green patches along their sides. They are quick to find anything dead in the water, and quick to find the young of other scavengers.

A pool gathers creatures that do not otherwise meet. Round burrowers push up through the gravel, their backs crusted with stone dust. They drink until their bodies swell and then retreat, sealing the holes behind them with damp pellets. Flyers settle with their wings raised clear of the ground. They scoop up water in the loose skin beneath their throats and carry it to nests high in the curtain trees. The folded predators come too, though they seldom hunt at the water. For a few hours a basin may contain more flesh than the surrounding forest does for miles. By morning there is only a dark stain in the stone, a litter of skins and the tracks.

Some of those tracks lead straight to the pool and do not lead away. The luminous scavengers disappear into the herds’ shadows; the burrowers go down. Others vanish because their owners have found a route no longer visible. The seam walkers sometimes leave a pool by crossing it in single file, each animal stepping into the print ahead. The last one can be seen from a ridge while the first is already lost, though the basin is scarcely wide enough to hold them all.

The folded hunters follow the herds through the mineral foliage. Their ribs can lie nearly flat, and the long skin between their shoulders and hips settles around them like a dark skirt. They wait against ridges that are warm enough to conceal the heat of their bodies. When prey passes close, the hunter rises first at one end and then the other, unfolding much more quickly than its shape suggests.

They usually take old or injured animals, but not always. A hunter may walk openly beside a herd for half a day, ignored by the adults and watched by every youngster. It is waiting for a moment of confusion: a pulse traveling through the ground, a sudden movement in a seam, or the brief rearrangement of a familiar path. The herds have learned to bunch together at such times. Hunters have learned to wait.

Not all of the herds travel. In warm hollows there are squat, wide-backed browsers whose plates grow into thick shields. They remain near the same fissures for years, moving as the heat moves beneath them. They feed on the undersides of leaves, drawing the pale threads into their mouths without tearing away the leathery tops. Their young sleep beneath the adult bodies, pressed against the warm rock. When a fissure cools, the whole group may leave within an hour, including calves that have never walked farther than the edge of the hollow. They follow the adults through the forest until they find another place where the stone feels right.

The forests provide food, shelter and routes. The low, spreading trees send woody limbs over the rock until they find cracks in which to root. An old one may have dozens of trunks, none much larger than a leg, and cover ground that takes a day to cross. Beneath its foliage is a dim, damp space inhabited by pale insects, root-eating grazers and animals too soft-bodied to survive on exposed stone. Some of the low trees shelter several generations of the same burrowers. Their tunnels admit air and carry water to the roots; in return, the roots hold the tunnel walls together when the ground shifts.

The taller trees grow more slowly. Their smooth trunks have few footholds, but the hanging foliage at their crowns forms deep pockets. Broad-winged flyers nest there, suspending their young in woven slings among the leaves. The young are born with their wings folded tight against their bodies. For a long time they can do little except grip and feed. The adults return from the pools with water stored beneath their throats, and from the ridges with mouthfuls of soft insects. When a young flyer first opens its wings, it may hang for days without letting go, testing the faint currents that rise through the forest. The first flight usually begins when the nest tree sways or the ground gives a low shudder.

Far above the herds, mature flyers circle with little movement. Their wings are broad and thin, marked by dark branching veins that stiffen as they warm. They can sense changes in pressure across those veins and gather over places where vapor is about to escape. After a fissure opens, they descend through the haze to feed on creatures driven from the rock. They also carry seeds. Some stick to the edges of their wings; others pass unharmed through their bodies and fall in a scattering wherever the flyers roost.

Along the active seams live the stalk forests. Their bases are crowded by swollen bladders, each one connected to a web of fine roots in the cracks below. The stalks bend as vapor rises, and the bladders fill and empty in slow succession. Metallic dust collects on the leaves and on the backs of the beetle-like gatherers that come afterward.

The gatherers have wide legs fringed with stiff hairs. They comb dust from the bladders, pack it beneath their bodies and carry it into crevices. Their colonies reach deep into the stone, following warmth and moisture. Some chambers contain eggs; others are lined with pellets of the metallic dust. The adults press the pellets into growing threads of crystal. Where the threads join, a faint charge passes through them. The eggs develop most quickly in those chambers, trembling together when a pulse comes through.

Aboveground, the same pulse closes leaves and splits seed cases. It also changes the behavior of animals that cannot be seen from one another. Burrowers stop digging. Flyers tilt into the air. Seam walkers lift their heads and hold them still until the last blue-white light has passed under the forest. The youngest sometimes turn too late and find themselves facing a different direction from the adults. One or two always hurry to correct themselves.

At high elevations, where the cushions cover the ground between sheets of stone, the inhabitants are smaller and often brightly colored inside. The cushion plants have tough outer leaves, but their packed centers are wet and tender. Cushion feeders open them with hooked mouths and eat in short, rapid bouts before the leaves fold shut again. Their own bodies are flattened and plated, with limbs tucked underneath. When a shadow passes, they press themselves into the exposed wet center and are closed safely inside.

Other animals spend their entire lives within a cushion. Tiny thread-legged grazers travel through its damp passages, feeding on damaged tissue and laying eggs in pockets warmed by the stone. Their young emerge nearly transparent. As they grow, they take on the color of whatever part of the plant they inhabit: pale near the roots, green in the outer leaves, occasionally a vivid orange where mineral-rich water has gathered. When a large feeder tears a cushion open, hundreds may spill out. Most crawl back before the wound closes; a few are carried away on the feeder’s mouth and begin new colonies elsewhere.

There are also creatures that seem to arrive with the changing landscape. After a forest has occupied several positions at once and settled into one, its animals may include a kind no herd has met before. Some resemble familiar grazers but have too many joints, or mineral plates arranged in a pattern that no local young animal grows. Others fit the forest so well that they are difficult to pick out until they move. They feed, drink and avoid the hunters. Within a season their tracks are as common as any others.

The established animals do not always accept them. A herd may crowd around an unfamiliar grazer, smelling it and touching its hide, then move on while it follows at a distance. Sometimes the stranger joins them at the next pool. Sometimes it remains apart for years, traveling a little behind, using their paths but never allowing the young close enough to touch.

When the ground rises, most inhabitants leave before the new escarpment is visible. The beetle colonies move upward through their tunnels. Cushion animals retreat into the plants. Flyers lift from the trees, and the herds file toward cooler stone. The low forests cannot leave, but their many rooted limbs hold the soil and smaller creatures in place. By morning an entire community may be standing higher than it was, looking down from a fresh wall of black rock.

Then animals begin to return. They inspect the exposed face, find new ledges and cracks, and feed on whatever the rising stone has brought within reach. Beetles enter the bright seams. Seeds lodged in moss or carried on wings settle into the sheltered places. Before the sharp edges have cooled, something is already living among them.
