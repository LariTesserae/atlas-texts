The translucent animals are alive, although the difference between their living and their being merely present is difficult to see. Each has six limbs, forked at the tips, and a body made of clear sections that admit the town’s faint light without much altering it. Along the underside runs a fine dark thread. It lies straight in one animal and slightly kinked in the other.

They feed on what reaches them through the stone. The continuous note passes into their limbs, and minute structures within their bodies turn under its pressure. After a long while the dark thread changes shape. A section at the rear clouds over, separates, and is left behind on the wall. The discarded pieces are almost as clear as the animals themselves; there are many of them in the crevices, fitted closely to the rock.

They are not the makers of the town. They came later, though perhaps not much later, and settled on surfaces where the note was strongest. They neither avoid the doorways nor enter them. One may remain on the same patch of wall through the whole of its life, which is long enough for its shed sections to grow into a small, transparent ridge beneath it.

The town’s inhabitants are harder to find. Their bodies are made chiefly of water, kept apart from the ocean by differences of pressure so precise that even here, under the weight of all the sea, they retain their shapes. In the basin, the sphere is one of them, or the part of one that can be seen from the square. The pale lines entering the basin continue through it, drawn close together by the shortened space inside. They end at a tiny structure near its center, visible only from certain angles: a ring of dark points, each point holding its position against the others.

There are more in the rooms. Some fill the larger interiors without appearing to touch their walls. Some lie folded into the thickness of a wall, occupying chambers whose entrances have no width that can be measured from the street. The bubbles of empty space are places they have made around themselves. Inside each bubble, a few threads of water cross without falling, held at their ends by the clear boundary.

The inhabitants have no faces. Their boundaries are their most sensitive parts. They know an approaching animal by the change its body makes in the water, and know one another through the pale lines, which carry small alterations from building to building. The low note in the stone is not a voice, exactly. It continues when no message is passing, and changes when several inhabitants attend to it at once.

They built close together because distance is troublesome here, even when it is arranged. The courts give them room to meet; the walls that enclose nothing visible mark places where space has been drawn out thin. There is one court in which the far side can be reached only by crossing it twice. Another has been left open to the cliff, and the water there has the same distinctness as the sphere, though it is spread so broadly that its edge can be seen only where it crosses the lines in the stone.

What passes among them does not require haste. One inhabitant may hold a change at its boundary for years while another takes it in. An answer can be prepared in a room, carried along a pale line, and kept in the basin until it is wanted. The sphere has grown more intricate in this way: not larger, but more closely divided within itself. What appears from outside to be perfect stillness includes a great deal that has not yet finished happening.

The clear animals sometimes settle over the pale lines. Their limbs interrupt the note so slightly that the inhabitants do not dislodge them. A discarded section remains there after its animal has gone or died, and the pressure of the line passes through it differently. In time, the inhabitants make use of the difference. Several such fragments are set along the rim of the basin, where they catch a little light and hold it at the edge of the water.
