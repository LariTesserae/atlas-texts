The pale drinkers spend most of their lives pressed together inside the stone. Their chambers are narrow and branching, with smooth floors polished by generations of feet. In the deepest rooms, where the heat changes least, they tend their young among fragments of seedpod and the cast-off skins of other animals. They are almost colorless until they drink. Then a dark line travels from the mouth down the length of the body, dividing into finer lines in each jointed leg.

They do not all emerge at once. The first few feel their way along the crack when the rock begins to cool. Others follow the vibration of the falling drop. By the time the basin has gathered enough to reflect a patch of sky, the floor around it is moving. Each drinker takes several rapid mouthfuls, turns, and tries to return by a different path. Those that reach the crack pass among the dry bodies waiting there, touching them briefly as they go.

The bronze hunters know the sound of their departure as well as the sound of the water. They live in shallow pits high on the wall, where their flat bodies fit so closely against the stone that only their eyes catch the light. Each makes a line from a pair of openings beneath its body and pays it out as it descends. The line is sticky near its end and almost impossibly fine above. A hunter can hang motionless on it, legs spread, until a drinker crosses the right patch of floor. Then it drops, folds itself around the smaller animal, and climbs.

The bark-colored creatures among the fern catch them on the way up. Folded, each has the thickness and mottling of a dead leaf stem. Unfolded, it has six long legs, a pair of hard wings, and a soft pale mouth that opens much wider than seems possible. It springs without aiming quite where its prey is; instead it strikes the thread and lets the thread bring the hunter within reach. Sometimes both fall. The impact is one of the sounds that rolls out of the hollow, much louder than such small bodies ought to make.

The fern shelters animals that take no part in the chase. Under its lowest fronds are slow, velvet-backed grazers whose mouths rasp at the dark film on the stone. Their droppings collect around the roots and are broken down by still smaller things. In the dry pods caught there live animals with swollen hind bodies and tiny hooked feet. They carry grains of grit into the pods, arrange them around their eggs, and close the openings with chewed fiber. When a pod splits in the heat, the young spill out and climb at once toward shade.

The red-haired succulent has its own visitors. Minute pale-winged things creep between its leaves at night, drinking from punctures made by earlier mouths. They emerge at midday and circle above the fern, far enough apart that they rarely touch. Each beats its wings at a slightly different rate. As the hollow warms, their flight quickens and their separate notes rise together. Some of them are caught by the long tongue lowered from the rim. The rest scatter, then return to their loose circles once the shadow has moved on.

The flier comes only when the ringing carries far enough. From below, little can be seen of it but its gripping feet, the edge of a narrow chest, and the tongue, which bends and searches through the air. Occasionally it pulls itself higher and a bright eye appears over the rim. It may remain there after feeding, watching the fronds lift and the small wings pass beneath it, until the hot stone becomes too much for its feet.

The animals waiting at the broken groove are unlike those that hurry to the basin. They are squat, dust-colored, and built low to the floor, with mouths that open upward. Before dawn they take positions beneath the gap, sometimes shouldering one another aside. When the silver line carries the water across, they remain almost still. When it breaks, they catch what they can before it touches the rock. A few have pale scars across their backs where drops have fallen on them and hissed away.

They also gather around the three slabs, though they never climb them. They rest in the thin strips of shade between the stones and lick the inner faces where the condensation runs. At times one presses its head against a slab and stays there for an hour or more. The shifting patterns pass over its skin. When it moves away, it is cool enough for dust to cling to the damp print of its body.

Something below the floor tends the old chambers. It can be heard at night, scraping for a while in one place, then clicking from another. After a fierce rain, when grit has washed into the cracks, small heaps of it appear beside the openings. Sometimes a polished pebble appears too, lifted from somewhere deep and left on the surface. By morning it has usually been taken underground again.

Nothing in the hollow seems to guard the slabs or carry food to them. Yet their groove is kept clear. When dust gathers in it, the squat animals push their narrow mouths along the channel, and the minute winged things settle there to pick at what remains. On mornings when the slabs are wet and the rest of the rock is dry, every waiting place beneath the gap is occupied.

After sunset the bark-colored hunters settle into the fern, folding their legs one at a time. The bronze creatures draw in what remains of their lines. Beneath them, pale bodies move through the chambers, carrying moisture farther into the stone. The last winged things descend among the succulent leaves.

The hollow grows quiet enough to hear individual sounds again: a pod shifting in the roots, a grazer rasping beneath a frond, the scrape from under the floor. Then, under the overhang, a drop lengthens on the cool rock. Animals in several different shelters turn toward it before it falls.
