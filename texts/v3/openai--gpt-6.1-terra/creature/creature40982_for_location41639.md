A little flatworm lives under the fern roots, where the black sediment is thickest. It is less than a centimetre long and nearly transparent. When it has eaten, the branching shape of its gut shows through its body as a faint brown stain. When it has not, it is difficult to distinguish from a strand of mucus on the concrete.

It moves on a film of its own making, slowly enough that a root lying across its back may bend before the animal can slip free. Two dark eyespots at its head register the brightness from the glass. They cannot make out the square above, but they help keep it beneath the fronds, where the light is softer and food collects.

It feeds on minute animals and scraps of soft matter among the roots. Sometimes the current draws a loose fragment toward the grate, and the worm follows it a little way out from the wall. There is less to hold to on the bare floor. Usually it turns back before it has crossed even the width of a frond.

The roar reaches it through the water and through the concrete beneath its body. It has lived with that trembling since it hatched; it can still feel the nearer pressure of a root shifting or another small animal moving against it.

There are others along the border, though not many. Their eggs adhere to the undersides of leaves and to sheltered places in the sediment. A worm may pass its whole life beneath a few overlapping fronds, while overhead one leaf browns, sinks into the water and gradually comes apart, and another unfolds into the space it leaves.
