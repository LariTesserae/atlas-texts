The bats occupy the ceiling according to its shape. They crowd into the deepest folds, where each can find a grip and a little shelter from the air rising through the chamber. Their fur is dusty in places, darker where condensation has settled on it. Most of the day they hang quietly, though quiet is relative: one adjusts a foot, another opens its wings a fraction, and the movement passes through its neighbors as they make room.

At dusk the first bats drop from their holds and turn toward the opening. Others follow in an uneven stream, squeezing past one another through the narrow passage. Outside, they scatter across the slopes to feed. By morning they return with full stomachs, bringing into the chamber food gathered far beyond it. Some of that food will pass through them and fall to the floor; some will go to the young held beneath them on the ceiling.

The largest accumulation below the colony belongs to the smallest residents. Beetles move through the droppings, often keeping to the damp layers beneath the surface. Some are pale-bodied; others are darker and harder-shelled. Mites pass among them, too small to be easily noticed until a patch of material is lifted and the specks begin to separate. They feed and breed in the accumulated matter, as do other minute animals and microbes. Shed fur, dead insects and the bodies of bats that do not survive add to it.

Crickets live throughout the chamber but favor the cracks, especially those with several exits. Their long antennae sweep the stone ahead of them. They emerge to investigate droppings, fragments of insects and other edible scraps, then retreat quickly when disturbed. The many calls that fill the chamber come from individuals spaced along ribs and shelves; near one crack a single cricket can be heard, while a short distance away its rasp is lost among the others.

The spiders place their webs where something traveling close to the stone may pass. A strand across a gap can catch a small fly or a young cricket. The webs collect dust too, and must be repaired when grit or a larger animal tears them loose. Moths rest nearer the entrance, where they can come and go between the chamber and the slopes outside. One that ventures farther in may settle on a wall for hours, its wings folded tightly against its body.

The mouse knows the ledges by repeated use. It noses into shallow depressions, lifts flakes of stone and feels beneath them with its whiskers. Beetle larvae are soft enough to eat readily; an adult may need to be pinned and bitten several times. The mouse leaves pieces of shell behind, along with crumbs too small for it to bother with. Beetles and mites eventually work through those remains as well.

Around the few persistent films of water, the surfaces stay dark and slick. Animals gather there to drink or shelter from the drier stone. When one hollow dries, they find another if they can, crossing exposed rock where a spider, cricket or mouse may encounter them.

Very little grows here by using the daylight. The tuft at the mouth and the moss near water catch what light reaches them, but the deeper community depends largely on what enters from outside: bats carrying their meals home, moths and other insects wandering in, the mouse finding its way between the chamber and the mountain above. Inside, those arrivals are used repeatedly, by creatures of different sizes, until little remains but grains among the gravel.
