The small animals in the motor housings are not all alike. Those nearest the bearings are flat and many-jointed, with pale seams between their plates. They can work their way into a gap scarcely wider than a sheet of metal. Others live farther back among the coils, where they grow rounder and move less. Their shells carry a fine crust of dust and spent lubricant. When a housing is opened, they remain in place for a few moments, their legs drawn close, before making for another source of heat.

They feed on what the machinery leaves behind. The powder in an old bearing contains traces of oil, worn metal and the dry remains of other animals. A stopped motor may hold several generations: empty shells caught behind a bolt head, smaller animals beneath them, and a larger one pressed into the warmest corner. They lay their eggs in the packing around the shafts. The young are almost transparent when they emerge; within a short time the basalt grit adheres to them, and they take on the color of the housings.

A few venture into the storage rooms. They shelter between the tiles, where the stacks hold warmth and the narrow spaces admit little light. There is less food here, but also less vibration. Their passages leave faint curved tracks across the impressed streets. Sometimes an animal dies in the hollow of a planned courtyard and becomes part of the dust that collects there. The tiles remain stacked around it, awaiting whatever use the city has for them.

The animals are too small to move paving stones or dismantle walls. Visitors have found places where such work is beginning: a seam cleared of grit, a bolt removed from a gate, a line of fresh chips at the foot of an old tower. On returning, they may find that nothing further has happened, or that the tower is gone and its stone has been fitted neatly into a wall several streets away. No one has caught the workers in the interval.

There are spaces in the city that seem made for them. Low doors open into passages beneath workshops, and narrow stairways descend alongside the turning shafts. Some passages have been kept clear; others are full of spare parts, sorted by size and wrapped in cloth that has grown brittle with age. The handles on a few doors are smooth from use, though a visitor can wait beside one for a long time without hearing movement beyond it.

At the presses, new plans come off the conveyors warm and faintly glossy along their edges. Most are carried away into storage. Now and then a tile is removed from a stack, leaving a rectangular patch of cleaner stone and a little fall of dust where its neighbors have settled together. Much later, a visitor may recognize the plan in the street outside: a passage widened, a wall set back, a square opened between two halls. The changes are small enough that a person might walk past without noticing, unless they had known the earlier arrangement well.

Whatever tends the city has kept the motors running through these alterations. A shaft continues to turn while the building above it is shortened; a press is shifted to a neighboring hall, and the tiles begin to arrive there instead. The animals follow the warmth into the new housings. For a time their old shelter remains warm too, and some stay until the metal cools enough that they begin to move. Then, one by one, they cross the floor toward the low pressure of the working machine.
