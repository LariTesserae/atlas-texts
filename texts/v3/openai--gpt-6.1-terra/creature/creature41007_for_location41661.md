The herds are not all the same kind, though at a distance their outlines have a family resemblance. Those that follow the broad red shelves are long and low, with many short legs and mouths spread along the undersides of their heads. They take stone in shallow layers, leaving behind smooth terraces that hold the day’s heat into the next morning. The taller animals feed at the escarpments. Their jaws close around whole projections of rock, and the force travels slowly down through their bodies until their feet settle deeper into the plateau.

An animal’s color changes with what it has eaten. Iron-rich stone darkens the plates along its back; pale seams appear after a long passage through quartz. The black glass is difficult to digest. Some animals avoid it, turning their heads aside when it rises through the red stone. Others seek it out and grow nearly transparent at the joints, where their inner rings can be seen turning in opposite directions.

There is no hurry in their feeding, but there is appetite. A young one at a ridge will press its whole body forward, bright limbs trembling, while its small jaws work at a patch already polished by the older animal. It may spend a season making a groove scarcely wider than its mouth. The older one does not take the ridge from it. Sometimes, after feeding, an adult leaves a thin edge of fractured stone exposed, where a smaller jaw can get purchase.

They carry much of the plateau inside them. Stone enters cold or sun-warm and passes through chambers that grow progressively hotter. What cannot be used moves toward the flanks, where it is laid down as new armor or released as fine grit. Useful metals travel along soft, pulsing channels. Copper gathers around the nerves; iron goes to the load-bearing parts. Other elements are kept in small amounts in places that have no obvious purpose until an animal is injured. Then a sealed pocket opens, and material held there for centuries begins to flow.

A worn bearing is not removed. The tissue around it loosens, grows inward, and dissolves the damaged surface while a new one forms beneath. During this time the animal favors the limb. Its gait develops a long unevenness that can be heard across the plateau: four deep contacts, a pause, and then a fifth, lighter one. Members of the herd alter their paths around it, sometimes years before it has finished crossing theirs.

They touch infrequently. Two adults approaching head-on may take decades to bring their faces together. When their plates finally meet, the contact sends a clear tone through both bodies. They remain joined while patterns of vibration pass between them, changing slowly in pitch. Afterward they part, or continue feeding side by side. An animal that has lost part of a limb may lean against another for a long time, the weight shifting almost imperceptibly as the damaged structures grow back.

The young develop in sheltered folds beneath an adult’s armor. At first they are soft and dark, threaded with fine metallic lines that move as they grow. Their internal works begin turning at different times: one ring, then another, then a tiny joint that flexes against the parent’s skin. The parent feeds heavily during this period. For years after the young animal emerges, it stays close enough to enter the shade beneath the larger body when the heat becomes too great.

A young one’s bright limbs darken as its plates thicken. They do not necessarily grow in a fixed number. A limb may divide near its base as the body becomes heavier, or two adjacent supports may broaden and fuse. Old animals bear the record of these changes in the seams of their armor: branching lines, filled scars, plates grown over plates. Some move on arrangements of legs so asymmetrical that the first sight of them makes their motion seem impossible. Nevertheless, their weight comes down evenly, and the stone beneath each foot takes it.

They know the others nearby through pressure as well as sound. The grinding of a feeding animal travels through the ground with a pattern particular to its body. So does the slow pulse of its circulation, and the intermittent striking of its joints. A herd spread across hundreds of miles is in continuous, imperfect contact. When one voice stops, the change passes through the stone. Heads turn by degrees. Feeding pauses in widening circles.

Death takes a long time to become visible. An animal may stand with its head lowered for years after its inner movements have begun to fail. The heat at its jaw fades. The rings beneath its hide slow one by one, and the electrical light between its teeth appears at longer intervals. Others may gather near it, their feet overlapping its tracks. Eventually it settles. The weight opens cracks in the plateau, and grit flows into them.

Its body remains useful. Young animals feed on the exposed edges of its plates, where generations of minerals have been concentrated and worked into forms easier to take in than raw stone. Adults may feed there too, carefully, leaving the larger structures intact until they loosen of their own accord. For a very long time a dead animal can be distinguished from a ridge by the curve of a joint or the smooth circular wall of an emptied chamber. Later, even these soften.

The distortions around the herds persist for a while after death. A footprint may lead into the lee of a body and emerge on the far side of a cliff. Two animals feeding at opposite ends of the same dead giant may sometimes see one another through an opening in its ribs, each against a different horizon. They continue their work. Their sense of the plateau includes these passages, and they navigate them as readily as they navigate the shelves and hollows.

When evening comes, the smaller creatures move toward the shade of the larger ones, though their journeys may last well beyond sunset. The cooling bodies tick at different rates. Beneath those innumerable sharp sounds the grinding continues, steady enough that its pauses are more noticeable than its presence. Far off, one of the tall animals raises its head from a ridge it has been eating for most of its life. A pale trench opens beneath its jaw. It stands over the cut as the heat drains from its mouth, then begins, very slowly, to turn toward another.
