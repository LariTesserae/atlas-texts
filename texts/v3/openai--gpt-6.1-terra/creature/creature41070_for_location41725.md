The largest inhabitants are the rooted animals on the terrace edges. From a distance they resemble stands of pale, many-legged trees, though their joints are unmistakable: rounded hinges, thick at the base, diminishing toward the long arms that hold their fans. Each leg enters the green ground through a collar of living tissue. Fibers from the animal and the plateau lie so closely together there that the boundary between them is difficult to find.

Their bodies are broad and low, sheltered beneath plates the color of old ivory. The feeding fans rise above them in tiers, delicate as lace and crowded with minute mouths. A fan may be several times wider than the animal’s body. Its ribs carry the deep brassy sound that fills the water around the terrace. Every great animal has its own arrangement of tones, held in the surrounding sea: low notes close to the body, higher ones spread through the fan. Where several stand together, their calls overlap in dense, resonant fields.

Among their legs live smaller creatures with no apparent distinction between shell and shelter. They are flattened against the plant mass, their backs ridged with grooves in which other tiny lives are lodged. Some have transparent edges through which their organs show as white branching lines. Others are green throughout, filled with the same fibrous tissue that runs through the plateau, and seem to belong as much to the ground as to the animals upon it.

The great fronds support their own populations. Their trunks are deeply furrowed, and the pale whorls attached to them are colonies of soft-bodied feeders. Each whorl consists of hundreds of individuals joined along a fleshy seam, their mouths directed outward into the open water. The largest colonies form bands around the trunks. In the silver light their bodies are nearly colorless, but within each one is a dark green thread, continuous with threads in its neighbors.

The grazers beneath the canopies are shaped like leaves themselves: thin, broad, and marked with branching lines. Their undersides are silver or cream; their upper surfaces are dark enough that, seen from above, they can be difficult to distinguish from the frond. Their mouths meet the leaf along narrow rasping edges. Around those edges the plant tissue is fine and translucent, and the grazers’ own bodies contain green chambers whose branching forms resemble the veins beneath them.

In the open reaches, the silver fish hold their loose crescents with spaces between them. Their eyes are large and black, their fins almost invisible. Each school has its own faint ringing presence, a high, clear sound spread through the water occupied by its members. Between the schools are the hunters: long creatures as transparent as the water, visible mainly by the silver crescents seen through the curves of their bodies. Their jaws are fine frameworks of glassy teeth. Some have dark internal organs that hang along the spine like seeds.

There are smaller lives in the machinery. Inside the exposed chambers, among the interlocking disks and charged filaments, lie creatures no larger than grains of sand. Some have hard, faceted bodies; others are soft threads pressed against the membranes. In places they occur so densely that a valve’s green surface appears dusted with gold. Their bodies carry minute repetitions of the surrounding structure: nested rings, fine channels, branching blue lines. They are not separate from the machinery in any simple way, nor are they merely parts of it. Their forms fit the living chambers with extraordinary precision.

The plateau itself is the most extensive life here. Its fronds are widely spaced, but beneath them their roots meet in the terraces, entering stone and compressed plant tissue alike. The exposed cliff faces show that continuity: green fibers through pale mineral, then ranks of chambers, then the heavy layered mass from which the next terrace rises. Its ringing occupies the water around every canopy, while its low mechanical roar is strongest beside the open cross-sections.

All these inhabitants are present in their relationships to one another: the grazers against the leaves, the whorls on the trunks, the great animals rooted at the edges, the tiny chamber-dwellers among the disks. Their forms contain feeding surfaces, channels, mouths, and organs, yet nothing passes through a sequence of acts. The animals are alive with their fans extended and their jaws set; the plateau is alive with its charge and its immense, fixed sound. Beyond each visible terrace, more canopies stand in the blue water, and more animals occupy the spaces beneath them, as far as the light reaches.
