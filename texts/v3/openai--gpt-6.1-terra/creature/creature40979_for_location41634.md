Their bodies are the color of the clay where it has not quite dried: pale, with a gray seam between each segment. A larger one is about as long as the last joint of a finger. Its shell is thin enough that, when it pauses in a shallow well, the movement of its gut can be seen through it. The smaller ones are nearly translucent. Their broad feet spread against the wet tiles, lifting with a faint tack at every step.

Each has a hard pair of jaws and, beneath them, a pocket of softer skin in which it carries its bit of clay. The fragment is not food. The creatures graze on the dark growth along the wall and on the slippery films that form beneath the pavement. They also rasp at dead stems after a flood has softened them, leaving the stems furred with fine tooth marks. A stem may stand through several wet seasons, growing thinner each time, until it breaks under the weight of a passing body.

When the water begins to rise, the smallest emerge first. They slip from seams too narrow for the adults and travel close to the wall, sometimes climbing over one another where a groove carries a thread of water down to the floor. The larger creatures follow from beneath the tiles. Their opposing streams meet and part continuously. A little one may be lifted clear off the pavement by an adult’s passing foot, then set down again and continue in the same direction.

Their clay fragments differ in color. Some are scraped from the red ledges; some are blackened crumbs from the ash layer; a few are nearly white, taken from the exposed edge of a broken pot. When a creature finds a crack it can reach, it turns its head sideways and works the fragment in with its jaws and front feet. Others may add to the same place, packing the crack until its surface is smooth and damp. The repairs hold for a while. They hold longest where the water moves gently and where no tile bears much weight.

Under the floor are chambers formed by old pottery, their curved walls enclosing pockets of still water. The creatures lay their eggs there, fastening them in clusters to the undersides of the shards. A cluster looks like a patch of pale grit until the eggs begin to move. The young hatch with narrow feet and remain in the chambers at first, feeding on the film that coats the clay. As their feet broaden, they venture into the seams and join the smaller stream above.

The bowls at the wall shelter creatures of every size. Some gather there before any water has entered them, pressing into the cracked bottoms. Once a bowl fills, they crowd along its inner slope, their feet and bodies shifting it a little on the uneven tile. A tipped bowl leaves them briefly heaped together in the spill. They right themselves quickly, though an overturned animal will sometimes lie a moment with all its feet spread before finding purchase.

During dry intervals the basin grows quiet, but it is not silent. Close to the wall one can hear a jaw working, or a foot withdrawing from a groove. Beneath a loose tile there may be a single click, followed after a long pause by another farther away. The creatures wait in the cool spaces they have kept open, among the shells of earlier generations and the fine clay dust that will become soft enough to carry when the rain returns.
