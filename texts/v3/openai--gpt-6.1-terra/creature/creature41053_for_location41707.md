The workers are small enough that two can shelter beneath an upturned water pan, though they seldom do. A full-grown carrier reaches roughly to a person’s waist. Their backs are broad for their height, their arms long, and the soles of their feet spread inside soft wrappings of woven fiber. The wrappings are thickest at the outer edge, where a foot can bridge a narrow seam without slipping into it. When they wear through, the workers mend them with strips pulled from old slings.

Their hands are pale and blunt-fingered. Gray dust gathers in the creases, and most have the shallow white scars of pinched skin. Their faces are difficult to read at a distance: the eyes sit under heavy ridges, and the mouth is small. Close by, their attention is unmistakable. They watch a moving block, a companion’s hand, the shifting tension in a rope. A carrier can tell from the angle of another’s shoulders when to take more weight.

They speak while they work, but much of what travels between them is not speech. A mallet laid sideways means something different from a mallet laid with its head toward the joint. Two quick taps on a lever call the others back; a steady tapping tells them to keep pressure where it is. In the roar of the passages, a worker may press a palm against a neighbor’s wrist to signal a change. Crews that have worked together a long time seem to move before any signal is given.

The young begin with the pans and wedges. They learn which hollows hold water and which only appear to, and they are sent through the crowded lanes with clay pegs held against their chests. They make games of balancing on the higher seams, until an older worker calls them down. Even then, the older one usually checks the block they chose: a child has a way of finding the edge that rocks.

Inside the houses, the floors are raised in little shelves. The inhabitants sleep curled against one another, with their feet toward the doorway, where any water entering will reach them first. Tools hang above the sleeping places in loops of sling fiber. There are cups of gritty paste, bundles of dry cord, and thin pieces of shell rubbed smooth enough to catch a weak reflection. On some walls, short scratches mark heights reached by successive courses; other scratches are lower down, where children can add their own.

They eat several things that the plain does not readily show. In the protected joints, they peel away the thicker translucent films with curved pieces of shell. These are rinsed and pressed together until they form a quivering cake, which is eaten fresh or kept under an inverted pan. Workers also bring back soft nodules from deep seams, gray on the outside and fibrous within. Some are shared immediately. Others are left in damp hollows for days, during which they swell and acquire a sharp smell. A stranger might mistake them for the beginning of another block.

The films are more than a food source. A thin colony will spread over a wet wedge and make it easier to draw free; a thicker one can hold a fine crack closed for a little while. Workers carry fragments on damp clay and place them where water is slow to reach. The colonies grow toward the moisture, sometimes crossing a joint no wider than a hair. When conditions change, they contract into folds almost too small to see. A joint that looks bare may come alive again after a shower.

The pale drinking animals are called by a short sound made low in the throat, though they do not always come to it. Their padded feet are divided underneath into many soft ridges, and grit clings to them. They have small eyes set well back on the blunt head; when testing a block, they lower the head and touch it with a pair of short feelers beside the mouth. They eat the swollen films in sheltered seams, drawing them up slowly, and will nose at a pan long after it is empty.

They do not appear to belong to anyone. One may rest for a night beneath a weighted roof, then leave for the outer plain. Some return often enough to be recognized by a torn ear-fold or a dark mark along the spine. A worker who finds one waiting beside a dry pan may carry water to it, though water is not wasted casually here. In return—or perhaps merely because the same places interest them—the animals pause at joints that have begun to move. A crew will sometimes follow their attention and find a wedge loosening beneath the surface.

There are smaller animals that almost never cross open ground: flat, jointed scavengers that shelter under pegs and eat the dried edges of film. They scatter when a peg is lifted, disappearing into gaps the width of a fingernail. The young catch them in cups, examine their folding legs, and release them near the houses. Whether they are useful there is a matter the adults disagree on; some clear them away, while others leave them undisturbed.

The workers are born among these structures and grow old with the sound of them. Those no longer strong enough to carry still sort wedges by size, mend slings, and sit near the basin listening. They know the difference between an ordinary settling knock and one that repeats too cleanly, or returns from an unexpected direction. When the third report comes, the nearest crew falls quiet. Often it is an older listener who points out where to look.

Not every seam can be reached at once. Some must be watched for days; some open and close too quickly for a hand to enter. The workers mark those places with pegs and leave a pan nearby if the blocks seem thirsty. A crew coming through later will read the marks, add water if there is any, and stop to listen with a cheek against the stone. Then they gather their tools and carry on, making room for the next block.
