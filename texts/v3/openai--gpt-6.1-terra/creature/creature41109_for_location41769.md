The workers who remain are difficult to pick out among the stems. They stand upright and have jointed limbs, but their surfaces are layered in narrow leaf plates, pale at the edges and dark where the plates overlap. A worker at rest may hold one arm raised for so long that a young vine fastens to it. When the worker moves on, it carefully unwinds the vine and leaves it supported on a hook.

Inside each worker, powder circulates through a framework of fine tubes. The powder changes over a lifetime. Some of it is inherited from the chamber where the worker was made; some comes from the floor underfoot, or from the columns where they sleep. Workers who have spent years in the same factory tend to develop the same gray streaks in their plates. Those who travel carry several colors at once, which can be seen through the thin joints of their hands.

They are made in small groups, usually near the sorting chambers of a healthy column. A young worker begins as a folded bundle suspended among seed cases. Its first movements are slight: a hand opening against its own face, a foot pressing the surrounding leaves apart. Older workers keep the chamber warm by turning its outer foliage toward the brightest light. When the young one is ready, they open the bundle and help it find a seam in the floor. It learns to walk by following the seams from one junction to the next.

The workers tend machinery, though they do not always distinguish tending it from tending the plants around it. They clear fibers from rollers, turn leaves toward the light and remove seed cases from girders that have grown too crowded. When a press produces a flawed panel, they do not throw it away. They lay it flat in a quiet place and set a little powder along the broken veins. Most such panels take root. A few remain where they are, growing thicker and more intricately folded, and become useful as seats or low tables.

They speak by tapping their plates together. Close by, the sound is much like the soft click of the presses; at a distance, it disappears into the foliage. A worker wanting someone’s attention may instead lift an arm and let its shadow cross the other’s feet. This is considered more abrupt than calling.

The small shell-bearing animals are tolerated almost everywhere. Workers call them through a column by tapping near its base, then wait while the faint answering ticks move through the wood. They make narrow channels for them around damaged chambers and leave portions of dead fiber near the entrances. Sometimes the animals carry powder where it is needed; sometimes they carry it somewhere entirely inconvenient. A chamber that has received the wrong powder may produce leaves of an unfamiliar shape for years afterward.

The many-legged grazers come in from the open plain. Their feet are broad and delicate, with pads that can feel a weak leaf before it gives way. They feed on the fine growth that gathers along seams, combing it loose with flexible mouthparts. A grazer will stand motionless beside a worker for a long time, tasting the floor, then step around them. Its shadow may return before it does. Workers who notice one on the plates usually leave the seam undisturbed.

In the unfinished district, several workers spend their bright hours beneath the arches. They place frames around the shadows of the future rooms and fill the frames with powdered leaf, a handful at a time. The outlines change as the arches grow. A doorway shifts; a wall narrows; once, a whole room appeared several paces beyond its frame. The workers moved the frame and began again.

One of them rests there after the others have gone. Its plates have grown thin, and powder gathers slowly in its lower chambers, making the walk home longer each season. It sits beside a shadow that now holds the first low ridge of a wall. When the silver front passes, the worker turns its face toward the reflected light. For a few moments it is difficult to tell how far away the new wall stands. Then the leaves settle, and the worker can reach out and feel it growing.
