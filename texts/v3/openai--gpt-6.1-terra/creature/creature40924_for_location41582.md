The swifts are the chamber’s most conspicuous inhabitants, though for much of the day many of them are absent. They leave through the openings in the vault and range over the country for insects, returning one after another with food for their young. At the upper tubes they hesitate for an instant, wings beating, then catch the worn clay rims and disappear inside. Their nests are tucked so far back that the occupants are often heard before they are seen.

The young call from the tubes when an adult approaches, and the adults call to one another across the chamber. A bird returning to the wrong opening may be met by a sharp protest. By evening the upper wall is crowded with arrivals: brown wings folding, small feet gripping clay, birds edging past one another at the bowls before climbing back to their nests. The smoothness of the tube rims is older than any of them.

The geckos live among the birds but move through a quieter, lower world. Some are brown and plainly marked; others have pale, dusty skin that makes them almost disappear against the tubes. They wait along the undersides of shelves or inside empty openings, watching for moths and other insects. When they move, it is often in a quick rush, followed by perfect stillness. Their toes leave faint tracks in the dust on the tiles.

At the baskets, spiders occupy the spaces between rim and wall. Their webs are thick with old silk, fine debris and the remains of insects; some sag under their own weight, while others have fresh strands stretched taut across the gaps. Moths settle there to rest, sometimes escaping when the baskets sway, sometimes becoming another small parcel in the silk. Below them, beetles work through feathers, husks and droppings. They are most easily seen when the sweepings are disturbed and a patch of floor begins to move.

The wiry plant in the basket has little company. Its roots hold the old nesting material together, and its stems lean toward a strip of light. The moss at the roof joints lives where the clay stays damp longest; after a dry spell it shrinks close to the surface and is easily overlooked.

The people who tend the chamber do not live among the tubes, but the animals have grown accustomed to their visits. A person coming down the stair makes the pots rattle and sends a few birds out into the light. Others remain on their nests. The visitor works around them, testing a loose basket, moving a bowl beneath a drip, sweeping only enough to open the path. When the person has gone, the disturbed dust settles over the tiles, the geckos come out again, and the chamber fills with the sound of wings.
