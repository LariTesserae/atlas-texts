The people of the basin know the ground by how it gives underfoot. A child learning the paths is taught to test the end of a plank before putting weight on it, and to step around the places where the moss looks unusually smooth. In those places there may be nothing firm for an arm’s length down.

The children are sent on errands early. They carry small baskets of eggs or firewood along the raised paths, and they are useful at finding ducks’ nests in the sedge, though they sometimes bring back cold eggs from nests abandoned weeks before. By the time they are older, they can recognize each household’s ducks from a distance. The birds range freely through the wettest part of the basin and are called home with handfuls of grain at dusk.

Most households keep a few goats on the terraces and take turns watching the common flock. The goats provide milk, hair and manure, and occasionally meat. They must be led farther up the slope when the basin’s open patches have been grazed short. A watcher carries a hooked stick for pulling branches down to them and another length of cord for the animal that will certainly find a way loose.

There are small gardens wherever the soil can be persuaded to drain: beds of greens and onions on the higher shelves, and herbs in boxes under the eaves. Some food comes up the mountain on people’s backs. At intervals a party goes down below the cloud, taking cheese, woven baskets and lengths of cord to exchange for grain, salt and things the basin cannot readily produce. They return tired, and for a day or two afterward the storehouse is a place of particular interest to everyone.

The houses are close, but their occupants are not always in sight of one another. Much of the day is spent in the spaces between them. Someone sits under an eave splitting poles while a neighbor strips bark from willow rods. Two people lift a section of path and find a frog pressed into the dark beneath it; they set the frog aside and argue briefly about where the water entered. The repair may take the afternoon, especially if others stop to help and bring news with them. By evening there is a fresh patch of pale timber among the older, blackened lengths.

Keeping things dry is work that never quite ends. Firewood is stacked on racks above the ground and moved inward when wind drives rain beneath the eaves. Clothes hang near the hearth, then are turned and hung again. Seeds and grain are kept in raised storehouses, in tightly covered vessels that are inspected for damp. People know which roof begins to drip first and which hearth draws badly when the cloud lies low. An invitation to sit by a good fire is readily accepted.

The older inhabitants remember changes in the water. One can point to a stand of alder where there was once a pool deep enough to wash a blanket. Another remembers the year a slip came down behind the upper pens, bringing stones and mud as far as the spring yard. After a long rain, several people will go together to look at the banks, clearing loose branches from the drains and listening to the streams above the roofs.

The other animals use the settlement as confidently as the people do. Frogs shelter beneath doorsteps; shrews work their narrow runs through the banks; beetles and their larvae occupy the rotting centers of timbers that still look sound from outside. Chickens wait near anyone turning compost or lifting a board. The small birds in the alders are quick to take fallen grain, and the swallows take midges over the yard until the light begins to fail.

At night the goats settle on their terrace and the chickens are shut away. Doors close, though voices still travel between houses through the wet air. For a while someone may be heard calling a late duck, and another person answering from farther along the path. Then the water becomes the loudest inhabitant again, running under the walkways and spilling from the roofs while the people sleep above it.
