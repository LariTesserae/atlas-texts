The people who live in the hollow know the town partly by what has to be cut back. In the mornings someone is usually at a doorway with shears, holding a vine away from the jamb with one hand and snipping with the other. The cuttings are left in a pail or swept into the canal-side cart. A branch that has grown across a window may be tied higher rather than removed; by the next rainy season it will be thick enough to shade the room.

Many households have someone at the mill, though their work differs. Some cut reeds on the wet ground and bring them in bound bundles. Others feed stalks between the rollers, watch the motors, wash the processed fiber or make repairs when a belt begins to slip. They come home with damp cuffs and pale strands caught on their clothes. At shift change they stand under the awning for a few minutes, talking over the machinery, before dispersing up the narrow streets.

There are people whose days are spent away from the mill. A woman keeps a small shop in a room raised two steps above the street, selling cooking oil, soap, batteries and packets of seeds. When rain drives in, she moves the lowest crates farther back. Two brothers repair pumps and small engines in a yard roofed with mismatched metal; their tools are laid out on a board to keep them above the water that collects around the drain. A few children pass through on their way home, stopping to watch if an engine has been opened up.

The children learn the routes through town by changes in the pavement: the ridge made by a root outside one house, the loose stone near the canal, the step that stays slippery after the others have dried. They are sent to fetch things, to bring in washing, to clear leaves from the grating outside a door. They also make boats from broad leaves and follow them along the canal until the current draws them under a building.

People grow food wherever a container will stand securely: herbs on window ledges, beans climbing cords between balconies, greens in tubs beside the steps. They exchange cuttings and argue amiably about which plants can tolerate the deepest shade. A household with a good crop of chilies dries some under its eaves and gives the rest away before they soften in the damp.

The older residents remember walls before the roots lifted them and can point out where a channel once ran. They know which repairs lasted and which merely moved the water into a neighbor’s house. When a blockage forms, several people may come out to deal with it, bringing a rake, a length of wire, a bucket. They work until the water starts to move, then stay a little longer to make sure it keeps moving.

At night, those nearest the mill become accustomed to its sound, though they notice at once when it stops. Windows stay open for air. From one room comes a radio, from another the clatter of dishes; someone calls across the street for a borrowed pan. Rain begins on the roofs, lightly at first, and the voices rise until it has passed.
