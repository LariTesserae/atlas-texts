The people of the plateau are easiest to see when they move. At rest, their backs have the same warm gray as the stone, and the plates over their shoulders overlap like shelves of weathered rock. They are taller than the pale-backed animals that gather in the city, but not by much. They travel with their bodies held low, on four strong limbs; a smaller pair of arms folds close beneath the chest when not in use.

Their plates grow slowly. A young person has a smooth, almost pearly back that marks readily, while an old one may have ridges and pits deep enough to hold a season’s dust. The working limbs remain flexible throughout life, supported by cords and cushions of mineral gel. When they have been out in the cold for too long, their movements become deliberate and stiff. They will pause beside a warm wall, press their whole underside against it, and wait.

They are sensitive to charge, though less so than many of the animals. They can feel a herd turning before they can see it, and an approaching dust bank has a distinct pressure that gathers over their plates. Close together, they speak partly by shifting the fields around their bodies. Their other language is made with small hard surfaces inside the mouth: ticks, scrapes and sustained notes, often too quiet to carry beyond a few paces. In a foundry, where the machinery fills the air and every surface holds a charge, workers use taps on rails and walls. The patterns pass along a line of people until they reach the person meant to answer.

A great deal of their work consists of keeping things apart. Dust must be sorted by composition; useful metals must be separated from those that make a plate brittle or a wire slow to conduct. The pale gas is kept under pressure in vessels sunk into the warmer rock. Workers inspect the seals by touch, laying a sensitive inner arm along each joint. A leak gives almost no sound, but it cools the casing in a narrow line.

The enclosed conveyors are busy even when the streets appear empty. Inside them, trays move ore, broken tools, finished parts and carefully graded dust from one district to another. The people ride there too, crouched among the cargo or walking along narrow service ways. In a storm, when the piers vanish beneath drifting dust, someone must still go out to clear the intakes. Such workers fasten a line to the nearest rail and wear smooth covers over their joints. When they return, others brush them clean before the covers are removed.

Their dwellings are built against the deeper walls of the foundries and storage towers. A room may hold several generations, with shallow resting places worn into the floor and a common surface for tools. People bring home pieces of metal that have failed inspection: a length of wire with an uneven core, a plate too thin at one edge. These are worked again by hand or saved until there is enough to send back through a furnace. Objects pass between households in the same way. Ownership is understood, but few things are expected to remain in one room for their entire use.

Young people spend much of their time near the radiators, where the stone stays warm and the visiting animals offer company. The smallest grazers will climb over a still body and settle in its shadow. The young learn to distinguish their faint signals from the stronger ones of their companions, and sometimes follow them out along the piers. An older person goes after them before dusk, not usually to bring them back at once, but to show them where the charged dust lies thick enough to draw predators.

The people do not herd the animals, although they know their habits closely. They clear paths when the heavy pale-backs crowd a street and mend the guards the animals rub loose. If one dies beneath a radiator, workers carry it beyond the collectors. Some of its plates may eventually be gathered and returned to a furnace; the softer parts are left on the stone, where smaller creatures come to feed.

There are people on the old roads who may not see the city for many seasons. They travel between mines and scattered depots, checking rails, carrying replacement parts and recording where the dust has settled. They navigate by the copper fans as everyone does, but keep several routes in memory for the times when a fan has moved or disappeared. At each depot they leave a record cut into a metal strip: the state of the road, the direction of the latest banks, the warmth remaining in the storage beds.

Those who work at the escarpment have a different sense of distance from those in the city. They know the ledges by the feel of the wind and can tell which mine entrances are open by the dust rising from them. Some descend for long shifts and return with fresh scores across their backs from the narrow passages. They bring ore, news of damaged rail, and sometimes dust unlike any found on the upper shelves. It is examined carefully before being sent to the collectors.

At the end of a shift, a worker may cross an entire district without meeting another person. The rails are warm underfoot; beyond the conveyor walls, animals lie in loose ranks beside the factories. From time to time a field passes through them and their heads turn together. The worker waits for a gap, steps through, and continues home, carrying the day’s vibration in their limbs.
