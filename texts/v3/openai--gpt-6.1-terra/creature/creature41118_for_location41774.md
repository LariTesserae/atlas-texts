The growth that covers most of the valley has no single trunk. It begins as a mesh of pale roots, fine enough to enter a crack in stone, and thickens wherever water lingers. Branches rise from the mesh and divide close to the ground. Each bears overlapping scales that turn their waxed faces toward the light. In dry weather they lie tight against the stems; when water reaches the roots, they spread and expose the softer surfaces beneath.

A branch can live for years after the root that fed it has died. It sends down new strands where it touches another mat, and these take hold among the older fibers. The oldest parts of the cover are difficult to distinguish from the soil they have made. They are dark, compressed and full of grit, with living threads still passing through them.

On the lower slopes, the jointed stalks grow out of this same tangle. Their broad, flattened leaves catch water moving over the surface and direct it toward the drainage channels. The hairs on the leaves retain mineral grains and fragments of dead growth. As these accumulate, smaller plants take root in them: cushiony colonies with translucent tips, and a fine moss that grows in circles around each persistent drop.

The dark crawlers live mostly below the light. They have short legs suited to pushing through root fibers and a flexible covering that lets them flatten themselves against the underside of a mat. They eat the softened inner layers of dead stems, leaving narrow galleries packed with fine, damp pellets. When a gallery is exposed by a shift in the cover, the crawlers retreat toward its colder end. They can remain there through a long dry spell, hardly moving, until water reaches them again.

The pale moths spend much longer in the valley than their occasional flights suggest. Their young feed inside the thickest stalks, following the seam between the hard outer layers and the moist tissue within. A stalk may peel for several seasons before a small opening appears in it and an adult emerges. The moth rests on the scales until its wings have dried, then flies low over the channels. Its pale color makes it conspicuous in the open but difficult to see against frost or the newly shed skin of a stalk.

Other life is smaller still. Thin films grow in the basins where meltwater stands for a few days at a time. They dry to a faint stain when the water drains away and darken again with the next rain. In the deepest pockets, where ice lasts longest, they live along its lower edge. Tiny grazing animals move through the films there, feeding in the narrow space between ice and stone.

The valley’s inhabitants leave places for one another without holding those places open. A crawler’s tunnel fills as the roots around it thicken. A moss hollow is buried when a waterlogged crown settles over it. A moth may lay its eggs on a stalk that is later carried sideways by a sliding mat. Most of the eggs will be lost; a few remain against living tissue and hatch there. By the time the next adults emerge, the channel beside them may have moved several lengths downhill.
