The most numerous inhabitants are rooted along the water seams. Each silver-green cushion is made of thousands of fine shoots, packed so closely that a hand pressed against it meets a single resilient surface. Beneath the surface, the older shoots have darkened and flattened into a felt that holds water against the stone. The living tips grow out over this felt, taking the light and the cold.

The broad-leaved plants grow more slowly. Their leaves rise from thick, pale bases buried in the wet moss. Each leaf can remain green for years, accumulating scratches from windblown grit and a faint white rim where ice has repeatedly formed and melted. When one dies, it folds into the bed around its neighbours. Its place is taken so gradually that the outline of the bed hardly changes.

The black fronds favour colder water. They grow where a basin freezes around their stems, or where the shade of an engine hall persists through the long daylight. Their glossy surfaces shed water before it can freeze thickly upon them. Seen close to, the fronds are not quite black: each has a deep green centre and a margin thin enough for light to pass through. They produce their new growth below the ice, where it is protected from the wind.

All three kinds spread along the narrow outlets. A basin may seem to be an isolated garden, but its plants follow the damp crack beyond it in small patches, sometimes for kilometres. The patches become sparse on bare stone and then gather again at the next pool. After a long time, a new bed appears to have been there all along.

The districts support them in less obvious ways. Water returned from the engines carries a little warmth and finely ground mineral matter. It runs beneath the beds, bringing moisture to roots that would otherwise dry when the wind is strongest. The plants, in turn, bind the damp ground around the foundations. When water shifts its course, their growth follows it; the new mat slows the flow and gives it another course to follow. Some of the shallow channels around the halls have been shaped by generations of this growth.

The machinery is their other neighbour. A pump can fail, and for a while one note disappears from the plateau’s chord. Water gathers above it and recedes below. Frost advances across a basin; the black fronds endure beneath it. Then water finds another route, or the pump begins again, and the margins slowly return to where they were.

It is difficult to say where the occupied part of the plateau ends. The beds are plainly alive, and their roots extend into cracks too narrow to see. Beyond them, water moves through rock and metal in paths that depend on the plants, just as the plants depend on the water’s return. A distant district may be hidden for years by the curve of the ground, yet its turbines are felt through the stone, and some portion of the water it has lifted may eventually reach the basin at one’s feet.
