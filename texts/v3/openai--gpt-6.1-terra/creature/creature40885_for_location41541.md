The builders are small enough to pass beneath a springtail without touching its belly. They have soft, jointed bodies, six walking limbs, and two finer limbs held forward, which they use to read the knots in the bridges. The tips of these limbs can feel the difference between a strand of grass that has been recently cut and one that is beginning to soften. They can also feel one another’s breathing when they rest close together.

Each carries a piece of flaked stone. A good edge is kept through many moves, wrapped in root fiber and tucked against the body when not in use. The stone is useful for cutting grass, but it also makes a clear little tap when struck against a stem. Builders working out of reach of one another tap to say where they are. The sound travels better through the plants than through the air.

They do not build houses within the chamber. They sleep in folds of moss, or in slings woven between stems, above the floor. The youngest sleep several together, with an older builder laid across the opening. When water comes down the wall, the sleepers nearest it wake the others by pulling on the sling. There is usually time to climb.

They eat tender root tissue and the pale growth they scrape from damp fibers. They also gather what other creatures leave: husks, soft eggs, the bodies of mites caught against the moss. A hunter at a tunnel mouth is given room. The builders know its place by a particular scatter of dry fragments, and they renew that landmark when the floor floods.

Their bridges are wider than one builder needs. Two can pass by gripping opposite edges, though a carrier with a bundle of grass has to wait at a crossing. The raised knots are spaced for the length of a walking limb. At a junction, three close knots mean a clear way; a loose loop warns that the bridge beyond is damaged or has been taken up. Young builders learn these marks by following an adult, touching each knot after it has been touched ahead of them.

The fern has become the center of their work. Its furled tip is smooth and tight, with a cool seam running along it. On the side where it advances, the builders take down their slings before the roof grows too low. Behind it, they test the new opening with their reaching limbs and lay the first strands across. Sometimes a space that seemed large enough in the evening is pinched shut by morning. The stranded grass is cut free if it can be reached; otherwise it remains there, pressed into the growing wall.

After a heavy footfall above, they emerge one by one. They feel along the bridges for broken bindings, pull drowned springtails from the lanes, and tap to locate anyone still sheltered among the roots. Then they carry the stone edges forward again, into the space the fern has just made.
