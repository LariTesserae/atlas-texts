The smallest grazers spend much of the day lying flat, with their legs folded into the shallow depressions between cushions. Their backs are pale and finely mottled; at a distance they look like pieces of stone weathered on one side. Only the ears, which they can turn independently, give them away. They are not listening for calls so much as feeling changes in the rush of air and the faint tremors that arrive through their bodies.

They feed slowly, taking the tender tips of silver leaves and leaving the dense centers of the cushions intact. When one finds a damp seam, it licks along the edge, following the water into cracks too narrow for its muzzle. Its feet are broad and flexible underneath. On a bare slab it can press one foot down several times in a quick pattern. A grazer some distance away may pause, press back, and settle again. At times several animals answer in succession, the movements passing across the basin too slowly to resemble a single alarm.

Their young are darker than the adults and lie so close to the vegetation that an insect crossing the leaves may walk over them. They begin pressing their feet against the rock before they can travel far, making small, irregular signals that the adults seem to distinguish from the wind-shaken gravel.

The long-legged animals use more of the plateau. Their lines converge at the hollows where water lasts longest, then spread apart as each animal moves on to feed. They can crop growth from narrow fissures, bracing themselves with their forelegs while they work their heads between the stone edges. Their legs are jointed in a way that lets them stand for long periods without much movement; from far off, a resting line looks almost like a row of thin rock projections.

At the water pits they wait with surprising patience. One drinks while the others stand around it, heads angled into the wind. A pit may hold only a mouthful at a time, replenished drop by drop from an overhang. The animals know many such places. Their routes between them are not fixed paths but broad, repeated crossings, adjusted for the dampness of the season.

Beneath the plant cushions lives a finer, busier community. Wingless insects move through the packed stems, feeding on dead leaves, roots, and one another. When the air warms, narrow-winged adults climb to the exposed tips and let themselves be taken. Most fly low, dropping back into shelter when a gust catches them. Their eggs are laid deep in the cushions or in grains of soil lodged against the roots. There is moisture there long after the stone around them has dried.

The birds belong partly to the plateau and partly to the country below it. Many leave the ledges in the morning, slipping over the rim and disappearing against the blue distance. They return with full crops, sometimes after hours out of sight. Their feet are strong and their feathers lie close to the body; on a ledge they turn into the wind and lower themselves until they are difficult to separate from the crowded rock.

A bird returning to a crowded ledge may carry a pebble for some distance before finding the slab it wants. It strikes with the side of its beak, producing a note that cuts briefly through the roar. Nearby birds lift their heads or spread their tails. Sometimes another bird picks up the dropped pebble and strikes with it in a different place. The ledges are scattered with these small, handled stones, some brought from the plateau above, others from lower slopes.

Their droppings collect in cracks below the nesting places. In the sheltered stretches where a little dust also gathers, cushions grow thicker than they do out on the open table. Grazers visit those places when the birds are away, and insects shelter there year-round.

Even where no animal is immediately visible, the plateau is occupied. Something is moving inside the vegetation, resting under an overhang, or standing beyond the next stone rib. The great distances between the larger bodies leave room for the wind to dominate everything, but the living things continue through it: feeding, waiting, answering a pressure in the ground, or finding their way back to a ledge among thousands of others.
