The largest living things in the high country are difficult to count. A wall that appears to be one plant may be several, joined at the roots and carrying water through one another’s tissues. A cable that looks separate may belong to a trunk a long distance away. Some of the oldest growths have been broken repeatedly and have continued from the pieces that remained caught. Their dead parts persist among the living, lending stiffness to a span until they soften or are pulled apart.

Most of their leaves are made for dimness. They lie almost flat around openings, overlapping like the scales of a hand held toward the light. Near the upper rims the same plants may bear smaller, tougher leaves, scarred by wind and ice. Food made there travels slowly down into shaded stems and roots. Falling leaves, dust, dead insects and the droppings of animals also collect in the branching shelves. Pale feeders spread through those deposits, digesting them and passing some of what they gather into the roots around them.

The water cups are short-lived, but many animals know when to find them. A shelf may seem nearly empty until wet air arrives. Then small shapes emerge from bark crevices and uncurl from beneath leaves. Some drink by pressing a flattened mouth against the water; others stand at the rim and draw beads from the fine hairs on their forelimbs. They drink quickly and fill folds in their bodies before the cups freeze or the wind takes the water away.

The cable grazers travel farther. They are low, long animals with flexible spines and thick pads on the undersides of their feet. Each pad can spread over a rounded stem, while the hooked toes close around it. On a wide route they move in loose groups, cropping as they go; on a narrow crossing they follow nose to tail. The adults carry worn places on their bellies where their young have clung during the first journeys away from the birth shelves. A very young grazer can hold on for hours, even while asleep.

They eat the tender ends of shoots and the soft tissue inside swollen joints. Their browsing changes the paths they use: repeatedly cropped branches grow dense and knotted, making good footholds, while untouched shoots can lengthen across a route and obstruct it. Herds remember dependable crossings, but they must also learn new ones. After a contraction closes a familiar passage, they may spend a day testing the neighboring growth before the first animal commits its weight.

The gliders are smaller and less bound to particular routes. They climb to a shelf edge, wait with their heads raised, and launch when a current begins to lift. Their gliding membranes are furred on the upper side and nearly bare beneath; folded, they make a warm wrap around the body. They feed on seed clusters, soft films and the blue-tipped shoots. At night, pollen catches in the fur around their faces. A glider visiting several lights may carry it between plants that have no other easy connection.

Not everything that flies can glide in the open. Some small night-fliers work close to the growth, following its surfaces with rapid, uneven wingbeats. They shelter in the hollow stems and come out to feed when the blue tips open. Their wings are easily wet, so during a condensation they gather beneath overhangs, sometimes in such numbers that a resting cluster looks like a patch of dark bark.

The predators are patient animals, built for holding as much as for pursuit. One kind lies along a cable with its limbs gathered underneath, feeling through its body for the tremor of approaching feet. Its coat is rough and streaked by sap; old individuals may have bits of fibre permanently caught in it. At a junction it can brace against two stems and seize a grazer as it passes. A miss is dangerous for both animals. The predator will usually let go rather than follow prey into a fall, though young, hungry ones sometimes hold too long.

Lower down, scavengers find what remains. The pale many-legged animals live inside the mats of fallen material, where moisture lasts longer than it does on exposed branches. They cut and knead leaves with their mouthparts, carry fragments into the mat and leave behind fine dark waste. When a carcass lands, they gather at the sheltered side first. Larger scavengers may be drawn by the disturbance, but the many-legged ones remain after the bones and hair have been scattered, working into places nothing else can reach.

The people who travel here know which growth will take a cut without dying back, and which should be left alone because too many routes depend on it. They travel with cord, stone blades and containers nested inside one another to save space. Before crossing a cable they may pull on it, listen, and wait for its movement to settle. At uncertain places they fasten a line and send the lightest person across first.

Their shelters serve particular routes rather than fixed boundaries. A family or traveling group may return to the same shelf for years, repairing the roof, clearing roots from the hearth and replacing the stones that hold the walls down. They gather water during the wet currents and store it deep in cork-wrapped joints. They dry strips of grazer meat where the wind can reach them and collect seed, fibres and useful bark. Some keep small bundles of blue shoots near a shelter, both for their light and because the night-fliers they attract can be caught.

Markers are made to be understood by people coming after, including people the maker may never meet. Fresh fibre at a crossing means someone has been there recently, but it does not guarantee the span beyond is sound. A split branch can close a path; several notches may show that it has been closed for a long time. Travelers add to old marks when they can and remove misleading ones. Sometimes there is no safe way to reach a marker that has moved out over a gulf, and it remains there, pointing toward a route that no longer exists.

At the abandoned shelters, the plants have taken hold gradually. Roof bark sends down roots; bindings disappear inside thickening wood. Animals use the dry space beneath the eaves, and people sometimes use it too, if the shelf still feels firm. Someone may clear the old hearth and sleep beside it for a night, then leave in the morning along the worn span, cutting a fresh mark where the old ones have grown shallow.
