The people of the plateau know one another partly by the sounds their shoes make. Felt soles are common in the courts, where a hard heel carries strangely far between the walls. On the elevated tracks, where the wind moves more freely, people wear ridged rubber and call to one another from a distance. Children learn to identify the slow drag of a grazer’s feet, the patter of a shell-creature disturbed in a tray, and the faint change in a machine’s hiss that means a valve is opening.

Most households have a place on more than one level. A family might sleep in rooms above a loading bay and keep its cooking things in a warm chamber below the paving, reached by a stair that passes three inspection hatches. When a connecting passage changes, they move what they can carry and leave a note for anyone who comes looking. Notes are written with the date, the writer’s name, and a description of the route as it was that morning.

The mapkeepers do not try to preserve every version of a district. They work in small groups, walking the avenues and comparing their pencil marks, then rubbing out what no longer helps a traveler. Their oldest maps are kept for other reasons: a vanished court may be the only place where someone remembers meeting a friend, or where a family knows it once lived. Such maps are folded carefully, though the paper is often grey with erasures.

Disagreements over a new bridge can last for years. One person remembers crossing it on the way to school; another remembers watching it arrive on a crane. Both may be right about everything they can check. People learn to ask, gently, which route someone remembers, rather than insisting on the route that was there yesterday. There are still arguments, especially when a change puts a door inside a wall or makes a familiar journey too long for an elderly person to manage.

The grazers are neither quite wild nor wholly kept. They wander among the loading bays and know which joints hold the thickest moss. Their six padded feet spread their weight over the paving, and their felted coats collect pale dust from walls and rails. Some families comb them when the weather is cool enough for the animals to stand still, gathering shed fibers to line shoes or make soft covers for pipes that drip near a bed. A grazer will sometimes follow a person for an entire avenue and then stop, apparently satisfied that they have reached the right building.

The small grey shell-creatures eat the delicate growth that forms around warm cables. Their hinged backs close so neatly that a sleeping group looks like a row of spare fittings. Workers lifting a tray usually tap it first, giving them time to unfold and move away. They are quick when awake, with many fine legs underneath, and can pass through gaps too narrow for a finger.

The white moths feed at the sumps and rest in dense patches beneath bridges. When a new wall appears, people go to look for them, though no one agrees whether the moths find it before anyone else or whether they have always been there once it exists. Their young scrape a film from the undersides of beams. Birds follow them from ventilator to ventilator, stepping with great care over the moving parts.

The fish in the sumps are watched as closely as any instrument. In a shallow pool, their clear bodies show the dark line of what they have eaten. They gather beneath a joint that is beginning to drip and disperse before a sump dries, slipping through narrow drainage channels into neighboring pools. Children sometimes mark the water level beside them, returning through the day to see whether it has risen.

People work in and around the factories, although few claim to understand a whole one. They clean glass, test railings, open and close inspection hatches, and listen for machinery that has fallen out of its usual rhythm. When an object appears at a loading dock, someone records its dimensions. Someone else usually lays a hand on it, checking for warmth.

Then they wait. The object will be gone eventually, and there will be a place that needs finding again. On those mornings, mapkeepers set out early, while moths rest beneath the bridges and the grazers are still lying in the bays. They take pencils, water, and enough food for a court that may have become much wider overnight.
