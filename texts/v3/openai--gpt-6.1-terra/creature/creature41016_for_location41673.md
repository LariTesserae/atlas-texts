The chamber is alive before anything moves through it. The plant is not a lining laid over earth but the whole enclosure: wall turning into ceiling, ceiling into floor, every fold continuous with the next. Its body is young enough that its newest edges are still sharp. It makes more surface than it seems to need, testing leaves as scales, threads, and membranes, then drawing some of them back into itself.

The pale mite lives within that body’s folds. Against the rind it is difficult to distinguish from a lifted edge or a bead just beginning to form. It has room to disappear, though the chamber is small enough to hold in two hands. When a thread stretches across the open space, the mite may venture out onto it, moving slowly while the plant sounds around it with cracks and deep, swelling notes.

It carries at least one piece of the world outside: a grain of stone, small enough for its forelegs to roll, hard enough to open a seam. The mite feeds where it has pressed the grain in. The plant closes afterward, keeping the stone in its tissue. Whether the mite will find it again is unclear; the folds shift, and fresh leaves lift over old ones.

The plant grows, the mite crosses, and the distance between the walls remains one slow crossing wide. New surfaces appear without much warning. A shadow can arrive before the leaf that casts it, and a bead can briefly hold a view from somewhere the mite has never been. The mite does not seem to require an explanation for any of this. It goes on living in the folds, emerging when there is a thread, finding food where it can make an opening.
