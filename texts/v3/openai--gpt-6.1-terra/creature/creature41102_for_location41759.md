The plateau is inhabited by a growth with no clear boundary between one plant and the next. A bundle of stems may draw oil through vessels that begin a great distance away; a root may serve one crown for years, then be incorporated into another as the stands press together. Cutting a trunk does not necessarily separate anything. Its fibers may already have been fastened to the trunks beside it, and the exposed end will harden, sprout, and gradually close.

The tall stems are the most visible part of it, but not the most enduring. They live in the moving surface, where there is room to spread a frond or put out a comb. A young shoot is pale and flexible, with its working fibers laid in parallel. As it thickens, it draws them tight and builds collars around itself. The square trunks result partly from this binding and partly from the pressure of neighboring growth. Few remain upright for long. Their crowns are pulled toward heat, open space, or another stand offering a surface to fasten against.

Their leaves are thick and overlapping. Some gather heat; others form channels that carry hot air across the younger tissues or away from machinery that must not be smothered. The combs collect mineral grit a little at a time. When full, they fold inward, and the grains are crushed between resinous surfaces. What cannot be used becomes part of the hard packing around the roots, or is worked upward and shed from the moving surface.

The growth has many kinds of root. Fine ones enter joints and seams; heavy cables brace drums and haul whole masses of stalk into new positions. Others lie motionless for so long that they become structural members, indistinguishable from the old resin and compressed fiber around them. Even these can be digested from within. The plants recover oil and useful material from their dead parts, leaving pale husks that accumulate under the living layer.

The machinery is kept in service by this same patient expansion. Roots grip an axle where a bearing has worn away. A shoot enters a gap beside a gear and hardens against its teeth; later growth reinforces it until the plant-made tooth carries part of the load. Not every repair preserves the machine in its original form. Some drums now turn within living housings, and some switches move because a band of fiber tightens when heated. The growth depends on the heat and motion below it, while the works depend on being held together by the growth.

The pale shapes at the vents are the plateau’s means of spreading. They form where heat rises steadily, jointed and open enough for the warm air to pass through them. Their grains come loose gradually rather than all at once. Most settle nearby, caught in combs or in folds of leaf. A few lodge in a seam where there is heat, grit, and a little oil, and begin sending fibers into it. A seedling has no need of an open patch of ground; it needs only a place where the existing surface is not yet quite closed.

Individual crowns are continually lost. They are buried when a stand folds over, crushed when a mound settles, or cut off from the vessels that supplied them. Their material remains useful for a time, then is broken down or pressed into the foundation. Far below the surface, growth that once gathered heat may now ring when struck and bear the weight of machinery. Above it, new stems are already taking shape, binding themselves to whatever is warm enough and firm enough to hold them.
