The most visible inhabitants are the leaf-coloured grazers, though even they spend much of the day out of sight. They are plump, soft-backed animals with short legs and mouths broad enough to take a strip from a wall. They feed on the tender growth around doorways and along the undersides of the leaves. Their grazing leaves pale tracks that slowly darken and close. Where many of them pass, the town grows a thicker, sweeter skin.

They move in loose files along the façades, pausing with their feet spread whenever the floor begins to sound its deeper call. A grazer that has wandered too far from the wall presses its belly down and waits. Once the call has ended, it tests the surface with one foot, then another, before committing itself to a direction. The young are slower to learn this. They sometimes set out for an entrance that has become unexpectedly distant and must be fetched by others, who crowd around them and guide them back against the wall.

The pale climbers live higher up, in narrow passages where their jointed feet can grip on every side. They have flattened bodies and long, mobile limbs, and can turn around in spaces scarcely wider than themselves. By day they remain packed into the walls, scraping at the living surface with small hard mouths. At dusk they emerge in streams, climb over the rounded roofs and disappear among the leaves. Some feed there; others are predators, waiting for the winged animals as they come out. From the floor it is difficult to distinguish them until one catches another and the two tumble together down a façade.

The thin larvae occupy the smallest channels. They are almost transparent, with dark contents showing through them, and lie head to tail in such numbers that a cut or opening in the wall may expose a slowly moving braid. They feed on what the larger inhabitants leave behind: loosened tissue, droppings, dead eggs and bodies too small for anything else to bother with. Moisture drawn out by the great call reaches them first. They swell after it, pressing against one another, and their faint rasping joins the louder sounds that travel through the houses.

Beneath the floor, warm-bodied animals sleep curled together in shallow chambers. They have dense, pale fur, sensitive feet and small eyes that are usually closed while they move underground. Their chambers are worn smooth and kept open by use; when a group dies or leaves, the entrances gradually contract. At evening they come out to hunt larvae and fallen grazers. They rarely cross the hollow directly. Instead they follow the grooves, stopping at the damp pockets along the rim to drink. Their shadows often appear there before them: little hunched shapes gathered at water that is otherwise undisturbed.

Predators enter the town by the upper passages or burrow in from the plain. Some are scarcely larger than the animals they hunt; others are large enough to make an entire wall heave as they force their way through it. The inhabitants do not need to see one to flee. A change in the scraping and rattling travels ahead through the crowded passages, and creatures begin pouring from openings far in front of it. The predator may arrive at a chamber to find it newly empty, its walls still trembling.

The winged animals are the most numerous emigrants. They spend their immature lives inside the houses and emerge with folded, damp wings that must be held open against the warm air. In the evening they climb over one another at the entrances, then rise above the rim in dense, wavering streams. Some return before morning, carrying pollen or fragments of food; many are taken beyond the hollow and never return. Their empty cases remain fastened inside the walls until grazers or larvae consume them.

The town itself shelters and feeds them, and is altered by their presence. A frequently used chamber stays spacious; a neglected one slowly fills with new growth. The animals keep passages open with their bodies and score feeding paths into the living walls. The organism closes over injuries, draws water into itself, and sends out its deep call through every room.

To its inhabitants, the noise is part of the place, as constant as the surface underfoot. They find mates within it, follow changes in it, and feel danger moving through it. At times, when the deep call has just ended and every creature is paused against the wall, the hollow may look almost empty. The façades shine with moisture. Then a shadow lengthens across the floor, its owner comes through a puckered doorway, and movement spreads along the street again.
