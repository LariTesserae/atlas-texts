The pale creatures are easiest to see when they are not moving, which is most of the time. Beneath the water their bodies lie so close to the stone that they resemble deposits left by the pool. Each has a small, flattened center and a spread of long jointed limbs, some ending in fine brushes, others in blunt pads that hold it against the rock. The limbs are not all alike, even on the same animal. A broken one grows back slowly and may return shorter or with an extra joint.

Their skin holds water. When mist settles on it, a dark layer beneath the pale surface becomes visible, following the limbs in branching lines. As the mist clears, the outer layer dries and turns opaque again. A creature exposed by a falling pool can remain tucked into a damp hollow for many days, its limbs folded tightly and its breathing too slow to notice.

They feed on films that grow where water emerges from the stone. These films are almost invisible except at the edges, where they gather grains of mineral into a faint brown or greenish line. In the largest seams they can be thick enough to peel away in soft sheets. The creatures draw them in with the brushes at the ends of their limbs, passing the material inward a little at a time. Around a pool, the grazing leaves small clean arcs on the submerged rock.

There is life within the stone as well. In wet passages too narrow for the larger animals, mats of minute organisms take what they need from the water and the minerals it carries. They grow slowly but continuously. Fragments wash out into the pools, and when a seam opens higher on a slope, a new film may appear below it before anything larger arrives.

The creatures seem to find those places through the rock. Their pads are sensitive to vibration, and an animal resting at the edge of a pool will sometimes lift one limb and set it down elsewhere, as if comparing the movement under each foot. A change in the deep hum may send it toward a newly wet seam. The journey can take days: it moves in short stretches, then stops under an overhang or presses itself into a cool channel until moisture gathers again.

A few smaller animals live among the crusts at the pool edges. They are dark, no larger than mineral grains, and spend the dry hours sealed inside clear cases attached to the stone. When droplets reach them, the cases open. The animals creep across the damp film, leaving tracks that disappear as soon as the water settles. The pale creatures take them too, though whether by choice or along with the films is hard to tell.

On rare occasions, two of the larger animals occupy the same pool. They touch limb to limb for a long while, remaining almost still as the water trembles around them. Later, one may leave a cluster of translucent beads in a sheltered seam. Most dry out or wash away. Those that remain open into tiny pale forms, each with only a few limbs at first. They stay near the seam, feeding where the water has fed the film, until they are large enough to cross the exposed stone.

In the quieter places, an animal can sometimes be heard moving: the faint scrape of a pad, a pause, then another scrape. Farther out, the hum covers even that. A pale shape rests under a pool while the rock vibrates beneath it, and around its limbs the thin living film begins, slowly, to grow back.
