The first things to recolonize a cleared drain are the soft, pale threads that live in its stone. They spread toward the warm water seeping through the joints and thicken where the water carries a mineral tang. The root mats take hold among them. Much of the plateau’s growth is fed this way, by partnerships between the great rooted organisms and the minute life drawing sustenance from what rises out of the rock. A fallen trunk can remain warm for years as its inner chambers are emptied and occupied again.

The grazers find these places by smell. They are low animals with blunt, flexible muzzles and teeth that continually grow forward. A group feeding makes a sound like several people filing wood at different speeds. They keep in contact through their feet, tapping as they move. When one finds a hollow beneath the mat, it stops and gives a short series of stamps; the others gather to tear the roof open and feed on the rich growth inside.

Some hollows have inhabitants. A long-backed animal lies curled in the deeper ones, its skin covered with loose folds that take up vibrations from the surrounding roots. It eats grazers, but seldom catches more than one from a group. The first alarm sends the others scattering, and their confusion is often enough to bring a larger hunter.

These larger hunters have narrow heads and fanlike ears that can close against the rain. In ordinary weather they click rapidly, making small movements between each burst to separate the returning sounds. In a storm they hunt differently. They stand beneath the heaviest water, letting it strike their backs, until they feel a grazer running nearby. Then they launch themselves out of the downpour. A successful rush ends with a brief, hard drumming of feet; an unsuccessful one may carry the hunter straight into a pool.

The broad-mouthed pool dwellers are not much troubled by either outcome. They wait on the bottom, mouths angled toward the surface, and engulf what falls near them. Their lips are fringed with sensitive threads that can distinguish a leaf from a kicking animal. In the side chambers of the rivers, where the current turns back on itself, the largest specimens grow so broad that they must fold their fins to pass into the main channel. They can remain there through a flood, wedged against the slow pull of the water, feeding on everything the river has disturbed upstream.

Above the pools, small winged animals roost in folds of bark. Their wings are thick and short, useful for darting between trunks rather than crossing open shelves. They eat the insects that breed in collected water and, in turn, are eaten by almost anything quick enough to seize them. After rain they leave their roosts in great numbers. A person walking beneath them feels the air continually stirred around the head, and hears the tiny knocks of bodies striking leaves and recovering.

The herds are made up of animals called **drummers** by the workers. A drummer stands higher than a cart and carries much of its weight on its forelimbs. Its feet are broad, with a hard central pad surrounded by softer lobes. It can strike a shelf sharply enough for animals a long way off to feel the impact, then stand with its head lowered, waiting for the ground to answer.

Drummers graze on tough stems and strip the rope-bark from fallen trees. They travel by established routes, though those routes change when water opens a new chamber beneath them. The young learn the ground alongside older animals, making clumsy strikes and shifting their feet while the echoes are still returning. A herd testing an unfamiliar shelf can keep it trembling for hours.

The people of the workshops learn to recognize the herds before they meet them. An approaching drummer has a particular rolling step; a herd on the move produces a confusion of impacts with long, heavy pauses between them. Travelers who feel those pauses through a staff will leave the route and wait. A cart driver caught too close loosens the team and lets it turn aside. A frightened animal may run over a cart without noticing it.

The people themselves are broad-shouldered and sure-footed, with long hands whose fingertips are ridged and densely sensitive. Their ears lie close against the skull and can be partly sealed when they work near engines. They use voice freely at short range, but in the workshops many instructions are given through the frames of the machines: a knuckle pressed twice against a rail, a palm held there to acknowledge it. A worker can feel such a signal while both people are wearing hearing protection.

Each workshop has its own rhythms. A new apprentice spends weeks walking the floor, touching housings and pipes, learning how a properly running press passes its motion into the stone. The work becomes precise: measuring a shaft by a gauged collar, finding the start of a thread with a fingernail, reading the raised marks on a cutting tool. Parts intended for another shop carry marks on several faces, so they can be identified even when stacked or wrapped.

People who travel between the buildings carry a weighted staff, a coil of line, and food sealed in waxed cloth. They know the route posts by the arrangement of their notches. At a fork they may stop to feel the old cuts in the wood, then strike the ground and compare its answer with what they remember. A newly fallen tree or a flooded hollow can make a familiar stretch difficult for days.

Crews maintain bells at dangerous crossings, but a bell is not always rung by a person. Some are hung low enough that rising water sets them moving. Others are fixed where wind can reach them only when it comes from the direction of an exposed shelf. Their tones are kept distinct, and travelers learn them along with the feel of the ground.

At the buildings, animals grow accustomed to the warmth. Small grazers shelter under pipework and rasp insulation from cable joints; winged insect-eaters crowd the spaces beneath roof edges. A few pool dwellers have learned to wait at drain mouths for scraps washed out of kitchens. The crews put screens across the drains, then lift and clean them after every storm.

When a machine stops unexpectedly, the silence is felt first by the person nearest it. Others pause at their stations, hands resting on metal. For a moment the building holds the remaining motions—the slowing shaft, water still driving against the turbine, a loose fitting tapping as it settles. Then someone gives a signal on the rail, and the room begins to move around the fault.
