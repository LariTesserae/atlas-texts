The basin’s most numerous inhabitants are the films inside the pipes. They are almost invisible where the water runs quickly, but in the warm bends they grow thick enough to lift in translucent shreds. They feed on what the water brings and on one another. When a pump starts, some are torn free and carried out across the plates, where they collect in bolt recesses and along the roots of the sedge. Most freeze or dry there. A little is washed back into the channels.

The crustaceans follow the films. Their pale bodies are jointed and flattened, well suited to the narrow spaces beneath pipe collars. They have no use for eyes in the dark runs behind the machinery, but their antennae are busy, feeling the current and the surfaces ahead. They graze with small, steady movements, often holding themselves against the flow with their rear limbs.

A pump surge can carry them a long way. Those stranded on the grating fold inward, protecting their softer parts while frost gathers around them. Some wait through several cycles of the press before water reaches them again. Others are caught where no channel runs; beneath certain gratings there are little accumulations of their empty shells.

The sedge holds the few places where grit has stayed put. It grows in tufts rather than a continuous stand, each clump braced against a bolt or a raised seam. The oldest leaves are torn at their ends and lie along the direction of the current. New ones push up between them, stiff at first, then bend as the water finds them. Fine threads from the pipe films cling to their bases, and bits of broken shell lodge among the roots.

The moss has a different arrangement with the machinery. It grows above the waterline on pipes that remain warm when the surrounding metal is rimed. Its dark cushions are sparse and uneven, interrupted by joints, brackets and patches where the pipe becomes too hot. During the fog intervals they swell and shine; afterward their edges draw tight against the metal. The little white growths under the staircase are more sheltered. They have spread through the insulation fibers in a delicate mat, fastening the loose strands together and raising tiny pale heads wherever moisture settles.

Moths spend much of their lives inside the lamp housings. They press into the seams behind the reflectors, where the glass gives off a little heat and the moving air is less violent. When they emerge, they fly close to the lamps, passing in and out of the yellow beams. Some settle on the warm wall brackets. Others descend toward the machinery and return with dust on their wings, if they return at all.

Workers cross the basin in insulated coats, following routes they know between channels and machinery. They notice the living things chiefly when these get in the way: sedge pulled from a drain, moss scraped off a pipe joint, moths shaken from a lamp before its glass is cleaned. A worker lowering a boot into the water may see crustaceans scatter from a pipe mouth. By the next shift, they have gathered there again.

At the unfinished edge, welding light reaches creatures that have never had light before. The fresh plates arrive bare, with their sharp corners and casting marks intact. Water is already moving over them, carrying grit, scraps of film and occasionally a seed or a small folded animal. Most of what arrives is swept onward into the receiving chamber. Some catches at a seam, and remains there when the water falls.
