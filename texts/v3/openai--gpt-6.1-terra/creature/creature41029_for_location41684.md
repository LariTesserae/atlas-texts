The birds are black only from a distance. Their feathers have the dull color of wet iron, with a pale seam along each shaft. They are small enough for the fern stems to hold them, though their feet are large and many-jointed, wrapped around the stems without bending them. Their throats are open at different widths. The notes held in the air are as distinct as the birds themselves: a clear high thread, a rough interval beneath it, and, close to the ground, something too low to hear except through the soles of the feet.

The white insects have broad wings with no visible veins. Each is turned slightly toward its neighbors, so that their arrangement has the look of a surface folded into the air. Between them there is always room for another. When the eye finds one that seemed not to be there before, its place among the others is already exact.

Under the leaves are animals like small, soft brushes, their legs gripping the leaf along both sides of a central body. Their backs bear minute beads of water, each bead as steady as the larger drops on the fern tips. Some have long, translucent mouthparts laid against the green flesh. Others hold their mouths open, showing a circle of teeth too fine to distinguish individually.

The fish are easiest to see by what they contain. A dark line runs the length of each one; a few have a pearl-colored knot behind the head, and some show the faint outline of a smaller fish inside. Their fins are spread against the imagined current. In certain pools they face the rock; in others they face the roots of the trees, where pale animals lie among the liverwort with their eyes just above the water.

The eyes in the moss do not all belong to the same kind of creature. Some are set on stalks no thicker than hairs. Some are large and flat, with tiny stones caught around their rims. One pair belongs to an animal whose body is a cushion of moss itself, complete with a fern growing out of its back. Beneath a nearby stone is a broad, breathing thing the color of the stone’s underside, its ribs visible through skin stretched into a shallow curve. The curve holds the full shape of an indrawn breath.

Among the roots there are larger inhabitants, though their boundaries are difficult to follow. A pale flank appears between two pools; farther away, beyond a tree, a folded ear rises from the moss. A dark muzzle rests against the edge of a stone, its nostrils open to the cold air. The animals may lie separately, with their bodies concealed under the ground, or they may be joined beneath it. The moss is dense around each visible part and undisturbed.

Their eyes are open. Seen close, each holds a small image of the hollow: the pale trees, the black rim, and the white insects spaced between them. In one eye there is also a figure standing at the edge, looking in. The figure is small, and the longer it is watched, the more animals can be made out around its feet.
