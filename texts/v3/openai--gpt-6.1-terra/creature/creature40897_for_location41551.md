Under the stones live the smallest and most numerous inhabitants. They form dark, thin films in the hollows that the current cannot scour clean. Some feed on dissolved material in the water; others live on the remains of their neighbors. When a stone shifts, part of the film may be exposed and stripped away. The cells left beneath it spread slowly over the newly sheltered surface.

The pale tufts above them are colonies of tiny animals. Each has a soft body fixed inside a tough little tube, with a crown of feeding limbs that opens into the current. The crowns bend in the flow and catch particles too small to settle. When silt comes in a dense sheet, they fold shut. A colony can remain closed for a long time, though it must eventually open again to feed. On the sheltered side of a stone, several colonies may grow close enough that their feeding crowns touch. On the exposed side there may be only one, flattened against the rock.

The translucent scavenger finds them by touch and by traces carried in the water. It has many jointed legs, a narrow body, and mouthparts that can pick at a carcass or scrape soft growth from a surface. Its gut sometimes shows through its skin as a dark line. It does not travel quickly. When the current strengthens, it grips the stone and waits, its legs spread wide. If the stone offers no shelter, it presses itself into a crack and lets the sediment pass over it.

Out on the softer floor, the buried animal lives in a lined tube. Its feathery appendage is divided into fine branches, each coated with mucus. Food and silt collect there together; the animal draws the branches into its burrow and sorts what it has caught. Rejected grains come back out in small bound packets that are heavier than loose silt. For a little while, these mark the mouth of the tube. Then a current rolls them away.

There are other burrowers whose openings are harder to find. Some swallow sediment and pass it through their bodies, taking what nourishment they can from it. Some extend a narrow snout into neighboring tunnels. They may go months without meeting another animal large enough to eat. Their young drift in the water, carried over much of the basin before settling wherever the current lets them.

The larger animal that passes above the floor has a long, soft body and broad fins that move with little apparent effort. It follows the faint scent of dead matter. Sometimes it arrives at a carcass already crowded with scavengers; sometimes it finds only a scatter of bones and disturbed mud. It feeds where it can, then moves on, stirring a low cloud behind it.

When the cloud reaches the stones, the feeding crowns close. The scavenger braces. The feathery appendage vanishes into its tube. Gradually the cloud thins, and the crowns open into the water again.
