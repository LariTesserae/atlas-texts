The basin belongs most visibly to the shell-creatures. They cover the submerged stone so densely that a bare patch may be no larger than a hand. Each holds fast with a muscular foot and draws water through a narrow opening, taking what it can from the cells and particles carried in the seepage.

Their shells are pale inside and dark outside, stained by the water and scoured where grit moves over them. They close against shadows, sudden pressure, and the passage of other animals. One closing seems to disturb its neighbors, until the response runs across a pool and into the next. The great rolling clatter is made by creatures that hardly travel at all.

Smaller grazers work over the rock between them. They are difficult to distinguish from the wet grit until they move, scraping the bronze films of living cells from the stone. Where many have fed, their paths cross in pale tangles. The films return slowly, thickest around the cracks where fresh water wells up. In a basin without plants, these inconspicuous cells are an important source of food, although the seepage also carries material in from beyond the places where it appears.

The pale-backed hunters take the shell-creatures. Their many legs find purchase on submerged stone, and their flattened bodies pass through water scarcely deeper than they are. A hunter works at the edge of a shell until its grip gives way, then carries it to a gravel bar. The feeding places are conspicuous: white fragments lie among the dark stones, some old and rounded, others sharp enough to catch the weak light.

In the deeper pools live the long blind swimmers. They hunt the grazers, moving through water clouded with pale grit and turning so sharply that their tails strike the surface. They are seldom visible whole. A length of body appears beneath the gray reflection, followed by a heavy slap, and then the pool looks empty again.

The people who built the shelters are harder to place. Their fitted stones and worn tools show that they spent time here, perhaps returning often enough to repair a roof or deepen a groove in the wall. The pebble-lined pits might have held food, water, or something else altogether. Some shelters are still intact, but that does not say when anyone last used them. If people come back, they arrive in a place already crowded with lives: grazers under the water, hunters among the shells, and the shells themselves carrying news of every passing shadow from pool to pool.
