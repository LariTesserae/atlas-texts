At the edge of a colony, where the new fronds are still low enough to arch over one another, the grazers live in passages pressed through the growth. A passage may serve generations. Its floor is packed with plant powder and polished grains, and its roof is scored by the slow upward movement of the fronds.

A grazer is pale all over, even in the joints of its legs. It has six of these, each with several bends, and walks with its body held close to the ground. The body is broad and softly ridged, protected by overlapping plates that can lift a little when it breathes. From the front extends a short, blunt snout. Two dark sensory patches sit on either side, shaded by rims of hard skin; in the open, they catch movement against the bright ground more readily than they resolve a shape.

The grazer eats the powder of fallen fronds. It pushes its snout into a drift and draws the fine material through a row of combs, swallowing the richer fragments and leaving behind little fans of clean mineral grains. The animals do not strip living plants. Their slow feeding clears space beneath the colony, and their droppings, dark with concentrated oils and salts, give young roots places to take hold.

Most remain under cover through the brightest part of the day, though not because of heat. Light reflected from the flats makes them conspicuous to anything looking down on them. They emerge along the margins when a drift has accumulated outside, or when the dust is moving and their outlines are less distinct. A crossing animal may stop often, its feet braced wide, and hold itself so motionless that powder gathers along the upper edges of its plates.

They obtain moisture from their food and lose very little. In the deepest passages, where the air is cooler and carries the scent of old oils, they leave their young in shallow hollows. The young hatch with flexible coverings and stay beneath the colony for a long time. At first they feed on the finest powder their elders have loosened; later, they learn to work apart the brittle ribs of fallen fronds.

Other small lives are much more numerous. In the folds of living blades are soft, nearly transparent creatures no larger than grains of grit. They shelter where the wax is thinnest and feed on traces of oil that seep through it. When a frond is burdened by a descending veil, they move inward, deep into its joints. Some spend their entire lives on one plant; others travel on fragments that break away, carried across the flats in the lower currents of dust.

There are also colonies within the colonies: films of minute organisms growing on the undersides of fallen fronds, where the light is dimmer and dust settles slowly. They soften the dead tissue before it bleaches and crumbles. If a fallen crown is lifted, its upper side may be white and dry while underneath it is streaked amber, grey and faint blue. Grazers seek out these softened places, and their feet carry the films from one fallen frond to another.

The larger animal comes rarely enough that the fringes may grow over its last tracks before it returns. It is taller than several grazers standing one upon another, with a narrow body carried between four long, widely spaced legs. Each foot spreads under its weight, pressing deep into loose deposits but leaving a shallower mark on stone. Its skin is mineral-grey, ridged and dust-coated. At rest beside an old fallen frond it is difficult to distinguish from the broken crown.

It follows the grazers, though it cannot pursue them far into the dense growth. Along the colony’s edge it waits, sometimes for days, with its head lowered toward a passage. It senses the faint contact of feet through the ground. When a grazer comes out, the larger animal can move abruptly, covering a short distance in a few long steps. If the grazer reaches the fronds first, the pursuit ends; the larger animal stands outside, listening, and eventually moves on.

It feeds infrequently. Between visits to colonies it crosses the bare highland, carrying reserves in thick pads along its flanks. Dust gathers on those pads, and small travelling organisms shelter in the creases. Some are left behind each time the animal rests. It may take them farther than wind could carry a fragment of plant.

After a great veil descends, nearly everything withdraws. Grazers seal themselves into inner passages with loose powder. The larger animals lower their bodies in the lee of ridges, where their backs are gradually buried. Within the colonies, fronds bend until their tips touch the ground, enclosing the passages in dense green chambers.

When the air clears, the grazers begin digging outward. The larger animals rise slowly, leaving hollows that persist until the next moving sheet of grit fills them. The plants take longer. For days their heavy fronds lift and separate, spilling fine streams from their joints. Beneath them, in the newly opened ground, there is fresh powder to eat and room for young roots to spread.
