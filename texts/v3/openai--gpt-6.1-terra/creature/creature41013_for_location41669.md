The basin’s inhabitants are easiest to notice late in the day, when the glare lessens and the water separates into pools and threads. They appear first as movements of shadow: a darkening that travels uphill, pauses at a seam in the rock, and then draws together with others. What gathers there is not quite a body. Its parts may lie several paces apart, connected by a wet groove so fine it is difficult to see.

They live in the changing distances between the basin’s surfaces. A pool can hold a cliff close to it even when the cliff is far away, and one of these beings can occupy both places for a while. Its shadow might collect under the cliff as its other end moves across the pool. When the water dries, the connection narrows. The shadow retreats into a crevice or follows a runnel toward another wet place.

They do not control every reflection, and much of the basin’s movement has nothing to do with them. Rain stirs pools, mist rises, and shadows from the shelters turn with the sun. The inhabitants can be distinguished by their persistence. They return to the same seams after showers; they gather where a trickle has gone dry, remaining there until water comes again. Sometimes several collect around a dish in the stone and hold its reflected ridge steady while the water is shaken by falling drops.

The rings were made for steadiness of another kind. Inside them, distance stays where a person would expect it to be, and the fins remain in place. An inhabitant entering a ring cannot spread as far as it can outside, but neither can a sudden change in the water pull its parts away from one another. When rain is hard and the pools are full of competing reflections, several may crowd into a ring, their shadows layered so closely that only an occasional movement at the edge reveals how many are there.

They have ways of working on stone, though not quickly. A hammerstone set on a wet shelf can be made to shift as the reflected shelf draws closer; a wedge can be held in a crack while rainwater fills the crack behind it. Much of their building consists of waiting for a favorable shower and then taking advantage of a movement too small to notice until a slab has settled into a new position. The roofless shelter has been repaired this way more than once. Its hammerstone rests where it was last left.

The shelter’s shadow is ordinary, but it is useful. As the sun moves, its edge passes over a row of shallow hollows in the floor. The inhabitants gather in one hollow, then another, following the shade while thin films of water persist there. They sometimes leave small chips of stone in the hollows, perhaps to deepen them, perhaps to mark which held water longest.

After a dry interval, an inhabitant may become difficult to find. A faint shadow remains in a fissure, or a reflection lingers in one of the deeper dishes. The others do not always wait beside it. They move through the basin as water allows, returning when rain reaches that part of the stone. Then, among the renewed dripping, a shadow may lengthen from the fissure and begin to climb.
