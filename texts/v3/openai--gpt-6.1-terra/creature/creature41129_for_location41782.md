The inhabitants are thin colonies growing inside the sheets of pressed fiber. Most begin as a discoloration: a pale branching at one edge, easily mistaken for the mark left by a sheet beneath it. As they grow, they bind the fibers together with a transparent film. Held to a lamp, an inhabited sheet shows a fine network of wet lines, some straight, some gathered into knots.

They are not plants, though they depend on what the plants shed. Nor do they resemble the animals once recorded in the swollen ledgers. They have no organs that can be lifted from a sheet and examined separately. A colony can lose half its material to rot or a careless tear and continue in the other half, sometimes with an altered manner of answering its neighbors.

The sheets beneath the galleries are not simply stock awaiting use. The upper sheets receive falling moisture and transmit its weight to those below; the lower sheets keep the stack damp through the long intervals between drops. A stack that has stood together for years becomes difficult to separate without distress. Its members have grown into the pressure of one another. Where their edges meet, they exchange water, salts, and minute fragments of the branching film.

They also exchange accounts of the hollow. The pumping station’s thud travels through the galleries and into the stacks, and each colony feels a slightly different version of it. A loose railing makes one sheet tremble after its neighbors have settled. A blocked drain changes the sound coming up through a particular wall. By comparing these disturbances, the colonies know where water is accumulating and which parts of the settlement have begun to fail.

They speak most readily through contact. A change in tension passes from one wet edge to another, too slight to see except perhaps as a darkening along the fibers. The older colonies are slow to answer and capable of sustaining a pattern for days. Young ones interrupt. They have not yet learned which variations in the engines matter and which are merely the engines being themselves.

The turning belts and rollers make new sheets continuously, but not every sheet becomes inhabited. Some are placed beneath established stacks, where a little of the living film may enter them. Others remain blank for years. A few develop a colony at a folded corner, far from any stack, by means no one there has determined. These are watched with particular interest. They sometimes answer familiar patterns in unfamiliar ways.

The colonies can influence the settlement through small movements, especially where several sheets have been fitted together. They swell against a catch or dry unevenly around a lever’s end. By such gradual means, a grille can be shifted, a feed opened, or a sheet released onto a moving belt. The machinery supplies the force; the inhabitants have learned where the smallest pressure will direct it. A repair may take them weeks, and some failures are beyond them. There are rooms they have lost because a door settled in its frame and could no longer be opened by any pressure they could bring to bear.

They do not read the clock as an hour. Its moving hands are an announcement that water has reached a height it may not reach again for months. The advance is felt throughout the square: a change in the cistern’s weight, a strain in the mounting, a tremor as the hands move together. Colonies nearest the square pass the news outward. Those in remote rooms may receive it long after the hands have stopped.

Around the reservoir lid, the clear ring is kept clear for the draft. Fine living films extend into the gap from beneath the iron, where condensation gathers. A drop caught there briefly touches several films before it falls, carrying traces from one to another. This is the quickest meeting place in the hollow. Colonies that cannot share a stack may still come into contact through the suspended water.

Not all meetings are peaceful. Some colonies take up more water than they give back; some incorporate fragments of another without returning a recognizable answer. Afterward, the aggrieved stack may close its edges and refuse exchange for a season. One sheet near the pumping station has remained apart for so long that its former companions have grown around the space it left. It still receives news through the floor.

When a colony dies, its branching lines remain visible for a while, though they no longer respond to pressure or moisture. The sheet is usually left among its neighbors until the film begins to break down. Then it may be fed back toward the rollers. What returns is only fiber, water, and whatever small things have persisted within them. A new colony growing there will sometimes repeat a pattern the old one used, but never long enough for the others to agree on what it means.
