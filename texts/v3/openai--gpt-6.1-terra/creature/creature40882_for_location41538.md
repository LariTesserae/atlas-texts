The broad animals are called baskers only because they lie so still. There is little warmth in the air for them to take. Most of their heat comes from below, where their bellies rest over the seams in the rock, and from the slow, forceful work of drawing water through themselves.

Their backs are not smooth at close range. The skin is folded into low ridges, and each ridge bears a scatter of pale, hard nodules. Ice grows over them while they rest, joining one animal to the next in thin bridges. The bridges break with soft, frequent clicks as the animals breathe. Around each mouth the cartilage rings can spread or close, changing the width of the opening. When the water is rich with suspended grains, the rings loosen and the dark mouths gape. Later they tighten, and the water comes out warmer and almost clear.

The smallest baskers are nearly colorless. They gather above the adults’ discharge, where the water is warmest, turning their transparent jaws toward the rising clouds. A plume may carry hundreds of them. They drift apart when it thins, then cluster again at another opening, their tiny pumping movements too quick to hear. Some settle in the folds of an adult’s back and remain there as the skin darkens and thickens around them. Others are swept away beneath the ice. A few return much larger, pushing up between their elders until a place opens.

The animals under the ice are longer and paler than those above. Their jointed limbs fold close to their bodies as they travel, then brace suddenly against stone or ice when they find prey. Their revolving mouths can grip the tough skin of a basker even in the moving water. They do not always drag an animal down at once. Sometimes one holds fast beneath it for a long time, turning slowly, while the basker continues to pump. The surrounding animals close around both of them, and the struggle can be seen only in the shifting of their backs.

Pieces left after a feeding do not remain recognizable for long. The smallest baskers collect at the torn edges, and finer creatures, visible only as a gray movement in the water, settle on exposed flesh. Even the cartilage rings are gradually reduced. Their fragments lodge in seams in the ice or sink into the metallic drifts, where passing bodies stir them up again.

There are quieter inhabitants in the mineral crust. Under a flake, threads no thicker than scratches run through the damp rock. They are brittle when exposed to air, but in water they bend and slowly branch. The grains that gather around them darken from silver to rust. When the crust breaks, the loose threads can be carried into a pool and caught by the baskers’ mouths; their branching continues inside the folds of the pumping chambers.

A bank of baskers begins its great pulse with one animal changing its rhythm. Its neighbor’s back dips into the space it leaves, then rises on the next discharge. The movement passes from body to body, gaining force. Animals in distant pools take it up even when no connecting water can be seen. The smaller ones retreat into the folds or press against the rock until it passes.

The pulse can bring the hunters to the surface. It can also reveal where water has gone: a place that looked solid a moment before may begin to heave with submerged backs. The animals make use of such openings without haste. They slide through and emerge elsewhere, among neighbors whose marks and injuries are different. A basker with a white notch in its mouth ring may be seen on one side of a pool, then heard pumping behind the observer before it has disappeared from view.

When the cloud comes down, the smallest creatures rise into it with the warm mist. For a while their jaws can be seen opening and closing in droplets held above the ice. Some fall back when the water grows heavy. Others remain suspended in thin sheets after the vapor has moved away, making faint specks across a path that was empty moments before. Beneath them the crowded backs continue to rise and settle. When the cloud lifts, they are already arranged along the new edges of the pools, and the grinding under the ice has resumed wherever there is room to move.
