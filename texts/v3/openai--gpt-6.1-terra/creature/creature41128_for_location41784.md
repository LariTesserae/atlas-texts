The most numerous inhabitants are the termites. They spend nearly all their lives inside the earth and the walls they have made from it, moving through passages just wide enough for their bodies. Workers bring in bits of dry stem and leaf, feed nestmates and tend the young. Others repair breaks in the galleries, pressing fresh, damp pellets into place. Near the surface, soldiers with enlarged heads and jaws wait where a passage has been opened.

Each tower shelters a colony and bears the marks of generations of work. Some chambers hold food and brood; others serve as routes between the nest and the scattered dead vegetation beneath the scrub. The outer clay dries hard in the sun, while deeper chambers retain moisture. The termites keep much of their activity hidden from the hot, moving air, although a freshly sealed patch of wall may betray where they have been.

The plants are inhabitants too, and much of the plain gathers around them. Woody shrubs hold their small leaves close to their stems, exposing little soft surface to the wind. Low mats spread between them, catching dust and fallen fragments. As soil accumulates, new shoots grow through it; old branches disappear into the hummocks at their bases. Beneath a thick patch, the ground is cooler and more protected than it is a short distance away in the open.

Beetles use the stems as ladders, and other small invertebrates shelter in the litter collected underneath. Lizards lie close to the towers, pale against the clay, watching for movement. They make brief dashes into the sunlight to seize insects, then return to the narrow shade. When the wind rises, they can vanish into a crack at a tower’s foot or beneath a mat of foliage.

The birds are easier to see by their movement than by their colors. They travel loosely from shrub to shrub, scratching and pecking where the ground is exposed, then slipping back into cover. One may lift a fallen stem while another investigates the insects beneath it. Their calls carry briefly above the dry rustle of the plants; when something larger approaches, the group may become silent and scatter.

The hunter moving through the scrub need not be large to unsettle them. A small carnivore, a snake, or a bird working low over the plants can make the animals beneath it freeze. Often the only sign is a widening tremor in the foliage, followed by a lizard’s sudden sprint or a burst of wings.

After dark, the surface cools and some of the life kept out of sight during the afternoon comes farther into the open. Insects cross the bare spaces between hummocks. Animals follow their tracks through the fine dust, leaving prints that may last until morning, when the wind begins to fill them. Underneath, in the close passages, the termites continue carrying, feeding and building.
