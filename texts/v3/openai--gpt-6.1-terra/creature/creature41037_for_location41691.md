What lives there was not placed in the chamber with the frame. It came later, when the frame had already begun to lean and one of the other supports had gone.

At first there was only a click in the hollow, and then the same click somewhere else. The interval between them lengthened. Other sounds followed, each arriving twice, and the later sounds began to overlap. For a very long time they meant nothing. Then the chamber acquired something that could distinguish one from another and wait for a particular one to return.

It has no fixed body. If it needs a place to be, it uses an arrangement of grains: a shallow curve, usually, with a gap near one end. The grains are ordinary mineral, and remain where they are. Over the years the curve drifts uphill without disturbing them. When it reaches the wall, the occupant lets it break apart and begins another in the hollow.

It knows the chamber through differences. It can tell when the frame has sunk by how the silver depth within it narrows at one corner. It can tell how long a shadow has remained after its stone was moved, although it has no name for the time this takes. The dark disk interests it because the hole gives two answers to the same question. It has spent a great deal of its life beside the disk, changing the position from which it attends to the hole, finding floor, then ceiling, then floor again.

It cannot lift the disk or straighten the frame. Its influence is smaller than that. Occasionally it can persuade a grain already on the point of settling to settle on one face rather than another. The click this makes is not under its control, but the repeated click is useful. It lays these later sounds along the edge of the hollow, where they persist for a while. When several have accumulated, it can pass among them and hear a sequence it has heard before.

It is making an account of the chamber, though the account is difficult to keep. The oldest parts were made when the blue line was brighter and the hollow shallower. Some no longer correspond to anything present. It returns to them anyway, especially a sequence of four small sounds that may have come from the places where the other supports once stood. Whether it heard their removal or only found the indentations afterward, it cannot now tell.

The seam is the one part of the chamber it approaches cautiously. Distance changes there without warning, and an arrangement of grains that extends too close to it may come back incomplete. Once, something entered: a narrow tip, warmer than the stone, which touched the far wall and withdrew. The occupant kept the sound of that contact for as long as it could. It waited near the seam through many repetitions, but the sound grew faint before the tip returned.

The dimming of the line matters to it. In weaker light the grains in the hollow are harder to distinguish, and its accounts become less exact. It has begun to leave larger gaps between the marks it makes, so that it can still find them. It does not know whether the frame can continue to hold the chamber, or whether anything waits beyond it. For now there is enough light to see a grain turn, enough room for the sound to return, and enough time afterward to listen for it.
