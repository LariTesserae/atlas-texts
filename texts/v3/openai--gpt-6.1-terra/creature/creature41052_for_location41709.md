The largest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the valley itself. They live beneath its surface, where the roots form loose chambers among the pale wooden ribs. From above, a chamber may show only as a slight fullness in the green or a line of leaves that trembles when there is no wind.

Its occupant is a broad, many-lobed animal called a *mender* by anyone who has watched one work. It has no hard shell or bones. Its upper surface is covered with short, velvety tendrils, usually the same color as the floor around it; its underside is pale and deeply folded. A mender moves by fastening one fold of its body to the roots and drawing the others after it. The movement is slow enough that someone standing still might feel it as a gradual change in the ground’s firmness.

Menders eat the parts of the valley that are ready to come away. They follow the faint chemical traces of a loosening root or an old leaf, take it into their bodies, and leave a space no wider than the plants around it can close. In places where growth has become too crowded, they thin the roots. Where a channel wall begins to sag, they settle against it, holding it up for days while new stems grow through their tendrils and take the strain.

A mender can live with stems passing through its body. When it moves on, it draws itself carefully free, leaving behind a little sleeve of nutritious tissue. The young roots spread into that material readily. Some of the neatest seams in the valley have been made this way, though the menders leave no marks that can be identified for long.

The pale gliders in the crowns depend on the menders without ever meeting them. They feed on soft seeds and on the insects that gather around the upper leaves. When seeds are plentiful, a glider carries more than it can eat in pockets beside its cheeks, storing them in folds of the crown. Forgotten seeds sometimes germinate there. Their roots descend through the column, and when they reach the floor, a mender may make room for them.

The gliders spend much of the day pressed flat against the broad leaves, their membranes folded into fine, translucent pleats. A passing shadow sends them to the sheltered side of a crown; a change in the water’s note may bring them out again. Their large ears are not much use for hearing calls across the valley, but they are sensitive to vibrations in the stems. Gliders resting on the same column communicate by tapping with their hind feet. A rapid series of taps can empty a crown before a gliding predator reaches it.

The patient insects beneath the pool leaves are smaller than a fingernail. They hold themselves in place with hooked feet and drink from the damp undersides of the leaves. Droplets collect on their backs because each has a shallow, polished hollow there. When the hollow fills, the insect tilts, letting the droplet fall into the pool. Its young wait at the water’s edge for these falls: each impact briefly breaks the surface film, allowing them to reach the air before they sink beneath it again.

There are also creatures almost never seen whole. Fine white grazers move through the hollow stems, eating the films that would otherwise narrow them. They can flatten themselves against the inner wall when water rises, then unfold and continue upward when it falls. Their eggs adhere in tiny rings just above the usual waterline. When a channel changes course, the eggs dry; months later, if water returns, they open.

At night the gliders settle, and the insects draw their legs close beneath them. The menders remain awake. In the darkness under the floor, they pass one another by touch, yielding where roots are thick and gathering around places that need support. Above them, water continues through the hollow stems. A leaf gives way; a new one opens over the small space it leaves. By morning the surface looks much as it did before, although a great many things have eaten, moved, grown, and made room for one another.
