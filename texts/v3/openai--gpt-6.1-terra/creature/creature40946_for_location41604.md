The largest animals are broad through the shoulders and long enough that several seams pass beneath them. Their hides are fitted in overlapping plates, pale above and dark at the edges where the water reaches them. Each has a narrow head, a blunt muzzle and two small eyes set high enough to remain clear when it drinks. Beneath the ribs, valves open in sequence: one side, then the other. The air they release is warm and faintly mineral.

They stand close, but their bodies do not press together. A hand could pass between one flank and the next, following the same gap from the shoulder to the root of the tail. When one lowers its head, the animal beside it makes a small adjustment of its feet. Farther along, others adjust in turn, until the movement has traveled beyond sight and the first animal is drinking.

The creatures in the tile joints are easier to miss. They are no larger than fingernails, with soft-looking bodies held beneath clear hinged lids. They open into the shallow flow and close again over a bead of the film. Through the lids, tiny structures can be seen turning, folding and unfolding around what they have taken in. A closed lid has almost the same sheen as the wet ceramic; an open one catches the light at its rim. Across a broad stretch of plain they open and close in shifting bands, following something in the water too slight to see.

The workers on the basin walls have no room for such concealment. Their bodies are flattened against the lining, and their mouths are wide ridges that work continuously from side to side. They progress in narrow lanes, each leaving a clean strip behind it. At a rivet they lift just enough to pass over its head, then settle back against the metal. Their waste trails downward as a pale thread and vanishes through the drainage grilles.

Below the plates, the pipe swimmers have thin bodies that flex from end to end. Their skin is translucent around the throat, where the clear film passes through a little chamber before disappearing into the body. They have no visible eyes. Folds along their sides respond to changes in pressure, and at each junction they turn into the current that reaches them. In an inspection window a swimmer appears, crosses the circle of light and is gone. Water continues past the glass; then another swimmer arrives.

The winged animals above are built on a larger, lighter plan. Their wings are stretched over jointed frames, with fine reinforcing lines visible where the light passes through. At rest they fold them carefully along their backs. Their feet close around the crossbeams in paired grips, and their heads hang forward, watching the wet surfaces below—or facing them, at least, with eyes too dark to read.

When a rank lifts, each animal opens its wings into the space made by its neighbor. They rise only a little above the beam before moving along to the next one. For a moment their shadows cross the towers and the crowded shallows. Then the feet take hold, the wings fold, and the animals stand as before, one beam farther on.

All of them are warm. The small creatures keep their warmth under their lids; the swimmers carry theirs through the pipework; the larger animals give it slowly to the water around their legs. At a basin edge, a plated animal holds its muzzle just above the surface. A drop lengthens from its chin and falls. It closes its valves briefly, then opens them again, and another drop begins to gather.
