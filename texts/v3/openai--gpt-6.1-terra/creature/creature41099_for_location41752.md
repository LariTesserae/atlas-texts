The grazers are called shinglebacks by the people who walk among them. Each is about as broad as two hands, with a low, segmented body and legs set far enough to either side that it can cling to two overlapping leaves at once. Its back bears a row of dull plates, green when young and brown in age. From above, a herd looks almost motionless until the light catches thousands of legs moving beneath it.

A shingleback eats the wax and the soft tissue immediately below it, leaving the tougher leaf veins intact. It cannot bite through a healthy stem. When a herd reaches a stand of young growth, the animals at the front scrape until their mouths are clogged, then climb over the backs of those behind them. As the eaten leaves bruise and brighten, the herd becomes visible as a broad patch of amber moving across the floor.

They lay their eggs in the white fur on the undersides of leaves. The hooks hold the eggs even when a leaf turns or falls. Newly hatched shinglebacks are nearly transparent and feed at first on the wax that collects around the hooks. Many are eaten there, before they are large enough to join a herd.

The things that eat them are often mistaken for leaves themselves. They are thin, jointed hunters, folded along a central ridge, with bodies the gray-green color of leaf undersides. A hunter waits beneath an occupied plate, feeling the vibration of legs above. It can thrust a narrow mouth through the leaf and draw a grazer down by the softness of its belly. Where hunters have fed for several days, the plates become stippled with bright wounds, each sealed under a bead of resin.

The small winged animals that rise from the curtains include dozens of kinds. Some eat the powder released by broken stalks; others hunt in the clouds that form when the curtains shake. One pale kind spends most of its life without flying at all. It crawls among the folds with its wings held close and drinks the droplets that condense where a roof seam admits cool air. When the chamber heaves, it takes flight with the rest. Its wings are broad and weak, and many individuals are carried into the rising columns and scattered far from the curtain where they grew.

Among the most conspicuous flyers are the clappers, whose hard wing cases make the dry rattling sound heard throughout the chamber. They gather around fresh breaks in stems. A clapper works its head beneath the forming skin and feeds on the oily sap until the wound closes too tightly to admit it. Several together can keep a small wound open for hours. They cannot, however, prevent a stem from growing: they are often found caught in the healed surface, their wing cases still visible beneath a clear amber layer.

The great blind animals under the floor are called burrowers, although they seldom touch stone. They travel through the packed stems, parting them with a blunt head and drawing themselves forward with muscular folds along the body. Their skin is covered in short, dense fibers that lie flat in the direction of travel and catch when they back up. This makes them slow to retreat. A burrower that meets another in a narrow place may have to force its way upward, bursting through the leaves with its head and shoulders before turning aside.

They feed on the swollen bases of shoots, where mineral-rich sap gathers before rising into new growth. A feeding burrower leaves a hollow that fills quickly with broken leaves, small animals and red rooting fingers. Some of those hollows become nests. Others are pulled shut by the growth around them, leaving only a slight dip in the floor.

The burrowers have no use for the bars of daylight, but they are sensitive to heat and pressure. The people say that a ridge moving steadily toward a warm seam will pass it on one side or the other; a ridge that stops may mean the animal has found a place to feed. The distinction matters. A traveler can often step aside from a moving burrower, but the floor around a feeding one may collapse without further warning.

The hammering comes from animals much smaller than the sound suggests. Each lives inside a tough stem, in a chamber it makes by eating away the pith. It braces its hind limbs against the chamber wall and strikes with a thickened plate on its head. The stem carries the blows a long way, especially where it joins an arch. Animals in neighboring stems answer, sometimes for hours. If an arch begins to fail, the hammering in it changes: the calls become rapid and uneven, and the animals chew their way out before the stem tears apart. People working beneath an old arch listen for that change.

The people live in small groups on the stone shelves and on the oldest, broadest ribs of the chamber. Stone is prized because it stays where it is put, though a shelf may crack or tilt during a heave. Each dwelling has more than one way out. The entrances are kept low to hold back loose leaves and the finer swarms, and the walls are woven loosely enough that a person inside can hear what is happening beyond them.

Their wrappings are made from leaf fiber soaked in resin and beaten soft. The outer layers catch the white hooks before they reach the skin. At the ankles and wrists, the wrappings are thick and easily replaced; those are the places most often torn. A traveler’s pole has a broad foot for testing whether a leaf will bear weight and a hooked end for pulling curtains aside. Many poles are marked with cuts that record the heights of past growth. A shoot measured in the morning can be measured again on the way home, and the difference tells a traveler how quickly that part of the path is likely to close.

They collect resin for sealing vessels, fastening tools and protecting cuts. A little is also burned in sheltered lamps, though the plants provide enough light for most work. Water is gathered from the cooler roof seams in folded leaves and stored in stoppered stone or resin-lined cups. The collectors watch the seams closely: a place that drips for several days may dry when the stone shifts, while another begins to bead.

Food comes from the country around them, but gathering it takes knowledge. Some leaves can be made edible by scraping away the wax and pressing out the sap. Certain young shoots are cut before they open, when their cores are tender. Shinglebacks are collected from the edges of herds and roasted in their plates; clappers are shaken from wounded stems into bags. The people also tend patches of growth near their shelters, trimming them back to encourage the parts they want. A patch must be visited often. Left alone, it may become a thicket overnight or be found stripped by a passing swarm.

Children learn the paths by maintaining them. They carry cut stems away from entrances, press down leaves that have begun to rise and place stones where a route crosses uncertain footing. They learn the chamber’s sounds alongside its shapes: the scrape of a herd approaching through a curtain, the change in a hammering call, the heavy tearing noise that can precede a burrower’s emergence. They also learn landmarks above the paths, because the paths themselves cannot be trusted to remain.

After a heave, people come out with their poles and call from shelf to shelf. They check on neighboring shelters, find where the old routes have gone and begin opening them again. Often the work uncovers something useful: a new seam giving water, a stand of edible shoots, a fallen arch full of clappers. By evening, the first travelers may already be using the cleared way, stepping over the cut leaves while red fingers reach from their ends and begin to grip the floor.
