The builders are small enough that a single animal can be missed on a crowded wall. Each sits in a chalk-white cup, with a soft crown of feeding threads spread into the water. The threads bend with the current and draw inward when something touches them. A few cups open onto bare stone, but most are attached to older cups, their rims packed so closely that neighboring animals brush against one another as they feed.

The walls they make are alive only at their surfaces. Deep inside them are the sealed cups of animals long dead, bored through by worms and stained with mineral deposits. New builders settle wherever the water brings them food without tearing them loose. On the sheltered underside of an arch they grow thinly; along its outer edge they form a thick, uneven fringe. When part of a tower falls, the survivors may spend the rest of their lives facing a different direction.

Many other animals make use of their work. Fine red worms extend feathery mouths from pinholes, withdrawing so quickly that the wall appears to ripple. Clear sheets of soft-bodied creatures lie over the chalk and over one another, their edges lifting when water passes beneath them. In the cracks, flattened scavengers pick at dead tissue and shed fragments. They are jointed and pale, with legs that reach sideways into spaces too narrow for the rest of the body.

The blind fish shelter in chambers large enough for them to turn. Their bodies are delicate, almost colorless, and their heads bear fine sensory threads that tremble in the flow. They hold position with small movements of their fins, taking what drifts toward them. A hunter entering a chamber feels different in the water from falling debris: the shoal parts before it arrives. The hunter moves slowly, its broad mouth close to the wall, searching the ledges and crevices where smaller animals have pressed themselves.

At the springs, pale bacterial mats cover stone and creep over the bases of the towers. Grazers pass across them, leaving narrow clean tracks that soon disappear beneath new growth. Some animals keep bacteria in folds or chambers of their bodies, supplying them with the spring water on which they depend. Others feed directly from the rising water, holding out plumes and sieves where the chemical-rich flow meets the sea. The spring communities are dense, but their edges are ragged: an animal a short distance farther from the warm water may be smaller than its neighbor, or absent altogether.

Beyond the walls, the buried feeders lie with their bodies deep in the silt. Their arms spread over the surface like loose roots, gathering particles and pulling them toward hidden mouths. A disturbance sends the arms curling down; after a time, they emerge again, leaving little furrows in the drift. Among them move occasional solitary animals, including the pale creature that briefly opens its body to show blue light. When it closes, the fish and worms and feeding crowns remain where they were, working in darkness.
