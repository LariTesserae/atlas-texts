The residents dress for the cold in layers that are easy to remove without setting anything down. Their coats fasten along the forearms and open at the back; a person carrying a container can shrug out of one with help from a neighbor. Most wear pale overshoes with a strip of dark fabric tied around each ankle. The strips make it easier to tell which feet are theirs when several people are standing close together.

They work in small crews, though the crews are not permanent. At the beginning of the day, people gather in the square and compare what has changed overnight. Someone has found an extra landing above a kitchen. A conduit has warmed enough to melt a thumbprint in the frost beneath it. A door that was weighted open now leads into two rooms, both occupied. The news is given quietly and often repeated by whoever stands nearest, since a voice may not reach even across the square.

There are builders, measurers, and people who tend the warm pools. There are also people whose work consists of waiting beside things. They sit on folding stools near the shifting end of a bridge or outside a room that has grown deeper, keeping a mark in view and noting when it moves. They carry hot stones in their pockets and notebooks with narrow pages, so they can write while wearing gloves.

The hillock has two attendants. One watches the grains rise from the tray; the other checks the slope for hollows with a small brass instrument. They change places at midday. Neither has walked on the mound, although one of them remembers a hill from somewhere else and sometimes describes how it felt underfoot. The other listens and adjusts the instrument.

Children are allowed to go almost anywhere in groups of three. One goes first, one follows, and one stays where both can be seen. They play a game in which they try to catch their own voices when the walls return them. A child who hears a familiar murmur will hold out both hands; the others run to see whose words have come back. Sometimes the words are from a conversation the children have not heard. These are taken home and offered to the adults over supper.

The adults take such offerings seriously, though not always as warnings. A returned voice may be useful: a reminder to fetch a coil of cable, or the end of a recipe someone thought was lost. Most households keep a shelf of small unfinished messages, written down as accurately as possible. When a visitor recognizes one, it is crossed out.

People eat together wherever there is room and warmth. They bring covered dishes through the lanes and set them on folded cloths to keep the contents from cooling too quickly. A meal may move from one room to another if the first becomes difficult to measure. Nobody remarks on this unless someone has been left behind; then a person goes back with a lamp and calls from the doorway until the missing diner answers.

The white animals are not kept as pets, but many residents leave them crumbs near the vents. Children study their tracks and try to guess where an animal will come from. This is harder than it looks: the tracks sometimes end beneath a person’s boots. When that happens, the person stands still until the animal appears and passes.

At night, the doors remain weighted. Someone in each household stays awake a little longer to look into the rooms and see that their far walls are still visible. They do not need to watch continuously. They knit, mend insulation, sort fasteners, or sit with a cup held between their hands, looking up from time to time. In the morning they tell the others how the rooms have been.
