The copper growths are the most numerous inhabitants, though a traveler may cross a kilometer of them without noticing. Each patch is thin enough to follow the grain of the stone beneath it. Its branches enter pores in the basalt and spread along salt-filled cracks, so that two patches on opposite sides of a rock may be parts of the same organism. The exposed foil is only what they risk putting into the light.

They grow slowly. A patch the size of a hand may have been feeding on the same stone for decades, extending one edge as another is abraded away. Where the ground opens, some are torn in two. Both halves can survive, provided enough of their buried threads remain attached to mineral seams. They are most abundant on stones that have lain in one position for a long time, but even there they rarely cover the windward face. In an exposed place the dust strips them faster than they can grow.

The small jointed grazers shelter among them. At dusk they emerge from cracks or push up through the loose grit, their low bodies divided into overlapping plates. They have no obvious eyes; fine feelers sweep ahead of them and sometimes lift toward the air. A grazer follows a copper branch to its edge, rasps away a narrow section and moves on. By morning the patch may show a pale, scalloped margin and a line of pinprick tracks leading back to a fissure.

A grazer caught on open ground before a storm folds its limbs beneath its body. Dust piles against it until it resembles one more dark pebble. Some are buried too deeply to emerge, and their empty plates can later be found in the lee of a stone. Others wait through several days of moving dust, then dig upward when the surface becomes quiet enough for their feelers to distinguish it.

The pale gliders are harder to account for. They appear singly, high above the plain, and hold their wings so steadily that they can be mistaken for scraps caught in a current. Seen from below, a glider may seem nearly motionless while the dust curtains race past it. Then a small change at one wingtip sends it across an enormous distance, until its shape is lost against the haze.

What brings them down is seldom witnessed. On a few sheltered ridges there are long, smooth hollows with pale scales scattered around them. The hollows face different directions, and they are often erased when the stone plates shift. A fresh one may contain the crushed remains of grazers, or nothing at all. No one has found a nest in which a glider could safely raise its young, though there are places beneath the rims of the plateau that have never been examined.

The domed shelters also have occupants, if only intermittently. Travelers use them when they can find them. The denser air inside lets a person rest without the constant strain of the plateau, and the clean patch of floor is a good place to set down a pack or work grit out of a fastening. People leave marks near the entrances: a date scratched into stone, a direction, a warning that a neighboring shelter could not be found. Some marks are freshly cut; others have been rubbed almost smooth. The shelters are too far apart to form a settlement, but their visitors learn to look for one another’s traces.

Occasionally someone finds a grazer inside a dome, moving along the boundary of the clean floor. It does not cross that boundary. Whether it senses something there or merely finds no food is unclear. Copper growths do occur just inside some entrances, where the stone receives a little light. Farther in, the walls are bare.

The three leaning slabs attract different life. Fine dust gathers beneath them in a shallow, unusually even bed, and grazers sometimes come there to feed on what has fallen. Their tracks cross and recross, then vanish where the grains begin to hang in the air. A glider has been seen circling the slabs at dusk, descending lower with each pass, but it turned away before landing.

The makers may have lived here, or may have used the plateau for purposes that left no recognizable dwelling behind. Nothing in the surviving structures gives a clear answer. The fitted lines of stone sometimes pass beneath a living patch; the bending fragments turn up in grazer burrows, cut into small pieces with edges worn smooth. Whatever the plateau was to its makers, it has since become a place where other things take shelter, feed, pass overhead and disappear into the dust.
