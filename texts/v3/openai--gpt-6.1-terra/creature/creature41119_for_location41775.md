The people of the town are accustomed to looking down as well as ahead. A patch of cream on a causeway may be a sleeping grazer, and the thin water beside it may be full of smaller bodies. Children learn early to test the ground with a toe before putting their weight on it. They also learn the difference between a feeding track and a crack in the mineral crust, though even adults sometimes bend for a closer look.

Most people wear loose, pale clothes with long sleeves and coverings for the back of the neck. Workers who spend the day outside protect their eyes with narrow screens or darkened lenses. In the houses, shoes are left on raised racks, where the water drains from them into basins. The soles wear smooth quickly on the stone; mending them is a steady occupation.

The town’s sign language began as a way to work across the factory yards, but people use it everywhere. Some signs are practical and exact: open the channel, stop the pump, someone is below. Others have grown less formal. Neighbors greet one another from opposite roofs; a child can ask for a drink through a window without entering the house. At night, when hands are harder to see, people speak more. Visitors sometimes mistake this for shyness during the day, until they notice how much conversation passes over their heads.

The wells, pumps and cooling channels require constant attention. A worker listening at a wall may detect a change in an engine before an instrument records it. Others tend the brine beds, rake the drying salts, or sort deposits whose color gives some indication of what they contain. The work leaves a fine residue on cuffs and skin. People carry little scrapers for cleaning their nails, and the water used for washing is often saved for another task.

Food comes partly from outside the basin, in loads brought along the high approaches. The town also raises thick bacterial cultures in shaded trays and keeps some of the smaller sediment-feeders in channels where their movement can be watched. Meals have a sharp mineral taste unless care has been taken with the water. Cooks are valued for knowing which well to draw from and how long to let a vessel stand before using it.

The broad grazers wander through the town as if the causeways were merely another set of stone shelves. Some have favorite resting places beneath the outlets of cooling channels. People recognize the older ones by scars and irregularities in the edges of their bodies, and give them names that are seldom agreed upon. A grazer blocking a doorway is usually persuaded away with water poured along the stone in front of it.

The hunters are treated with more distance. They can stand for hours among workers and carts without yielding their place. Their narrow mouths are capable of taking a careless hand along with a smaller animal, and children are taught not to approach them from behind. Yet a hunter that waits faithfully at the same crossing may become part of the neighborhood: people report when it is absent, and someone will notice if it returns with an injured leg.

Toward dusk, householders set out vessels to collect the condensation that gathers under shaded ledges. People sit near the channels, where the moving water offers a little relief, and talk while they mend screens or clean mineral dust from their tools. Across the flats, the hunters begin to move. The grazers loosen themselves from the stone. Smaller creatures rise through the warm water, comb the sediment, and disappear again at the passing of a shadow.
