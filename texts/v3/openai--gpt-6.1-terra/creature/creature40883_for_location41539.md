The broad-backed grazers are called ridgebacks only where their bodies rise above the leaves. Most of the time they are felt first: a slow pressure through the ground, then the stems bending apart. They have six short legs set wide beneath them and a head low enough to sweep the litter as they walk. Their mouths are divided into two tough lips, which can pull a creeper taut while a row of inner teeth saws through it.

They feed without hurry, even when the noise around them is greatest. A ridgeback can close its ears against the din; it finds its herd by the vibrations their feet make. At the edge of a hollow it pauses, one forefoot raised, until another answers from below. Calves stay close enough to touch the adult ahead with the stiff feelers along their jaws. When a herd crosses fresh stone, the calves slip and scramble, and the animals behind stop until each is upright.

A whole community travels in their wake. Small furred seed-pickers ride in the folds behind their shoulders, climbing down when the herd rests. The grazers seem indifferent to them but sometimes stand with their legs spread while the pickers work through the hair around their feet. Wingless insects gather at the dung before it has cooled. Their flowing bands can reduce a mound to scattered fibers in an hour, leaving seeds exposed for the pickers and tiny pale hunters exposed in turn.

Those hunters lie beneath leaves with their limbs folded alongside their bodies. They are nearly flat when waiting, though their abdomens swell after a meal. The great choruses do not disturb them. They respond instead to the touch of a foot on the leaf above; their front limbs punch upward, seize, and pull whatever they have caught through the softened tissue. When a swarm strips a patch bare, they abandon it, lifting themselves high on their legs and walking awkwardly toward cover.

The stone-turners are heavier animals, with blunt noses and thick wrists. They work the litter in pairs, each pushing its snout under a rock while the other braces against its flank. When the stone rolls over, both dart at the exposed ground. They eat grubs, fungal knots, and sometimes the pale hunters themselves. In dry spells they turn the same stones repeatedly, returning after rain to places where moisture lasts.

The pod-breakers are smaller and quicker. They live in loose bands along the wooded ridges and spend much of the day searching the litter. A pod-breaker selects a pebble that fits crosswise in its mouth, then braces a pod between its front feet and raises its head to strike. Young ones often choose stones too large to lift or hit the ground beside the pod. They learn partly by watching and partly by taking broken pods from adults who have not guarded them closely enough.

A successful strike brings the others running. There is shrieking, jostling and a rush of narrow hands into the split shell. Sometimes the animal that opened it gets almost nothing; sometimes it carries a fragment away into the ferns. The pebble usually stays behind. A band leaving a feeding place is followed by the soft clatter of abandoned stones as the litter settles around them.

At night, the pod-breakers rest pressed together on ledges above the basins. Their ears are broad, but hearing is of limited use in the continuous uproar. They groom one another by touch and keep contact with their nearest companions through long, sensitive tails. If a ridge shifts beneath them, the first to feel it tightens its tail around another. The movement passes through the resting group before they scatter.

Farther down, in the warm litter, green larvae feed on the fungal mats. Their lights show through their thin bodies as they eat. Some are taken by hunters, and some survive long enough to crawl up a stem and hang there until wings unfold from their backs. After rain they rise in clouds that brush against the ridgebacks and collect briefly on the waxy leaves.

By morning, many have fallen. They lie among the shed leaves and broken pods, where the soil insects find them. Above them, new shoots are already opening in the passages the grazers made, and the pod-breakers are coming down from their ledges to turn over what the night has left.
