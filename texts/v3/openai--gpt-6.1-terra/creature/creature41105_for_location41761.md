The inhabitants are the growths themselves: not one plant repeated across the lowland, but several kinds living so closely together that their boundaries are easiest to find in the mud.

The tall hollow ones draw most of the current. Their roots descend into mineral seams at different depths, and the difference between those seams sustains the charge that passes upward through their folded walls. Each wall is made of thin living layers, separated by films of sap. In the dark interior, where the folds nearly meet, droplets form and are taken up again before they can fall.

Around them stand the narrower plants, which do much of the work of making. Their leaves receive concentrated sap through the root network and return it changed: some enrich it with compounds the taller growths cannot produce efficiently, while others remove substances that would accumulate in the channels. Their leaves are black on the outside and pale at the seams. When one is pressed aside, it gives slowly and then returns, carrying its burden of moisture without spilling it.

The level skin underfoot belongs to a third kind. Its broad roots lock around one another, holding the shallow water in place and admitting it through pores too fine to see. Beneath the firm surface, new root tips move through the warm mud, following mineral gradients and laying fresh channels alongside the old. Where a seam grows poor, the channels serving it narrow; elsewhere they widen. The change is slow enough that a pool may keep the same clean outline for a very long time, even as the living rim beneath it is replaced.

There are younger growths among them. Some rise only a finger’s breadth above the root skin, their folded leaves still pressed into tight pale-edged points. Others have opened into low cups and begun to gather water. They grow from buds carried along the common roots, drawing from their neighbors until their own contacts with the mud are sufficient. A mature growth may remain attached for its whole life. What passes between it and the plants beside it depends on their immediate needs: water toward a drier patch, salts toward a receptive root, concentrated sap toward a leaf still developing its working layers.

The growths are not all of one age, but little here looks old. Surfaces that cease to conduct are broken down from within; their material is taken into the roots or passed back into the mud. A failing leaf can be reduced to a thin film at its base while a new one unfolds beside it. The edges stay sharp because they are continually renewed.

In places, the root mass rises into young ridges, and the tall forms grow close enough for their slick sides to brush. On the flats they stand farther apart, with shallow water held between them. The same exchanges continue in both places. There is no leading growth directing them, and no interval in which the field rests. Each living part responds to the small differences around it, taking in what it can use, releasing what another part may take, and maintaining the dark, wet surface in which the next growth will find room.
