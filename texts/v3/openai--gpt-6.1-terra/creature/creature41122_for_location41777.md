The people of the basin are broad through the shoulders and short in the legs, with hands thickened by stone dust and the pressure of carrying. Their skin varies from clay-brown to a pale gray that shows every fresh scrape. They wrap their heads against falling grit, leaving their faces open; even indoors, someone sitting beneath a roof will sometimes tilt their head to listen to the slab.

They know the city chiefly by its small differences in height. A newcomer sees a level court; a resident sees where a bowl will fill first, where water will pause against a lip of stone, where a settlement crack has made a new low place since the last rain. Children are sent out with shallow dishes to learn this. They pour a little water, watch where it goes, and fetch it back with sponges made from the soft bodies of shelled animals.

The animals are food, tools, nuisances, companions, and, depending on the household, objects of considerable affection. The blunt-snouted grazers are caught in woven traps and kept in low pens, where they nose through sifted material brought down from the surface. Their fur makes warm linings, though its finest use is for brushes: a channel cleaner can feel the texture of a stone through the handle of a good brush. The shelled colonies are tended on damp walls. People take the larger animals and leave the young, marking a harvested patch with a line of chalk so that neighbors will give it time to thicken again.

Material from above enters their lives in many forms. After a storm, people gather beneath the wider openings to catch washed-down scraps: soil, broken roots, seeds, bits of wood, sometimes the body of an unfamiliar surface animal. These are sorted in the bright courts. Some become food for the penned creatures; some are dried and stored; a sound length of wood might be saved for years before anyone agrees that a job is important enough to use it.

The storage chambers are not all empty. Some hold water jars or stacks of fitted slabs ready for repairs. Others are kept clear because a family may need to leave its house quickly. In a few, people sleep during the hottest hours, when the brilliant bands reach the streets and the stone gives back a hard white glare.

They speak softly at close range, but quiet is not a rule. Children shout during games, and arguments can carry across a court. Much of their communication is made with the hands simply because people are often too far apart to hear one another: a raised scraper means a channel is blocked; two hands held flat and lowered mean that dust is coming down. Across a long street, the gesture may pass from person to person until workers at the far end look up.

A morning inspection is usually done in pairs. One person clears the channel while the other watches the adjoining walls and marks changes on a strip of stone. A seam no wider than a fingernail may be watched for months. When a house needs its roof reset, neighbors come with poles and wedges, and the household brings out its stored water. The slab is lifted a little at a time. Someone brushes the wall top clean; someone else checks the ground for the animals that have been exposed. Once the slab is lowered, everyone waits to see whether it has found a steady place.

The dead are laid in chambers that have become too narrow or uncertain for storage. Their useful things are taken out first, including any sound blocks from the doorway. Families visit with lamps, sit for a while, and sometimes bring news of a wall the dead person helped build or a channel they used to keep clear. The chambers are not sealed permanently. When the rock shifts, a passage may have to be opened or abandoned, and the families decide together what can be moved.

On an ordinary afternoon, many people rest in the shade of their separate houses while the animals cross the bare courts. A few work at the cisterns, lowering bowls into the cool dark. Farther off, where a roof has slipped, a group gathers around a slab. They examine it from several sides before putting their poles beneath it, and even after the work begins, one person stands back, watching the wall.
