The most numerous animals are almost invisible at a distance. They hang above the ridges with their feeding limbs spread, each limb carrying a fringe of fine hairs. The current holds them open. When a particle strikes the fringe, the limb folds inward, wipes itself across the mouth, and extends again. They spend so little energy that a few particles can sustain them for days.

Some are transparent except for a white gut; others have dense, chalk-colored bodies and short tails that they use to shift from one current to another. Their young are carried much farther than the adults ever travel. A summit that has stood empty for years may acquire a population when a slow cloud of larvae passes over it. They settle wherever the water rises along a face of rock and brings food within reach.

Along the cracks live the feelers. They are flattened animals with six or eight jointed limbs, most of their length taken up by a flexible abdomen. They brace themselves on the sharp stone and reach into openings too narrow for their bodies. The foremost limbs are thin and densely furnished with sensory hairs; the others end in hard, replaceable hooks. A feeler can remain anchored through a current strong enough to lift loose fragments of mineral from the seabed.

They eat the small animals that gather around the seep water, along with soft deposits growing inside the warmer cracks. Their mouths are sheltered under the head. When one finds a productive opening, it may stay there for months, its pale back gradually acquiring scratches from the passing grit. Two animals sometimes meet at the same fissure and test each other with their reaching limbs. Usually the smaller withdraws. If neither does, they grapple briefly, feet clamped to opposite sides of the crack, until one loses its hold and is swept away.

The silver schools feed higher in the water. Each animal is scarcely longer than a hand, with a thick, muscular body and a narrow band of reflective scales along either side. The scales are not especially bright until the school turns. Then hundreds of bodies flash at once, and the light travels across the group in a quick sheet. They feed on drifting animals and on the particles that the currents lift from below. Their mouths open downward; an individual passing over a ridge can strip a suspended feeder from its hold without slowing.

The schools do not keep to the same formation. In open water they spread into long, loose clouds. Near the mineral fans they contract, moving through the angular spaces between the sheets. A hunter following them may have to choose a single gap while the school divides among many. The survivors gather again on the other side, sometimes with fewer than half their number.

The hunters are pale as well. Some have long bodies and broad, nearly motionless fins, and can pass so close to a ridge that their bellies almost touch it. Their eyes are small and set deep under bony rims. Along the head and flanks are rows of pressure-sensitive pits, protected by fine lattices that keep larger particles out.

The roar appears not to prevent them from finding prey. They are most responsive to changes close by: the sudden displacement of water by a turning school, or the repeated movement of a feeler drawing its limbs from a crack. A hunting animal often approaches slowly, then makes one forceful movement over a short distance. Afterward it may settle into a depression in the rock, where only the edges of its fins move.

There are larger hunters, though one can spend a long time on the plateau without seeing one. Their bodies bear old scars and patches where the skin has grown back in uneven folds. They travel from ridge to ridge and sometimes follow the cables leading away from a machine. At the towers they circle once or twice, holding their heads close to the iron, then continue along the cable into the dark. Whether they are tracking the vibration or the animals gathered around it is difficult to tell.

The scavengers under the motor housings are smaller than the feelers and rounder, with overlapping white plates. They crowd into the narrow space between a warm casing and its mounting. There they pick at anything the current brings: torn feeding limbs, dead silver animals, the remains of eggs. The warmth lets them remain active while similar scavengers on the open rock move only occasionally.

Their young hatch beneath the adults and shelter there until their plates harden. Some eventually leave, dropping from the casing to the ridge and allowing the current to take them outward. Most are eaten. A few reach another warm surface, or find a seep, and begin a new cluster.

Occasionally a machine stops. Its casing cools gradually, and the animals underneath spread out over the adjacent stone. For a time they continue to return, pressing their undersides against the iron. Then the cluster thins. If the lamp has failed as well, the tower becomes difficult to distinguish from the dark ridges until one is very close.

On the quieter side of each tower, small animals settle in the wake where particles accumulate. They also settle behind loose slabs and in folds of rock. Their pale bodies give these sheltered places a faint texture, visible only nearby. Farther off, the summits still look bare.

The animals have no common season that can be seen across the plateau. One school carries eggs while another is full of young. A feeler guards a clutch deep inside a fracture while its neighbors continue feeding. Something is always hatching, growing, or being eaten, though the distances are so great that many of these events take place without another large animal nearby.

Above them the ice shifts, opening clear patches and closing others. The columns of light move across the ridges. Animals exposed by the changing light may turn their reflective sides away or descend toward the stone. Those beneath the machines remain where they are. The lamps burn in daylight and darkness alike, illuminating the crowded undersides of the housings and a little of the cold water beyond.
