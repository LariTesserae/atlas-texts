The people of the plateau learn the city first by its water. A child may know the way to a neighboring court by the channel that passes through it, and know when to turn home because the water there begins to run toward the sleeping rooms. At first they are warned away from lifting stones. Later, under someone’s eye, they learn which slabs cover drains and which are part of the walls.

Most people live in groups of several households around a court. The rooms are small and thick-walled, with low openings that can be closed against wind and rain. Bedding is lifted onto shelves during the day; food is kept in covered jars on the driest ledges. A household has its own hearth, though in cold weather several families may cook together rather than tend separate fires. The smoke finds its way out through gaps near the roof and leaves the stone above them dark.

Dryness is something people work to make and keep. They spread cloth on the roof when the sun comes, then bring it down at the first sign of a cloud settling over the ridge. They sweep water from thresholds with bundles of rushes. In some houses a narrow groove has been cut around the hearth so that a seep in the floor will pass by without putting out the fire. Even so, there are mornings when a whole room must be emptied and its things carried into a neighbor’s court.

Some residents spend much of their time tending the channels. They know the districts above them and the condition of the courts below. After a prolonged rain, they walk the courses with others from their neighborhoods, feeling beneath the water for leaves, grit and loose stones. The work is shared, though not always peaceably: opening a passage may save one group of houses while sending more water toward another. People argue over such choices in the courts, sometimes ankle-deep in the consequence of them.

There are growers too, working the sheltered basins and the strips of soil gathered behind walls. They raise hardy greens and roots where they can, and cut rushes from the wetter ground. Farther out, herders take small, sure-footed animals between the thin meadows, watching that they do not strip a patch down to mud. Grain and other foods come into the city along the broad ways, carried in loads that must be kept above the wet stone. Storehouses stand on raised courses where possible, and their keepers are attentive to every darkening joint in a wall.

Stoneworkers are found throughout the city, though nearly everyone can repair a loose piece of paving. The most practiced builders can look at an old wall and judge how it has settled, where its weight is going, and whether a room above it can safely remain. They often work slowly, taking away a few stones and waiting to see what shifts before taking more. Their knowledge is asked for when a passage sinks, when a roof begins to bow, and when a family wants to build another room over the old ones.

The city’s size is hard for any one person to grasp. Many people know several neighboring districts intimately and have relatives a day’s walk away, but have never seen the farthest inhabited courts. Travelers recognize the repeated arrangement of walls and water while noticing local differences: a channel roofed with especially broad slabs, a court where every threshold is raised, a terrace whose households dry their washing on lines stretched between stones. People give directions through such details. A stranger who follows only the broad ways can pass quite close to a destination without finding it.

The waterbirds live among them with little concern. One may shelter in a drain through a busy morning and emerge when the court has quieted, stepping over the marks left by bare feet. Insects hatch in brief abundance from the shallow margins, and birds and small creatures take them while they last. In the outer districts, where abandoned rooms hold standing water, moss spreads over the lower stones and rushes grow through the broken floors. Grazing animals sometimes wander into these places and must be coaxed out.

At night, people close the slabs or shutters over their doorways and listen to the channels through the walls. A familiar trickle is reassuring. A new sound—a fall of water where there was none before, or the scrape of a stone shifting in a current—may bring someone out with a lamp to look. By morning there will be neighbors gathered around the place, deciding whether it can wait until after breakfast or whether the water needs their hands at once.
