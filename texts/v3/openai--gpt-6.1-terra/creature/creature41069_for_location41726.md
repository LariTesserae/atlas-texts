The inhabitants are plants, though the boundaries between one and another are difficult to establish. A strap leaf hanging beside the pump may belong to a root crown high on the wall, or to the low tangle beside the basin. Its roots pass through older roots, through mineral crust, and behind curtains of leaves that have been growing there longer than the pump has been turning.

The largest plants occupy the condensation channels. Their leaves are thick at the base and folded lengthwise, with edges that have dried to the colour of old paper. When moisture returns to the ceiling, water runs down grooves between their roots. Some is caught in the cups of their leaves; some reaches smaller plants growing beneath them, which have little hold on the wall except through the accumulated fibres of their neighbours.

The fernlike growth keeps to the shelves and hollows. Its fans are stiff enough to support a dusting of red grit, and the pale moss grows over them as readily as it grows over stone. The moss is thinnest near the hot basin and thickest behind the curtains, where it remains cool and damp for a little longer after each spell of dripping. There are patches that look dead until water reaches them. Then, over a day or two, their surfaces darken and lift.

The white flowers are easy to miss. They open flat against the rock, sometimes beneath a leaf or inside the empty sheath left by an older stem. Their pollen does not travel far in any one breath of the pump. It catches in folds and seams, gathers there, and is disturbed again by later strokes. Seedlings appear in those same sheltered places: narrow pale blades at first, then leaves with enough weight to lie against the stone. The ones whose roots reach an occupied seam may persist. Others dry where they began, remaining visible for years.

At the basin’s edge, mineral ridges have grown around the feet of several plants. Their lowest leaves show green in the pool’s light; farther up, the same leaves disappear into darkness. The glow brightens and recedes with the pump, but the plants change too slowly to follow it visibly. Whether the light belongs to something living in the water or to the hot minerals themselves is not apparent from the shore.

The machinery has become part of their terrain. Roots cross the buried rail and enter gaps around the concrete plinth. Leaves hang against the silent length of the broken pipe. Each knock shakes loose a little grit and trembles through the old growth, but nothing pulls away. The plants continue to put on leaf and root around it.

There is life throughout the chamber, and a great deal of what that life has left behind. Dead stems still stand among the living; hollow root sheaths carry newer roots toward water. In the driest alcoves the old forms are crowded together so densely that, when a fresh shoot appears, it seems for a time to be just another pale remnant.
