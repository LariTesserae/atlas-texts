The people of the notch know the ground by its seams. They can tell which cracks will be damp at dawn and which are deep enough to swallow a dropped seed. Children learn to lift the collection stones without turning them over too quickly, so the beads underneath run into a dish rather than back into the gravel. On mornings after heavy mist, everyone carries water. On dry mornings they leave the smallest beads where they are until the air warms.

Their houses hold little that cannot be lifted. Sleeping mats are rolled against the walls each day, above the level where the flat creatures gather. Seed is kept in lidded clay vessels on stone shelves, and dried food hangs in loose bundles where its condition can be seen. A householder checks the bundles by sight: a slight darkening, a line of blue-black specks, a leaf that has become thin as a scraped skin. The grazers can pass through surprisingly small gaps. They are easier to divert than to exclude.

Each doorway has its own scatter of offerings. Some people use fern tips; others save the soft inner peel from the stems they eat. The strips must be replaced before they dry. A good scatter draws a visible current across the paving: hundreds of clear bodies, hardly distinguishable on bare stone, becoming dark as they feed. The flat hunters follow more slowly. They pause with their limbs spread into the cracks, then gather the grazers beneath themselves, their soft pouches gradually rounding.

The hunters are tolerated inside, though not handled much. A person may lift one gently on a piece of bark when it has settled in the way of a hearth or beneath a sleeping child. They are delicate at the joints, and their bodies flatten against the bark as they are carried. Usually they find their own way out when the floor warms. In cold weather, when they crowd together indoors, people set their feet down carefully and sweep clear only the narrow strip they need.

The plants require a firmer hand. Much of the day’s work is cutting and tying: freeing a doorway, opening a path to the cistern, pulling a pale leaf away from a roof drain. The cuttings are sorted in the court. Sound leaves become food or fodder for the grazers; tough stems are split for trellises; damp, spoiled pieces are carried to the seams beyond the paving. The people leave dense growth around the water-bearing cracks but keep the stones immediately beside them clear, so a dish can be reached and emptied without crushing what lives underneath.

They do not walk quickly, even where the paving is level. A dark stream crossing a path may be grazers descending from a roof, and what looks like a patch of pale gravel may unfold into several hunters when a shadow passes over it. Visitors learn to watch the ground after the townspeople do: a hand held out before a child, a pause halfway across the court, a foot placed in an open patch while the current goes by.

At evening the doors are left open as long as the mist allows. People sit near their thresholds, trimming stems or mending ties, while the animals move between the houses and the greenery. The little openings remain unobstructed when the larger doors are closed. From inside, a person can see the pale limbs pass through one at a time, followed by the flattened body, and then another creature behind it.

By morning there are fresh beads beneath the stones and new shoots across the paths. The floors have been visited, the peel outside the doors has thinned, and the fern fronds are clearing of their dark spots. Before drawing water, the first person awake cuts a way to the dishes.
