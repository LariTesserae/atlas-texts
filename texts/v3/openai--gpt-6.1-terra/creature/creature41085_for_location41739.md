The great grazers are called lowbacks by the few creatures that have reason to distinguish them from the ground beneath them. A resting herd can be difficult to count. Their legs fold almost entirely out of sight, and their white coats lie level from one animal to the next. Only the black faces remain visible, turning occasionally toward a call.

Each lowback has two horns, flattened along their length and divided by fine seams. These grow from the sides of the head and curve forward, so that the animal can push into a stand of fronds without bringing its eyes among the hard segments. In the morning the horns collect moisture. The lowback tips its head, and the water runs down grooves toward its mouth. By midday the grooves have frozen over.

A gray animal no longer than a claw lives in the seams. It has a soft, divided foot with which it grips both sides at once, and a mouth that can be extended into cracks too narrow for its body. It eats the films that grow there and drinks the water before it reaches the lowback’s mouth. When ice seals the seam, it remains inside, breathing through channels in the horn. Young seam-dwellers leave by climbing onto the loose hair of the creatures that cross the herd.

Those travelers are much larger: black, quick-footed animals that move over the lowbacks’ shoulders and flanks, stopping to scrape ice from the coat or pull out shed hair. They carry the hair in folds beneath their bellies and use it to line nests among the fronds. A lowback may tolerate a dozen on its back, though it shakes vigorously when they gather around its eyes. The travelers wait until the shaking stops, then climb on again.

Calves are born dark and become white gradually, as their first coat grows out. For their first season they shelter beneath the adults, reaching upward to feed and pressing against the warm skin of the belly. In a crowded herd a calf can travel a considerable distance without ever seeing the sky. It learns the calls before it learns the shapes of the animals making them.

The pale climbers on the cliffs spend much of their lives in contact with one another. They have many short legs, each ending in a pad that conforms to the smallest irregularities in the rock. Their upper surfaces bear long, hollow filaments. When the climbers gather, these overlap and hold a layer of comparatively still air against the cliff.

They feed on the thin growths that spread over stone beneath the ice. A cluster advances by degrees, the animals at its edge moving onto fresh rock while those behind pass over them. From a distance the movement is visible as a slow change in the direction of the filaments. In severe cold the whole cluster becomes still. Individuals near the exposed surface are gradually displaced inward, while others take their places.

In the sheltered valleys, the rooted animals are called bellows. Each begins life loose in the water held by moss. A young bellow is transparent and almost flat, with a fringe of vanes around its body. It can creep, though not far or quickly. Most are eaten there by small valley animals, or freeze when the moss dries out. Those that reach a crack deep enough to contain liquid push into it and begin to thicken. Their lower tubes grow downward; their vanes remain above the surface.

A colony forms as the young settle beside older bellows, often along the margin of a valley where water runs through broken stone. The adults are joined by pressure rather than tissue. Their bodies are so closely fitted that an individual dying in the interior may remain upright for years, its chambers gradually filling with mineral dust. The neighboring bellows continue to contract against it. Eventually its surface splits, and younger animals establish themselves in the sheltered cavity.

The brittle sheets excreted by the bellows vary from white to dark red according to the stone below them. They collect in the folds between bodies and around the vents, where they are broken up by small, heavy-jawed animals. These animals drag pieces into the frond forests and cache them beneath the roots. They eat some of the salts; others dissolve during the brief rains and enter the moss. The fronds growing around an old cache often have wider leaves than those nearby.

Several hunters follow the paths between colonies and herds. One is long-bodied and low to the ground, with a dark underside and a coat marked by broken patches of white. It rarely attacks a grown lowback. Instead it waits where the herd must pass between outcrops, looking for a calf separated from the animals around it. The adults turn toward its approach, lowering their horns. Their calls change, becoming shorter and closer together, and the calves gather beneath them.

Even the hunters pause when the bellows fall silent. In that interval the usual tremor leaves the ground, and the ticking of the fronds can be heard from farther away. After the lightning has passed and the first chambers begin to contract again, the valley returns slowly to motion: the salt-carriers emerge from the folds, the climbers resume their passage over the rock, and the lowbacks lower their heads to feed.
