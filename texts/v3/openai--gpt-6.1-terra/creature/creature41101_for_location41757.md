Most of the inhabitants are never seen whole. A translucent joint appears in a seam, bends, and withdraws. A row of legs passes behind a grating. At the edge of a lamp’s light, their bodies have the color of old glass; deeper in the ducts, they are visible only where they catch a glint from a turning shaft.

They are built for close quarters. Their legs fold flat against them, and the plates of their bodies slide slightly over one another, so that an animal can make itself narrow enough to enter a crack. In the warmest passages they lie several layers deep, holding to the metal and to each other. They are always moving, but a patch of them may stay in the same place for a very long time: one animal works forward, another takes its place, and the surface ripples without opening.

They graze where condensation gathers. The bacterial film is too thin to see except as a faint dulling of the pipe, but their mouths find it. They scrape the soft mineral crust from joints and valve housings, leaving little clean arcs that disappear beneath the bodies following them. In the drains they stand in the shallow water on the undersides of grates. Their hard backs break its surface; their legs work below.

A few venture onto the streets. They move with a deliberate, uneven gait, stopping where the paving is damp and feeling along the stone. In the open they are easier to distinguish from one another: some are longer, some cloudy, some missing a leg or carrying a chip in a plate. They pass through the cracks beneath doors and into the dwellings, where they gather behind cold radiators and inside the walls. The rooms are otherwise vacant. The lamps there are lit too, though their shades have filled with dust.

Inside the factory the animals make room for everything that moves. Around the great wheels they withdraw from the approaching teeth and press in again after they have passed. When a belt shudders, the tremor travels through them before it reaches the next pulley. Their rubbing changes pitch with it, then returns to the broad, even rasp audible throughout the town.

There are young among them, small enough to travel between the plates of the adults. In the ducts they are carried along in the shifting mass until they find a place against the warm metal. A body sometimes comes loose and is conveyed to a drain, where others settle over it. No pile accumulates there. By the time the drain is next opened, the space is filled as closely as before.

At the retaining wall, animals occupy the mortar joints all the way to the top. Their movement can be felt through the stone as a fine vibration. They do not seem to cross the rim in any number. Beyond it, the shelves offer little moisture and no enclosed warmth; within it, every pipe and wall gives them another passage, and the machinery keeps passing heat from one crowded place to the next.
