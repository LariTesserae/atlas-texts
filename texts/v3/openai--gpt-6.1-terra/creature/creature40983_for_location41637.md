The glass animals are easiest to find at dawn, when the yellow film has settled on everything but their backs. They hold themselves a fraction above the ground on their folded feet. Vapor passes underneath and condenses there, leaving a narrow pale outline around each body.

They are not made of glass, though their upper surfaces fracture like it. Beneath the dull shell are overlapping plates, a dark digestive chamber, and a row of fine gripping organs. They eat the little devices that gather at the roots of the seam-plants, selecting them one at a time. Some devices they swallow whole; others they hold against the ground and open, taking the soft packet inside and leaving the articulated casing behind. The plants grow better where the animals feed: too many collectors at a root will draw its channels dry.

A larger animal lives below the ridges. It is long and flat, with a body divided into sections that can move independently through different layers of the ground. The front sections loosen seams and taste the traffic within them. The rear sections close the seams again, pressing displaced sheets together and laying a thin adhesive along the edges. Sometimes a ridge develops a slight swelling that travels several meters in an afternoon. This is usually the only sign of one passing.

They eat mineral deposits, damaged circuitry, and occasionally one another. Two meeting in a narrow layer may remain joined for days, exchanging material or consuming it; from the surface the two activities look much alike. When one dies, its sections continue their separate work for a while. They become less coordinated, then settle into the ground, where other small inhabitants take them apart.

The plants have no such freedom of movement. Each black stem is a colony grown from a single root-thread, and the widely spaced plants may be connected under the terraces, following channels too fine to expose without destroying them. Their translucent leaves admit light to the chambers inside the stem. At night they collect the yellow film, folding it into their surfaces before it can evaporate. In the morning, when the leaves spread again, faint yellow veins can be seen for a few minutes.

The flat-growing plants are older. They put down no single stem but extend a thin, living skin over the warm surface. Dust accumulates on them and is gradually drawn through openings too small to see. They keep the grains they can use and release the rest at their margins. A patch may grow only a handspan in a century, yet cover the remains of several generations of the small glass hunters.

Other inhabitants are smaller than the dust. They move grains, clear pores, regulate the exchange of air and maintain the narrow mineral streams on which the plants depend. Some reproduce within the ground; others are assembled at fixed points from materials brought to them. They vary across the plain. A device that opens vents on one terrace may be unable to recognize the vents on the next. Where their territories meet, the soil sometimes breathes in uneven pulses until the two kinds have adjusted to one another.

The suspended structures tend to problems too large or too deep for these inhabitants to handle. They move slowly enough that their shadows are difficult to distinguish from the changing color of the plain. Each carries long, dangling filaments, ordinarily drawn up so high that they cannot be seen. When a buried region begins to fail, a structure lowers several filaments through the air. Their tips find openings in the ground and remain there for weeks, supplying material and receiving information through the fine circuits beneath the terraces.

The structures do not appear often over the same place. They leave behind repaired channels, altered populations of microscopic workers, and sometimes seeds in protective shells. The seeds lie where they fall until a seam opens close enough for a root to enter.

No single creature oversees the whole plain. The high structures can redirect the work below them, but much of that work continues without their attention. The plants close their leaves at dusk. The hunters wait among them. The long animals travel through the ridges, opening and sealing the layers as they feed. Below all of these, the smallest inhabitants keep the air moving and the mineral streams from drying out, making room, slowly, for another day.
