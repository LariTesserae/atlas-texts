The people of the city grow up judging distance by more than sight. A child can see a friend waving from the far side of an avenue and still be called back before attempting the crossing. Children learn to look for the surveyors’ flags, to listen for the transit office’s announcements, and, if a street begins to lengthen around them, to choose an end and keep walking toward it.

Most carry something that makes a noise. A tapped tin or a hand bell can tell a waiting family that someone is still moving on a long street, even when the person has become too small to see. On quiet mornings the sound is faint beneath the machinery. After rain, when crossings are uncertain, little bells may be heard for hours.

The inhabitants are practical about the living city, but not indifferent to it. A wall that is growing well has a particular firmness under the palm and a smell that returns when the air is damp. People notice when these things change. In older apartments, the residents know which window needs its frame loosened each spring and which patch of floor must be kept clear because a ridge is slowly rising through it. Furniture is often made in sections that can be taken apart and carried through a newly cut door.

Building keepers make rounds with mallets, moisture gauges, and thin knives. They listen to girders and scrape away patches of bark to inspect the tissue beneath. Beetles are collected in small glass tubes and brought to them for examination. A few beetles in a roof beam may mean a local injury; many, arriving over several seasons, can mean that a whole portion of a building has ceased to draw water. Then the keepers and residents decide whether to graft it, brace it, or let the workers cut it away.

The western districts have families who have worked among the engines for generations, though few people do only one thing throughout their lives. A pump mechanic may spend a winter helping to train a harvest frame; a belt crew may be called east to reconnect a pipe after a shift in the streets. Their work is noisy and often communal. When a belt falls, several workers lift it with poles while another watches the pulley and signals the engine to turn. The poles are stored upright in racks beside the elevated tracks, their ends polished by use.

Factory workers learn one another’s gestures as carefully as their voices. A hand held flat means wait; two raised fingers mean turn it slowly. Those who have lost some hearing still feel a change in an engine through the soles of their shoes. In the housing near the factories, children sometimes know that a parent’s shift is over because a familiar vibration has stopped.

Surveyors have a different kind of patience. They work in pairs, setting markers and checking them against long reels of measuring line. Their books contain figures for the same avenues over many years, including stretches that have remained stable through dozens of showers and others that have lengthened in clear weather without warning. The transit office pays close attention to their reports, but the surveyors are frequently argued with. Someone always has a delivery to make or a relative waiting at the other end.

Tram conductors keep water and blankets aboard for passengers delayed between districts. They also know where there are sheltered platforms if a route remains unsettled into the night. People who travel regularly tend to leave word of their intended route, and households are accustomed to setting aside a meal for someone who may arrive very late. A delay is not treated lightly, but neither is it unusual enough to stop the rest of the evening.

Water is everyone’s concern. Cistern attendants read the silver bands on the reservoir trunks and report the levels to their districts. In dry periods they inspect leaf gutters for clogs and ask residents to save wash water for young grafts. The pumps begin after the day’s measurements are taken, and people recognize the sequence of their starting: one deep pulse, then another farther off, until a faint tremor reaches the upper floors.

The inhabitants make room for pleasure within these routines. On clear days, market tables appear in the broad squares, selling pressed wood utensils, woven fiber, preserved food, and small pieces of lichen grown in careful patterns. Workers pause when a bird lands on a chimney or a high branch. Someone usually knows its name; someone else disputes it. By the time the argument reaches the square below, the bird is often gone.

At evening, people sit on balconies or in doorways while the pumps run. They can hear conversations from other buildings, though the speakers may be nearer or farther than they were that morning. Friends call across the gaps and arrange to meet when the routes allow. Above them the leaves hold the last yellow light, and below, the damp streets carry the sound of footsteps toward homes whose doors are left open a little longer for anyone still on the way.
