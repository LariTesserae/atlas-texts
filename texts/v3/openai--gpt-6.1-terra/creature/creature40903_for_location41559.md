The dark crust is alive. Fine threads bind grains of grit into a thin skin; among them are organisms that can wait through long dryness, then take up water quickly when rain comes. The skin grows slowly. A foot placed on it would break more than the eye could easily distinguish.

The shrubs are alive in a different measure of time. Their leaves lose little moisture, and their roots reach beyond the small mounds gathered around their stems. Beneath them lie old leaves, seeds, the remains of insects, and the minute lives that work on these things when conditions allow. A branch may look dead for a long while and still carry green just beneath its bark.

There are seeds in the grit, too, most of them waiting. Some may have been there for years. After a rare soaking, a few will open, send down a root, and hurry through their brief season before the ground dries again.

Other lives are harder to place. A small beetle may shelter under a stone, pressed into a seam too narrow to see from above. Ants may be deeper down, where the day’s heat reaches more slowly. A moth might come through the hollow on a warm night and leave without settling. The absence of tracks does not say much about creatures that are light, or that have not come out tonight.

For now, little moves openly. The shrubs hold their leaves close. The crust lies dark against the pale ground. Beneath it and among the roots, life continues slowly, with much of its work postponed until water returns.
