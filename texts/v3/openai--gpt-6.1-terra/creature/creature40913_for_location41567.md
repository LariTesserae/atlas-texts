The inhabitants are easiest to see beneath the eaves. There, on the damp undersides of the beams, grow pale, branching films, thin as the skin on cooled milk. They spread along the timber, cross the stone walls and descend into the channels. In the basins they thicken into folded, translucent mats, anchored by threads that disappear into the clay.

Each house holds one such growth, though a growth may extend far beyond its house. Its threads pass beneath the paving stones and through cracks in the bedrock, joining patches that look, from above, quite separate. A pale edge on a pool, a soft gray lining in a wall, a tuft among the reeds: these may all belong to the same inhabitant. Where two meet, a darker seam forms between them. The seam can remain unchanged for years; sometimes it slowly shifts, leaving one growth with more of a channel and the other with less.

They live on what the water carries and on the light that reaches their outer surfaces. In bright places they are greenish or bronze; under the roofs they are nearly colorless. The mats in the basins gather fine sediment, which the growth binds into ridges. These ridges alter the flow by very small degrees. Over many seasons, a basin may deepen in one corner and fill in another, or a trickle may be led toward a dry stretch of wall.

The inhabitants keep the town’s wet places connected. Threads grow across a channel when its water drops, then thicken as the damp returns. A blocked notch is gradually lined with soft tissue that swells and contracts with the weather, working loose grains of clay. They cannot always clear it. Some channels have gone dry, and the films beside them have dwindled to brittle traces. Elsewhere, new growth follows a seep down a wall and reaches the pool below.

They do not occupy the houses as people would. The rooms are part of the ground they live on: shaded hollows with reliable drips, sheltered timber and cool stone. Who fitted the slabs and laid the paths is not evident from the growths themselves. The oldest of them have flowed around fallen poles and through gaps left by shifted walls; younger ones have reached the distant shelters by way of the low, wet paths.

After prolonged rain, small rounded bodies appear along the mats. They swell until their skins split, releasing grains fine enough to drift in water. Most settle close by. A few travel out through the marsh or lodge in a crack on another rise, where, if the damp holds, a new film begins.

From one day to the next, almost nothing about them seems to change. A basin watched through a season tells more: a pale margin advances across its floor, a thread bridges two stones, a dark seam bends around a reed stem. The town remains quiet while its inhabitants spread through it, keeping to the water and making, little by little, more room for themselves.
