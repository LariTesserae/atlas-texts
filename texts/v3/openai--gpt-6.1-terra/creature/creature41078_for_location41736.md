The people of the shelf tend to know one another by their hands. A person who works the sieves has a pale dust settled into the lines of the palms; a cistern keeper’s fingertips are softened by water and scored by the edges of metal lids. The mechanics touch things as they pass: a bearing housing, a belt, the side of a cup trembling on a bench. They can often tell which engine is running without going inside the works.

At dawn, the water gatherers move through the outer terraces carrying nested pans. They leave the fullest ones for last, so that more moisture can collect while they work uphill. Children old enough to be trusted with a load come along before school, learning which stones shelter a pan from wind and which attract birds that overturn it. In a dry week there may be arguments over a few mouthfuls, but even then most households put out a saucer for the cliff birds. The birds keep insects from the gardens, and many people enjoy having them close.

The birds are small and dark-backed, with pale throats that flash when they turn beneath a bridge. Their nests are made of grass, root fibers, and whatever thread they can pull from a frayed belt or line. During nesting season, repairs to the bridges are planned around them when possible. A mechanic may have to wait until the young have flown before replacing a cable, or work one-handed around a nest while an angry parent darts at their cap.

The long-bodied grazers come down most often in the hour before dawn. They have narrow, careful feet and coats the color of damp stone, darker along the spine where moisture collects. They eat the pale grasses and the thick-leaved mats, taking a little from each patch before moving on. Their young stay close under the adults’ bellies on exposed ground. In mist they can pass surprisingly near the town; people find their rounded sleeping places among the moss, or a clean, blunt bite taken from a balcony vine.

A few families keep watch for them from the upper gardens, partly to protect seedlings and partly because the grazers are difficult to see elsewhere. Old residents remember individuals by torn ears or crooked horns, though they disagree about how long the animals live. When a grazer dies, its body is usually found by the birds first. Later, people may bring the bones down for tools and small fittings, leaving some of the finer pieces in place to weather back into the slope.

Most households have work both inside and out. Someone who tends a pump may also cultivate beans in a ruined workshop; a tile presser may spend evenings clearing a neighbor’s drain. The gardens are shared in uneven, practical ways. One person owns a room with good warmth, another carries soil up the stairs, and a third knows how to coax fruit from vines that have wandered down into the dark. Harvests are divided accordingly, sometimes after lengthy discussion.

The children grow up with a strong sense of levels. They know which courtyard can be reached by descending through a house, which bridge leads to a roof rather than another street, and where an old threshold lies hidden below the paving. They are taught to stop when cloud fills a street and wait until they can see the next wall. They learn, too, to keep grit out of the seams of a door: a little left there each day will make it hard to close by winter.

In the wet stone there is something else that may be alive. The brief glimmers appear most often in fine cracks where a little water remains after the surface has dried. Scraped into a dish, the material looks like an ordinary gray film threaded with green. It keeps its faint light for a short while, then goes dull; after several damp nights it may begin to glimmer again.

People have tried keeping it in jars, with mixed results. It persists longest on a chip of its own stone, left where air can reach it. No one knows whether the light comes from the film itself or from something smaller living within it. Children call it seam-light and carry bits home until a parent discovers the damp stones on a windowsill.

Along the oldest stairs, the film has been allowed to spread. Each evening, as the lamps falter, its small lights show the worn edges underfoot. People descending put a hand against the wall and watch for the next glimmer, while above them, under the bridges, the birds settle into their nests.
