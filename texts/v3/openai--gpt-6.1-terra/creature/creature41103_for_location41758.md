The red ropes are not all vegetation. Some have a second cord running through their centers, almost colorless, which can pull free when a stem breaks. In quiet water it spreads into a fringe of fine threads. In the current it gathers itself again and travels, fastening and unfastening its threads against the stones.

Each cord belongs to a larger body, though the body may be scattered over a great distance. It has no fixed outline. It occupies the hollows in a plant bed, the spaces beneath folded rock, the flooded rooms of the pumping station. Its threads wrap around roots without drawing anything from them. They enter cracks and widen there; they adhere to copper, to glass, to the undersides of loose sheets of metal. Where many threads meet, they thicken into a pale knot, soft on its outer surface and hard at the center.

There is one such knot inside the intact station. It lies against the casing of a turbine, and with every revolution it changes shape slightly. From it, threads pass under the doors and out through the broken conduit. Some follow the cables into the plants. Others extend down into the outcrop, where the black stone is still separating along new seams. When a seam opens, the threads inside it are often cut. Their ends recoil, then begin to branch.

The one in the station has been there long enough for several of its outer knots to have become bodies of their own. They remain joined to it by cords, although the cords are thin and sometimes broken for days. A separated knot can persist if it finds a place where the water strikes with sufficient force. It spreads a membrane over the opening, and the membrane shivers until it begins to grow.

They do not distinguish the pounding of the machines from the other movements of the basin. The turbine gives them a steady measure against which smaller tremors can be felt: a ridge coming apart, a cable striking its pylon, the long tearing sound of a plant bed being carried away. Their threads are most sensitive at the tips. A knot with threads in several places can feel a single blow arrive through each of them, and by the differences between those arrivals it can locate the blow.

They also feel things before they happen. At first this seems to be no different from feeling a vibration at a distance: a thread tightens, a knot turns, and later a stone falls. But sometimes the stone does not fall, or it falls somewhere the knot cannot reach. Sometimes a thread withdraws from an opening that has not yet appeared, leaving the place bare when the rock finally splits. The older knots make fewer needless withdrawals. They have accumulated a sense of which warnings can be followed and which must be endured.

The collapsed station shelters another body. It reaches through the fallen roof and into the same conduit as the first, though the conduit is crushed beneath a slab of stone. Where the two stations occupy the same outcrop, their threads come close enough to touch. The knots there swell and blanch. Cords are exchanged, then pulled back; new branches grow along paths the other body has already taken.

The two bodies share some of their outer knots. A knot may respond to the intact station for a while, then to the collapsed one. In the interval its threads remain still, even when the current presses hard against them. If the stations draw apart, the shared knot is stretched between them until one of its cords gives way. The torn end seals quickly. The remainder continues to answer whichever station it is still attached to.

They reproduce most often where two depths of ground meet. Their loose threads collect there with the grit and plant fragments, and are pressed into small dense pellets. Most are crushed. A few emerge with enough unbroken material at their centers to open again. These lodge in sheltered places and send out their first filaments. For a time each young knot is simply a patch of trembling tissue. It grows toward repeated blows, learning the shape of the basin from what reaches it.

A young body near the leaning pylons has spread across three stones and part of a green mat. Each electrical flash contracts it. In the darkness after a flash, it loosens and extends a little farther along the cable. The mat supporting it has torn free of the vertical water and now hangs a short distance away, still roughly upright. Some of the body remains attached to the mat, and some to the cable. The connection between them is thinning.

When it breaks, both parts may live. The part on the mat will drift until it catches, or until the mat itself comes apart. The part on the cable will continue to grow toward the flashes, drawing back each time and advancing again after the water has gone dark.
