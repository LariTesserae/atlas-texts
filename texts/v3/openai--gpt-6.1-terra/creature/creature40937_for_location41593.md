The oldest inhabitant is a plant with no obvious center. Its leaves rise all across the hollow, but they are joined beneath the floor by a branching body wrapped around the roots of the ceiling. A fan near one wall may be nourished by water drawn in at the other. If a stem is bitten through, the neighboring stems slowly bend toward the gap; in time their tissues meet and knit.

Its broad leaves are nearly transparent. The luminous bands moving through them reveal the narrow veins and, sometimes, the dark shape of a grazer clinging beneath. Each leaf makes a shallow cup where it joins the stem. There, water stays liquid longest, and several kinds of small animal spend their entire lives within reach of a single cup.

The smallest are flat-bodied scrapers, no larger than seeds. They have six hooked legs and a rasping mouth on the underside of the head. Their bodies are pale except for the gut, which glows faintly after a meal. They eat the luminous surface tissue in narrow trails, leaving the veins intact. The plant repairs the trails slowly, and an old leaf carries a record of generations: clear stripes, healed ridges, and the dark specks of eggs tucked beside the central vein.

The eggs are laid in clusters beneath a thin film. When the cup overflows, some are washed down the stem into the floor pelt. A few hatch there and climb toward the light. Most remain where they fall, feeding on the finer growth until it sends a blade upward and carries them into the leaves.

Beneath them move the many-legged grazers. They are soft, blind animals with blunt heads and close-set legs that grip even the most delicate strands. Their backs are dusted with the pale growth they have passed through. They eat it from below, cropping the pelt without quite breaking its surface, and leave behind smooth tunnels that gradually fill again. When they encounter a young mineral nodule, they work around it. Older nodules hold the shape of their passages as ridged hollows in the forming stone.

At the warmest seams, the grazers gather so densely that the floor lifts in slow waves. They also gather around the burrower when it sleeps. Its body gives off little heat of its own, but the root cavity where it curls is lined with living tissue, and moisture collects in the fur along its belly.

The burrower is broad and short-legged, with a heavy muzzle and eyes sealed under the skin. Its claws are worn flat from pushing through the pelt and worrying at soft mineral ribs. It eats roots, nodules and the animals it finds among them. Its coat is coarse on the outside and fine underneath, where spores lodge close to the skin. Some germinate while it sleeps, sending pale threads through the fur. When it wakes and squeezes out of its cavity, these threads tear, and living fragments catch on the floor behind it.

It rarely travels far. It may feed through one long waking, scrape a fresh hollow beside a water seam, and sleep there until the plant has grown around its back. Its old sleeping places can be found by their roundness and by the unusually thick tufts of growth at their edges.

The glassy fruiting bodies belong to several plants, not just the great connected one. The needle-furred stalks hold theirs in tight rows, protected beneath stiff scales. The curled blades produce little clear beads at floor level, where grazers brush against them. Inside each fruiting body is a speck of living tissue wound around a fragment of the vein that keeps it warm. If carried somewhere damp, the speck can begin again, drawing water toward itself and laying down its first grains of mineral matter.

The deep hum brings almost everything to a stop. Scrapers press flat against their leaves; grazers brace in their tunnels; even the burrower, if awake, becomes still. The roots shift against the ceiling during the note, and ice grains sometimes fall from their forks. When the sound subsides, feeding resumes. A fallen grain melts in a leaf cup or sinks into the pelt, and the water is taken up again.
