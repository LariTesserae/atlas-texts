The people of the hollow are known first by their hands. Even children have the pale, roughened fingers of people who work in salt water, and elders can tell one kind of stone from another by touch beneath the surface. They keep their nails short. In the bright hours their eyes are hidden behind slotted shades, so that strangers have to learn to read their faces from the mouth and the set of the head.

Most households occupy more than one level of a dwelling. The lower rooms are useful when they are dry, but no one expects them always to remain so. Beds and stores stand on stone feet; baskets have loops by which they can be raised to the rafters. A family may eat upstairs for a week while water passes through the room below, then carry everything down again when the channels change. The oldest people remember where water has entered before. They are listened to when a new wall is planned, though they do not always agree.

Children learn the lanes before they learn the roofs. They are taught to test a stepping-stone with the side of a foot and to leave a loose one where an older person can see it. They also learn which bridges have a scavenger underneath. The creatures do not generally trouble a person who lets them be, but they dislike being surprised, and a child who drops from a bridge onto one may be bitten through a boot.

The scavengers are called **underfeet** in some households, though other families use names derived from the noises they make: a breathy hiss when crowded, a low clicking when feeding. Their fur looks white at a distance and almost transparent when wet. Salt gathers in it in fine crusts. They have long bodies close to the ground, small ears, and feet that open widely on ice, each toe joined to the next by a tough fold of skin.

They eat fish offal, dead birds, shed shells and whatever can be picked from a drain. People tolerate them because they keep the channels from choking, although a careless household may find a whole fish dragged from its doorstep. An underfoot can pull more than its shape suggests. When several find something too large for one, they grip it at different points and work backward without haste, pausing whenever the ice moves beneath them.

They rear their young in dry cavities behind walls. In spring, or what passes for spring on the ridge, a mother may appear with three or four small ones following so close that their feet seem to overlap. The birds watch these processions from the masonry. If a young underfoot strays toward a nest, the birds make almost no sound, but the warning passes along the wall as wings lift and settle.

The birds are small and densely feathered, with dark skin around their eyes that reduces the glare. Their nests are shallow cups of down, fish fiber and whatever soft material they can steal from human doorways. A curtain with a frayed edge may be stripped clean in a morning. People mend their curtains rather than drive the birds away. A wall full of nesting birds gives notice of an approaching underfoot, a person on the roof, or a sudden change in the water below. Someone working at a gate will glance up at the masonry now and then; a whole row of birds facing the same way is worth looking at.

In the wet stone live the shelled grazers. Separately, each is a modest creature, pale as an onion skin, with a dark fleck of gut visible through its back. Their jointed legs cling to surfaces that seem too smooth to hold them. Together they can cover a wall from the waterline to the height of a hand, shifting in slow waves as individuals climb over one another to reach fresh growth.

They do not all move at once. At the edge of a draining pocket, the outermost animals turn first, and those behind follow where the stone remains wet. If water arrives, a few venture into it; more come after them, until the crack appears to pour animals. People gathering water lift them out of their vessels and put them back against the wall. Occasionally a large mass enters a house through a seam in the floor. The family opens the door and guides it away with strips of wet hide, careful not to crush more than they must. Crushed grazers bring underfeet, and underfeet bring the birds down from their nests, and soon the whole threshold is occupied.

The grass at the central seep has its own keepers. They are not a separate class of people, but members of several households who live close enough to notice when a stone has shifted or a root has been exposed. They replace the stones loosely, leaving room for the blades. In the coldest weather they sometimes lay a screen over the patch, supported above the grass so that it is not flattened. No one cuts it for fodder. Its roots have held the bank through more than one season when the water came hard.

There are dogs in some houses, though few enough that each is known throughout the settlement. They are thick-coated and narrow-eyed, with their feet wrapped when they must travel beyond the hollow. Inside it, they are trained not to chase the birds or worry the underfeet. An old dog may sleep through a scavenger passing its nose, yet wake at the first click of a gate being lifted: that sound means someone is going out, and going out may mean food.

The people mend constantly. They mend gates, boots, curtains and the woven sleeves that protect their arms when they reach into cracks. They keep odd lengths of root and bone because a gate that fits one channel may need only a small addition to fit another. Their clothing is marked by the work: knees darkened by wet stone, cuffs stiff with salt, shoulders polished where baskets rub.

In the hours when the glare is strongest, those who can retreat indoors. They eat fish, stored fat and broths made with water chosen from the least salty seeps. They talk quietly, partly from habit and partly because the houses share walls. A person can hear a neighbor setting down a bowl, or a baby being soothed, or the patient scraping of a shade being recut for a child whose face has grown.

News travels by these shared walls and by the lanes. Much of it concerns water: a new seep behind a sleeping room, a stone that has begun to turn, a gate needed at the upper bridge. Other news concerns births, quarrels, repairs and journeys. A household expecting a child clears a high shelf for the things that must stay dry. When someone dies, their tools are cleaned carefully; useful stones from their house remain where they are unless the family chooses to move them.

When the flow reverses, people look to see what it is carrying. A loose stone traveling uphill can lodge beneath a bridge just as firmly as one traveling down. Someone turns a gate, someone farther along raises another, and a person on a roof may signal where the water has begun to gather. Underfeet emerge and face into the current. The birds draw in their wings. The grazers come past on the ice or cling to the stones beneath it.

By evening, if the water has settled, the inhabitants walk the altered lanes. They check the lower rooms and the places where old walls stand inside new ones. They set stones for tomorrow’s crossing, knowing some may not be there in the morning. Then curtains fall over the doorways. Behind them people eat and talk, while the birds settle into the masonry and the underfeet take up their places beneath the bridges, close enough to the lanes to feel the next change.
