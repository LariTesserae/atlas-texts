The people of the hollow are used to looking down as they walk. The paths bend around living things, and a misplaced foot can crush a cushion plant that has taken longer than a person’s life to spread across a handspan. Children learn the paths before they learn the names of all the cracks. At first they follow one another with their arms out, stepping from one smooth patch to the next. Later they take shortcuts, and are corrected when the shortcuts begin to show.

Their clothes are made in layers: soft combed fiber against the skin, tougher woven strips over it, and hides where the wind finds a way beneath the shelf. The fiber catches dust and resin smoke, so that an old coat may be difficult to tell from the stone on which it has been laid. Some people wear little carved toggles or pieces of polished bone. Others keep their tools close to hand, tucked through belts or hung around their necks on cord.

They do not all work slowly. A bird falling from a nest, a jar breaking, or the cry of a grazer caught in a pen can bring people running. But much of their work has to be done at the pace of the material. A root is pressed a little at a time. A stalk is soaked in its own sap and teased apart before its fibers can be twisted. A loose roof slab is shifted, tested, and shifted again until it sits without rocking. People work beside one another without needing to fill every silence, though the hollow itself is rarely quiet enough for silence to last.

The bitter fluid pressed from roots is strained through fiber. The youngest children dislike it, and some older people still make a face as they drink. A portion is kept in sealed jars against a season when the plants yield less. The jars are checked often: a small flaw in a stopper can undo months of care. Empty root skins are not thrown far away. They may be dried for fuel, softened for a pen animal, or worked into a warm fissure where smaller creatures will break them down.

The grazers know the people’s paths almost as well as the people do. Some live in pens, where they are watched when their young are small; others sleep under the shrubs and come close enough to steal trimmings. Their ears are thin and mobile, turning separately toward a voice or a scrape overhead. They feed in short bouts, then settle with their legs tucked beneath them. When a shrub has been clipped too hard, its bare twigs can remain visible for years, and the pen is moved.

The lizards take what the grazers do not. They wait near the warm cracks for insects moving between the stalks, or climb the stone rings to catch those drawn to resin smoke. Their flattened bodies fit under slabs that seem to have no space beneath them. In cool weather they lie close together, but when the rock is warm, each tends to claim a patch of its own. Children sometimes mark a favorite by a torn toe or a pale scar and look for it each morning.

The birds overhead are harder to know individually. They leave and return through the bright mouth of the hollow, passing for a moment across the view of the distant peaks. On the nest ledges they are heavy and awkward, shifting their feet among old feathers and droppings; in the open air their wings hold them up with little visible effort. Their young call from the holes long after they have grown nearly as large as their parents. Beneath a favored nest the ground is whitened, and certain plants grow more thickly there.

The dark insects occupy the stalks from root to seed head. Some eat the soft tissue within the joints; others hunt them through the narrow passages they leave. A stalk brought into a workshop may contain a whole hidden traffic. The person stripping it taps it first against a stone, and insects fall out and hurry toward the nearest cover. A few are saved for bait or fed to a young lizard kept temporarily in a clay bowl.

The people know which dwellings have grown colder and which walls need lifting as the plants press against them. When someone dies, their house is not necessarily emptied at once. A relative may use the roof for shade while sorting tools, or leave a jar where it has always stood until there is need of it. If no one takes up the dwelling, dust gathers in the corners, cord loosens, and the cushions begin to close over its threshold.

The children who once played there will later remember where the doorway was. Their children may know only that the pale growth is unusually level in that spot, and that, if a tool is pushed carefully beneath it, the edge of a fitted stone can still be felt.
