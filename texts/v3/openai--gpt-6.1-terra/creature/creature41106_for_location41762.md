The most abundant inhabitants are the small plants of the floor. Each has a narrow crown of leaves and a pale, jointed stem that runs just below the surface. The stems fork whenever they meet open water or a tear in the mat. A white thread enters the gap first; then a bud follows, thickens, and opens a new crown. Many crowns can be traced back to one stem, though the older connections are difficult to find among the blackened leaves.

Their broad leaves are thin enough for the veins to show through. In dry air they lie open, gathering light. Rain striking them causes their bases to swell, and the blades fold inward along the veins. Water runs from their tips into the channels between the stems. When the shower passes, they reopen unevenly, exposing patches of the shining floor one after another.

The tower plants begin among these low crowns as clusters of blunt shoots. Each shoot grows beside the others, adding a length of hollow stem and binding itself to its neighbors with flat green straps. The central space remains open. As the bundle rises, older stems die and become part of its wall; new ones climb over them, so a tower may contain both soft growing tissue and stems toughened by years of wetting and drying.

Water collects inside the chambers. Fine roots grow down the inner walls and hang in it, taking up whatever has washed from the crown. The low notes of the towers come from air moving past these curtains of roots and through the narrow joints between chambers. A tower with a split wall sounds different after every shower. Some go silent as they fill, then begin to knock again as the water drains away.

The plants that make the temporary walls grow faster and more loosely. Their stems lie parallel near the surface, joined by leaves that trap floating fragments. As water gathers on one side, the stems swell and turn upward together. New growth rises along the top while roots grip the floor below. Eventually the weight of the pool pulls some stems free. Those that remain attached may hold for a while, stretched nearly horizontal, before the whole wall comes apart. Each floating length carries buds and short roots; many are already growing when the water comes to rest.

At the margins of pools live small, soft plants with nearly colorless leaves. They are most conspicuous at dawn, when they lift above the water on clear stalks. By midday they have usually folded down again. Their roots enter the dark felt beneath the pool, where old leaves are breaking apart. When a basin drains, these plants collapse onto the wet floor, but their buds can remain buried there until water returns.

Other life is finer than the white threads of new roots. Films of bacteria coat the submerged stems and give the undersides of old leaves a slippery surface. Fungi spread through the dead layers in branching strands, entering the torn ends of fallen stalks. Together they soften what the larger plants have built. Water carries the dissolved remains downward, where living roots take them up again.

The plateau’s warmth comes partly from this steady work in the deep, wet layers. In places, a hand pressed into a fresh break would meet cool rainwater at the surface and warmer felt beneath. The heat brings vapor up through the gaps, and the roots continue growing into it: white tips entering old dark seams, dividing there, and holding the loosened material in place.
