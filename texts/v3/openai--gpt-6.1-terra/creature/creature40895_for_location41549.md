The people are broad across the shoulders and narrow through the hips, with long arms that hang below their knees when they stand on a terrace. Their hands have thick, sensitive pads; the skin there is almost colorless, and the faint ridges of bone show through. They test a new deposit by pressing it with the whole palm, sometimes keeping their hand there while others pass, feeling the small changes as the warm water runs through it.

Their faces are wide and rather flat, with eyes set far apart. Folds below the jaw open and close as they draw water over their gills. A person carrying a heavy stone may stop and brace it against a pillar, the folds working rapidly, before going on. Their hair grows in short, dense curls that catch the white sediment. After a cloudy spell, children pick it from one another’s heads in little clumps.

They are dark when young. With age, pale lines appear along their arms and backs, marking old cuts and places where mineral grains have lodged beneath the skin. The oldest people are often mottled white. They sit near the seep basins with their hands in the warmer water and sort the day’s gathered stone by sound, knocking pieces together and setting aside those that ring clearly.

Most sleeping chambers are flooded. The people rest in shallow hollows lined with soft strips of leaf, sometimes several to a hollow, their limbs loosely crossed so that a sleeper stirring in the current does not drift away. The air chambers are useful for keeping certain stores dry: bundles of fiber, dry mineral powders, and fired-clay pieces that would soften again if left soaking. Someone entering one rises through its flooded shaft and holds on to a rim just below the surface, working with their head in water and their hands in air. Children learn the reach of each chamber before they are trusted to fetch things from it.

The pale strands grown in the flooded rooms are gathered in handfuls and pressed into cakes. People also cut the tender centers from cultivated stalks and scrape small animals from the undersides of rocks. On feeding days, the paths fill with loose fragments, and little silver-bodied swimmers follow the workers home, darting in to take what they can. They are tolerated until they find the storage rooms. Then a child may be sent after them with a basket, returning some time later with a few fish and a great deal of stirred-up silt.

The outer fields support other lives. Clear, many-jointed creatures cling to the living fences and pare away dead material. Tiny grazers leave winding marks across the mineral crusts; where enough of them feed, the stone remains dark while the untouched patches grow pale. Long worms live around the warm basins, their tips showing above the sediment when the water is still. The people leave most of them alone, though a basin thick with worms is cleaned before it becomes a place for food.

The great grazer is known by a scar on one flank, a white fork visible through the fronds before the rest of it appears. It has passed through the settlement for longer than the youngest adults can remember. When it comes, someone follows at a distance to see which fences it breaks. The repairs are usually made by several households together; a loosened fence lets the field soil drift, and what is lost from one terrace can cover a path on the next.

The children begin with loose stones. They build low walls in sheltered places and watch what settles against them. A wall may catch a fan of clean sand, or a mess of leaf fragments, or nothing at all. Older children are given a short section of path to keep clear. They learn to notice a swelling seam before it has pushed the rock apart, and to move the marker rope while there is still a firm place to put their feet.

When the ridge clouds, work farther down the slope continues for a while. People return carrying loads that become whiter as they approach home. If the cloud grows too thick to see the next marker, they wait wherever they are, touching the rope now and then to keep their place. Voices travel through the water from one waiting group to another, low calls with long pauses between them.

Afterward, everyone helps clear the deposits from the most-used routes. The silt is brushed from thresholds and shaken from the field leaves. Some of it is carried to weak places in the terraces, where roots may bind it. On the roofs, much of it is left. The weight is useful, provided the slabs beneath are sound; each household listens as its roof is tested, and if a slab answers with a scrape, the stones around it are lifted away.
