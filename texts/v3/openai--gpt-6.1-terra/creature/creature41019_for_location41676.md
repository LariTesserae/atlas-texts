Under the leaves live the smallest grazers, clear-bodied creatures no longer than a fingernail. Their soft feet grip the wet surfaces, and a dark green thread shows through each body where it has eaten. They feed in rows, leaving faint clean tracks in the film on the leaves. When a leaf tilts, they gather along its lowest edge, riding it down until its tip touches water. Some let go there; others turn and climb back.

The heavy amphibians in the channels have broad, flat heads and eyes set far apart. Moss and loose green filaments lie across their backs, so that from above they resemble patches of submerged ground. They breathe through folds at their throats, opening and closing them with a slow, wet pulse. Now and then one lifts its head to take a grazer from a low leaf. The leaf springs upward, scattering droplets onto the animal’s closed eyes.

The long-legged animals step carefully between pools, raising their feet high through the fronds. Their legs are jointed in several places and darken toward the tips, where the toes spread against the yielding soil. They browse on the tender rolled leaves at the centers of plants. When they pause, their long necks sway a little above the vegetation, following movements too small to see from the ground. Their young stay close beneath them, picking at fallen fragments.

In the moss, many-footed animals travel along the hidden channels. Their overlapping plates are dull brown on top and pale underneath. They eat dead stems and the soft growth that gathers around submerged roots; fine particles drift behind them and settle wherever the water slows. The pale fish follow these trails, nosing through the disturbed water. They can cross from one pool to another beneath the moss, though occasionally a back or a flicking tail breaks the surface.

Higher up, winged creatures move in short flights between the fronds. Some have narrow wings that tick against the leaves; others have broad, damp-edged wings and settle heavily, folding themselves around a stem. They call from concealed perches, each answering a voice somewhere deeper in the green. Near the ground, the amphibians answer one another too, their throats swelling into pale round sacs.

Everything has somewhere to feed, and everything is fed upon. A fish takes one of the small clear grazers; another grazer slips into the water from the same leaf. A long-legged browser moves on, and the bent plants rise behind it, crowded with the creatures that remained. Across the plain the calls continue, changing from moment to moment without becoming quieter.
