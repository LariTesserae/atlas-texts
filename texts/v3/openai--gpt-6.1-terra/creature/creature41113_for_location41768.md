Beneath the shelves lives a pale, flattened animal called a seamling. Most are shorter than a finger. Their bodies are soft enough to pass through cracks that look too narrow to admit them, and their undersides grip the wet stone with a fringe of minute, moving cups. In daylight they remain below, where the water runs through the rock.

A seamling feeds on the film that grows along submerged surfaces. It also catches the tiny creatures carried in the current, spreading a pair of translucent lobes into the flow and drawing them slowly back to its mouth. Its skin is almost colorless, though the green or brown of a recent meal can sometimes be seen through it. A dark thread runs along each side of its body: rows of sensory cells that register changes in pressure and vibration.

The constant roar does not seem to trouble them. They live within it as surface animals live within daylight, noticing the small disturbances that pass through it: a shifting root, a stone settling, the different pressure of rainwater entering a seam. When a surface cup begins to overflow, seam­lings sometimes gather beneath its outlet. The fresh water brings suspended matter down with it.

After rain, especially at night, a few come above the stone. They move slowly through the moss and over the thinnest films of water, their bodies drawing up and lengthening like wet leaves. They graze beside the pale snails, whose clear tracks occasionally cross their own. By morning they have usually returned below. A lizard may catch one that lingers on an exposed shelf, though most keep to the shadow beneath roots.

Their eggs are laid singly in sheltered cracks, each fastened to the stone by a short clear stalk. Newly hatched seam­lings are almost transparent and spend their first weeks in the quieter water near the basin’s upper wall. As they grow, they move into deeper channels. The largest may live for several years, following the water through passages no wider than their bodies, while the grove above fills, spills and clears.
