The long grazer has no head distinct from its body. Its mouth opens along one side, a seam that travels slowly from end to end as it feeds. It takes only the soft margins of leaves, following the places where a current has just turned them pale. The bitten plants seal themselves with a clear film, and for some time afterward its route hangs through the forest as a line of small, shining scars.

Other grazers find these trails. They will follow one for hours, though the animal that made it may have moved away days before, or may not yet have passed. Where two trails meet, the grazers sometimes coil loosely around one another. Fibres caught on their skins work free and drift between them; some settle and grow. The animals separate without any visible haste, leaving a new tuft of vegetation where they touched.

There are small creatures in the braided trunks. Most are almost transparent, with dark digestive threads that show through their bodies. They live on the loose tissue shed when stems rub together, and on the fine spores that lodge in the folds. When a trunk bends, they move deeper into it. When it straightens, they emerge to feed, extending delicate limbs into the water and drawing them back with particles caught at their tips.

The silver-bellied animal above the crowns is seldom seen whole. It may be one of several, or the same one crossing a place whose distance has folded. Its belly is covered in flat scales that tilt independently, catching what light reaches that depth. Beneath it, the upper leaves close a little, then open again. Small creatures rise from them in a cloud and settle elsewhere. Whether the animal eats them or simply disturbs them is difficult to tell.

In the clearings, the stones support thin colonies of life, though nothing takes root in the enclosed silt. The colonies are made of tiny cups joined at their bases. They open toward a passing current and close when it has gone. Some open in sequence around a ring, one after another, until the movement returns to where it began. In the brighter clearing, the cups remain open longer. Minute bodies detach from them and swim into the light; many return to the stones, but some vanish against the brightness.

A larger inhabitant sometimes rests beside a ring. It is broad and flat, with the texture of a leaf worn nearly through. It lies partly over the stones, letting water pass beneath its body, and gathers what the current brings. When sediment lifts from the bed, it raises its edges and waits. Grains settle on its back; pale organisms crawl among them. By the time it moves on, a small community has formed there, and the forest closes behind it.

Near the places where distance stretches, roots and stems shelter animals found nowhere in the ordinary spaces between trunks. Some have long bodies that pass into a gap and remain there while their feeding limbs work on either side. Others live entirely within the stretched distance, visible for a moment when leaves part, then too far away to distinguish. A few carry pieces of familiar growth on their backs: a dark cord, a fan of pale tissue, a leaf whose edge is still healing from a grazer’s bite.

The forest feeds on what falls through it. Dead tissue loosens from the crowns and is caught below; soft bodies graze it down, smaller ones finish what they leave, and fine material settles among the roots. At intervals the sediment rises again, carrying spores and fragments with it. When it comes down, some grains lodge in the clearings, some against the trunks, and some in places that were farther away when they began to fall.
