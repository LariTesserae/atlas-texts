The people of the basin know many animals first by the sounds they make against stone. A grazer walking alone produces a slow, hollow sequence, each foot set down with care. A group makes a softer, almost continuous scraping. The little followers are quicker: their claws tick over the pavement and stop whenever the larger feet stop.

The grazers are taller through the shoulder than the back, with thick folds of hide over their legs and broad, flattened heads. Their pale mouths open against the wet surfaces of the drainage fields. They draw their lips along the stone, lifting the dark film in strips, and sometimes pause to work a loosened piece free with their tongues. Their calves begin by feeding on the low walls and the undersides of steps, where the growth is sheltered from the rain. Adults reach farther up. On walls beside the older canals, their feeding has left a long pale band at a fairly uniform height.

They are generally untroubled by people, though they dislike being pressed into narrow passages. A full-grown animal can brace itself across a doorway and remain there for most of a morning. Shopkeepers in the busier districts fit their gates with rounded bars and leave a little room between the gate and the goods inside. Children learn not to come between a calf and the animal accompanying it. They also learn which individuals will allow a hand to rest briefly on the warm hide behind the shoulder.

The small followers have narrow faces and flexible forefeet. They pick through the scraps raised by the grazers, but also hunt in the shallow water for worms, larvae and anything else stirred from the seams. They sleep in groups under platforms, packed together in dry pockets above the waterline. Around the foundries their coats are darkened by soot; near the reservoirs they are often pale with mineral dust. They come into workshops when doors are left open, and workers sometimes find their small prints across a bench that had been wiped clean the night before.

The reservoir birds are heavier than they look in flight. On the walls they stand on thick, jointed legs, their bodies pitched slightly forward, their wings folded in deep overlapping layers. From a distance a flock can look like another dark retaining wall. They feed in the water and along the wet edges, probing with broad bills, turning up films and the small animals living beneath them. Some dive, vanishing with surprisingly little disturbance and returning farther out among the floating bodies.

Their nesting colonies occupy warehouses whose roofs have failed or whose doors have been left permanently open. They gather scraps of packing fiber, wire, scales of rust and dried fungal growth into low nests. The chicks are loud, pale-mouthed creatures that sit close against the adults until they are large enough to move through the crowd without being trampled. During the nesting season, workers taking a shortcut past one of these buildings may hear a clatter start near the door and travel inward through the whole colony.

People collect eggs where the flocks are large enough to bear the loss. They also gather shed feathers from the edges of the reservoirs and fish out the dead before the bodies drift against the sluices. Some residents make a living from the birds directly; others encounter them chiefly as a recurring task. A pump operator may have to clear a screen twice in one shift after a flock has settled near an intake. A household above a nesting warehouse may keep its windows shut for several weeks, even in the warmth.

Much of the city’s food comes through the freight yards: sealed loads carried up into the basin and distributed by rail and tram. Within the city, people cultivate the dark films on wetted stone and grow fungi in chambers supplied with warm water from the works. Some of this feeds people, some feeds animals, and some is processed in the factories. Workers tend the cultivated surfaces carefully, controlling the flow over them and scraping them at the right thickness. A wall left too long can become slick enough that sheets of growth slide off into the channel.

The people who do this work often have their hands in water. Their skin bears the fine pale residue that dries around the nails and in the creases of the fingers. Workers elsewhere carry black dust from the brickworks or metallic stains from pipe fittings. At the end of a shift, they gather under the covered platforms, eat from tins or paper packets and wait while a herd clears the tracks. The delay is familiar enough that conversations begin and finish in it.

In the apartment blocks, families have made use of the height above the damp streets. Washing hangs on enclosed galleries. Boots stand on racks inside doors. Windowsills hold small useful things—jars, tools, cups set out to dry—and are wiped often where the haze and rain leave a film. People know the calls of the reservoir colonies and can usually tell, without looking out, whether the birds are settling or have been disturbed.

There are old residents who remember different routes through the city: a canal before it was bridged, a warehouse before the birds took it, a street before the larger grazers began using it regularly. Their grandchildren know the present crossings better. They watch from the stairs as the animals pass, count calves, and notice when an unfamiliar bird appears among the usual flock.

At night, people still work in the pump houses and at the furnaces. Others sleep with the machinery audible through their walls, interrupted by the drag of feet below a window or the distant rising cry from a reservoir. By morning, the water has continued through its channels, and fresh arcs have appeared on the dark stone where the grazers fed in the mist.
