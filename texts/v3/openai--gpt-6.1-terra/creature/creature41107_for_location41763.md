The shrimp are almost invisible until they move. Each is a clear little jointed body with a dark thread of gut, and thousands work through the shallows with their heads down. They pick at the film on stones and at particles settling out of the water. When a foot enters a pool, they rise around it in a pale flicker, then return to the disturbed mud before the footprint has filled.

The snails leave slower marks: narrow trails through silt and clean arcs on the undersides of shells. Some carry a fringe of younger snails on their own shells. Worms hold their rear ends buried in the floor while their feeding ends sweep the water; a passing fish makes them vanish into the mud, leaving a patch of pinholes. Beneath loose plates of mineral crust, crabs press together in layers. They come out to feed when darkness falls across a pool, though darkness may be no more than a person passing between the water and a lamp.

The fish are colorless, blunt-headed things, their eyes reduced to small marks beneath the skin. They move close to the bottom, feeling the uneven floor with sensitive mouths and sides. A fish will nose between a cluster of crabs and a patch of worms without disturbing either, then turn suddenly to take a shrimp. Their young occupy the narrowest spaces under the walkways, where larger fish cannot easily follow.

Around the warm vents, the larvae live almost against one another. They graze on the films that grow there, gripping the rock as water passes over them. Their skins catch the lamplight when a current or a shadow makes them bend together. Farther from the vents, other films spread over clay and limestone: pale bacterial growths in the dark, green algae near the lamps. Fine organic matter arrives with the water from above, and animals eat it, eat the films, and eat one another. Nothing that dies remains exposed for long, although shells and harder pieces may sink into the accumulating floor.

The ferns beneath the grate are small and tough. Their fronds collect droplets from the air and grit falling through the opening. Moss grows along the cables in damp cushions, thickest where the lamp is close; below it, green smears mark the masonry down to the waterline. These are bright places by the chamber’s standards, and shrimp crowd beneath them to feed on whatever washes down.

People still come to the running pump. They leave fresh footprints beside old ones, and sometimes a dropped bolt or a strip of cloth catches on a walkway leg. The animals gather around these things as they gather around shell and stone. A worker standing still may feel shrimp touching the submerged part of a boot, while snails travel up its side.

The pump’s noise fills their habitat without pause. Its vibration reaches through iron and stone, through the water and the soft floor. Fish and shrimp turn aside from a footfall carried through the mud; crabs tighten beneath their shelter when the walkway above them shudders. In the quieter spaces under the crusts, worms extend again and continue feeding.
