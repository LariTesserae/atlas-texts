On the undersides of some of the limestone shelves lives a thin, nearly colourless growth. It follows the stone closely enough that, seen through the water, it looks at first like a difference in the rock: a faint clouding along a groove, or a sheen where the surface is smooth.

It is made of many small organisms held together in a soft film. The ones nearest the water take what they need from the passing current. Those beneath them live on what filters down through the film and on the remains of their neighbours. Fine grains sometimes settle over them. Where the film is thick enough, its surface slowly rises around a grain until the grain is enclosed.

The growth is not spread evenly. It is most substantial where the current slips under a ledge and is slowed, but does not stop. In the deepest still hollows it becomes thin and patchy. On exposed stone it is rubbed back by moving silt. A few patches have reached the cracks where the green strands are rooted, and climb a short way up their bases before loosening into the water.

From time to time, a small piece separates. It may drift across the basin and catch in another groove, or pass out through the low channel. Most pieces come apart. A few remain lodged long enough to begin growing again.

The distant vibration passes through the colonies as it does through the stone. It trembles their loose edges and shifts water through their finest openings, though they grow just as slowly when it is faint. They have no evident way of finding its source.

In daylight, the green strands are easier to see. They make food in the light and hold fast through the long, nearly motionless hours. Around their roots and beneath them, the pale film gathers what the water brings. Between the patches lies a great deal of bare limestone, clean enough to show every shallow groove.
