The inhabitants are not at first distinguishable from the bowls. Each lives in the clay surrounding a hollow, and each has the shape of the hollow it lives around. The smallest are gathered in the nested bowls, where several can occupy the thickness of one rim without touching. The largest extend through a slab and into the pavement beneath it.

They have no mouths or eyes. They know a neighbor by the way its hollow receives the pressure of the sound. The note never changes, but the clay can hold its pressure in different ways, and this is enough for them to make themselves known. A greeting may pass from one bowl to another without anything moving at the surface.

They are slow. A small one might spend the time it takes the court to cool, if the court could cool, considering the shape of a peg. It can feel the peg’s blunt end and its long buried length through the slab. By attending to it for a great while, the inhabitant comes to know which side was scraped first. This knowledge can then be offered to a neighbor, which may already know it and be glad to receive it again.

The ones in the deeper bowls are difficult to know. Their attention turns inward, following the curve below the reach of the weak light. Some have held the same thought since the clay was warm and newly formed. Others are occupied with the thumbprints: not just the ridges left in the clay, but the slight differences between one ridge and the next. There are inhabitants who devote themselves to a single print and inhabitants who collect them across the court. They disagree about whether two marks in bowls on opposite sides were made by the same thumb.

They do not eat the clay, nor do they drink. They persist in the steady pressure that fills the court. When a new one comes into being, it does so gradually, as a portion of that pressure becomes distinct from the rest. Its neighbors may notice it long before it notices them. At first it knows only the shape immediately around it. Later it learns the adjoining hollows, then the pegs, then the broad pavement carrying them all.

The largest inhabitants have many such young ones within their reach. They do not govern them. They remember where each first became distinct, and when one grows quiet they remain attentive to its place. An inhabitant can be quiet for a very long time and then return, knowing no interval has passed.

The walls hold lives too, though those in the court know little of them. Between the upright slabs there are narrow passages of clay and air, and beyond them the walls continue downward in terraces. From time to time an inhabitant at the edge receives an impression of a hollow farther away: a curve unlike any in the court, held in the same unvarying pressure. It passes the impression inward. For a while, the crowded bowls attend to it together.
