The larger inhabitants are easiest to find in the pumping stations, where the walls are warm and the floor gives a faint report of everything moving below it. They lie along the stone with their heads turned sideways, one hand spread flat against the surface. In that position they can tell whether a flywheel is coming round, whether a gate has seated properly, and sometimes whether a bridge several streets away has begun to lower.

They have broad, flexible feet and low, heavy bodies carried on four limbs. When they need their hands they can rise for a time, bracing themselves with a thick tail against a wall or the edge of a channel. Their skin is dark gray, loose at the joints, and nearly smooth except for fine ridges along the palms and soles. Water gathers in these ridges and stays there. An individual crossing a dry patch leaves a pattern of narrow wet lines rather than a single print.

Their eyes are small, but not vestigial. A lamp in a shaft is visible to them from a considerable distance, and in its light they can sort tools and recognize one another’s faces. On the roads they rely more on touch. Their feelers, shorter and stouter than those of the drain creatures, sweep close to the ground. Before stepping across an expansion joint, they pause to feel the width of the water and the position of the opposite edge.

They live in groups in the lower chambers of the towers and exchange halls. The chambers are divided by hanging strips of ribbed metal that stir in the drafts from the shafts. Each group keeps a dry ledge for sleeping and a shallow pool where its members soak and clean mineral crust from one another’s skin. Youngsters spend much of their time in these pools. They are clumsy on the station floors at first, sliding on the polished stone, and learn the routes to the outer doors by following an adult’s tail.

Their work is mostly slow and close to the ground. They clear deposits from grilles, pack the edges of joints, and feel along pipes for places where the strain has become uneven. A pump beginning to turn may bring several of them to a station, though they do not hurry unless the motion is wrong. They carry wedges, scrapers, and coils of braided cord in slings beneath their bodies. At a difficult gate, one may hold the cord taut while another listens through it for the small change that means the mechanism has caught.

They eat the pale drain creatures, though not many at once, and cultivate sheets of soft mineral-feeding growth on removable plates. The plates stand where waters of different temperatures meet. Every few days they are lifted, scraped, and returned; the scraping is mixed with water and left to settle into a thick paste. After heavy rain the groups exchange plates, tools, and news in the halls. They communicate through touch as well as sound, pressing their hands together or against the same resonant surface. A conversation conducted on a metal panel can continue for some time without carrying beyond the room.

The small jointed grazers live throughout the drains, in numbers far greater than their rare appearances suggest. Their young shelter in seams above the ordinary waterline, where the stone stays wet but the current cannot sweep them away. When a warm flow changes course, whole colonies move after it. Their abandoned seams remain lined with shed skins, so thin they fold against the wall in the next rain.

In the open reservoir live long, flat-bodied animals that are seldom seen except when they rise beneath a lamp shaft. They rest against the submerged walls and feed on the growth there, shifting position as the masonry moves. The basin dwellers watch for them: when several leave a wall at once and gather near the surface, a change in the water may be coming before any gauge has registered it.

Not every movement is understood. Sometimes a group will leave its chamber and wait together on the causeway, all facing the same direction, while nothing visible happens. Later the stone may give a single dull pulse underfoot, or a line may cross the reservoir. At other times nothing follows. After a while they return inside, and one of them usually checks the grilles on the way.
