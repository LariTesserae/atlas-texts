The people of the city live mostly inside its walls.

A wall is thick enough to contain passages, sleeping hollows and chambers where several families can gather around a warm pipe. From outside, these spaces are almost impossible to distinguish from solid stone. Their entrances lie underwater or open into the enclosed walkways, where a hand can find them by following a raised seam.

The inhabitants are broad, low-bodied animals with six limbs. Four carry their weight; the forward pair are shorter and end in jointed pads, each pad divided into many independently moving points. They use these to grip wet stone, separate food from its shell, tend machinery and read the small movements passing through a wall. Their skin is dark and thick on the back, pale and densely folded underneath. The folds must remain wet. A person who has spent too long in a heated chamber will pause on the way out and lower their belly into a basin of cold water.

They have no eyes. Along either side of the head are chambers of fluid and fine, suspended membranes. These tell them the direction of a sound and, more precisely, the shape of an object that sound has passed through. In the enclosed walkways, where the animal roar sets everything trembling, they can sense another person at a considerable distance: weight on the floor, breath against the wall, the faint shift of pressure as a body passes through air.

They speak by pressing their forward pads against one another. A conversation can be held through a wall or floor, though the building carries some frequencies better than others. In a crowded chamber people keep several limbs in contact, so that a remark may pass around the group before its maker has finished. Strangers meeting in a walkway touch the wall first, announcing themselves from a distance. To approach silently is not necessarily threatening, but it is considered thoughtless.

Their movements are quicker than those of the great veil-feeders, though still deliberate. In open water they swim with slow strokes, carrying a pocket of air against the folded underside. They can remain submerged for hours, working among the piers and the crowded living crusts. They harvest the flat animals there with thin, flexible blades, taking a patch from one side of a pier and leaving its neighbors intact. In warm-water chambers they keep trays of smaller animals, turning the trays from time to time so that both faces receive the mineral-rich flow.

The people eat flesh, including that of the immense drifting animals when one dies near the city. A death can feed a district for a long while. The body is secured to the lower piers, and crews work on it in shifts, cutting away portions as the scavengers gather. Some is eaten immediately; some is packed into cool compartments in the walls. The remaining bones and tough sheets of connective tissue are useful for filters, brushes and the flexible linings of pipes.

Young are carried in a pouch beneath the body until their gripping pads are strong enough to hold on. Afterward they cling to a parent’s back, feeling the routes of the city through that body’s movements. They learn the walkways first by their rhythms: a hollow section that answers with a double knock, a warm stretch where the pipe pulses beneath the floor, a sagging joint that gives a little under each step. Later they learn to cross these places alone.

Most adults know a small part of the city intimately and the rest as a set of directions carried in stories: follow the wall that hums at two pitches; turn where the floor slopes toward the cold pipe; wait at the third suspended room until someone inside feels you there. Routes change. A shelf rises, a pier tilts, a lower passage fills. People who tend the buildings record these changes by setting their pads against prepared strips of wall, leaving patterns that other workers can feel months or years later.

When a tower begins to lean, its residents may continue living in it while the wall slowly brings it upright. They adjust shelves, move water basins and mark the changing angle in the stone. If a walkway buckles, they close it and hang a new one, fastening it to neighboring structures whose deep stresses they have learned to read. There are specialists who spend much of their lives listening to those stresses. They lie with their bodies against the thickest cables, feeling for changes too gradual to be recognized in a single visit.

People sleep in groups, usually in cool hollows close to water. They rest against one another or against the chamber wall, and the building’s vibration passes through them all night. Visitors who have come from a different district sometimes wake because the local resonance is unfamiliar. After a few nights they begin to sleep through it.

The city’s oldest inhabitants remember streets now submerged and rooms lifted far above the water by rising stone. They can lead younger people to a place where a familiar entrance ought to be, then feel along the wall until they find it higher than before, or slanting beneath the surface. If the room is still sound, someone may clear the entrance and use it again. If not, its contents are carried elsewhere, and the animals on the outer wall are left to spread over the opening.
