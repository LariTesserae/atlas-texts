The most numerous inhabitants are small enough to be overlooked. Inside the moss cushions live wingless insects with flattened bodies and pale legs. They feed on dead leaves and the fine green growth on wet stone, and they can go motionless for days when the cold settles in. A little warmth brings them out in numbers: they climb the fur of the root walls, turn their dark backs to the sun, and vanish at the first shadow of a bird.

Their eggs are laid deep in the cushions, where the temperature changes slowly and water remains after the lanes have dried. Some cushions have been occupied for generations. Pulled apart, they reveal narrow passages, shed skins and tiny grains of black droppings. The birds know this. They wait until the sun reaches a wall, then hop along it, probing into the green with slender bills. A bird may spend an hour at one cushion and leave with moss caught around its beak.

The root walls themselves belong to a plant that spreads outward wherever its roots can find moisture, then thickens around the roots of its neighbors. It has no trunk and produces no conspicuous flowers. Its leaves are dark, leathery and cupped so that beads of water collect at their bases. In dry weather the leaves fold close; after rain they open, and the walls seem to have grown overnight.

The plant is not one continuous individual, though it can be hard to tell where one ends and another begins. At an old fusion, roots grown from different directions share a hard collar of wood. One side may still bear green leaves while the other has died back, leaving a pale, hollow arch. Moss takes hold in those hollows, and insects take shelter in the moss. The walls grow best beside such old wood, where water lingers, so that a death can leave a particularly fertile doorway.

The grazer comes mostly at the edges of the day. Its coat is the color of the bare gravel, with longer hair between its toes that keeps it from slipping on wet stone. It moves cautiously through the courtyards, choosing the softer moss and leaving the root leaves alone. Where it has fed, the cushions have neat shallow scoops in them. These fill again slowly, sometimes over several seasons.

It has favored paths through the town. At the lowest arches it folds its legs and pushes underneath, brushing frost and loose moss from the roots with its back. In the square it pauses, often for a long time, before crossing. A sudden knocking from below can send it running, though it seems less troubled by the continuous roar of wind.

There are also lives beneath the square. Water carries scraps of leaf and moss through the crack, and in the damp stone below, pale mites and threadlike worms feed on what collects. They are seldom seen unless a heavy flow washes them out into a lower seep. The knocking comes from chambers deeper than they inhabit; it changes with the amount of water, and ceases when the last threads in the streets dry up.

By then the birds have gone to sheltered ledges, the insects have withdrawn into the cushions, and the grazer is usually beyond the rim. The walls remain exposed. As evening cold reaches them, their wet leaves draw together, holding a little of the day’s water against the roots.
