The people of the town are good at holding things without gripping them too tightly. Their hands are broad, with sensitive pads at the ends of the fingers, and they can tell from a root’s vibration whether someone is walking overhead, whether a cable is under strain, or whether the factory is about to pause. Children learn this before they learn the names of the streets. They are given lengths of dead vine to practice on, and their parents tap messages into the far ends.

Most people wear soft shoes. A hard sole sends too much noise through the roots and can mask a warning from farther along the road. The shoes have curved toes for catching on the ridges in the bark, and long ties that can be fastened around an ankle or a rung. When visiting, people leave them outside the house, hanging by the ties. A row of shoes beneath a window means there are guests, or that someone has gone somewhere without their feet.

The houses grow around their inhabitants slowly. A person might press a hand against a wall each morning for years before a shallow shelf appears there. Families inherit not only rooms but the habits that formed them: hollows where an earlier occupant liked to sleep, a patch of rind that closes at dusk because a child once feared the dark, a window that admits only the narrowest finger of air. These features can be coaxed into new shapes, though it is considered unkind to demand a quick change of a house.

The workers at the factory have exceptionally quiet hands. They feel the rhythm coming early and set their fingers against the fiber before the twist reaches it. A new worker often tries to follow the sound of the engine itself. This is too late. For several months an experienced worker stands behind them, touching an elbow or wrist at the necessary moment, until the body learns to answer the first, faint tremor.

The cable goes everywhere. It binds houses to roots and roots to one another. It makes ladders, slings, clotheslines, and the long loops people lower toward the leaf floor when something must be retrieved. Some of the strongest cable is kept in the lowest houses, coiled beside the detachable foundations. The people there check the knots at night and sleep with their shoes close by. They speak of the rising stone as a practical matter, but they also bring their children down to watch it through the gaps. Each family has a different account of its color.

Root-mice live inside the older roads, in spaces left around the pipes. Their whiskers lie flat when they run, and their fur grows in small overlapping scales that keep it from catching. They eat the soft linings of abandoned cable and carry away thread, moss, and occasionally a button. A mouse seen outside a road is usually looking for water. After a drop has fallen, they emerge in groups and follow the beads along the leaf veins, drinking quickly before the larger animals arrive.

The translucent many-legged creatures are called counters, though no one agrees what they count. They settle in a loose ring beneath a descending drop, each raising its foremost legs toward it. If the drop shifts, the ring shifts. If two drops are falling, the counters divide between them, sometimes traveling a great distance across the leaves. People have tried to keep them in houses as a way of knowing when rain is coming, but indoors the creatures gather under cups, cupped hands, and sleeping eyes, until they are let out again.

The birds eat moths and counters and, occasionally, root-mice. Their wings are thin and dark above, pale underneath, so a bird turning in flight may appear for an instant and then vanish. They have small hooked feet for sleeping against the roots. Their claws do not pierce the living tissue; each bird finds a crease and folds itself into it. Flocks recognize their own roosts by touch. When one bird dies, another may take its place, but usually not at once. For several nights there will be a bird-shaped vacancy along the underside of the road.

People are accustomed to meeting signs of things before the things themselves. A parent may see a child’s shadow move along a wall before the child has turned the corner. Workers sometimes hear a reel set down before it is carried in. They make room for it, and if the reel does not arrive they leave the space clear until the shift ends.

The hardest sounds to understand are the footsteps heard in stillness. They vary: some are light and hurried, others drag or stop for long intervals. The town keeps no official record of them. A few households write down what they hear, along with the date and the weather, and compare notes when they meet. More often, someone simply listens until the steps cease, then goes on with what they were doing.

When a new house appears at the end of a road, its nearest neighbors bring water and a little finished cable. They wait outside until a window opens. Sometimes a person is already looking through it, with their belongings arranged behind them and their shoes hanging by the door. Sometimes the house stands empty for days, making small adjustments to its walls. In either case, the neighbors leave the water where it can be reached.
