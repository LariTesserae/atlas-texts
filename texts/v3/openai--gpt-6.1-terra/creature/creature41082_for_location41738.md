The people of the basin learn to walk with their feet turned slightly outward, feeling for the edges of the paving through thin soles. Even children seldom run in the passages without looking down. A lifted slab, a cup set beneath a drip, or a board laid across a doorway can bring a person up short. Visitors are easy to recognize: they keep their eyes on the roofs and arrive at the pool with wet ankles.

Most households occupy two or three small rooms built at different times and different heights. The driest room holds grain, bedding, and whatever is difficult to replace. Its shelves hang from pegs or stand on stone feet. In the lower rooms, stools and tables have broad legs that can sit in water without sinking into the silt. People are accustomed to moving things twice: once when the rain begins, and again if the pool rises afterward.

Much of their work is done under shared roofs. They scrape hides, patch vessels, split reeds brought from outside, and work the dark local stone into wedges, paving, and weights. A few people go beyond the wall each day to tend fields or trade for wood and food; they return with mud of a different color on their clothes. The grass grown in the town is cut carefully, a little at a time. Its shortest strands are twisted into cord by children and old people sitting near the warmer upper walls.

The pool belongs to everyone, though particular steps and ledges are informally claimed at certain hours. Washing is done on the side where the water enters from the roofs, drawing drinking water on the other. When the level is low, someone will climb down to clear grit from the cracks in its floor. They work by touch more than sight, pushing a slender wooden tool into the seams and feeling for the places where water is coming through.

The notch-watch is not a separate occupation. Whoever passes the steps looks, and whoever sees a change tells the next person. At the third notch, those nearest the wall open the gates, while others begin moving boards and knocking at doors where someone may be asleep or away. The work is practiced, but it still produces arguments: about whether the gates were opened early enough, whether a neighbor’s new drain has made the water worse, whether a cup under a rock shelf is collecting water that ought to go into the pool. Later, the same people borrow one another’s tools.

The pale creatures under the paving are soft-bodied and many-legged, with narrow, blunt heads and a faint gray line showing through their backs. They curl when picked up. They feed on the fine growth along wet stone and on scraps caught in the drains, moving through spaces too small for a finger. People say a drain without them silts shut more quickly. Whether this is always true, they have learned to notice where the creatures are plentiful and where they have disappeared; an empty seam is a reason to lift the next slab and look.

After heavy rain, children collect them from the drain mouths on bits of bark and carry them back to sheltered cracks. Some adults are particular about where each one is returned. Others simply tip them out together and let them find their own way. By morning there may be only a few pale traces in the wet silt to show where they went.

The water beneath the town remains less predictable. Older inhabitants remember years when the pool rose every afternoon for weeks, and another when it stayed low through days of rain. They have names for certain sounds beneath the stone—a thin ticking, a hollow knock, a prolonged trickle from somewhere that cannot be reached. They compare what they hear, but do not always agree on what it means.

At night, when the fires have burned down, people still wake to small changes: a cup beginning to overflow, a board nudging against its sill, a neighbor crossing the lane to check the notches. Often the water has risen only a little. They set the cup upright, shift the board, and go back inside, leaving the passage open for the next person who needs it.
