The largest of them could stand on a thumbnail. Most are smaller. Their backs are made of overlapping plates, slate-gray when newly hardened and polished almost black where belts and stone have rubbed them. The plates part when they bend, showing pale seams beneath. Each of their six feet ends in a split hook; on the steep floor they walk with the deliberate ease of things crossing level ground.

Their white feelers are seldom still. An animal approaching a junction lays them against the rail, withdraws them, and lays them down again. Others coming the opposite way do the same. At crowded platforms their feelers make brief contact in passing, too quickly to follow. A belt rider can be carried through three levels of machinery without turning its body; it reads the approaching stops from the tremor in its feet and the brush of guides against its back.

Those that work the ore wear differently from those that ride. The carriers have blunt jaws and dusty faces, and their forelegs are thickened by the weight they drag. Furnace workers are pale around the joints, their plates stippled by sparks. Bridge workers have long hooks on their middle feet and flattened bodies that fit between the doubled struts. They hang beneath the traffic while they work. When a bridge shivers, they are often the first to leave it, passing down the cables to where coils of spare wire are stored. They return once the worst movement has passed.

There are animals that never seem to carry anything. They wait beside the grease channels, their feelers tucked back and their bodies pressed thin against the metal. A passing worker pauses to clean its jaws, and one of them comes out to feed on what it drops. Sometimes it takes the worker instead. The struggle is brief, usually silent, and may continue as the belt bears both animals out of sight. Elsewhere, attendants at the loading platforms pull injured animals clear of the wheels. The dead are carried to sheltered ledges, where smaller animals gather around them until only the hard plates remain. These, too, are taken away.

The youngest live in warm hollows behind the foundry, among metal filings packed into the crevices. They are soft and almost colorless at first. An adult entering the hollow lets them climb over its legs and under its plates; they pick dust and grease from its body while it rests. As their backs harden, they begin to venture onto the low shelves. Many return with bright grains caught between their jaws. Some do not return, and the hollows are cleaned without ceremony.

A newly opened mineral pocket draws animals from every level. They arrive along the walls in narrow streams, touch the exposed surface, and begin to rasp. The first scaffolds are often made from whatever lies closest: a bent rail, a length of cable, plates stripped from wreckage. Later, when carts can reach the pocket, the temporary supports are replaced. For a while the old and new structures stand together, carrying traffic over lanes still half buried in stone.

At the entrance, a few animals work directly beneath the cold sky. Grit blows onto their backs and gathers in the joints of their legs. They turn their feelers outward into the wind, then retreat to the warmer shelf when a gust comes through. A fallen flake may expose them to light for the first time; by the next shift they have built a covered passage across it, and the traffic resumes beneath the dark roof.

From inside the chamber, individuals are easiest to see when something stops. A belt jams, and its riders bunch together, feeling ahead over one another’s backs. A worker frees a trapped leg and remains beside the mechanism while the others pass around it. When the belt moves again, the animal waits through one full circuit before stepping aboard.
