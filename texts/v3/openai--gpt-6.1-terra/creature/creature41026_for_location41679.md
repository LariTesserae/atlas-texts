The people of the ridge keep their doors low. A person entering a shelter goes down on hands and knees, pushes through a curtain of woven blades, and comes up into a room just high enough to sit in. The ceiling is black stone on the inside. In a hard wind, the roof shifts a little, and the people pause in their work to listen to the weights scraping overhead.

They are broad through the shoulders and carry most things close against their bodies. Their hands are usually covered, but their fingers are long and deft, and the older people have thickened pads along the sides of their thumbs from drawing leaf-fiber through bone needles. They wear layers of the vegetation, softened by working and sewn with the green edges turned inward. A good outer layer sheds ice when shaken. A poor one grows stiff and heavy in rain.

There are not many shelters on any one ridge. A household may leave one standing for years while it travels among other basins, returning to find the paths risen and the walls furred with young shoots. Before entering, they test each stone with a hooked pole. A wall that has survived the weather may have been loosened by the growth beneath it.

The mats provide much of what they need, though not all of it willingly. The pale knots on the undersides can be roasted over a fissure until their skins split; inside is a small, oily kernel. The youngest shoots are eaten raw, folded into bundles so their sharp edges will not cut the mouth. Tougher leaves become cord, bedding, baskets and coverings for the pools where water is kept from freezing solid. Nothing woven lasts forever. Even indoors, a damp seam may put out a white thread, and a blanket left against the wall can attach itself to the stone.

People mend as they travel. When they find a tear beginning near a shelter or a pool, they stitch its edges together with long loops, leaving room for the living sheet to move. They do not attempt to save every mat. A sheet that has begun to pull its knots out of the rock is dangerous to stand on, and more dangerous to hold. Children learn to recognize the faint lifting at its edge and to get onto bare stone before the wind reaches them.

The six-legged grazers are called out by the sound of rain. Ice beads falling through the blades strike the roofs of their pockets, and the animals come up to gather moisture before it freezes. They are low, round-bodied creatures with pale jointed legs and dark mouths shaped for clipping. Their backs carry short, clear bristles among which fragments of leaf often lodge; a grazer sitting still looks like a knot of damaged growth. Beneath the mat, several may share a chamber, keeping it open by eating and packing the cut roots into its walls.

People catch them with loops set beside their round doors, or wait for a sheet to lift and collect those stranded on the exposed rock. They eat the meat and keep the bones. The long needles are made from the strongest leg bones, split, rubbed smooth and pierced with a hole finer than a hair.

The broad-winged animals are harder to approach. They live for long stretches in the air, traveling from fissure to fissure and resting where the updrafts meet. Their wings bend in several places, so that from below they can seem to be a flock moving with unusual precision. In quiet air they descend slowly, turning their pale undersides toward the ground; in a rising current they hold almost still while the chamber floor falls away beneath them.

When a mat tears free, they gather above it. Some take the grazers exposed on the rock. Others follow the drifting sheet, picking animals from its dangling roots. A large flier can carry off a young grazer, but it usually feeds on the ground, folding its wings over its body like a collapsed shelter. The ridge people give a feeding animal room. Its mouth is narrow, but the hooks at the ends of its wings are strong enough to grip stone.

Now and then a flier dies on a ledge, or is brought down by ice forming faster than it can shake it away. Such a find may supply a household for a long time. Its wing membranes make light, windproof coverings; its hollow bones become tubes for carrying embers from a warm crack; the curved wing hooks are fitted to poles for drawing loose mats within reach.

Travelers watch the air as closely as the ground. The passage of a low cloud tells them where water may be found, while the tilt of a distant sheet can warn of a current not yet felt. They mark useful routes with small stacks of black flakes, visible against the white growth. The marks disappear quickly: a fresh shoot bends around a stone, another catches beside it, and in time the little stack becomes an ordinary pale mound.

At the shelters, someone is usually working at a seam. A length of leaf-fiber runs from their hand to a needle, then through the edge of a mat laid across their knees. Outside, the pressed path is lifting blade by blade. Before leaving, they will walk it once more, both to find their footing and to make the way clear for whoever comes home after them.
