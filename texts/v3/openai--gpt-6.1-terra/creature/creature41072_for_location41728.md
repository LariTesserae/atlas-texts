The oldest inhabitants of the city are difficult to see. They are pale termites, soft-bodied and easily dried by the air, and they live almost entirely within the earth they have gathered around themselves. Their galleries run through the towers and down into the ground, branching into chambers where eggs and young are tended. In the deepest parts of the old mounds, generations have opened, sealed, and reopened passages until the walls contain the traces of many earlier arrangements.

Workers bring in the basin’s fallen growth: leaf stems, flakes of wood, dry grass, and pieces of seed head. Some travel beneath covered runways that reach into the scrub; others collect what has blown into the shelter of a mound. Soldiers stand where a passage has broken open, their darker heads turned toward the light. When rain or an animal’s claws damage a wall, workers gather at the breach with particles of soil, closing it again.

The mounds hold a community beyond the termites themselves. Beetles press into cracks beneath their rims, where loose grains offer shelter and scraps of plant matter collect. Tiny creatures live among the old fibers in abandoned chambers. Lizards wait at openings in the warmer hours, often so still that their outlines seem part of the rough earth. They seize a termite or beetle when one comes within reach, then retreat into shade as the ground grows hot.

The long-eared mammals make their homes below and between the towers. They come out at dusk with their ears raised, pausing often to listen before crossing the open courts. Some gather seeds beneath the shrubs; others pull at leaves and tender shoots where these can be reached. Their paths lead from burrow mouths to the nearest cover, and their droppings and scattered husks mark favored feeding places. On cold nights they may remain close to their entrances, venturing farther when the air is mild.

The trees and scrub support another population, much of it hidden by day. Moths rest against bark, grasshoppers cling among the thorn cushions, and beetles work through the litter beneath the branches. Cicadas settle high in the dense crowns. Crickets keep to the accumulated leaves and the sheltered edges of stones. As the basin darkens, their calls gather into the great, uneven chorus that carries across the flats.

The birds on the northern shelf are of several habits. Some spend the day in the surrounding desert and return with food for young tucked into the ledges. Others feed closer to home, taking seeds from the thickets or insects from the air above them. Even after they have settled, the nests remain restless: adults shift their footing, young birds call from beneath them, and neighbors answer along the rock.

Behind the birds, bats sleep through the day in the deeper fissures. Their bodies crowd together where the cracks narrow, and the rock below them is marked by long use. At dusk they wake, groom, and edge toward the openings. Once airborne, they spread over the thickets and the termite city, turning after moths and other flying insects. Their departure overlaps with the birds’ return, so that for a short time both are moving around the shelf.

Near morning the bats come back, folding their wings as they find purchase in the cracks. The small mammals withdraw below ground. Birds begin to leave the ledges, and the lizards emerge to wait for warmth. Beneath them all, in passages protected from the drying air, the termites continue carrying fragments into the city.
