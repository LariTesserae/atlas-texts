The animals nearest the opening are almost white. Farther down, where the warmth lasts through the long cold periods above, their skin takes on the color of wet ash. The smallest can shelter under a stone chip; the largest span a hand with their legs folded close. All have a narrow, flexible body that can be pressed into a seam. When one moves out onto an open surface, the legs unfold in succession, testing the stone before taking its weight.

They eat the dark-green growth. An animal settles over a patch and works at it with short mouthparts, leaving a clean shape the size of its body. The patch begins to grow back where water passes over it. In the wetter pockets the animals also pick through the fine material that collects at the bottom: shed skin, dead growth, the pale remains of animals that have fallen and not climbed out.

The crowd makes room for the living more readily than for the dead. An animal that stops moving may remain held against the wall by those pressed around it. Eventually the bodies beneath shift, and it drops a little, catches, and drops again. In the water pockets it comes apart slowly. The others drink around it.

Their young spend their first period under the adults. They are soft and nearly clear, and at first have too few working legs to cling to bare stone. An adult carries several beneath its body, although it is not always the one that produced them. When the wall loosens in a cloud of mist, the young pass from one shelter to another. Later they begin to climb independently, keeping to the covered spaces until their skin toughens.

The scraping goes on among them. A resting animal may draw its ridged limb once along the wall and wait. Another answers some distance away, then several more. When a section becomes crowded, the calls there grow close together; when the animals spread out after mist has wet the stone, long pauses open between them. A falling chip can still a whole wall for a few moments. Then one animal moves, and the sound starts again in scattered places.

Stone chips accumulate near the water seams. Some are no more than flakes, light enough for one animal to carry against its underside. The larger pieces take several animals to dislodge, though they do not always pull in the same direction. A useful chip may remain wedged for many days, guiding a slow bead out of a crack. The animals gather beneath its tip as they gather beneath the natural ledges. When it falls, they feel along the fallen edge and sometimes set it in place again.

Deep in the fissure, the gray sheen passes over the packed bodies. It is strongest on newly exposed skin and dimmest where animals have rested against one another for a long time. An animal climbing out from beneath a cluster leaves a faint pale track that gradually disappears as the others close over it. Nothing else down there gives light enough to see by.

The fissure is still changing. Small pieces break from the fresh walls, and the warm water finds new routes. Where a crack has widened, the animals enter it one after another until only the last legs are visible, folded against the black stone. When the cloud clears from the summit, the upper walls dry first. Those animals retreat into the shade below, leaving the opening bare except for the wet marks of their passage.
