The most numerous inhabitants are the pale grazers. They move in patient, overlapping files across the ceramic, each one feeling ahead with a pair of short limbs before drawing its mouth over the bacterial film. A cleared path shines black behind them. Where two paths meet, the animals pause and touch the surface with their front legs. They seem to distinguish a trail made moments ago from one already beginning to grow back.

Their bodies are jointed in many small segments, and beneath the translucent skin of some runs a ladder of silver bands. In others the bands are broken or absent. The grazers gather to shed in the lee of the leaves, where the flow is gentle enough to hold their empty skins for a while. Newly molted animals are almost transparent. Older ones acquire a faint greenish tint from the films in their guts.

The hunters lie where the current carries food toward them. At rest, each is a low oval with a soft, dark center and legs fringed like the edges of a filter. Water passes through the fringe; grazers do not always pass through unharmed. When one comes close, the hunter folds its legs inward so quickly that the basin appears to tighten around it. After feeding, it resumes its place, though not quite the same shape: one edge may remain curled, or a leg may take several minutes to spread against the water again.

They are not perfectly still while they wait. Their legs make minute adjustments, testing the pressure of the current and the vibrations traveling through the shelf. A grazer walking above a basin produces a pattern distinct from water falling into it. A larger hunter approaching sends a slower disturbance through the ceramic, and those in its path draw their fringes close.

The flying animals spend much of their lives suspended in the warm pockets under the ledges. They have narrow bodies and wings that are difficult to see except when the amber light catches them. Their young live at the waterline, feeding in the thicker films where the grazers cannot easily reach. Every few hours, when the flow deepens, the adults crowd into cracks above the surge. Some are caught anyway; their bodies travel down through the basins and disappear into the pores.

The plants offer shelter but not much stillness. Their stiff leaves tremble with the engine, and droplets gather at their tips until the weight overcomes the vibration and they fall. Around their roots, the film grows dense and dark. The grazers work outward from these patches, thinning them into delicate borders; the hunters station themselves along the borders and follow as they shift.

None of the animals needs to hear over the roar. They live inside it, reading the smaller disturbances carried through water and ceramic. A shelf emptied by a hunter fills again. A channel opened by generations of grazing draws a little more flow. When the engine sends the next pulse of water through the chamber, the inhabitants cling, fold, or rise into the air, and settle afterward into a surface subtly changed.
