The people of the town are narrow-bodied and low to the ground. Each has four weight-bearing limbs, jointed so that the body can be raised to look across the flats or lowered almost against them when a storm comes. Their outer surfaces are pale and finely ridged. Dust settles in the ridges, and a person who has been traveling can be recognized by the color of the country they have crossed.

They breathe the air maintained by the bright points. Away from town they carry it in stiff, folded bladders beneath their bodies, opening them a little at a time. A long journey requires caches: sealed chambers grown into the ground and marked by arrangements of dark stones. Travelers test a cache before opening their own bladders to it. Some chambers have been empty for generations, though the stones are still kept in place.

Their faces are difficult to read at a distance. They have no exposed eyes; light enters through bands of translucent material along the sides of the head. Their mouths are small, sheltered openings used for taking in oils and powdered salts. Speech is made elsewhere, by vibrating plates inside the chest. In the town’s pressure it carries as a dry, resonant voice. Out on the plateau, where the air is thin, people converse by touching a forelimb to the ground. The plant carries the vibration surprisingly far.

Children learn the street grooves by following their elders with a limb trailing behind. They are born soft, with broad dark patches through which they absorb warmth. For several years they remain mostly within the chambers, where the bright points hold the air steady. As their surfaces harden they spend longer outside, first in sheltered basins, then on the ridges above town. A child’s first sight of a grazer line moving across the open country is an event worth bringing others out to watch.

The residents do not cut their homes out of the plant. They make small openings, introduce prepared salts and wait for fibers to grow around shaped forms. A chamber may take most of a lifetime to reach its intended size. Families occupy it while it is still changing, moving their possessions as a wall thickens or a new arch begins to rise. The oldest houses are difficult to assign to a single family. Their chambers have joined and separated so many times that several households may share a wall without knowing when it was first grown.

The walls that open for residents are taught slowly. A person presses against the same place, carrying a particular mixture of oils on the skin. Over time the fibers respond to that pressure and scent. Visitors wait for someone inside to let them through, or sit in a public hollow until the wall loosens for them. There are old entrances in the town that still respond to people who died long ago; their descendants sometimes find a passage opening beside them when they pause in the wrong place.

The bright points are tended, though no one in town claims to have made them. Keepers stand on the highest ridges at dusk with polished plates, signaling when pressure has fallen in a chamber or when a point has drifted too far from the others. The points usually respond. During a storm they spread out along the approaching front, and the keepers remain below them, listening to the walls. If the singing rises too sharply, everyone closes their breathing bladders and moves deeper into the town.

Afterward, people come out to collect what the storm has left. Charged dust gathers in the lee of arches and in the sheltered folds of the great fans. Some of it is useful to the plant, and the collectors spread it thinly over damaged skin. Some is stored in ceramic vessels for heat, light and the preparation of food. The most powerful deposits are left alone until their crackling subsides.

Grazers pass through the town’s outer basins often enough to have their own grooves. They scrape the living surface with slow sweeps of their ceramic mouths, leaving pale tracks that close behind them over many days. The people follow them at a respectful distance, gathering the hard flakes they discard. These are ground into food or worked into tools. A grazer may tolerate a person beside it for hours, then abruptly lower its heavy head and force them away.

The runners are rarely seen close to town. When one crosses a ridge, residents hear its feet first through the plant, a rapid sequence growing stronger beneath their limbs. The grazers lift their bodies higher and move apart. A pursuit can carry both animals beyond sight, leaving the town to listen to their diminishing steps.

At dusk the transparent hunters descend from the warm air. They settle over open gas pores, almost invisible until their bodies catch the light, and wait for the smaller things drawn to the escaping gases. Children watch them from the chamber mouths. Sometimes one enters a house and becomes trapped against a wall, its clear body pulsing as it tries to rise. Someone will open an entrance and guide it back outside.

The town’s dead are carried into unused chambers and placed against the living walls. Their bodies are rich in the salts that nourished them, and over the years the fibers grow around them. People return to those chambers to touch the ground and speak. The plant gives back only the vibrations it receives, altered by distance and by the shape of the walls, but those echoes are familiar. Many residents can recognize the chamber of a parent or companion with their eyes closed.

Between storms, life proceeds by small sounds: the scrape of a vessel, a voice through a wall, the crackle of pores opening along the street. A resident waiting for an entrance may rest a limb on the ground and feel grazers moving miles away. Above them the bright points shift into formation, and below them the town continues its slow growth, making room for people who have not yet been born.
