The grazers look larger when they rise. At rest, each is a low mound of gravel-colored hair, scarcely higher than the stones around it. Standing, it reveals short, powerful legs and a body deep enough to shelter several young beneath. Its coat is dense at the roots and loose at the surface, where the outer fibers catch dust and fragments of dead lichen. The oldest animals carry miniature gardens along their backs.

Their mouths are remarkably delicate. The blunt upper lip can flatten against a rock or gather itself into a narrow point, working around a lichen’s branching edges without tearing away the thin living layer beneath. An animal may feed on one stone for an hour, moving only its mouth. Its jaws grind slowly, and it makes use of food swallowed many days earlier. Much of the cold season is spent in this way, sustained by what is already inside it.

The fringe underneath the body serves both young and adult. Droplets collect on its fine, tangled ends and are drawn toward the skin. A resting animal bends its head beneath itself to drink. When several are close together, the smaller creatures along their flanks drink there too: pale, many-legged things that comb the fringe for moisture and loose skin. They are most numerous on animals that have lain still for a long time. When the grazer rises, they retreat into its coat or drop into the hollow it has warmed.

Young grazers stay beneath their mothers through their first long season. They know the rhythm of her feet and move with her even in darkness, stopping when she stops. At first they feed on a soft growth found close to the skin at the base of her fringe. Later they begin testing the stones, mouthing patches already loosened by the adults. They are difficult to see until a herd starts moving and many small legs appear among the large ones.

The predators are narrower than the grazers and longer in the body, with coats that lie in flat, overlapping layers. They do not often run far. Most wait where a rock passage constrains the herd, or follow an animal that has fallen behind. Their attacks are brief and close to the ground. A healthy adult is formidable: it can turn on its padded feet and bring its full weight down with surprising speed. More often the predator takes a young, sick or very old grazer. For days afterward it may lie beside the remains, feeding a little at a time.

Other lives come to the carcass. Small jointed scavengers emerge from the gravel, followed more slowly by the dark growths beneath it. The coat persists longest. Seasons after the bones have settled, a pale mat of hair may still mark the place, holding enough moisture for moss and lichen to establish. Grazers eventually return to feed there.

The colonies in the wet seams have no fixed outline. Threads spread through fractures, thicken where water and minerals are available, and withdraw as a seam freezes. Neighboring colonies meet and sometimes share material; elsewhere a narrow band of dead filaments marks a boundary that has held for years. Their metal-rich crusts can be seen on freshly split stone as fine dark glitter. The warmth they make is slight, but in a sheltered crack it is enough to keep a trace of water liquid after the surface has sealed.

A few of the smallest animals spend their whole lives in those cracks. They graze the colonies as the great herds graze the surface, leaving clear trails through the dark film. When meltwater returns, they move upward through the gravel. For a short part of the year the underside of almost every stone is busy with them. Then the water recedes, the openings narrow with ice, and they go down again, while above them the herds continue their slow crossing.
