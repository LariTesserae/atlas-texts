The largest inhabitant is seldom visible all at once. It lives in the stone ribs, in the drowned bases of the columns, and in several of the deeper hollows where the water looks black. A portion of it may come out into the basin as a pale, jointless length, no thicker than a finger. More often only its feeding edges show: soft fringes pressed flat against the limestone, opening and closing as water goes by.

The fringes catch what the currents bring down: loose growth from the walls, dead insects, fine organic dust. When grit accumulates among them, they roll it into little pellets and pass them outward, leaving faint rows along the rib. These rows do not last. A surge scatters them, and the fringes begin again.

Each inhabited column has an opening near its base. The creature can withdraw through one opening and emerge from another; whether its body runs continuously between them or separates in the narrow passages is difficult to see. At low water, strands sometimes span the gaps beneath the surface, trembling in the current. They are translucent until disturbed. Then a cloudy whiteness moves through them, and they contract into the stone.

The column cups are important to it. When pressure drives water up between the columns, the creature sends thin branches into the cups and leaves them there after the flow has gone. These branches lie in the retained water for hours, slowly swelling. If a cup drains too quickly, a branch may creep over the lip and descend the column, searching for another wet surface. Once in a while one fails to reach it. By the next brightening it has dried to a curled, brittle strip.

The blind swimmers feed on those strips when they fall into the basin. They also pick among the feeding fringes, taking the small creatures caught there. The larger inhabitant does not seem to object. It closes around a swimmer only when startled, and then releases it as soon as the pressure against its tissue stops.

It is sensitive to changes in the chamber, though not always in ways a visitor would expect. A fallen sheet of water may strike directly above it without producing a response. But a new vibration in the stone, too faint to distinguish amid the roar, can bring all its exposed parts inward at once. Later, as the flow settles, they return one by one. Sometimes a fringe emerges from a crack it has not used for years, feels along the stone, and remains there, feeding.

The disk lies within reach of one of these cracks. When a bubble leaves it, the nearest fringes close. They stay closed until the report comes, then open toward whichever channels have begun to flow. Over long periods, this has shifted much of the creature’s body from one side of the basin to the other. Its abandoned openings remain visible as clean-edged patches among the mineral deposits.

It has also reached the black doorway. A pale branch grows through the stone immediately beneath it and divides on either side, following the thin streams that turn away from the frame. Neither branch touches the black material. In dry intervals they draw back, leaving two shallow grooves that might have been made by water alone.

New individuals begin in the cups. At certain times the swollen branches break into dozens of small, rounded bodies, each surrounded by a clear coat. Some wash out immediately. Others remain in the cup until the next surge carries them away. Most disappear into the cracks; a few settle in quiet hollows and put out their first feeding edges.

For a while the young are separate, moving slowly over the stone when a flow becomes too strong or too weak. Where two meet, their edges may join. The seam remains visible for a day or two as a brighter line, then is lost among the folds. In this way the inhabitant of the ribs has grown from many small beginnings. Parts of it still become stranded and start again elsewhere, so there may be others in the passages beyond the chamber, feeling their way along currents that have not yet reached the basin.
