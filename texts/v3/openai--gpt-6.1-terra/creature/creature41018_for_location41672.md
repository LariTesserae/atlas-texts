The pale grazers are called hinge-backs by anyone who has seen one unfold. At rest, each looks like a heap of pale, overlapping plates no larger than a bundled coat. Its legs are folded beneath it, its head tucked down between its shoulders. When it rises, the body lengthens first, then the limbs extend one joint at a time, lifting it just high enough to pass over the leaves.

A grazer feeds with two narrow lips set on either side of a hard, ridged tongue. It clips the fleshy outer leaves of a rosette but leaves the crowded center untouched. The leaves are bitter with salts; the grazer stores the salts in sacs along its flanks and sheds them later as dry flakes beneath the mat. Small rootlike organisms grow toward these flakes. By the time the grazer’s line returns, sometimes years later, the feeding places have thickened again.

The lines are not herds in the usual sense. The animals may be separated by hundreds of paces, following the faint warmth and chemical traces left by those ahead. When one finds a patch still wet from fog, it pauses and presses the underside of its jaw against the leaves. The animals behind it turn toward that place, though it may take them a day or more to arrive.

Young hinge-backs spend their first season below the foliage. They are translucent then, soft enough to curl into the spaces between stems, and they drink droplets collected on the silver undersides of leaves. Their pale plates develop slowly as mineral salts accumulate in the skin. A parent remains nearby, not in a nest but in a shallow depression it has cleared among the roots. It feeds there and leaves its shed salts within reach of the young.

The hunter is darker than the stone when dry, and nearly silver when wet. It has a long body carried on six legs, with a head so flat that it can push beneath the vegetation without lifting the leaves much. Most of the time it hunts under the mat, feeling its way through the stems for soft-bodied animals. It crosses open ground only when the smaller prey has become scarce.

A hinge-back does not run from it. It folds. The joints collapse in a practiced sequence, the head disappears, and the plates settle close against the mat. Fine hooks along the animal’s belly catch on the roots, so that a hunter trying to pull it free must tear up a large patch of vegetation as well. An experienced hunter may wait instead, lying with its chin on the ground until the grazer has to unfold to feed or drink. Such waits can last through several fogs. The hunter survives them by drawing its limbs close and letting its body cool.

Beneath both animals is another crowded world. Thin, many-legged scavengers comb the old leaves for eggs and dead tissue. Small, blind grazers scrape the mineral deposits from the sides of cracks, leaving pale, clean tracks that soon darken again. Among the deepest roots live colonies of soft, branching organisms that draw energy from the changing salts. They have no fixed outline. A colony may fill a seam for decades, then recede as the useful minerals dissolve and gather somewhere else. Pieces left behind can remain dormant in the stone and grow again when the water returns.

The vegetation itself is not a single continuous plant, though it can appear to behave like one. Hundreds of species share the mats: broad rosettes that catch fog, narrow-leaved forms that shelter beneath them, and pale, nearly leafless plants that contribute roots but take little space at the surface. Their roots meet and part in the rock. Water and dissolved minerals pass through some of these contacts; at others, thickened walls keep neighboring plants apart.

In a good fog, the whole mat tightens as water enters the stems. Afterward, small movements continue. A hinge-back raises its head. A hunter shifts its weight. Below them, the roots swell against the stone, and a dormant colony begins to branch into a newly wet seam.
