The ash-colored animal has fourteen legs, though they are seldom all visible at once. It keeps them folded beneath a body no thicker than a fallen leaf, with its head resting on the mineral ground. The head is blunt and bears a fringe of short, clear feelers. When one of them touches a root, the animal lifts itself a fraction and remains there, listening through its feet.

It eats the crystalline tips of the cushions. Each tip comes away with a small sound; the animal holds it between two dark plates until it has softened, then draws it in. The cushions continue to grow around the places where they have been grazed, sometimes enclosing the animal so closely that only its head remains exposed. It can stay in such a shelter for a very long time. When it moves, it moves all at once, crossing a clearing on its many legs and settling again before the last disturbed needle has straightened.

There are smaller creatures in the foliage. Some spend their lives inside the thick leaves, following the copper veins and feeding on what gathers there. Their passages are visible as fine silver lines, though a line sometimes ends at the edge of one leaf and resumes in another that does not touch it. The leaf closes behind each animal as it goes. On rare occasions two passages meet, and the pale bodies inside remain pressed together for a time before separating along different veins.

Others live among the roots. They are jointed and colorless, with broad mouths that fit tightly against stone. They rasp at the mineral surface, leaving shallow grooves that the roots later enter. The plants supply them with a soft deposit in return, though whether this is an exchange or simply two appetites finding the same place is difficult to say. In some patches the grooves form dense branching patterns. In others a single groove runs straight beneath dozens of plants, crossing more ground than the length visible at either end would allow.

The broad-leaved growth produces seeds in clusters beneath its leaves. Each seed has a transparent coat and a heavy copper center. The coat catches against stems and needles; the center pulls it slowly downward, often into one of the stretched gaps between plants. There it may remain, visible but out of reach, while a root grows toward it from a great distance. When the root arrives, the seed opens, and a new stem rises into a space the surrounding foliage has already made.

Not all the seeds grow into familiar plants. Occasionally a stem emerges with two crowns separated by a wide interval, although both draw from the same root. A leaf on one crown may fold when a creature touches its counterpart on the other. Sometimes the two crowns eventually grow apart in their habits: one is grazed, or loses a leaf, or turns its copper veins toward an opening in the thicket. The shared root persists beneath them.

The ash-colored animals gather around such plants. Several may occupy the same clearing, motionless and facing inward. When one lifts its head, another sometimes does the same. They are not always copies: their legs differ in number, or one bears a notch in its shell that the others lack. They remain together until one begins to feed or the space between them changes, then each goes its own way through the stems.

Along the glass ridges grows a plant with narrow, nearly black leaves. Its roots enter the fresh fractures and hold the sharp pieces where they lie. Inside the glass, small white bodies move from crack to crack, drawing nourishment from films deposited by the roots. Their presence may account for some of the fine crackling. It continues even when no body can be seen.

At the bare patch beneath the repeated leaf, an ash-colored animal lies with its head beside the impression. It has left no marks in the ground on its way there. Farther out, near another instance of the leaf, a similar animal rests among the blue needles. One has a broken feeler; the other’s are whole. Both raise their heads when a crystalline tip snaps somewhere between them, and both wait a long while before lowering them again.
