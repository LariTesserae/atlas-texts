The marsh has inhabitants, but it is not always easy to say where one ends and another begins.

The narrow creatures beneath the films of water are called runners, if a name is needed. They are scarcely thicker than a reed stem, with soft, dark bodies and a fringe of fine feelers along each side. They eat the pale growth that gathers on submerged stone. When disturbed, they flatten themselves against it so closely that the water seems to pass through them. In dry weather they retreat into the seams of the rock, sometimes in clusters of a hundred, and wait with their feelers folded.

They lay eggs inside the hollow stems of the rust-colored reeds. A runner finds a broken stem, enters at the waterline, and climbs until its weight bends the stalk. The eggs hatch when rain fills the hollow. The young slip out into water that may lead them only a few feet from the plant, or, after a strong shower, into a pool far across the country. Runners raised in the eastern basins sometimes bear the pale mineral stain of stones found only in the west.

The dark birds feed on them. Each bird has long toes that spread over floating leaves and a beak with a flexible tip, useful for feeling under moss. They are quiet in flight but not on the ground: a bird probing a pool makes a series of low, wooden clicks, and others nearby will pause to listen. Their nests are little more than platforms of crossed twigs among the pale trunks. They build above the highest old waterline, though in some years the water rises beyond any line left by previous floods. Then the birds carry their young higher, one at a time, holding them by the loose skin between their shoulders.

The translucent growths hanging beneath the trees provide shelter for smaller things. Each is a long, many-chambered bladder, clear when full and faintly blue when empty. Midge-sized animals live in the chambers, grazing on the films that collect there. When the air pressure falls, the growths contract and force droplets from their lower tips. The animals are carried down with the drops; those that land in water swim, while those caught on leaves wait for the next shower to wash them away.

Their discarded skins are found in the moss, threaded through its metal-laden roots. The moss takes up what the water brings it—iron, copper, the dust of worn stone—and lays some of it down in branching filaments. These are not nerves, though they carry changes from place to place. Pressure travels through them; so do weak currents produced where wet minerals meet. Their patterns are altered by roots growing around them, by stones shifting, by the slow accumulation of peat. Rain joins strands that have been separate for weeks. A dry wind can break thousands of such connections in an afternoon.

Something in the marsh uses them.

It has no body apart from the bodies that sustain it, and no center that can be found by following the threads. A grove may become unusually attentive: its hanging growths contract together, its lichen holds water long after the neighboring trees have begun to drip. For a few hours the grove responds to events in a distant basin. Then the connection fades, and the same attention gathers elsewhere, perhaps around a stand of reeds or beneath a broad shelf of stone.

At first the responses seem simple. Leaves close before a bank collapses; roots tighten around a stone before the current reaches it. Looking longer, one sees that the responses alter the place. A cluster of shrubs may divert a trickle into moss that has grown too dry. Reeds bend together over a nest exposed to rain. Mineral threads accumulate around a pool whose bottom is beginning to sink, binding the peat for a season or two before the water finds another way through.

Whether these acts belong to one inhabitant or many is difficult to determine. The marsh does not always agree with itself. One basin may draw water toward its thirsty moss while another closes the channels that would supply it. Threads grow across the obstruction, dissolve, and grow again. A stand of reeds can spend an entire wet season leaning against a current that returns every morning from a hollow higher than its source.

There are also moments that resist any account of cause. After rain, a runner may enter a seam and emerge from the same seam larger, its skin marked by months of feeding. A bird may strike at a reflection and come up with a fishlike animal no one has seen in the surrounding water. Once in a while, leaves close around a nest that has not yet been built. The bird arrives later, tests the twigs with its feet, and begins to lay.

The marsh keeps traces of such events in its growth. A thread laid down in response to pressure may remain after the pressure is gone; a root continues around a stone that has slipped away. Through these remnants, disturbances persist without being repeated exactly. The country appears to remember in the way a river remembers a fallen tree: by the altered course of everything that passes afterward.

When the rain is heavy, its separate attentions draw closer. Connections open between pools, and the pulses in the hanging growths fall into step across the groves. Stones knock beneath the water, the sound passing from basin to basin faster than the current can travel. Runners press into the mud. Birds stand still on their nests. For a little while, the marsh seems to be attending to something beyond any one of its creatures.

Then the shower passes. The threads lose contact as water drains from them. The groves loosen their drops, the runners come out to feed, and a bird steps cautiously across a leaf that has begun to lift at its edges. What gathered across the country is no longer there in quite the same form. Its traces remain, holding the ground together, until the next rain makes something of them.
