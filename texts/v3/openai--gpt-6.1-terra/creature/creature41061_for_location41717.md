People live in the walls as well as on the terraces. Rooms have been fitted into old service bays and behind retaining walls, where the blockwork holds a fairly steady warmth. Some have windows overlooking the valley; others look onto a passage or a little enclosed garden. Cloth screens keep out the strongest light. At night, doors stand open where there is enough privacy, and conversations carry along the walkways with the smell of cooking.

A household may have a roof bed, a few trees in a shared orchard, and an agreement with its neighbors about who tends the pipe that waters them. The beds are valuable enough to be argued over, especially when a trickle has weakened or a root has pushed through a channel. People mark the dampness of the soil with a finger and learn which plants can stand a missed watering. In very dry weather, wash water is carried out to the tougher shrubs, and the cistern levels become a subject of daily conversation.

Much of the work is done in short distances and at steep angles. Gardeners climb ladders into the figs and lower fruit in bags rather than dropping it onto the walkways. Workers on the walls inspect joints, tap loose blocks, and make notes on cracks that have widened since the last hot season. Pump tenders know the sound of each machine and can hear when one is drawing air. They keep baskets of washers, lengths of pipe, and old fittings whose threads are no longer made. When a lift stops between levels, someone has to go out along the guide and find what caught.

Children grow up knowing which rails are too hot to touch and which bridges sway under a crowd. They watch the lift cage pass and learn to recognize people by their loads: bundles of prunings going down, compost coming up, a replacement fan blade wrapped in cloth. Smaller children are kept away from the edges and the machinery, though the older ones find quiet routes through orchard paths and disused stairs. They collect fallen fruit, lizard skins, bright washers, and the papery wings that gather beneath lamps.

Some residents spend much of the day below the brighter terraces, where the air is moved by fans and the heat comes from machinery rather than the sun. Others work almost entirely overhead, among the condensation screens and rooftop beds. They meet at lift stops, water points, and the shaded stretches beneath the orchards. A person can live here for years and know only a few of the lower passages, or be able to travel the whole height by a route that rarely enters the sun.

The fruit birds live close to people, but not comfortably with everyone. They take ripe figs before the pickers reach them, tear holes in soft fruit, and leave seeds in gutters and cracks. Nets protect some trees; elsewhere a share of the crop is simply lost. Pigeons nest in ledges and cavities in the ironwork, bringing twigs that fall onto the paths below. The swifts spend much less time where they can be watched closely. They sweep over the valley in quick, dark groups, gathering insects above the warm currents, and disappear into narrow openings high in the masonry.

Lizards occupy the sunlit edges. Each has its favored length of rail or block, and gardeners come to recognize the larger ones by a missing tail tip or a pale scar. They eat insects among the pots and retreat into the vines when the pruning begins. Near the water outlets, smaller creatures gather in such numbers that the surfaces seem always to be moving: flies, beetles, moths taking moisture, and the birds that come to drink among their tracks. The pools must be cleared from time to time, but never stay empty for long.

The mammals in the ducts are seldom welcome indoors. They gnaw stored food and nesting material, and sometimes damage insulation, so cupboards are lifted on legs and openings are covered with mesh. In the orchards they are harder to separate from the work of the garden. They carry seeds, disturb the leaf litter, and feed on fruit that has fallen out of reach. At dusk, if a terrace is left quiet, one may emerge from a polished run, pause beneath a pipe, and continue along the base of a wall.

All of them live with the accumulated decisions of earlier inhabitants. A sealed doorway gives pigeons a shelf; a patched wall holds enough dust for a fern; a pipe moved around a thickening root leaves a sheltered space beneath it. People clear what threatens the lift or blocks a drain, save what gives shade or fruit, and leave some places alone until there is time or reason to attend to them. By then, something may have made its home there.
