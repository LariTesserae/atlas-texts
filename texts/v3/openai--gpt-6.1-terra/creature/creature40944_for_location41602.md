The smallest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the mineral scales among which they live. Each has a flattened body and a fringe of fine, moving folds. They crowd the fissures where warm gas seeps out, holding those folds in its path. Within them, minute partners turn the chemicals in the gas into nourishment. When the flow changes, the animals shift with it, opening gaps in their masses and closing them again.

They leave pale marks where they have fed: discarded scales, empty skins, and grains of sulfur caught on the stone. Larger grazers follow these traces. Their backs are divided into dark plates, sometimes smooth, sometimes ridged where old damage has healed. Beneath each plate are several short legs and a hard scraping mouth. They move slowly through the smallest colonies, shaving off mouthfuls as they go. A grazer may carry a trembling layer of its own food on its back, gathered there while it rested beneath a shelf.

The soft-rimmed animals share the same crowded routes. Their upper surfaces are leathery and often dusted pale, but their undersides gather into folds that grip and release. They can cross bodies that would give way under a heavier grazer, and can draw themselves into spaces too narrow for jointed legs. Some feed on the films left around fissures; others take the eggs or shed skins of their neighbors. When disturbed, they contract into compact shapes that are easily carried along by the movement of the crowd.

The hunters are rarely seen whole. A glimpse of one is usually a pale, hooked mouth appearing between two backs, or a length of jointed body working under the mass. They are longer than the animals above them and more flexible, with legs that can brace against several bodies at once. They feel their way by pressure. The uneven tread of an injured grazer, the repeated movement of a feeding animal, or the faint working of a rim against stone can lead one to a meal.

Even a hunter must make room for itself. It pushes between animals that grip one another tightly, and the pressure passes ahead of it. Nearby grazers lift their edges or turn, pressing against those beyond. The warning spreads farther than the hunter travels. Often it is only a small local disturbance; sometimes, beneath a heavily occupied shelf, it grows into the slow lifting that reaches across the hollow.

The builders are among the smaller grazers. They carry grains in a shallow notch near the mouth, packing each one against the last with the weight of the head. Their curved partitions need frequent repair. Passing bodies dislodge them, and the dry warmth makes the mineral crust brittle. Several animals may work on one wall without keeping together afterward; another may return to a damaged curve and begin again at its broken end.

The eggs behind these walls are pale and thick-shelled. They lie loose, touching one another, and are easily buried if the partition fails. Young that emerge must push through dust and fragments of shell before they reach the moving bodies outside. Many never reach a fissure. Those that do join the granular masses there, sheltered for a time by animals scarcely larger than themselves.

When the heat rises, age and shape determine how quickly each inhabitant can climb. The soft-rimmed animals pull themselves around edges and under the bodies above them. Jointed grazers take hold of whatever is nearest, sometimes riding upward on the backs of others. The smallest colonies break apart as the gas grows hotter; their members cling in the cracks or are carried up with the flow.

Afterward, the return is less hurried. Animals descend along the shelves, testing the warmth beneath their bodies. The grazers gather again where the smallest colonies have begun to thicken. Hunters enter the seams behind them. Around a surviving partition, a builder finds loose grains and starts pressing them back into place.
