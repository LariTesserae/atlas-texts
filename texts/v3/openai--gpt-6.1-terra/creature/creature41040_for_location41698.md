The runners are the most numerous animals in the grove. Their legs are jointed high above their bodies, so that they can pass over loose dust without dragging their bellies through it. They come out from beneath the plants in little parties, pause with their front limbs raised, and then scatter toward a freshly torn leaf. A bead is often too large for one to carry. Several will brace themselves against it and roll it home, while others pull at it from the opposite side. The struggle can go on until a gust covers the bead or one party succeeds in breaking a piece away.

Their burrows are shallow and branching. The tapping heard underground is partly the sound of excavation: a runner loosens the packed powder with a stone held between its forelimbs, then pushes the dust backward with its legs. The oil beads are stored in chambers near the roots. Some have been carried so often that their surfaces are cloudy and scratched; others remain clear enough to show a bubble or a strand of leaf sealed inside.

The creatures on the fronds are slower and much less exposed. Their soft bodies fit into the pleats on the pale undersides, and their ridges stand up between the veins. They rasp small openings in the leaf and feed where the oil wells out. If a frond is shaken, they flatten themselves against it; if it tears free, they remain attached as it blows across the hollow. Fallen fronds attract runners, which pick at the hardened oil and sometimes pull the clinging animals loose as well.

The flyers are light, flattened animals with many limbs spread around the edges of their bodies. They do not seem able to choose where a gust will take them, though they can alter their shape as they turn. They catch on fronds with hooked feet, fold themselves down, and begin to crawl. A crowded landing can strip a damaged leaf: flyers scramble for purchase, raspers contract into the pleats, and runners gather below for whatever falls.

There are young among all of them. Small runners keep close to the burrow mouths and retreat at a shadow. The smallest flyers are scarcely wider than the curled leaves around the stems; when the wind rises, some cling there while others are carried away. The raspers leave pale clusters tucked deep in the folds of new fronds, where the leaves are less likely to tear.

Nothing in the hollow stays where it falls for long, except in the lee of the southern tooth. Dust settles there around the three shelters and gathers against their walls. Runners visit the collapsed one, passing through the gaps between its stones. The hanging cord in the second shelter is beyond their easy reach. Its beads are larger and more evenly pierced than the pieces they store underground.

Whoever made the shelters may still return. The bowl and knife could have been left for another visit, and the cord is tied securely enough that the wind has not taken it. There is no one in the shelters now. When a gust sweeps the grove, the beads knock against the roof stone; afterward, beneath the settling dust, the animals resume their tapping and scraping.
