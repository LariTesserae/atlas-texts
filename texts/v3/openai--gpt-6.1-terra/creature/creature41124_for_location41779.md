The most numerous inhabitants are the small clear animals under the fronds. They have flattened bodies, six hooked feet, and a fringe of fine appendages that is usually folded close to the mouth. Against a clean wall they are almost invisible. Where sediment has collected in their guts, each carries a thin brown line through its middle.

A falling salt sheet brings them out. They gather along its edges and work their mouthparts over the rough surfaces, taking the minute fragments of dead matter and other material caught in the crystals. Several may feed on the same piece for hours. When it has dissolved or drifted away, they return to the nearest shelter, sometimes carrying grains much larger than their own heads. These are not food; the animals arrange them around the bases of the fronds, where the current has a harder time dislodging them.

The fronds benefit from the accumulation. They are not plants in any familiar sense, though their dark branches are stiff enough to hold their shape. A coating of microbes lives on them, drawing what it can from the water and the material that settles there. The animals crop this coating as well. A heavily grazed frond shows pale patches, which darken again over the course of many weeks.

Among them lives a larger animal, seldom more than the length of a hand. Its body is soft and jointless, with a broad sole that can grip stone or iron. It moves by pressing one part of itself down and lifting another, so slowly that it can be mistaken for a fold of dark material caught on a wall. When disturbed, it contracts into a low mound. Its skin is covered with short filaments that lie in the direction of the current; a change in flow raises them before any visible sediment begins to move.

These animals spend much of their time inside the factories. They feed on the translucent grazers, on the microbial films growing in the quieter pipes, and on the occasional visitor weak enough to be held against a wall. They also gather at the chilled plates after a release, following the salt down through the machinery. A single sheet may carry a whole cluster of them into the street. They let go one after another as it settles, leaving small clear impressions where their soles were pressed against it.

They are drawn to the wheels. One may remain beside a bearing for months, its filaments trembling as the mechanism turns. Fine particles collect on its skin; from time to time it rolls them together and releases them in a compact pellet. The clean patch beneath it widens. Whether this does the machinery any good is difficult to say, but the animals are commonest around the parts that still move, and absent from rooms where the wheels have stopped.

They reproduce there too. A parent fastens a row of small, leathery capsules to the sheltered side of a pipe, usually where the water changes temperature slightly with each turn of a pump. The young take a long time to emerge. At first they are pale, narrow creatures, liable to be carried away by currents that an adult would scarcely notice. Many disappear beyond the roofs. A few settle in the streetlamp branches, where salt flakes catch, and make their way down toward the fronds as they grow.

The larger visitors leave more than bent fronds. Sometimes the clear animals vanish from an entire wall, and the dark ones stay concealed for a long while afterward. Sometimes a visitor dies in the basin. Then the animals come from several streets away, feeling their way along the changes in the water. For a season or more, the body feeds a dense and shifting population. When little remains, the youngest dark animals shelter among the bones, and the fronds grow unusually thick around them.

Nothing in the town moves quickly unless it has been carried. A pump alters its speed, a salt sheet breaks loose, a crack opens overhead, and the inhabitants respond at the pace their bodies allow. Most of the time they remain pressed to their chosen surfaces, feeding or growing or holding fast, while the faint outlines around them form and come apart.
