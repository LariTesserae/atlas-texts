The people who live on the plateau are used to giving directions that will be wrong by the end of the month. They name buildings by their former use, by the color of a door, or by the nearest pump, and add a warning if the usual walkway has been closed. Children learn to check the route boards before setting out. Adults do too, although some still prefer to ask whoever is coming the other way.

Most homes occupy the upper terraces, above the settling ponds. They are built into old factory shells or stacked along sheltered sides of newer workshops, with insulated walls and windows set deep against the wind. Boots stay by the door; trays beneath them catch the water and fine gray grit. In wet weather, coats hang for days on heated rails. People grow herbs in window boxes and keep small gardens where the roof beds are deep enough, though every garden has a marked access strip for the workers who inspect the waterproofing below.

Many residents work on the plateau, but not all in the same way. Some tend pumps or sort material arriving on the conveyors. Some test the water as it passes through the reed beds, noting which channels have begun to clog. Others move between sites with tool cases, repairing sensors, gates and the housings around motors. Survey crews watch the slow slope in the east. They have marks painted on the wall and stakes set behind it; a shift too small to see from one day to the next can still change the next week’s work.

The people who care for the vegetation know it as both cover and load. They cut willow on a schedule, thin roots from cages and check whether a stand of reeds has begun to slow the flow. Moss is welcomed on some exposed surfaces and carefully removed from others. This distinction is taught to new workers, who may at first see only a green wall.

At mealtimes the workshops empty unevenly. Pump operators come off shift while a demolition crew is just beginning; surveyors arrive with damp notebooks and spread their maps beside someone else’s lunch. News travels with them: a bridge reopening, a nest found in a roof drain, a familiar stairwell about to be dismantled. People save useful things from buildings before the gantries take them apart—hooks, tiles, lengths of timber—and sometimes recognize them later in a neighbor’s kitchen.

The small birds stay close to the alders and the warmer recesses of buildings. They pick insects from leaves and vent grilles, then retreat when people cross the roof. Gulls are less cautious. They gather near the waste lifts, waiting on rails and housings until a load arrives, and rise together when a machine approaches.

Inside the homes, the pumps can sometimes be felt through a chair or the floor. Residents notice when that vibration changes. It may only mean a pump has stopped for maintenance, but someone is likely to look at the notice board, or call a person on the lower terrace, and ask what is happening.
