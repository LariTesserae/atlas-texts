The people of the plateau know one another by their districts, their herds, and the houses in which they grew up. A traveler may be recognized first by the cut of a hide coat or the way a pack is tied. Asked where they are from, they might name a particular court, or a house with a seep in its rear wall, or the ridge whose shadow reaches their door first in winter.

Their clothing is layered and repeatedly mended: soft hide next to the skin, heavier skins or felted hair outside, and boots with soles thick enough to be reshod. Fur is saved for collars, cuffs, and the places where a child’s coat must close against the wind. People carry small knives of stone or bone even when they have no journey planned. A strip of hide needs cutting, a drain needs clearing, an animal may have to be freed from a tangle.

Most households have members who move with the great herds and members who stay near the settlements, though the same person may do both in the course of a year. Herders know the grazing country at a scale difficult to see from a high pass: which hollow catches a little meltwater, which ridge loses its snow first, where a herd can descend without scattering the stones underfoot. They bring back meat, hides, hair and horn, and news of other districts. They also spend long days watching animals browse, with little to do but keep them moving toward the next patch of forage.

In the settlements, work is divided among many hands. Someone cleans the grit from a cistern. Someone sorts bones for needles, handles, and tools whose shapes are already familiar in the hand. Someone tests the stones in a wall that has begun to lean. Children fetch and carry, mind younger children, and learn which surfaces will hold their weight as they cross from one roof to another. They are sent to collect the small amounts of water that gather after rain, and taught not to spill it on the way home.

A house is seldom only the work of the family living in it. Its stones may have stood in older walls; its drain may pass beneath two neighboring yards. When a wall needs rebuilding, the people above and below it have reason to help. They argue over where the larger slabs should go, then lift them together. Repairs are occasions for visiting as well as labor, and a person who is particularly good at fitting stone may be called across several settlements.

The houses shelter other inhabitants. Small burrowing animals push gravel into the corners of courts and sometimes undermine a carefully laid step. They eat stored food when they can reach it, but their tunnels also loosen the soil where scraps and droppings have collected. People set traps near the stores and tolerate the colonies farther out. Dogs investigate the holes, usually without much success.

Birds nest wherever they can find a ledge protected from the weather. They take hair from the pens and carry off threads left drying in a court. Some feed on the insects and small animals among the walls; others follow the herds and pick through their dung. Their voices are among the first sounds a child learns to distinguish: the quiet exchanges under an eave, the alarm that runs along a cliff when a predator passes overhead.

The pale hares come close after dusk, clipping the plants that grow around the pens and investigating the sheltered beds in the courtyards. A household with roots growing may cover them before nightfall as much for the hares as for the cold. The dogs give chase when they are young. Older dogs often watch from the doorway, conserving their effort for an animal with a chance of being caught.

Beyond the houses, predators keep to the edges of the moving herds. Herders read their tracks in dust or old snow and make decisions long before the animals themselves are visible. A missing grazer is not always pursued: the ground may be dangerous, the herd too far ahead, or the tracks too old to follow.

People who live among these ridges grow accustomed to changes that are easy to miss. An elder remembers a route before a slide of loose stone made it difficult for loaded animals. A family knows that its seep is weaker than it was, and keeps vessels ready against the day when it emerges elsewhere. Neighbors notice when a dog no longer rises for strangers, or when the birds abandon a familiar nesting crack. These things are discussed in the courts while hides are scraped and tools are made, with the next settlement’s smoke visible beyond the ridge.
