The people who live here are used to seeing one another at a distance. Across a plaza, a person can be recognized by their walk long before their face is visible: the gardener with a basket held against one hip, the child who runs the last stretch to a crossing, the two neighbors who meet halfway between their towers and continue talking as they walk.

Most residents carry very little. Water is available throughout the city, and the covered walks provide shelter, so a person going out for the day might take only a light, a folding cup, and a strip of cloth to dry a bench after rain. The cloths are often brightly dyed. In the gray light, color is something people choose to carry with them.

They live in the towers and in lower buildings almost hidden by gardens. Rooms are arranged around shared courtyards, where the cooler air gathers and where people bring work that can be done sitting down. Some households occupy many rooms; others have only one or two, with unused doors along the same passage. A resident may know everyone on a floor and still hear unfamiliar footsteps overhead. The city has space for more people than it holds, and its inhabitants have made use of some of that space without feeling obliged to fill it.

Children learn the crossings early, first with an adult’s hand on their shoulder, then by making short trips alone. They know which blue lamps can be seen from which garden gates and which covered walks stay driest when the wind turns. The feeling of a missed step bothers some of them more than others. One child may cross a dozen times for pleasure; another will take the long way around for years. Both routes are well maintained.

The crossings close often enough that waiting is an ordinary part of a journey. People check the sky, but they also listen for the faint change in a lamp’s hum. When rain arrives suddenly, strangers share the dry places beneath the walks. They compare where they were headed, pass along news from districts farther out, and watch the first water run down the transparent channels. After a long dry spell, someone is likely to count aloud the seconds between the first drops and the moment a channel begins to flow.

The city’s caretakers include people who tend its plants, people who look after its machines, and people whose work falls between the two. They clear leaves from collector fins and inspect the threads stretched between towers. They open cistern covers to test the water and cut back roots that have grown too close to a seal. They also watch the gardens for changes that do not need correcting. A bed may be marked for weeks because something has passed through it, leaving the groundcover raised in a narrow ridge.

Whatever moves beneath those beds is seldom seen. Once in a while, in a quiet courtyard, a person hears a slow rustling travel under the flowers and stop. Leaves lift, settle, and lift again farther away. The caretakers have found shallow hollows in the soil and sections of tough root pared clean, but no one agrees on whether all the tracks belong to the same kind of animal. They keep paths open around the occupied beds and let fallen leaves collect there.

The gray lizards are less private. They flatten themselves against warm stone and dart after insects drawn to the building lights. Children sometimes wait beside a wall with their hands cupped, hoping one will climb in. The lizards rarely oblige, though they become accustomed to particular people and will resume hunting while those people sit nearby.

The birds are seen mainly as silhouettes. They nest high enough that their calls reach the streets thinly, mixed with the ticking of the collectors. At dusk they descend over the cistern vents, folding and opening their wings between the towers. Residents on the upper walks pause to watch them, especially when the green light is coming down the walls and the birds briefly pass through it.

Food grows in the deeper beds and in fields between neighborhoods. Some plants are raised for leaves and soft fruit; others are grown for the thickened roots they store beneath the soil. There are also enclosed growing rooms where the light is steadier. People who work there emerge with pale dust on their sleeves and often bring home cuttings to root in glasses of water. Meals are shared in courtyards when the air is comfortable and indoors when rain threatens. A guest from a distant district may arrive having crossed the city in minutes, carrying a dish still warm from its oven.

New buildings rise within sight of daily life. Residents watch their walls gain height and sometimes begin using the ground around them before the interiors are ready: planting a bed beside a foundation, leaving tools under an unfinished overhang, choosing where a path ought to meet the nearest walk. Later, when people move in, they find these small arrangements already there. They may keep them or change them.

The unnamed avenues give directions trouble. People refer to them by the things currently at their ends: the avenue of the split rock, the one past the three empty cisterns, the one where the evening light reaches all the way across. These names can last a season or a lifetime. As the city grows, a landmark may disappear behind a new wall, but someone will still give directions by it, and someone else will know what they mean.
