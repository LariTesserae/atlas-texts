The largest living things in the bowl are rooted so deeply that only a fraction of each is visible. A crown may spread wider than a house, but it rises from a root no thicker than a person’s arm. The root works slowly through seams in the mountain, loosening grains and drawing them upward. In daylight, the dark fronds sort what has been gathered: some substances become new tissue, some strengthen the fine joints, and the rest is shaken free as glitter.

The crowns do not resist the wind so much as accommodate it. Their filaments bend independently, and the branches that bear them can turn almost completely over. A crown flattened against the stone may seem ruined until the pressure changes and it opens again. Its silver underside carries a different arrangement of filaments, shorter and more densely divided. Small animals graze there while it is exposed, then drop away before the crown turns back.

The grazers are difficult to follow individually. Each has a clear body stretched over a branching framework and four or more little sails hinged along its length. In steady air they hold the sails open and drift; when a column catches them, they fold one side and spin around it. Their mouths are dark enough to see. They settle on a filament tip, grip it with their feet, and work at the surface until a piece comes loose. The dry tapping heard between gusts is made largely by their feet, though a few larger grazers scrape audibly as they feed.

Some spend nearly their whole lives among the outermost tips. Others descend at intervals to the roots, carrying the pale grains that cling to their bodies. They leave clusters of eggs in narrow fissures where the stone hums. The young emerge with their sails folded tightly around them and climb until the wind is strong enough to take them.

The hunters are clearer still. They drift with their sails held close, presenting little surface to the air, and extend them suddenly to cross the space between themselves and a grazer. A capture is brief: two small frameworks touch, tumble together, and separate again, one of them empty. Even the hunters are sometimes taken. There are wider, slower creatures that move through the loose swarms with fringed openings spread beneath them, gathering whatever fails to turn aside.

At the foot of each root lives a population seldom lifted into the air. These animals are flattened and pale, with gripping limbs arranged close to the stone. They take apart the frameworks that fall from above, working along each delicate joint until it gives. What they cannot use collects as bright dust in sheltered cracks, or is caught by a rising column and returned to the grove.

The arch has its own dependents. Several kinds shelter in the openings between its internal joints, though the openings shift and sometimes close. When the blue line passes through, the nearest crowns draw together and the air between them changes. Animals that have been drifting separately are pressed into closer company. Some mate in that brief congestion; others hunt there. As the fronds fly apart, the animals scatter again.

The platform attracts fewer creatures. Grains pause on it, and occasionally a small body lands among them. The animals usually leave quickly, rising straight upward with their sails half-folded until they reach the turbulent air above. One kind returns repeatedly: a narrow, nearly invisible grazer that appears to feed on the grains during the moment they are still. It can be seen best from the side, when a foot touches the platform and its shadow holds for an instant before lifting away.

Nothing in the grove stays arranged for long. A root may occupy the same crack for an immense time, while its crown takes a different shape from one breath to the next. The swarms form around openings in the foliage, lose their shape, and gather again. Above them, waste glitters and vanishes into the wind; below, the last remains of older animals are being reduced to dust.
