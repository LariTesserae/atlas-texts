The pale animals beneath the stones are called, for convenience, the packed ones. Each is a little larger than a fingernail, with a soft, ridged back and a fringe of short gripping limbs. Their bodies are not quite pale when seen closely: cream along the ridges, gray in the folds, with a dark line showing through where food passes slowly inside them.

They fit together because they grow that way. A young one settles into a space among older bodies and remains there through several molts, widening where there is room and flattening where there is not. When a stone is lifted, they tighten their grip on one another. The darkening that passes through a crowd is made by their translucent backs folding over the darker skin beneath.

They do eventually leave. In the cool hours they detach one limb at a time and move toward damp ground, grazing on films too thin to be noticed until a clean trail has been made through them. Some return beneath the same stone. Others encounter a crowded shelter on the way and press into a gap. A slab that has rested undisturbed for years may have several generations under it, none of them related to all their neighbors.

The largest grazers move in much the same direction, though much more slowly. Their lobes are muscular along the edges, and a ripple passing under one side can shift the whole animal a fraction across the rock. A large individual may take a season to cross a basin. It leaves behind a broad, faintly polished track, bordered by the narrow trails of animals that have traveled in its shade.

Their shelter is not entirely safe. Tiny shelled creatures feed on damaged tissue along their undersides, and the great grazers periodically lift and flex a lobe to scrape them away. Other small inhabitants clean the folds, eating dead skin and the grains of mineral that collect there. When rain comes, these passengers retreat farther inward as water is drawn through channels between the lobes. The dark stain afterward remains for days.

The translucent fliers begin life in the damp seams. Their young have no wings and feed among the shelled animals, sometimes taking one alive and sometimes occupying an empty shell until they outgrow it. An adult lives briefly above ground, making short flights at dawn or after rain. Most of its time is spent beneath an overhang, gripping the rock among hundreds of others. When the air cools, individuals change places in the layers: those nearest the stone work outward, while others press into the warmth they leave.

The dome builders are smaller than the packed ones and more active, though much of their work is hidden. They bring shed skins into their burrows and pack mineral dust against them, making walls that retain the night’s moisture. The narrow entrances are left open to the morning air. Waiting bodies crowd there because the first condensation gathers at the lips; those farther inside receive droplets passed backward from mouth to mouth.

A dome grows where the ground stays damp for several years. As its inhabitants deepen the chambers, the roof may settle, and they build it up again with more skins. Eventually the damp shifts or the supporting material fails. The colony moves in portions, carrying what it can, while other animals take shelter in the abandoned tunnels. A slumped ring can remain visible for a long time after its builders are gone.

There are hunters among all of them. Some are nearly transparent and follow the moisture trails into crowded shelters; some wait at the edge of a cushion plant for a grazer to extend its soft feeding surface. They take little at once. An animal that has been fed on may close over the injury and continue along its route, carrying the mark for the rest of its life.

The animals reproduce when moisture persists, not merely when rain falls. In a wet basin, clusters of eggs appear beneath flakes of mineral crust, and the smallest paths become dark with young. If the basin dries too soon, many remain dormant there, protected by their casings until another damp period reaches them. Some never hatch. Others emerge after the adults that laid them have moved away.

From a distance, none of this changes the plateau’s appearance very quickly. Up close, a shelter grows crowded, empties, and fills again. A dome settles. A grazer’s edge advances over a pale patch of rock. The paths endure because animals keep finding the same slight hollows, following them toward water and shade, and wearing them a little deeper as they pass.
