The people of the hollow know one another by touch as readily as by voice. In the louder hours a hand on the shoulder means *look at me*, and a tap against the wrist means *wait*. Children learn these signs before they are allowed near the engine, where a shouted warning may be lost even a few steps away.

They live in the rooms around the outer trunks. The doors fit loosely, and there are brushes beside them for sweeping visitors back into the street: mostly beetles, though moths come in when the lamps are lit and blind grazers sometimes nose their way beneath the beds. A few creatures are tolerated. The little hunters in the cable housings catch anything small enough to damage the insulation, and the spotted animals in the water cups keep their surfaces clear. Each household has its own opinion about the pale worms in the roofing leaves. They do no obvious harm, but they can be seen moving overhead at night.

The repair workers can tell which belt is slipping by resting a palm on the engine frame. They cut away growth that threatens the bearings and tie it back where it might serve as a brace. They have a great deal of practice retrieving tools from beetle streams. A dropped wrench can travel surprisingly far before its carriers meet an obstacle and begin patiently trying to take it apart.

At the pumping station, two sisters keep a record of the water. They mark its arrival by pressing a thumbnail into a strip of soft wood, then cut a notch when the last cup closes. Their marks are unevenly spaced. They have never managed to predict the moisture, although they listen to the knocking behind the walls and sometimes argue that a particular sequence means it is coming. Neither sister will say this in front of the other.

The factory workers make plugs from seed down for everyone in town. They also spin fiber, heat it, and bind it around lengths of pipe. The red light in their windows comes from a furnace that needs frequent feeding. On ordinary days they collect trimmings from the streets; after heavy feeding they can gather enough loose material to fill their baskets without cutting anything.

The children are useful when the lamps fail. They know where the floor changes underfoot and which roots can be followed in the dark. They are also the first to notice a new animal. One girl has been watching a beetle with a damaged wing case for several weeks, identifying it by a silver fleck caught in the crack. It disappears into the crowd for days at a time and then turns up under the repair-shop bench or near the fan, carrying something much too large for it.

When the water comes, people close their doors but leave shallow dishes outside for the moths. Engine workers stand ready with soft brooms. Everyone who can stays home, eating, mending clothes and waiting for the great traffic to pass. Afterwards, neighbors open their doors cautiously and check on one another. There is usually someone laughing in the street before the fan starts again, sweeping a last, bewildered burrower out from under a chair.
