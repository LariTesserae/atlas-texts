The broad leaves live close to the stone. Each grows from a short, tough stem tucked into a crack, and several stems may share the same narrow seam. Their milky veins are thickest near the base, where they divide and spread until the smallest are difficult to distinguish from the tissue around them. A leaf near the surface lies almost flat; farther down, it lifts and turns slowly, following what light reaches it. Old leaves become thinner at their edges. When one tears away, it may hang for months on a fin of rock, fading until only its veins remain.

The green stalks rise through these beds in groves. What looks like a single stalk is often several joined at the foot, with roots running over the stone before finding a place to enter it. The heavy leaves at their tips keep the branches under a slight, constant strain. Some stalks grow outward from steep faces and then turn upward, making low archways beneath themselves. In the clefts, their upper leaves are fewer and paler, and the branches stand farther apart.

The emerald cushions between the roots grow most readily where a little fine material has caught. Their silver interiors are damp-looking even in the water, made of close-packed threads that hold grains among them. A cushion spreads only as far as those threads can find purchase. On an exposed plate it may remain no larger than a hand for many years, rounded on one side and flattened on the other by the slow growth of a neighboring leaf.

The shelled grazers spend much of their lives beneath the broad leaves. Their shells are small and low, colored like cloudy glass, with a dark mark where the body is drawn in. They move along the leaf in almost imperceptible stages, feeding on its surface and leaving a clear track behind them. Several tracks may cross without the animals ever meeting. A grazer that reaches a vein turns aside; the milky fluid remains enclosed, though the tissue on either side may be worn nearly transparent.

The long swimmers pass through the groves singly. Against bare stone their outlines are visible: a fine head, a narrow body, a faint line of internal organs. Among the leaves they almost disappear. They bend once to cross an opening, then hold still beside a stalk, adjusting their position with movements too small to disturb it. At times one takes a tiny grazer from beneath a leaf. More often it waits where particles have settled in a hollow, picking through them before moving on.

On the open slopes, life begins at a scale that is easy to miss. A few grains lodge together in a shallow pit. A film forms over them, then minute green points, each holding the grains a little more firmly. These first plants do not cover the stone so much as mark places where something else might later take root. Many patches vanish when a fragment of rock breaks loose or a grazer passes over them. A few persist, and after a long time a dark cushion or the pale edge of a leaf appears among them.

There are fewer living things in the deepest clefts. Small leaves cling to sheltered folds, their pale veins visible against the dark. Sometimes a swimmer pauses near one, showing for a moment as a line across it. Below the last leaves, the rock continues downward, smooth and largely bare.
