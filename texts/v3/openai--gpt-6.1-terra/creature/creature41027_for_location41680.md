The leaf crust is not one plant. It is a close-growing community of many kinds, their roots and runners so entangled that a handful pulled free contains half a dozen lives. The brown upper leaves belong mostly to slow-growing plants that withstand the sun. Beneath them are softer, shorter-lived forms, green even in the hottest months, which spread whenever a grazer opens a gap. Some put out fine white flowers no wider than a fingernail. The flowers last only until the heat rises, but insects know where to find them.

The grazers are small enough to move under the cover without lifting it individually. Seen in a bowl, one resembles a pale woodlouse with a narrow head and two dark bars along its back. It has many jointed legs and a mouth that rasps rather than bites. The herds are made up of animals of several ages, from nearly transparent young to adults with hard, chalk-colored plates. When they pass over an old root shelf, their clicking changes pitch; people crossing the plateau listen for the change, because a shelf may conceal a hollow.

A herd does not travel as a single body indefinitely. It divides around dry or heavily grazed ground, and its edges join other herds. Where rain has fallen, the animals gather under the softened leaves and feed until the crust above them turns dark. Then they move on, leaving strips of green tissue that may recover if the plants have stored enough water.

Several hunters follow them. The most conspicuous are the long-legged striders, lean animals that hold their bodies well above the hot surface. Their feet spread their weight over the crust, and their narrow, jointed snouts can punch through it. A strider may stand beside a moving herd for much of the day, striking only occasionally. After each strike it draws its snout out slowly, with a mouthful of pale grazers. Smaller hunters live below the surface: flat-bodied animals that slip through the root beds and ambush grazers in their passages. These leave little sign aboveground except small, neat openings that close over by evening.

The climbers are more varied than they appear from a distance. The commonest are hand-sized, with gripping pads and blunt teeth worn down by bark. They travel in extended family groups, carrying young against their bellies until the young can hold on by themselves. They scrape the soft material from grooves in the columns and lick up condensation, but they also eat the bodies of insects caught inside the hollow limbs. Some groups occupy the same crown for years; others make dangerous journeys across the open ground when a column becomes crowded or begins to fail.

Their long calls serve more than one purpose. A group at a newly dripping groove makes a rapid, repeated call that draws its own members in. A slower call, often taken up by animals on neighboring columns, is heard most often toward evening. Travelers can sometimes tell from the calls whether a column ahead has water, though a wet trunk is not necessarily a safe one. At the richest grooves the climbers throw fragments of bark and rush intruders in a dense, chattering mass.

The columns themselves house many less visible inhabitants. Moths spend the day in the cool interiors of their branches. Small, soft-bodied animals live among the inward-hanging leaves, feeding on shed tissue and drinking from the films of moisture that form there. When a trunk opens its slits and releases warm air, some are blown out with the seeds. Others cling inside, closing their breathing openings until the rush has passed.

Near temporary pools, life appears with a speed that can make the bare ground seem deceptive. The ferns are already there, folded flat and waiting. So are tiny eggs in the creases of the crust and dormant creatures lodged among the roots. Within a day of a good rain, minute swimmers may be visible in a pool, feeding on loose green matter. Long-bodied flyers arrive from the columns to lay their eggs at the water’s edge. If the pool lasts, their young hunt in it; if it dries too soon, the eggs remain in the folds until another rain.

The people who cross the plateau know particular columns as places, not merely as sources of water. A reliable tree may be recognized by a bent limb, a split in its trunk, or the sound its crown makes in wind. Travelers remember which grooves begin to drip first and which are held by aggressive groups of climbers. They approach before dawn, set bowls where they can, and move away once the animals begin to gather. At some columns, old bowls have been left in sheltered cracks for the next party to use.

They take food from the plateau carefully and in small amounts: grazers collected where a herd is dense, fern shoots when a pool is fresh, and the tender growth found beneath a loosened edge of crust. A peeled strip of living ground can be used for binding while it is supple, then dried and kept. Its place remains visible long afterward. On routes used for generations, the scars make an uneven trail between shelters, interrupted wherever the ground has healed too well to show them.

At night, people shelter in the folds where mist gathers, though the root beds there can grow cold. They hear the herds passing nearby and the climbers settling overhead. When a pulse begins, they may feel it through a hearthstone before they hear it. Farther out, columns open in sequence, sending seeds into the dark; by morning the slits have closed, and the animals are back at the grooves, waiting for water.
