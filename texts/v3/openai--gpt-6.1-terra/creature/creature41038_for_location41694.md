The residents spend most of the day below street level. Their rooms are cut into the sides of the cisterns, above the waterline, with narrow openings into the covered drains. From the square these openings look like joints between stones. At dusk, when the gutters are sounding and the paving is cool, a few may come out to inspect a frame or carry a bundle of reed.

They are low-bodied, with four jointed legs and two smaller limbs folded close beneath the chest. They can lift themselves high enough to reach a wall or a roof gutter, but at rest they settle almost flat. Their backs are pale gray and finely ridged; moisture collects along the ridges and runs toward the edges. The young are darker and smoother. In a wet season, when several are moving together, they can be difficult to distinguish from the stones and their thin films of water.

Their eyes are small, set well apart, and useful mainly at close range. They find their way through the city by the slopes beneath their feet and by the different vibrations of the paving. A covered drain drums differently from an open channel; a new wall carries the gutter note more sharply than an old one. They know these differences as precisely as another inhabitant might know the colors of doors.

They do make sounds of their own: short clicks from plates inside the mouth, and a low rasp used between close companions. But the city’s long notes cover most calls. At a distance they communicate by pressing the flat of a forelimb to stone and tapping with another. A message can travel across the square this way, though it arrives slowly and may have to be repeated where a seam has opened.

The roof trees are tended chiefly for their leaves and soft new roots. The residents also cultivate pale growths in damp baskets beneath the terraces, feeding them with fallen leaves and the fine organic matter caught in the drains. Every cistern has a keeper, usually more than one, who clears the channels and tastes the water for the mineral changes that precede a new seam.

A household occupies a cluster of adjoining chambers. Its members are not necessarily related; they have taken responsibility for the same length of street, the same cistern, or an unfinished wall. Young residents move between households as they learn the work. They are given wicker to sort and mend long before they are allowed to set a frame over fresh paste. A badly placed frame can hold for years before its fault becomes visible.

When a ridge is too wet to build upon, the street simply stops. People continue to maintain its drains and keep its paving clear. They plant markers at the edge, replace them when they rot, and teach the young where the next houses are meant to stand. Sometimes the ground hardens within the memory of those who first framed it. Sometimes it does not. The plan is kept in the household nearest the ridge, incised on a slab that can be amended as the seam changes shape.

On still days, when the wind leaves the gutters quiet, more residents come aboveground. They pause in the square, feel the unfamiliar absence through their feet, and call to one another in the open air. The sounds are faint and numerous. By the time the wind returns, most have gone back below, leaving only the wet paving and an occasional mallet beside a wall.
