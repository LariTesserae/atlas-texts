The most continuous inhabitant is the green itself. Moss holds the water and makes a surface that is neither quite ground nor quite pool. Sedges rise through it in fine blades; dwarf willows spread woody branches beneath it, putting out leaves only a little above the wet. In sheltered places, small pale flowers lift clear of the moss for the brief season when insects can visit them.

The pools have inhabitants of their own. Green and rust-colored growth coats the submerged hollows, and minute organisms live among it, feeding, dividing, and settling into the accumulating peat. When the wind falls, small flies gather above the warmer water. Some rest on the sedges or on the dry upper faces of stones, waiting through the colder hours. Their lives are short beside the slow work of the moss, but many generations may pass while a lichen patch spreads across a slab.

A small mammal moves through the cover at ground level. Its tracks appear beside the walkway, where mud is exposed, but away from that strip its routes are difficult to see. It can pass under the flattened willow and between the hummocks, finding shelter close to the wet earth. An occasional bird comes down to rest or search for food, sometimes choosing a shed roof as a dry perch above the vegetation.

Lichens occupy the newest rock, thinly at first, especially where a rough surface gives them purchase. Moisture gathers in cracks, plants follow it, and dead stems and leaves begin to collect. The basin’s inhabitants are also making places for those that come after them: a little more peat, a little more cover over stone, another shallow pool edged with sedge.

People live here only intermittently. In the warmer season they arrive to tend the turbines and intakes, crossing the basin on the raised walkway and carrying tools between the sheds. They work in the machinery’s roar, then leave it running. For most of the year, the plants, insects, and occasional animals remain around the foundations, under the cables, and across the wide wet ground.
