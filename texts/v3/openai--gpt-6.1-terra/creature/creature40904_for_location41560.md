The people of the towers have broad hands and a delicate sense of pressure. They can tell, through a floor, whether someone is approaching in haste or carrying a heavy vessel. In the open they walk with their fingers against the ridges when the ground permits it, reading the basin as they go. A stranger who stands still may feel nothing but the great uninterrupted trembling; a resident feels the near wingbeats of gliders, the passing of a herd, and the places where something has gone quiet.

Their hands are scarred. The young stone cuts readily, and children must learn which edges can bear their weight. Inside the towers, where the walls soften the noise, they also speak aloud. Their voices are low and careful, used for long accounts and private disagreements. Much of daily speech takes place with a hand laid against another hand, or through a shared surface: a table, a doorframe, the narrow rail of a bridge. A person can be called to a meal by a sequence tapped into the floor from the room above.

They are not numerous. A tower may house several families, people who work there for a season, and others who have come from a place beyond sight. Some live alone for years, though rarely without visitors. The distances make hospitality a practical obligation. Every occupied tower keeps a cool room near its entrance, with water and food set out for anyone arriving across the stone. Travelers leave messages there, pressed into soft strips of mineral paste: news of a rain curtain, a damaged canopy, a herd moving east.

The inhabitants tend the great plants, but do not own them in any simple way. A canopy may draw on water collected by three towers and shelter animals hunted by a fourth. People visit its outer leaves on light frames, clearing dust from the thick green surfaces and mending broken ribs with bands of woven fiber. They know individual plants by the notes they add to the roar. When a rib splits, the change can be felt far away. Someone will eventually come to see whether the plant can be repaired.

The small jointed animals beneath the canopies eat fallen leaf tissue and the fine growth that appears where droplets strike stone. They spend the hot hours packed into cracks or arranged in layers beneath the roots, their limbs folded close. At cooling they emerge in such numbers that the ground seems briefly to flow. The people gather some for food, shaking them from shelters into deep baskets, but leave the oldest clusters undisturbed; those places hold moisture through the driest weeks.

The pale gliders are more difficult to catch. Their wings are nearly silent in the city’s noise, but their bodies interrupt the vibration traveling through the plant ribs. A skilled hunter can wait with fingertips on a rib and know when one has landed beyond sight. More often the gliders are left alone. They clear the swarms around the leaves, and during rain their folded bodies make a pale fringe beneath every canopy.

The grazing animals drink rarely and feed on the mineral-rich margins of the plants, stripping the tough growth that forms where roots meet stone. Their translucent skin is not as fragile as it appears. Dust settles on their backs, and old animals acquire a cloudy brown cast. Their calves stay near one another even when the adults spread across miles of ground; from a distance a nursery herd can look like a small congregation in an otherwise vacant city.

In the quiet behind the ridges wait predators with flat, dark bodies and joints that hold them clear of the stone. They hunt by listening to the sound that fails to return from an approaching animal. A hunter crossing the basin watches these places carefully. If the roar seems suddenly distant, they stop before taking another step.

Within the towers, the work of gathering vibration goes on day and night. Some inhabitants tend long banks of instruments whose fine parts must be adjusted by hand. Others lower sealed vessels into the seams at the foundations and receive them again warm, cold, or faintly trembling. The vessels provide heat, light and materials for the city, though even the workers do not always know what has happened to them below. When a seam opens, nearby conversations pause. People have learned to give it room.

Those who maintain the bridges travel more than anyone else. They carry dust from one end to the other, but also seeds, letters, tools and news. A journey that should take days may end while the traveler is still thinking about the person who saw them off. Some find this unsettling even after years of use. Others come to depend on it, keeping a household in one tower and a workshop in another whose outline has never been visible from home.

When rain arrives, work stops where it can. People go out with vessels and spread cloth beneath the leaves. Children lie on their backs in sheltered places and watch the drops gather on the transparent ribs overhead. For a short while there are more things to hear through the stone: water striking, animals moving, the changed pitch of leaves taking weight.

Afterward, the inhabitants continue to make their rounds. They empty the filled basins into covered stores and place smaller cups beneath the last dripping edges. At nightfall someone in a tower may feel a single drop striking the same spot again and again, long after the curtain of rain has vanished into the haze.
