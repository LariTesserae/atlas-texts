The kelp holds the shallows together in great, loose forests. Each plant grips the old rock with a many-fingered holdfast, and in the spaces between those fingers small worms, brittle stars and young crabs find shelter. The blades overhead are continually damaged: their edges fray, their tips are bitten away, and whole plants sometimes pull free. New growth rises from among the holdfasts, where the water is full of drifting spores and ground-down fragments of older plants.

Fish live at different levels in the forest. Small ones gather beneath the canopy, their flanks flashing as a school turns through a patch of sun. Others keep closer to the bottom, slipping between stones or holding themselves against the current behind a ridge. Young fish use the thickest growth for cover. When a seal enters, the school compresses and breaks apart around it; for a moment there are fish everywhere among the stems, then fewer, and the kelp is swinging in the animal’s wake.

The seals travel easily over ground that sends the water in several directions at once. They hunt with the tide, working along the edges of the ridges and into the channels where fish are carried through. Sometimes one rests near the surface, head raised among the floating blades, before folding down out of sight. Another may follow the same route later, investigating the hollows and the shadow behind the turbine.

On the rock beneath the kelp, anemones open wherever there is enough shelter for them to feed. Their tentacles catch particles and small animals brought past by the current. Some faces of stone are crowded with them; nearby, on a surface swept harder by the swell, only low, tough growths remain. Limpets cling there, and mussels fasten themselves into cracks and onto the ironwork, drawing food from the water as it passes.

Crabs move through the broken shell and loose gravel below. They pick at dead fish, torn kelp and whatever else has been left or carried down. A crab may spend much of a strong tide tucked under a stone, its legs braced, then come out when the flow slackens. Octopuses make use of deeper crevices, leaving heaps of opened shells outside their dens. Their soft bodies can pass through gaps in the rock that would stop a seal or a large fish.

Farther into the valleys, the kelp thins and the colors fade in the weak light. The animals there depend partly on what comes down from above: loose blades, scraps of flesh, fine organic material settling in quiet pockets. Worms work through the sediment; small crustaceans gather around larger pieces as they land. Not everything reaches the bottom. Some fronds travel for days between depths, rising and sinking with the water, carrying their own little communities until they are eaten or fall apart.

The iron structures have become habitat without becoming part of the rock. Mussels pack the turbine housing, and young fish shelter in its slower water. Its moving parts remain dangerous, its low vibration another signal in a place already full of sound. Around it, animals continue to settle, feed and grow; some are torn away by the next hard run of tide, and others take hold in the spaces they leave.
