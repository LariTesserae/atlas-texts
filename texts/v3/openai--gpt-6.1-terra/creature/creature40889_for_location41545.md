The snails live closest to the stone. They gather on its submerged edges, where a skin of green growth catches the light. Seen from above, they seem hardly to move; by morning, pale winding tracks have appeared behind them. Some are no larger than grains of gravel. Others carry shells thickened and dark at the lip, with bits of silt caught in their coils.

Small fish move over them in loose, shifting groups. Their backs are brown as the shallows, but when one turns, a silver flank flashes and vanishes. They feed on tiny creatures among the green mats and rise for insects that have fallen onto the water. At the approach of a bird’s shadow they scatter beneath the shelves, where the water remains after the wider pools have shrunk.

The frogs keep mostly to the muddier margins and the fresher water near the seeps. By day they sit with their eyes above the surface or tuck themselves among the sedge roots. At dusk the males begin calling from several pools at once. A bird stepping through the mud can send them into the water in quick succession, each frog leaving a widening ring. The calls stop locally, then begin again a little farther off.

The long-legged birds hunt through the day, each working a stretch of shallow water. Some move deliberately, lifting one foot clear before setting down the other; some hurry after what they have disturbed. They take fish and frogs, but also worms and small crustaceans brought up from the mud. When a pool becomes crowded, a bird may raise its wings and drive another away, then stand motionless again as if the interruption had never happened.

Smaller birds pick along the drying margins. They turn shells, probe the silt and gather where a line of moisture shows that water is still seeping beneath the surface. Their nests are tucked into gravel or under shrubs on the higher patches. The chicks leave the nest soon after hatching and follow the adults over ground that offers little cover. When danger passes overhead, the adults call sharply, and the chicks flatten themselves among stones scarcely larger than they are.

Under the shrubs, spiders fasten webs between stiff stems. Beetles shelter beneath pieces of drift and come out onto the damp sand at night. Moths find the little flowers that open after sunset; flies visit them by day. On the highest gravel, a small lizard warms itself beside a tree root, retreating into its shade when the rock grows too hot.

During long warm stretches, life gathers into the remaining water. Fish crowd the deeper hollows; snails cluster below the waterline; birds follow the retreating edges. Some pools are lost altogether. After rain, the shallows join again, and fish and swimming insects pass over ground that was dry the day before. The birds spread out. Frogs begin calling from places that had been quiet for weeks.
