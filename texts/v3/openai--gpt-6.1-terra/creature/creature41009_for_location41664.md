There are animals in the pools, though at first they look like deposits of the white sediment. Each is about as long as a fallen leaf, flattened against the bottom, with a soft body that widens into a shallow fringe. The fringe is made of many fine limbs. They move separately and so slowly that the animal appears to be holding still even as it makes its way around a root.

Its upper surface is translucent. Through it one can see a pale branching gut and, sometimes, the green of a leaf it has recently eaten. It feeds on leaves that have softened in the water, on the thin growth along submerged roots, and on the tender root tips themselves. The trees make more roots; the animals leave them places to grow.

Most pools hold one or two. In the smallest, a single animal may lie folded around itself, its fringe tucked beneath its body. When the water rises after rain, it unfolds and travels into a neighboring pool. At other times it crosses the damp earth by following a dark seam, pressing its body close to the ground. Such a crossing can take much of a day. It leaves a faint track in the mineral surface, which slowly fills with water behind it.

The young are carried under the parent’s fringe. They are almost clear and cling there in a row, taking on a little of the sediment’s color as they grow. Eventually they let go and settle nearby. For a time they remain close enough to touch, though they do not seem to seek one another out.

The animals have no visible eyes. Still, they turn away from a hand held above the water, and they gather beneath the reflections of the trees. Sometimes an animal will wait under a reflected leaf that has already opened overhead but remains furled in the pool. When the image changes, it moves on.

In the moss around the trees live smaller things: pale mites, soft-bodied larvae, and threads of fungus that bind damp grains of earth together. The broad cups of the plants hold their own minute inhabitants, some no larger than flecks of dust. When a cup overflows, they are carried down the stem into the moss, or out across the ground toward a pool.

None of these lives disturbs the basin much. A fringe passes through sediment; a root tip disappears; a leaf, held briefly in place by an animal beneath it, begins to come apart. By evening, the bottom of a pool may be arranged a little differently, although its surface has scarcely moved.
