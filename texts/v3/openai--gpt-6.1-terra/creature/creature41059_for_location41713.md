The shell beds are older than the shelves on which they lie. Their inhabitants came up with the water, attached to drowned gravel and to one another, and settled wherever the new stone gave them purchase. Each has a narrow foot and a shell with a soft, flexible seam. The outermost animals hold fast to the rock; those above them fasten to other shells, making a crowded pavement that shifts almost imperceptibly when the water rises.

They feed from the water passing between them. At intervals a whole bed closes its seams, producing the thin clicking that carries along the channels. A disturbance at one edge can set off the next cluster and the next, until the closing has traveled farther than the original pressure. Large animals sometimes rest beside a shell bed with part of their weight in the water. When the clicking reaches them, they wake slowly and lift their feet clear.

The grazers find these beds by touch and by the taste of the water around them. Most are as long as a person’s arm, though some grow much larger in the deep mud. Their bodies taper at both ends, and a band of short, muscular feelers surrounds the mouth. They sweep these over stone and sediment, gathering the films that grow in the warm water. Where a grazer has passed, the mud holds a shallow furrow with a finer combing along either side.

They eat the smallest shell creatures too, but cannot easily detach an adult. Instead they wait at the edges of a bed, where young shells settle on grains of gravel. A grazer takes the gravel into its mouth, works it for a time, and leaves a row of clean grains behind. The larger grazers carry communities of their own: tiny shelled animals attach along their backs, and insects settle in the damp folds above their breathing pores. Now and then a grazer presses itself under a stone and pulls through, scraping some of this burden away.

The insects spend much of their lives out of the air. Their young live in the cups of the fleshy plants, feeding on whatever collects there: shed skins, soft fragments of reed, the bodies of others that have fallen in. They breathe through a tube held against the surface. An adult leaving a cup pushes itself up the plant’s folded wall and rests on the rim until its wings have stiffened.

In the warm air the adults drift more than they fly. Their wings are broad and soft, with fine hairs that register currents from a moving body. They gather around animals for warmth and moisture, and around people for the salt left on skin. Most take nothing more. A few have mouthparts sharp enough to pierce, though their bite is slight and often goes unnoticed until the swelling comes.

The reed beds shelter smaller animals that seldom cross open mud. Some cling lengthwise to a stem and rasp at its surface; others live inside the hollow joints, entering through breaks made by larger mouths. One narrow animal cuts a reed near the waterline and carries the length before it, breathing through it while its body remains buried. When the water rises, the reed may drift away, and the animal must come up to cut another.

The heavier animals make paths that last until the next seep fills them. A single foot can press a basin into the mud deep enough for shell creatures to settle in its bottom. Their feet are broad and divided underneath by folds, which spread under weight and gather again as the foot lifts. They move slowly across uncertain ground, testing each place before committing themselves. A resting animal can remain in one position for days, drawing warm water beneath its belly. Small creatures collect in the sheltered hollow, and when the animal finally rises, they scatter into the channels it leaves.

They graze as well, though they can take the tough reeds that smaller mouths avoid. Their lips are thick and sensitive; they strip a stem between them, leaving the roots in place. A feeding animal may pull a whole patch toward itself and hold it there with one foot while it eats. Travelers sometimes find the flattened patch before they feel the animal nearby. It may be standing quietly beyond the reeds, waiting for the pressure of their steps to pass.

The young stay close to adults and learn their routes through the basin by following the long, low pulses of their steps. When a route crosses an overlap, the adults do not always hesitate. A young one may emerge onto a distant shelf before the adult reaches it, then remain still, answering the pressure of each approaching foot with a small stamp of its own. If the two places draw apart before they are reunited, the calling can continue for a long time through the ground.

The pumping houses provide another kind of shelter. Warm water leaks from joints and collects beneath their concrete islands. Shell beds grow thick around the outflow pipes, and grazers come to feed at their margins. Insects enter through the vents and rest against the casings. A worker opening a housing may have to brush them away from the bolts before beginning.

The people who tend the pumps know many of the resident animals individually by their habits. One large animal regularly sleeps across a guide rope near an island, pulling it down into the mud without breaking it. The workers leave extra slack there. Another has learned to wait by a discharge pipe and drink when the engine changes its stroke. Workers approaching that house feel the interruption in the pump’s vibration before they reach the island.

They also keep small cups near their doors, filled with fresh water from the tanks. The insects breed in them, and the workers empty and refill the cups when the larvae become too crowded. It keeps some of the adults away from the machinery. It also gives the people something reliable to check on: a cup remains beside its door even during the periods when a familiar route arrives at the wrong house.

When the records disagree, a worker may sit on the concrete and wait with a hand against the pump casing. The rope can be tested, the oil jar opened, the water tasted again. Around the island the shell beds continue their faint clicking. A grazer moves along the edge of the concrete, and somewhere farther out a heavy foot settles, sending a pressure through the wet stone. The worker feels it beneath the pump’s steady beat and waits for the place to become certain enough to travel.
