The lizards are the most numerous animals a visitor is likely to see, though at midday most have withdrawn into the stone. Their bodies are broad and low, and the scales along their backs collect dust until they are almost the colour of the ledge beneath them. They feed on small insects, soft growth around damp cracks, and whatever the carrion beetles leave behind. Older lizards learn the best condensation routes and wait beside them before dawn. When water appears, several may drink from the same fissure, darting in and away without wasting time on much fighting.

The beetles are active longer into the hot hours. Their hard wing cases are ridged, and some gather beneath overhangs where moisture forms on the stone. They feed on dead lizards and birds, on the dung of the herds, and occasionally on one another. A carcass does not remain intact for long: beetles find it from surprising distances, disappear into its shadow, and leave a scattering of light bones that the wind eventually moves.

The herd animals range widely. A gully that holds a dozen of them one evening may not be visited again for weeks. They browse the thorn shrubs carefully, stripping the smallest leaves and biting off new shoots before moving on. Their narrow muzzles can reach into crevices where fallen seeds and dried plant matter collect. Much of their water comes from what they eat, but they also know the dependable damp places. At these they stand quietly, taking turns at the rock, their ears held out from their heads.

Calves are born in sheltered cuts between ridges, usually after a period of rain or cloud. For the first few days they lie still among the slabs while the adults feed nearby. Their pale coats make them conspicuous on dark ground, but the broken country gives them many places to hide. Once they can travel, they stay close to the centre of the herd, placing their broad feet where the adults have passed.

The dark birds watch these movements from the rising air. Some take small live prey; others descend when a herd leaves a weakened animal behind. They also follow maintenance vehicles at a distance, investigating disturbed ground and anything exposed by the turning wheels. At night they roost separately on high columns, tucked into pockets of stone that hold a little warmth.

The reddish mats around the vents are communities in themselves. Fine strands bind mineral grains together, making a surface in which tiny mites and larvae can shelter. When moisture returns, the mats swell and darken, and small animals emerge to graze on them. Their edges grow slowly outward along wet streaks; a burst of steam or a change in the water’s course can kill a section just as slowly.

People come and go between the pumping stations. A crew may spend several days at one borehole, checking valves, clearing mineral deposits from the condenser and repairing damage caused by heat and grit. They carry their water and keep to the iron posts when travelling. While a station is occupied, lizards gather in the shade beneath its pipes, and beetles investigate scraps caught around its foundations. The workers learn to check boots and tools before putting them down.

After a crew leaves, the station stands quiet except for the movement of vapour through its pipes. By evening, animals begin to cross the open ground around it again. If cloud reaches the plateau, droplets form on the cold iron and gather at the bottom of each post. Lizards wait there as they wait beside the old mineral-rimmed cracks, drinking from whatever the night provides.
