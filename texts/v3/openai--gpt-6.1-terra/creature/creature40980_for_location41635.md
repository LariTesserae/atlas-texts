The most numerous inhabitants are small enough to live beneath a pebble and still have neighbors. Pill bugs press into the wet seams, their armored backs fitting together until one moves and the others loosen around it. Beetles travel farther, over the exposed silt and across one another, their feet leaving marks that a falling drop can erase. The pale crickets keep to the cracks. Their long feelers emerge first, testing the air while the rest of them remains hidden.

There are hunters among them, and animals that live on what the hunters leave. A spider tends the web in the shaft of light, repairing each torn place before the warmth carries another fly through. Under the stones are eggs in clusters, translucent grains stuck fast to the undersides. Some are guarded; others have been left with nothing but the stone between them and the moving floor.

The snails on the moss advance so slowly that the green seems to wear away of its own accord. They graze past one another without apparent dispute. When the seam dries at its outer edge, they gather closer to the middle, leaving a dark margin of scraped cement behind them.

The stem through the roof belongs partly to the chamber. Its leaves are outside, but its roots hang down among the animals, and moisture collects on the fine hairs. A pill bug may climb the wall to reach them. The grass at the entrance admits more visitors than it shelters: flies brushing past, beetles coming in from the hot ground, a cricket drawn toward the cooler dark.

None of these animals has had long to become established here. The broken surfaces are still sharp; the furrows are still settling. Their routes change when a pebble shifts or the pump runs. Some return to the same hollows nevertheless, turning under the same edges each time, as if a few weeks of use were enough to make a home.

After the motor stops, something holds them all in place.

The beetles pause in mid-step. The spider draws its web close, with the fly in it and the loose silk and the fragments caught over previous days. The snails do not visibly move at all. In the silt, grains rise onto their ends, crowded so closely that they seem to support one another.

Whatever continues the trembling has no visible body. It may be in the slab, or the packed earth below, or somewhere farther off along the pumping station’s buried works. Yet it reaches every inhabitant at once, and for those few breaths their separate movements cease.

Then the grains fall. The spider lets out its silk. Legs come down, feelers sweep forward, and the floor begins again, one small animal at a time.
