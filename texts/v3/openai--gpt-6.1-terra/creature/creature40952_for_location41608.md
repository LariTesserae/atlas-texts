The chamber’s inhabitants live chiefly at its edges. The open floor is crossed rather than occupied: by a bat making its circuit, a cockroach moving between the engine and a damp joint, the worker carrying a strainer to the light. Around that space, every seam has conditions of its own.

The cockroaches shelter where the engine housing meets the stone. Some press into the hottest cracks; others spread into the passages when the worker has gone. They find crumbs and other scraps brought down on boots, along with dead insects fallen from the lamps and the bats’ roost. Their young stay deep in the seams, among shed skins and fine black droppings. When the housing warms, the cluster shifts, making a faint, dry sound beneath the engine’s knock.

The midges gather where the lamps make the damp air brightest. Their larvae hang in the collection channels, dipping and bending when the water is disturbed. Some survive to emerge and rise through the chamber; others are caught in the strainer or taken by a bat. Adults rest in crowds on the wire cages. The trembling seen there is made by many small movements, not by the lamp itself.

High near the shaft, the bats occupy joints that hold cooler air. They are close enough to touch one another, though not all are still. A head turns outward; folded wings adjust against brick. From below, the colony is easy to mistake for irregular mortar until one animal lets go and drops into flight. The shaft connects them to the night above, but the chamber offers insects, shelter and a place to return to. Their droppings fall into cracks and onto ledges, providing food for smaller creatures the worker is unlikely to notice.

The algae nearest the lamp has a narrow advantage: water that does not dry and a little light that is rarely extinguished. Its dark coating follows the channel stone and thins where the lamp’s reach fails. The shoots under the shaft are more exposed. They receive what light and air come down from above, and they lean into the opening, delicate against the cut rock.

None of these inhabitants has had much time here. The drill marks remain sharp, and the thin mineral crusts at the seeps are still forming. Yet the chamber is no longer simply a space left over around a machine. The pump supplies warmth and keeps the water moving; the shaft admits air and flying animals; the passages bring occasional food and disturbance. After each visit by the worker, the roaches come out again, the midges settle, and the water resumes its slow rise along the stones.
