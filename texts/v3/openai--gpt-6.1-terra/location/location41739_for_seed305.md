The plateau is so high and broad that its far side has weather of its own. From any ridge, the land continues through a series of bright, angular rises until the horizon becomes indistinguishable from cloud. The mountains are young. Their black faces are still sharp enough to catch and hold slabs of ice, and their gullies end abruptly where the rock has not yet had time to give way.

It is cold even at noon. Sunlight pours over the plateau for most of the year, striking the pale stone and the backs of animals, but does little to warm the air. Moisture arrives in brief, fine rains that freeze on contact. At dawn, condensation gathers under leaves and in the hollows of horns; by afternoon it has become a skin of ice. There are few open pools. Most water is held in dark moss, in porous rock, or in the swollen bodies of plants.

The vegetation grows thickly wherever a surface offers purchase. Blue-green cushions climb the scree. Forests of jointed fronds fill the sheltered valleys, their translucent leaves layered so densely that the ground beneath them is green even in shadow. Some fronds stand taller than an animal; others creep between stones in intricate mats. They grow slowly, adding a clear, hard segment each year. When the wind presses through them, the segments rub together with a dry, metallic ticking.

Animals occupy nearly every space among them. Great, low-bodied grazers stand shoulder to shoulder across the flats, feeding on the fronds with broad combs of teeth. Their coats are white on top and black underneath, and their breath rises in such volume that shallow clouds form above the herds. Smaller creatures travel along their backs, feeding on ice and loose hair. Still smaller ones live in the seams of their horns. On the rock faces, pale many-legged climbers crowd together for warmth, so numerous that a cliff appears to be covered in fur until a section of it begins, very slowly, to move.

The noise carries for immense distances: the grinding of teeth, the ticking of fronds, the deep calls by which the grazers keep their places in the herd. Beneath those sounds is a continuous mechanical rumble.

There are no buildings on the plateau, but certain valleys have the force and order of factories. In them live colonies of massive, rooted animals, packed so tightly that their bodies form a single corrugated surface. Each takes in cold air through a crown of fleshy vanes. Deep inside, slow chambers compress it, warm it, and force it through tubes that extend below the frost line. The heat loosens mineral salts from the stone. The animals draw these upward and excrete them in brittle sheets, which other species break apart and carry away.

A colony may operate for centuries without shifting its outline. Its valves open in sequence; its chambers contract; the valley floor trembles. The exhalations emerge through vents along the animals’ backs, and on windless days they gather into a white bank that can be seen from the high ridges. Lightning sometimes travels within it. The animals are sensitive to the charge: before a discharge, their vanes fold, and the whole valley falls quiet for several breaths.

The herds wait through these silences. Then the vanes unfold, the rumble returns, and feeding resumes. Over many years the animals wear narrow paths through the fresh rock, while the plants extend their mats into every protected crack. Farther out on the plateau, where rain is scarce and the ground is bare, the bright edges of new ridges still stand untouched above them.
