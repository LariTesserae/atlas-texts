High on a warm mountainside, a shallow basin holds a grove no larger than a village green. Beyond its rim the slope falls away into cloud, but within it the ground is almost level, worn down over a very long time. Broad shelves of pale stone show through the vegetation. Their edges have been rounded into low steps, and their surfaces are pocked with little cups of water.

The growth is lush but widely spaced. Tree ferns rise from separate islands of dark soil, each with a thick crown spread over an open patch of stone. Between them are mats of moss, glossy leaves and soft grasses, all vivid from frequent rain. Long roots lie exposed across the rock before disappearing into cracks. There is room to walk among the plants without brushing them, though the ground gives slightly underfoot and water gathers in each footprint.

The basin is loud. Water seeps from a broken wall at its upper edge and passes through narrow seams beneath the stone. Here and there it drops into unseen hollows, producing a deep, sustained rushing sound out of proportion to the small trickles on the surface. The rock carries the noise throughout the basin. Standing on one of the broad shelves, you can feel a faint vibration through your feet. Occasionally a cup overflows and sends a clear, high note into the deeper roar.

In the afternoon the air is warm enough to smell the wet soil and the bruised, green scent of fern stems. Mist forms where the stone stays cool, hanging in thin bands a handspan above the ground. When sunlight reaches the basin, the bands brighten but do not immediately lift. They drift slowly between the plants, parting around trunks and settling again. From the rim they look like water moving through the grove, although the pools themselves are nearly still.

Animals are scarce. A small dark lizard may be found on a sunlit rock, and once in a while a single insect crosses an open space with a dry, rattling buzz. Beneath the fern crowns, tiny pale snails graze on the film that grows over the stone. They leave fine clear tracks that last until the next rain.

There are no paths or signs of habitation. Sharp flakes have broken naturally from the upper wall, their edges already softened by water. Some lie among the roots; others have slipped into the cracks and will take years to work their way down. The basin seems largely unchanged from one day to the next. Its pools fill, spill and clear, moss advances over a stone’s edge, and the hidden water continues to sound through the ground.
