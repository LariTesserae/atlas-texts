The city occupies a height that has no summit. Ridges rise behind it into a dimness the same color as the ground, and below it the escarpment falls away until distance ceases to be a useful measure. Looking down, you can see the undersides of terraces that ought to be above you. Farther out, the same terraces appear again, lit from a different direction.

The streets are pale mineral, hard and finely ribbed. They divide and meet with the regularity of a settlement: broad ways, narrow passages, enclosed squares. Along them stand ranks of tall, hollow forms, their walls folded like paper and worn thin at the edges. No hand shaped them. The city grows where the high ground buckles through itself, squeezing chambers and streets out of the rock as a body might raise a scar. Some districts have stood long enough for their corners to soften and their lower walls to slump into smooth aprons of dust. Others are emerging now, a floor lifting grain by grain, an arch slowly opening beneath it.

There is almost nothing in the streets. The spaces between the city’s structures are enormous, and the chambers themselves contain only cool air and the faint smell of struck stone. No pools collect in their hollows. No ice whitens the higher ledges. Powder drifts through the avenues, but it does not fall steadily: it pauses, gathers into loose horizontal bands, then sinks all at once when a change passes through the ground.

The light is neither day nor night. It comes from a dull opening in the sky that shifts position when not watched, so that the shadows on one side of a street may point toward those on the other. From certain squares the opening looks very small. From others it fills the space between the roofs, though no warmth reaches the ground.

Small animals live deep in the mineral walls. They are flat and gray, with bodies divided into overlapping plates, and they move by drawing their edges together. A few come out during the longer intervals of stillness to graze on a dark, powdery film that gathers in sheltered seams. The film has no visible leaves or fibers; after it has been eaten, the bare stone gradually darkens again. The animals are difficult to follow. One may cross a threshold and emerge from a doorway several streets away, leaving a shallow track that ends cleanly at both walls.

Most of the city’s movement is too slow to make a sound. Where old passages narrow, their walls touch and fuse. Elsewhere a street lengthens between two fixed intersections, giving its buildings more room to lean. Now and then a whole terrace slips a little way upward. Dust rises from its edges, and the animals press themselves flat until it settles.

At the outermost district, long rows of empty chambers stand open to the drop. Their floors have been worn smooth by passing grit. From there the city can be seen continuing across several neighboring heights, though the ridges connecting them have been eroded away. In the dim light the districts seem almost close enough to walk between. As you watch, one of the far streets folds gently out of view, and another takes its place.
