The place is a hollow in a broad, low marsh, small enough to cross in a few steps. It lies beneath an overhang of roots, open at one end to the ordinary daylight. The floor, walls and ceiling are all alive: a continuous skin of moss, liverwort and fine-rooted fern, worn into shallow folds by water that has followed the same routes for a very long time.

Inside, there is room to stand. Only a few stalks rise from the green floor, widely spaced, their leaves held flat as hands. Between them are pools no deeper than a thumb joint. Water gathers on the root ceiling and drops into the pools, though they never appear to fill. The ground gives slightly underfoot and releases the smell of bruised leaves and warm soil.

There are animals everywhere once the eye adjusts. Pale mites travel the rims of the pools; beetles move beneath the clear water with bubbles caught against their bodies. Tiny frogs sit apart from one another on the moss, their throats swelling. Most of the noise comes from insects hidden in the living walls. Their calls overlap so densely that the hollow seems to vibrate, a loud, steady rasp with individual clicks breaking through it.

Beneath that is another sound: the low, regular thumping of pumps at work beyond the marsh. A factory stands somewhere outside the hollow, too far away to be seen through the vegetation. Its machinery runs day and night, drawing water from the low ground and sending it back warm through channels farther out. Here, the heat arrives as a faint mist in the entrance and as a warmth in the pools. The roots have grown around the old seepage paths; no pipe or wall enters their chamber.

The insects sing over the pumps rather than against them. The frogs lift their heads at the same moments, and droplets tremble on the leaves. Nothing holds still, yet the little room keeps its shape: water arriving and leaving, roots thickening and wearing back, generations of animals occupying the same minute ledges. Even the loudness feels settled, as much a part of the hollow as its damp green floor.
