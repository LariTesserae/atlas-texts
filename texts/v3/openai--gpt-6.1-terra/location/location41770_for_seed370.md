High above the rest of the built world is a court no larger than a small house. Its floor is a close-set pavement of clay tiles, and its walls rise from the pavement without a seam. Beyond their upper edges, in the dimness, other walls descend in terraces until they disappear.

The court is crowded. Upright slabs stand shoulder to shoulder across it, leaving passages scarcely wider than a hand. Bowls are fitted into the slabs; smaller bowls are nested inside them. Pegs fill the spaces between. Every surface bears the shallow, parallel marks of a stone scraper, softened until they are almost too broad to recognize. The corners of the slabs are round, the bowl rims thick and blunt. The whole place has the worn, settled look of something immensely old.

There is no soil beneath the tiles, only more tiles, packed edgewise and faced with clay. The walls are made the same way. Nothing grows in the joints. There is no moss in the bowls, no insect in the narrow passages, no trace of moisture anywhere. The air is warm and dry, with the faint smell of fired clay.

Light comes from pale mineral set into the mortar. It is just enough to show the nearest surfaces: the rest must be read by their edges, where one slab blocks the weak light from another. The bowls are dark inside. Some have been rubbed smooth by the tool that formed them; others still hold the coarse imprint of a thumb.

The sound is immense. It fills the court with a single, low, abrasive note, loud enough to press against the body. There is no identifiable source and no silence from which it might be distinguished. The note holds exactly steady. It has always been present in the clay, as the warmth has, and neither the sound nor the warmth wears the place away.

Nothing falls from the walls. No dust shifts in the bowls. The court remains at its great height, densely furnished, dimly lit, and deafening.
