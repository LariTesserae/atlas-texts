The city stands on a high plain so old that its mountains have been worn down into long, almost level swells. From the eastern rim you can see for a great distance: districts of pale green towers, broad avenues, factory roofs and, beyond them, the plain continuing under a cold, bright sky.

Nothing here is built on stone. The ground is a continuous mat of living root, compressed over ages into something as hard as fired brick. Where an avenue has cracked, fine white fibers show through. The buildings grow from the same foundation: their walls are layers of bark pressed flat, their girders trained trunks, their windows openings held in shape by the slow tension of living wood. Even the paving is alive, though it grows so slowly that a broken slab may take a century to close.

There is abundant space in the city. Towers rise in widely separated clusters, and the avenues between them are broad enough for daylight to reach the ground at noon. The apparent emptiness is misleading. A person crossing a square walks on a single organism, under the suspended roots of another, through air full of drifting spores. The vegetation fills every surface without much filling the air.

It is cool throughout the year. Rain arrives in brief, slanting showers that darken the bark and run quickly into narrow seams in the streets. In the mornings, condensation gathers on the upper floors and drips from leaf gutters into cisterns. The city has learned to keep this water for a long time. Reservoirs sit inside the thickest trunks, and their levels are marked on the outside by bands of silver lichen. Most days the surfaces are dry enough to rasp beneath a hand.

The factories occupy the western districts, where the wind carries their exhaust away from the denser quarters. Their tall chimneys are hollow stems, blackened at the mouths. Engines turn beneath ribbed roofs, driving saws, presses and pumps. Belts run from one building to the next along elevated tracks; workers use long poles to lift fallen belts back onto their pulleys. The timber they process comes from structures that have been deliberately grown for harvest: immense, leafless frames raised over decades in the open squares.

The sound carries everywhere. Machinery thunders through the root-ground, and the wind sets miles of hollow stems booming at different pitches. Leaves slap against metal shutters. Drainpipes whistle after rain. At times the city seems to be making a single enormous noise, but when an engine stops, its absence can be heard several districts away.

Animals are uncommon. A few gray moths live in the warm factory vents, and small beetles drill through the older roof beams. People watch for them, because a cluster of beetles can reveal a part of a building that has stopped growing. Birds are rare enough that, when one appears, workers come out onto the balconies to identify it.

Distances in the city do not always remain fixed. An avenue may take ten minutes to cross in the morning and most of an hour by afternoon, though its two ends remain plainly visible to one another. The change is most common after rain. Surveyors hammer markers into the paving and measure the streets each day, calling their results through speaking tubes to the transit office. Trams wait at junctions until a route has settled enough to be safe. Anyone caught on a lengthening street is advised to keep walking; standing still can leave a person very far from either end.

The buildings suffer less from these shifts than their occupants do. Their roots stretch through the changing ground, and their crowns lean apart or draw together without breaking. Sometimes a factory finds itself beside a square it has not faced for years. Crews then move the belts, extend the pipes and cut new doors where they are needed.

Toward evening the light turns yellow on the upper leaves. Pumps begin their nightly work, sending stored water through the districts, and the broad squares fill with the smell of damp bark. From the rim, the city looks orderly again: towers spaced across the plain, smoke rising straight in the cold air, and the long green avenues running between them farther than they did that morning.
