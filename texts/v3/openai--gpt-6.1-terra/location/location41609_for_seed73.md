The high country begins above a wall of cloud. For a long way the land rises in green shelves, then gathers itself into a plateau so high that the lower weather is hidden beneath it. The air here is hot and thick enough to breathe. It carries the mineral smell of wet stone and the sweeter smell of leaves warming after rain.

Water lies close to the surface. It collects in shallow basins among the roots, spills over the edges of the shelves, and seeps from the dark seams of the rock. Some of the falls descend only a few meters before vanishing into moss; others pass through the cloud floor and cannot be seen to land. Along their rims, ferns stand taller than a person. Their fronds are heavy with droplets, although the sky overhead is clear.

The plateau is not level. Rounded ridges of black and rust-colored stone divide it into long, open valleys. Erosion has softened their outlines without making them gentle: cliffs still rise from the valley floors, their faces furrowed by innumerable old watercourses. Gardens of pale, fleshy plants cling to these walls. Their roots enter cracks no wider than a hair, and their leaves turn slowly during the day, following warmth rather than light.

Animals are everywhere once the eye learns their scale. Small copper-backed grazers move through the high grass with only their ears showing. Long-bodied creatures, jointed like insects but covered in fine grey fur, pick their way along the wet cliffs. Above them, broad-winged animals ride the warm air without flapping. They cast shadows that travel slowly over the valley, sometimes passing in the opposite direction from their owners.

The sound carries far in the dense air: the continuous hiss of falling water, the dry clatter of seedpods, calls from the canopy and the answering calls of animals concealed in the grass. Near the pools, a chorus of low clicks rises and falls so gradually that it is difficult to say when it began.

There are traces of building, though no settlement is visible. On one ridge stands a small shelter made from a translucent material that has grown around the stone rather than resting upon it. Its doorway remains open. Inside, the floor is dry and slightly warm, and a bowl set into the wall contains a little clear water. Farther out in the valley, three slender poles project above the trees. They are too thin to support their own height, yet they do not bend in the wind.

The heat is held here by something old. On certain evenings, when the sun lowers behind the western cliffs, faint arcs appear between the poles and the distant ridges. The clouds below brighten from within, and for a few minutes the plateau’s shadows become very sharp. Then the arcs disappear. The water continues to fall; the animals continue feeding.

Nothing seems hurried. A fallen trunk may lie for decades across a stream before the moss covering it thickens enough to divert the current. A cliff garden may spend a century spreading from one crack to the next. Even the strange shadows cross the grass at a grazing animal’s pace. From the edge of the plateau, the world below looks like a white sea, while behind it the green high valleys extend, one after another, into the haze.
