The hollow is no wider than a village square, though it takes time to understand its boundaries. Its floor lies high in a range of black ridges, cupped between walls of stone that have been worn into shallow, overlapping shelves. Above them there is a narrow opening to the sky. Very little light comes through it, and what does is swallowed by the crowded floor.

The heat rises from below. Near the rim, the air is thin and cool enough to bite; down among the shelves it has the dry, enveloping warmth of a kiln after the fire has gone out. There is no water here, not even as a film on the stone. The pale dust is powdered salt and brittle mineral scales, and the sharp smell is of hot metal and sulfur.

At first the ground seems to be moving because the heat distorts it. Then individual backs can be made out: small, flat creatures, dark as the rock, pressed edge to edge across the hollow. They climb over one another without apparent hurry. Some have jointed legs tucked so close beneath them that they travel like sliding tiles; others pull themselves forward with soft, pleated rims. In the cracks are smaller bodies still, packed so densely that the seams appear to flex.

They occupy the walls as well. Under every shelf hangs a layer of them, gripping the stone and each other. When a patch loosens, it does not fall far. The animals below part just enough to admit it, then close again. A hand laid on the floor would find almost no bare rock.

The creatures feed on the hollow’s slow exhalations. Warm gases seep through fissures and collect beneath the lower shelves, where the smallest animals gather around them in trembling, granular masses. Larger ones graze upon those masses, scraping them loose with hard mouthparts. Still larger hunters travel beneath the crowd, following the pressure of feet above. Now and then a ripple passes across the floor as something pulls an animal down into a crack. Its neighbors flow into the space before the struggle is finished.

There is little sound for so much activity. Feet move against backs rather than stone. A dry ticking comes from the walls, irregular and faint, and occasionally there is a soft rush like a handful of grit poured from one palm to another. The animals appear to feel one another’s movements more readily than they hear them. When a hunter passes, the warning travels outward through contact: a slight lifting of bodies, shelf after shelf, until the whole hollow seems to take a shallow breath.

The rock has been shaped by ages of this traffic as much as by wind. Its ledges are rounded and polished along the routes most often traveled, but rough beneath the oldest clusters. Fine dust drifts down from the rim and is carried inward by countless feet. Some of it settles in the fissures; some is worked into thin, curved partitions on the warmer shelves. These sparse little walls, no higher than a creature’s back, shelter pale eggs from the current of bodies. Most have crumbled or been trampled flat. A few are being built up again, grain by grain.

At intervals the heat below intensifies. The floor becomes more restless, and the lower layers begin to climb. Within minutes the walls are thick with ascending bodies, leaving dark patches of stone exposed around the vents. A hot breath emerges, carrying a yellow dust that catches briefly in the scant light overhead. Then the flow weakens. The animals descend, the hunters follow, and the hollow fills from the bottom once more.

By the time the last bare patch disappears, the surface looks almost still. Only the slow shifting of its edges shows how much is living there.
