The city occupies a low basin so wide that its far edge cannot be found from the streets. In the darkness it is known instead by changes underfoot: the flat, warm silt beyond the walls gives way to paving that has grown in close-fitting plates, and the shallow water begins to run between them.

The buildings are pale mineral, laid down by seepage over a very long time. They stand in blocks separated by broad, straight channels. Some are low and sealed; others rise in terraces around interior courts where water drips from one level to the next. Their walls are ridged like the insides of caves, yet they meet at corners and open onto passages with the regularity of a planned city. No marks of cutting remain. There are no doors, hinges or other things made to move. The settlement appears to have formed around routes that the water took, then kept forming after the water changed its mind.

It is hot even in the deepest passages. Moisture gathers on every surface and falls again, so that the air is full of a slow, nearly silent rain. The water in the streets is often ankle-deep, though its surface cannot always be distinguished from the stone beneath it. Where it passes over a lip or drops into a cistern, it makes a soft, hollow sound. Farther away comes the occasional low crack of a wall settling.

Very little grows here. A few dark films cling to the undersides of ledges, and in the open courts stand isolated fans of stiff, leafless stems. They draw water through their skins and shed a fine salt that collects in white crescents at their feet. Sometimes an entire fan folds inward over the course of a night, leaving a wet mark on the wall behind it; by another night it has opened again. There are no insects among the stems, no calls from the water, no tracks in the silt.

The darkness is almost complete, but it has gradations. The mineral walls retain a faint warmth that lets their shapes be felt at close range. In certain pools a little grey light appears without showing what casts it. It reveals only what is directly beneath the surface: a pavement, a drowned stair, a scattering of small round stones. Looking up from such a pool gives nothing back. Looking down again may show a different pavement.

The streets do not stay quite where they are. Water entering a channel can be heard arriving in a court much farther away than the channel should reach. A person following it may pass the same recessed doorway twice, once on each side, without having turned. The buildings themselves seem settled and old; their surfaces are deeply worn by water, and their corners have softened over ages. What changes is the distance held between them.

At the center of the basin, if it can be called a center, the channels widen into a shallow square. Rows of buildings face it from all sides. Water crosses the square in thin, overlapping sheets, each moving in a different direction. When the sheets meet they rise briefly into clear vertical seams, then sink flat again without a splash. New mineral gathers along those seams. Over years, or perhaps in a single long interval of darkness, the lines thicken into walls and divide the square into smaller courts.

Elsewhere, walls dissolve. Warm water wells up through their bases and carries them away grain by grain, leaving pale fans of sediment in the streets. An avenue can widen while another closes. The city is never finished, but neither does it fall into ruin. Its empty rooms continue to take shape around the moving water, and the water continues to find ways through.
