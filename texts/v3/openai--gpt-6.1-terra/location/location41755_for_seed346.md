The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few city blocks. From its rim you can see nearly all of it: roofs crowded beneath roofs, narrow bridges passing through walls, towers that rise only a few stories before bending sideways into their neighbors. The ground beyond is flat and pale. There are no distant mountains, only a low horizon and a sky the color of unpolished tin.

It is intensely cold. Dampness collects nonetheless, beading on the undersides of rails and the edges of doorways. A fine rain visits occasionally and leaves black dots on the stone; most of it freezes before it reaches the drains. In sheltered places, shallow pools remain liquid around some buried source of warmth. Their surfaces are crowded with slow-moving interference patterns, though nothing falls into them.

The town is densely built but not quite finished. Streets end against clean slabs of rock, as if the rock had been placed there yesterday. Foundations emerge from the ground with their measurements still glowing faintly along them. In one courtyard, a hillock is being made: grains lift from a tray in a steady, silent procession and settle into a low mound. Its slope is smooth and untouched. A sign beside it asks pedestrians to keep off until weather has been installed.

The older structures are difficult to distinguish from the infrastructure that supports them. A building may have a staircase on its exterior, a second staircase enclosed within the first, and a third visible only in the reflection of a window. Thin conduits cross the lanes at every height, carrying heat, light, and things for which there are no local names. The lamps produce an ordinary white daylight, bright enough to show the salt on the paving and the frost caught in seams. They have no visible bulbs. Their light comes out of the space between two closely fitted surfaces.

Movement here is patient. A bridge takes several hours to decide which bank it connects. You can watch the far end drift across a courtyard by marking it against the windows behind it. Residents know to cross near the middle, where the distance remains more or less reliable. They leave their doors weighted open in the morning, because an unobserved room sometimes becomes deeper than it was overnight.

There is vegetation, though it has little ground to claim: stiff gray moss in the mortar, a few wiry plants growing from warm vents, a mat of tiny leaves around one of the liquid pools. The leaves turn very slowly toward anyone standing nearby, then stay pointed in that direction after the person has gone. Once in a while a small white animal comes out from beneath the conduits to drink. It resembles a vole until it moves, when its body seems to arrive a fraction of a second before its feet. Tracks appear on the frozen paving, but only for the part of its journey it has not yet made.

The place is mostly quiet. Water ticks as it freezes. Somewhere inside a wall, a mechanism gives a soft double knock at intervals of several minutes. Voices carry poorly, even along the straight streets; they seem to be absorbed by the close-packed buildings, then returned much later as a murmur from another direction.

At the basin’s center is an open square, small enough to cross in a dozen steps. Every entrance leads into it, although there are more entrances than the square has sides. People pass through without looking up, carrying sealed containers, coils of translucent cable, bundles of insulation. In the middle stands a plain stone bench, damp with condensation. Sit on it long enough and the town begins to acquire a second horizon, low down among the doorways. It is possible to see a pale strip of open ground there, and buildings still being assembled beyond it. Then someone crosses the square, and the view closes behind them.
