The city lies in a shallow basin, so low and broad that its far edge is difficult to distinguish from the weather. From the upper floors of the old works, the streets appear as a pale grid between black roofs and stretches of standing water. Beyond them are settling ponds, levees and bare flats, all under a low brown-gray sky. Even at midday the light is dim. Heat shimmers above the concrete, but the sun is only a brighter patch behind the haze.

Rain falls often enough to keep the ground wet, though it rarely cools the place for long. It gathers in tram grooves and loading bays, runs down the sides of warehouses, and seeps back out through the lower courses of their walls. The basin has no easy outlet. Pumps lift water over the levee in slow, measured volumes; when they stop, it begins to return through the soil.

The buildings have been weathered into the landscape. Brick corners are rounded, mortar has washed out of the joints, and salt has gathered in white crusts beneath the windows. The older streets sag toward their drains. Some drains have long since filled with silt, leaving shallow pools whose edges advance and retreat with each shower. There are no trees along the avenues, no weeds in the paving cracks. The ground is too saline, and the heat close to the surface is fierce.

The city is not entirely abandoned. At the western end, an industrial works still separates salts from water brought in by canal. Its pipes cross the flats on concrete supports, and its evaporation ponds spread over an area as large as the town itself. A few inhabited buildings stand near the pumping station, their lower windows coated white against the heat. Workers move between them in the early hours and again toward evening. During the hottest part of the day, the streets are usually empty.

The machinery is not very loud at a distance. There is a low hum near the pumping station, an occasional knock from a loose sheet of metal, and the thin splash of water dropping through a culvert. Much of the basin is quiet enough for the small sounds to carry: droplets falling from a roof into a tank, grit sliding down a concrete slope, wings ticking against a window.

The water is crowded. In the fresher channels, clouds of insect larvae hang beneath the surface, rising and sinking when a shadow crosses them. In the salt ponds, brine shrimp move in dense, rust-colored patches. Tiny crustaceans collect along the sheltered edges of basins and in the lee of submerged rubble. After rain, flies emerge in enormous numbers. They cover the warm concrete around the ponds and lift away in loose sheets when a bird walks through them, settling again a short distance behind it.

There is little for the animals to eat that can be seen from above. Water arriving through the canal carries fine organic debris from distant districts. Bacteria work through it in the sediments, and the smallest animals feed on the suspended material and the films coating the pond bottoms. Beetles hunt among the wet stones; birds follow the shallows, picking through the larvae and shrimp. Their footprints overlap so densely at the pond margins that fresh rain erases them before the mud has time to dry.

At evening the heat remains in the walls and paving. Water continues to gather under the pipes and run through the low streets. The birds settle on ledges and levees, the flies become less active, and the pumps keep their slow rhythm. From a roof above the town, one can see a great distance across open ground and empty streets, while just below, in almost every pool, something is moving.
