The town lies below the surrounding plain, in a broad excavation open to the sky. From the rim, its roofs make a close-set field of clay and stone, interrupted by pale courtyards, narrow lanes and the occasional shaft of daylight between walls. The far side is visible in clear weather, though distant enough that people moving there look like flecks of grit.

The ground is new to the air. Layers of yellow clay, gray marl and loose gravel show in the cut banks, their edges sharp where spades have left them. At the working margins, people are still clearing ground for houses. Fresh blocks of mud dry in ranks beside low walls; heaps of stone occupy the spaces where streets are meant to continue. The town has not so much worn into the landscape as begun to fill it.

Almost every patch of level ground has a use. Roofs hold drying reeds and shallow trays of grain. Courtyards contain hearths, water jars and stacks of split stone. Lanes pass between these enclosures, sometimes narrowing to the width of a loaded handcart before opening into a work yard or a small square. From several points the view carries a long way, but there is little empty ground within it.

The air stays cool below the rim. Rain reaches the town in brief showers, darkening the clay and gathering in lined cisterns. For a day afterward, thin films of water shine along the paved channels; then the surfaces dry again, leaving dampness in shaded corners and beneath the walls. In the morning, condensation beads on stone jars and runs down the north-facing sides of buildings.

Plants have taken hold where water lingers. Short grasses fringe the drains, and a few tough shrubs grow on the upper banks, their roots threaded through gravel. People tend small beds near the cisterns, protecting them with low borders of stone. A sparrow comes down from the rim to pick among the grain; now and then a mouse slips beneath a stack of bricks. Such sightings are uncommon enough to draw a glance.

Work continues throughout the settlement without much noise carrying. A wooden mallet taps, a blade scrapes over clay, someone pours water into a trough. Roofs are patched, channels cleared, blocks turned to dry on their other sides. Near the edge of town, a fresh wall is rising one course at a time, while beyond it workers cut the next courtyard into the bank. By evening the new surfaces have lost their wet shine, and the stored warmth leaves the stones.
