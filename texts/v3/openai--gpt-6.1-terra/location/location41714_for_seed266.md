The plateau begins above the haze and goes on beyond sight. Its surface is warm gray stone, worn into broad shelves and shallow basins. Here and there a ridge rises from it like the back of an enormous buried creature, its crest thinned by ages of wind. The air is clear enough that distances are hard to judge: a dark notch on the horizon may be a cleft a day away or a pass through a range several hundred miles distant.

Nothing grows in the usual sense. There is no grass in the cracks, no lichen on the sheltered rock. The ground holds its warmth well into the long night, and in the morning the sun brings out a faint metallic smell from the dust.

A city occupies one of the higher shelves. It is spread very thinly: ranks of squat foundries, storage towers with fluted sides, long empty streets marked by rails. Its districts are joined by enclosed conveyors that cross the open stone on low piers. Seen from a distance, these connections appear more substantial than the buildings themselves, as though the city had been drawn first and filled in only where necessary.

The furnaces run almost continuously, though there is little smoke. They separate useful metals from the plateau’s dark ores and draw a pale gas from seams deep below the city. Tall radiators give up the waste heat. On calm days, the air above them bends the outlines of distant ridges. Machinery turns behind thick walls; outside, one hears only a low vibration through the soles of the feet and, occasionally, the small settling click of hot metal.

The city does not stand at the summit of anything. The plateau is so high and extensive that its interior has weather of its own. Slow banks of dust move across it, shading hundreds of miles at once. When two banks meet, fine grains rise between them in a column that can remain standing for days. Their lower ends are broad and indistinct; their tops spread into flat, copper-colored fans. People navigating the old roads use the fans as landmarks, although none remains in quite the same place from one season to the next.

Animals are everywhere once the eye learns to separate them from the ground. Most lie still for long periods, taking warmth and charge through broad plates pressed against the stone. Their bodies contain soft mineral gels rather than water, and many feed by grinding conductive dust between internal surfaces, drawing a trickle of energy from the reaction. At dusk they lift themselves on jointed limbs and move toward fresh deposits.

There are thousands in the basins: flat, six-legged grazers no larger than a hand; heavy, pale-backed animals that leave parallel grooves behind them; thin-winged things that sail low over warm rock, their wings crackling faintly with gathered charge. Predators follow the moving herds, sensing the electric fields inside their bodies. A chase may last an hour and cover only a few hundred yards. When it is over, the herd closes around the place again.

Some animals come into the city. They gather beneath the radiators and along the conveyor piers, where charged dust collects. The workers have fitted guards around exposed terminals, but otherwise leave them alone. At night the streets can be crowded with pale backs, all angled toward the warmth of a factory wall. The animals are nearly silent; their feet have worn broad and soft, and they communicate through shifts in the charge around them. A person walking among them may feel the hairs lift along one arm before the nearest bodies turn.

Far beyond the city, the plateau breaks into an escarpment. From its edge the lower world is hidden by a tawny veil, and the cliffs descend through the daylight until their details disappear. The brink has been rounded by weather into terraces and bowls. Old rail lines approach it, then divide, following ledges down into mines whose lamps are too distant to distinguish from points of exposed ore.

The wind at the edge is warm and steady. It carries dust upward from the lower country and lays it across the plateau in layers so fine that a single passing animal can disturb years of accumulation. By the time the dust reaches the city, the machinery is ready for it: collectors along the southern streets draw it in, and inside the walls it becomes wire, plate, and the dark fuel that keeps the furnaces warm.
