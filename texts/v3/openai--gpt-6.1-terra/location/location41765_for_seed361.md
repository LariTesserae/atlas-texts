High on an old plateau there is a hollow no wider than a room. The land around it has been worn so long that its ridges are little more than differences in color: pale stone, paler dust, a dark seam exposed for perhaps a million years. From a distance the hollow is invisible. You find it when the ground gives way beneath a ledge and the noise rises to meet you.

The stone inside is smooth and warm. Once it may have been a pool, though no stream reaches it now. Rain arrives only in brief, fierce showers, and most of it runs away through cracks before it can gather. A little remains under an overhang, where drops form each morning on the cooler rock. They fall into a basin the size of a cupped hand, making a clear, regularly spaced note.

Vegetation grows only where that moisture can be caught. A thick fern spills from one fissure, its fronds broad and glossy, almost black underneath. Farther along, a single mat of succulent leaves spreads across the stone, each leaf edged in soft red hairs. The rest of the hollow is open floor: ocher grit, a few polished pebbles, and long stretches of bare rock hot enough to hold the day’s warmth after sunset.

The animals are everywhere, though at first there seems to be almost nothing alive. They shelter beneath the fern, inside the dry seedpods caught in its roots, in the hollow stems, and in chambers bored through the stone. When the first drop falls into the basin, hundreds of small pale bodies emerge from a crack no wider than a fingernail. They have translucent backs and jointed legs; they crowd around the water, drinking so quickly that a visible dark band passes through them.

Their movement brings others. Flat, bronze creatures descend the wall on threads that glint and disappear as the angle changes. They seize the drinkers and draw them upward. Larger animals, looking much like scraps of bark until they unfold, spring from the fern to catch the bronze hunters. Wings rattle. Bodies strike stone. The sound is astonishingly loud for the size of its sources, a rolling clatter punctuated by shrill calls that carry out across the quiet plateau.

For a few minutes the basin is the center of a frantic, many-layered pursuit. Then the water is gone. The pale drinkers retreat, the bark-colored hunters fold themselves among the fronds, and the bronze threads are reeled back into the wall. A scatter of empty skins remains on the grit. Even in the lull, something scrapes and clicks beneath the ground.

Near the entrance stands a small structure made of three thin, upright slabs. They do not touch, yet each keeps the others from falling. Their surfaces are the same color as the surrounding stone until a shadow passes over them; then fine patterns appear, shifting faster than the eye can follow. The slabs are cool in full sun. Condensation gathers on them on mornings when nothing else in the hollow is damp, and runs down their inner faces into a narrow groove.

The groove leads toward the fern, but it is broken halfway there. Some days the water crosses the gap anyway, passing through the air in a trembling silver line. On other days it falls onto the bare floor and vanishes with a hiss. The animals seem accustomed to both outcomes. A few wait beneath the gap before dawn, their mouths open.

At midday the heat drives most life into shelter. The fern slowly lifts its fronds, exposing the shaded stone beneath, while tiny winged things circle above it in loose, widely spaced loops. Their wings hum at different pitches. As the air warms, the notes climb until the whole hollow seems to ring. The sound draws a shadow across the opening: a long-bodied flier has come from somewhere beyond the plateau, too large to enter. It clings to the rim and lowers a narrow tongue toward the circling wings.

By evening, dust has settled on the slabs and on the backs of the waiting animals. The stone releases its heat. Above the hollow, the plateau lies almost flat under an ordinary, bright sky; below the ledge, the fern closes over its crowded roots, and the first cool drop begins to gather.
