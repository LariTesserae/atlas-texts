The city occupies a low, unfinished plain. From a distance its roofs look almost level with the horizon: a close-set field of chimneys, glasshouses, cable gantries and pale ventilation towers, with darker strips of vegetation growing wherever a structure has left even a hand’s breadth of space. Beyond the city the plain continues for many miles, marked by survey stakes and the first frames of buildings. The ground there is still being made.

It comes up slowly through seams in the older surface, a cool gray material with the fine texture of potter’s clay. In daylight it stiffens and takes weight. Crews lay rails and foundations across it almost at once, before another seam opens and the new ground folds gently over their work. Nothing here has been worn into cliffs or gullies. The banks beside the drainage channels have crisp, soft edges, and the little ridges between them look as though they were pressed into shape that morning.

The cold keeps most moisture close to the ground. Rain is infrequent and fine; on some mornings condensation gathers beneath bridges and drops for an hour after the air has cleared. Cisterns stand under the gutters, each with a lid to slow evaporation. The drainage channels are usually dry, though dampness remains beneath their stones, and workers know which inspection covers will have a fringe of moss.

That is enough for a remarkable growth of plants. Low evergreen shrubs crowd the embankments. Thick cushions of silver-green moss climb the brick walls, and tough vines pass through factory windows and root in the dust on the floors. In the glasshouses, beds of dark leaves are stacked on racks several stories high. Outside, the plants grow more slowly but occupy almost every surface: roof seams, rail beds, the spaces between paving blocks, the insulated pipes that run in bundles above the streets. They draw what water they can from mist and brief rain, then hold it through long cold weeks.

The city is always rearranging itself. A foundry may spend a winter making replacement street panels because the ground beneath an avenue has risen in a broad, shallow swell. Railway crews lift a track, pack new soil beneath it, and lay it again farther east. Whole workshops travel on low motorized platforms; a building that stood beside a glasshouse yesterday may be at the edge of a fresh foundation by evening, its cables trailing behind it on wheeled supports.

Much of this happens quietly. The engines are enclosed against the cold and mounted on thick felt pads. Electric carts move over rubberized rails. Inside the factories, machinery makes a continuous low tremor, but the walls are double-skinned and packed with plant fiber, so that outside one hears mostly the faint tick of cooling metal and the brush of leaves against glass. Workers use lights and hand signals across the wider yards. At shift change, hundreds of people can pass along an elevated walkway with only the soft clatter of boots.

Animals use the same crowded routes. Pale mice live in the insulated cable troughs; long-legged birds pick insects from the roof gardens and follow the slow workshops as they move. There are foxes in the outer districts, their coats dense and nearly white, and small beetles that shelter inside the curled leaves of the wall vines. At night, warm air from the vents brings moths out of cracks in the masonry. They gather by the hundreds around the work lamps, then vanish when a vent closes.

The strangest ground is found at the city’s advancing edge. Here, surveyors place iron markers in a fresh expanse of gray and return a few hours later to find the markers closer together, or standing in a different order. The ground has not visibly cracked or shifted. A stake driven deep enough to resist it may reappear lying neatly on its side, with its numbered face turned upward. The crews have learned to build in short sections and leave generous joints. They also leave bowls of rainwater beside their markers. By morning the water is usually gone, and the ground nearby is firm enough to carry a machine.

On a clear afternoon, ordinary daylight reaches every part of the plain, though little of it reaches bare earth. It falls on moving roofs, nursery frames, packed streets and the bright leaves of plants growing around hot pipes. Farther out, workers walk over the new gray surface with their measuring poles. Behind them, vehicles bring the first rails; behind those, the city follows, adjusting itself to each fresh portion of ground.
