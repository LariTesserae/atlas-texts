The high country is almost entirely hollow. From one rim to the next there is room for storms, migrating herds, and long hours of falling. The living surface occupies only a fraction of that space, but wherever there is a surface, it is plant.

It grows in walls and immense tilted fins, in cables that cross the dark at irregular heights, in thin branching shelves suspended from the cables. Some of the larger growths have gathered around new ridges of black stone; others span the gaps between them without visible support. Their roots grip and braid around one another until there is no clear distinction between root, trunk and ground. A traveller can walk for days on a rough green-brown surface, hearing it creak underfoot, and never put a foot on rock.

The ridges are young. They rise in sharp, uneven ranks, with fresh fractures showing pale mineral beneath their dark skins. Very little has yet worn them smooth. Loose shards rest where they fell or have been caught in the plants’ tough basal growth. In places the plants pull slowly at the stone, widening cracks. In others they hold precarious masses together long after they would otherwise have collapsed. When a fin finally breaks away, its fall may strip hanging gardens from several levels before it disappears into the dark.

Cold air drains continually through the hollows. Most of the time it is dry enough to chap exposed skin and stiffen the smallest leaves. Then a current arrives from some lower, wetter country, and condensation spreads over everything it touches. Beads run along the plant cables and collect at their lowest points; broad, shallow cups unfold beneath them. For a few hours there is dripping everywhere. The cups fill, freeze at their edges, and become crowded with feeding animals. By the next day, wind may have emptied them again.

The plants have many ways of waiting through the dry intervals. Some keep water deep inside swollen joints, protected by corky scales. Others spread almost transparent films over the empty space, so thin that the wind passes through them with a hiss. These films gather moisture and food from the air, then contract when cold becomes severe. When thousands contract at once, an entire wall changes its shape, drawing its outer branches inward. The movement is slow enough to miss while watching a single leaf, but a familiar route can be closed by morning.

There is little light. Above the highest ridges, a dim sky sometimes reveals the uppermost crowns, but it rarely reaches far into the living structure. Leaves in the hollows are dark and broad, arranged around every opening where light might enter. Paler growths cluster beneath them, living on what falls or what their hosts provide. Some young shoots carry a faint blue glow at their tips; it helps the small night-flying animals find them, and helps the shoots get pollinated. A traveller who sees a scattered field of those lights may be looking at a nearby branch or at the far side of a gulf.

Animals make heavy use of the few solid routes. Long-bodied grazers move along the cables, gripping with pads and hooked toes as they crop the soft growing tips. Their young ride close against them where the crossings are narrow. Small gliders launch from the upper shelves when the air warms, folding themselves around the larger stems to sleep. Predators wait at junctions, or follow the sounds of feeding through the dark. Beneath all of them, pale many-legged scavengers work through mats of fallen leaves, dead wood and the remains of animals that missed their footing.

The place is seldom quiet. Wind enters one opening and roars through another. Hollow stems whistle at different pitches; loaded branches knock together with reports that travel a long way. When a colony of seedpods dries, the pods snap open in bursts, startling the grazers into stampedes along the cables. Rain is louder still, rattling on the upper leaves and sending delayed cascades down through the layers. Yet sounds are hard to locate. A call can seem close enough to answer and then arrive again, weakened, from another direction.

On a few stable shelves stand shelters made by hand. They are low frames of bent wood, covered with overlapping bark and weighted against the wind with stone. The people who use them cut their steps into old growth with stone blades and carry water in stitched leaf-cups or hollowed gourds. They leave markers at crossings: a length of pale fibre, a split branch tied across a path, a notch at hand height. Such marks need frequent tending. A branch thickens around a binding; a bridge twists out of reach; a whole stand of growth lifts as its supporting trunk pulls toward the light.

Some shelters have been abandoned in place rather than broken. Their roofs have rooted into the shelves beneath them, and fine shoots rise through the seams. A hearth may remain dry under the eaves while the rest of the structure becomes part of the surrounding growth. From there the old path leads away into darkness, along a living span that still bears the shallow cuts of many feet.
