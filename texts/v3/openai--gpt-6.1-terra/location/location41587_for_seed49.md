The city begins below the level of the sea, though the sea itself is a long way off. Its floor is an old basin of dark stone, so broad that weather forms over it without ever reaching an edge. From above, the basin would look filled to the brim: towers, ducts, retaining walls, reservoirs, transit arches, housing terraces and the overlapping roofs of markets cover nearly every available surface. Even the water has structures floating in it, and the air above carries its own crowded layers of traffic.

Rain is common and brief. It sweeps across a district in warm, hard sheets, rattling on metal roofs and turning the stone black, then moves on while sunlight returns behind it. Water collects everywhere: in seams between paving blocks, on the ledges of towers, inside transparent conduits and in broad canals whose banks have been built and rebuilt until the original rock is difficult to find. The ground is always damp. Walls weep from joints high above the streets, and drops fall for hours after a storm has passed.

There are no trees or fields. Roofs are crowded with machinery, tanks and nesting animals rather than gardens. The few soft-looking growths on the lower walls are mineral deposits, pale crusts laid down by generations of seeping water. On hot afternoons they dry at the edges and crack; by morning they have swollen smooth again.

The lowest streets are partly submerged. Their paving is made of old stone, newer ceramic and sheets of dark composite fitted around whatever came before. Water runs through them around ankles and wheels, carrying grit toward grates that never stay clear for long. In places the current is strong enough to bend the legs of the small, pale animals that feed in it. They cluster downstream of waste outlets, gripping the pavement with hooked feet and sifting the water through trembling fans. When a vehicle approaches, they fold flat; the wheels pass over them, and they rise again in its wake.

Higher up, the city is packed just as tightly. Buildings press shoulder to shoulder, but their upper levels have been cut through by elevated channels and walkways. Some towers lean against their neighbors. Others are held apart by great braces that flex slowly as the temperature changes. The oldest structures have rounded corners and deep vertical grooves worn into them by rain. Newer additions have been grown directly over their surfaces: seamless ribs of material that change stiffness under load, windows that cloud to keep out heat, bridges that adjust their own tension as crowds cross.

The place is deafening. Water hammers through spillways, vehicles thrum overhead, machinery knocks inside the walls, and thousands of voices are amplified by the close-built streets. A warning horn sounds somewhere almost constantly. In enclosed passages the noise becomes a pressure felt in the chest; people speak there with their mouths close to one another’s ears, or use lights sewn into their sleeves. When a gate opens and a torrent is released, conversation stops altogether until the rush has passed.

The gates open often. There are too many sudden storms and too much water arriving from the uplands for the canals to be held at one level. Whole streets are designed to flood on a few minutes’ notice. Shopkeepers lift their goods into ceiling racks; pedestrians climb the nearest stairs; vehicles find purchase on higher tracks. A brown surge comes through, carrying loose objects and startled animals, then drains away into the lower channels. By evening the street may be crowded again, its pavement steaming in the warmth.

Animals live throughout the machinery. Broad-bodied swimmers gather in the canals, sheltering under barges and rising to snap at anything washed from the streets. Small gliding creatures nest in the warm exhaust galleries, launching themselves between towers in wavering groups. On the high retaining walls, colonies of shelled animals cling in dense bands, opening and closing with a dry, rapid clatter. Their shells are polished on the outward side by rain and airborne grit. Maintenance crews scrape them away from sensors and moving joints, though the animals return within weeks.

Farther out in the basin, districts disappear into mist. A traveler can ride for days through the same continuous city and still find changes in its grain: narrow wet alleys giving way to immense freight halls, residential towers giving way to stepped waterworks, markets occupying the interiors of old flood barriers. Some districts are ancient enough that nobody can identify the original purpose of their lowest chambers. Others are being assembled now, their structural frames moving slowly across the skyline as autonomous cranes place each section.

Nothing here stays still for long, but the underlying forms have had time to settle. Water follows courses established centuries ago, even where engineers have enclosed them in pipes or lifted them into aqueducts. Storms wear exposed corners down. Sediment gathers behind every obstruction. People and machines clear it, build upon it, or divert the next flood around it. Over the vast basin, clouds keep forming above the warm surfaces, and the city continues beneath them: crowded, wet, loud, and always making room for the water that is about to arrive.
