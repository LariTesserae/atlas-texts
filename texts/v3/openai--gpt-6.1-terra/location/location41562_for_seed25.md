The land lies low, a basin of dark limestone spreading between hills too shallow to be called mountains. Its floor is worn into long, rounded ribs. In the troughs between them, old water has cut channels deep enough to hide a moving animal, though most of the channels are dry. They cross and rejoin for miles, their edges polished by floods that arrive only a few times a year.

There is almost no light. A dim grayness sometimes collects above the basin, enough to separate the nearest ridges from the sky, but the hollows remain black. The stone is cool to the touch. Moisture beads on it after rain and, on colder nights, gathers from the air. Drops travel slowly down the ribs and vanish into cracks.

The noise begins before the animals can be distinguished from the ground. It is a continuous, overwhelming roar, made up of clicks, rasping pulses, deep bellows and a thin shrilling that seems to come from everywhere at once. The limestone carries the sound as well as the air does. A hand laid against the rock feels it trembling.

The basin is crowded with life. Pale, many-legged grazers pack the damp channels, their bodies overlapping as they scrape thin films of bacteria from the stone. Where a crack exhales mineral-rich water, the films grow thick and gray. The grazers press around these places so densely that those beneath them continue feeding by touch alone. Above them, narrow-bodied hunters move across their backs on long jointed legs. Some snatch at the grazers; others wait for the smaller hunters to draw close.

Farther out, heavy animals the size of seals work their way along the troughs. Their skins are folded and pebbled with sensory pits. They lower their blunt heads to the rock, listening, then dig with broad front limbs wherever a cluster of grazers has settled in a fissure. The digging exposes damp mineral surfaces, and within days the bacterial film begins to spread there.

Most of the calling comes from creatures no larger than a thumb. They cling in layers to the sides of the channels, vibrating plates inside their bodies. A single call is a dry, hard rattle. Millions together fill the basin. Their pulses shift as larger animals pass, opening brief corridors of silence that close behind them. Predators appear to follow these disturbances; the callers nearest a hunter fall quiet, while those farther away grow louder.

Rain changes the entire basin. Water spills from rib to rib, filling the channels and driving the grazers upward in heaving sheets. Swimmers emerge from holes that have held a little water through the dry weeks: flat, translucent things with gripping mouths, and larger, blind forms that take shelter beneath the stone as the flood recedes. For a day or two, pools remain in the deeper hollows. They are crowded so quickly that their surfaces quiver without wind.

As the water drains away, the noise alters. The submerged rattlers fall silent, then resume one by one as the air reaches them. Heavy bodies settle into cracks. Hunters pick through the stranded animals left on the ridges. By the time the last open pools have disappeared, the basin is roaring again, and condensation is already gathering on the stone.
