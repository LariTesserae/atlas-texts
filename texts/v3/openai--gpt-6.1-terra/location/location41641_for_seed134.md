The summit lies a few fathoms below the surface, a small crown of black rock rising from a drowned mountain. On a clear day, sunlight reaches every part of it. Ripples overhead throw bright, restless bars across the stone, although the water itself moves very slowly.

The crown is no wider than a house. Its ridges are sharp enough to catch and hold the skins of passing animals, and its fissures show the pale interiors of rock broken open only recently. There is no weed, no moss, not even a green film. The bare surfaces are crowded instead with shell, flesh and mineral growth: white tubes as thick as fingers, translucent bladders, layered collars of rust-red crust. Animals occupy nearly every crevice, pressed so close that when one opens its feeding plates, its neighbors must close theirs.

Warm, mineral-rich water seeps from the fractures. A fine black deposit settles around each opening, and communities of colorless microbes feed on the chemicals in it. Small jointed creatures graze the deposits; broad, soft-bodied hunters lie among them, almost indistinguishable from the stone until a feeding limb unfolds. Above the summit, silver animals hang head-down in ranks, drawing water through their bodies. Their bellies flash as they turn, one after another, with the deliberate timing of a slow wave.

The place sounds like heavy machinery heard through a wall. Deep knocks travel up from within the mountain. Shells rasp together, feeding plates snap shut, and the columns of seep water give off a continuous, resonant hum. Now and then a louder report passes through the whole crown: a crack in the rock extending by a fraction, or a great bladder collapsing and filling again. The animals are accustomed to it. After each report they remain still for a long time, then gradually resume their work.

Nothing here was made, but the summit has the steady occupation of a factory. Water enters through the rock, passes through countless bodies and leaves slightly altered, carrying pale grains down the mountain’s flanks. Over years the animals shift toward newly opened fissures, and the old ones close beneath accumulating shell. From the surface the crown would look almost motionless. Down among the tubes, every available space is in use.
