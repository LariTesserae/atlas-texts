The settlement occupies a single excavated chamber, not much larger than a market square. It lies below a hot plain, beneath a roof of dark basalt held up by brick piers and iron ribs. The excavation is recent: the walls still carry parallel drill marks, and flakes of rock gather beneath edges too sharp to have weathered. In places, pale mineral dust clings to the bricks where the builders have not yet brushed it away.

There is very little open floor. Narrow buildings rise in tiers around the perimeter, their upper rooms projecting over workshops and service passages. Pipes cross between them at head height, bundled so closely that a person can stand in their shade. A raised walkway circles the chamber, with ladders descending to the machinery below. Doors open onto other doors; a window in one wall looks directly into the lit stairwell of the building opposite.

Heat comes through the floor as well as from the engines. The iron railings are warm to the touch, and the air grows hotter near the central well, where a pump draws mineral water from a seam in the rock. There is not much water in it. The pump runs for several minutes, then waits for the seam to fill again. What it raises is divided among a cooling tank, a public tap and a shallow settling basin. Most of the basin’s surface is dry, crusted white around a pool the size of a serving dish.

On the coolest pipes, droplets form during the pump’s brief runs. They collect beneath the elbows and fall into little tin cups fixed to the walls. A few tough plants have taken root where the cups overflow: thin, jointed stems, a dark mat of liverwort, one narrow-leaved shrub trained along a crack in the brick. They grow slowly, but their roots work into the fresh mortar. Someone has placed wire guards around them to keep passing shoulders from breaking them.

There are people here, though they are not always visible. A machinist opens a shutter to let heat out of a workroom. Two workers cross the upper walkway carrying a length of pipe, pausing at each corner to turn it. Behind a frosted pane, a hand wipes clear a small circle of condensation. No animals live in the chamber; there are no insects around the lamps, no birdsong from the shafts, no scratching in the walls.

The light is weak and carefully directed. Caged bulbs burn above doorways and machine beds, leaving the ceiling in shadow. A narrow ventilation shaft admits a little daylight, which moves across the far wall during the afternoon and briefly reaches the settling basin. Dust and steam become visible in it, drifting in different directions as the fans pull air through the chamber.

For all the equipment, the place is mostly quiet. The pump gives a low knock while it runs. A belt whispers over its wheels. Occasionally a valve releases a short breath, or a dropped tool rings against the floor and is answered by a much softer sound from the pipework. These small noises overlap without becoming a roar.

At intervals the cooling tank is drained. Warm water runs through a channel cut across the floor, darkening the stone before disappearing into a lower sump. The channel passes close to the shrub, and during those few minutes its roots receive more water than they have had all day. Then the flow stops, the surface begins to dry, and the workers return to the pump to prepare it for its next short run.
