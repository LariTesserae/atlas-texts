The plateau is high enough that clouds collect below its rim, but so old and worn that there is no longer a clear ascent to it. For hundreds of miles the land rises almost imperceptibly through shelves of pale stone, until one looks back and finds the lowlands hidden under weather. The plateau itself is nearly level. What once may have been mountains survive as long swells, their roots exposed and polished by ages of wind.

It is cold even in full daylight. Frost remains in the shaded seams of the rock, and rain comes thinly, at irregular intervals, as though a cloud were leaking rather than breaking. Water seldom travels far on the surface. It gathers in shallow basins, beads on leaves, disappears into cracks. Some mornings the stones are dry everywhere except beneath the plants, where each patch of green has made its own small wet climate.

The vegetation is lush but widely spaced. Great beds of moss occupy hollows a mile apart, thick as mattresses and folded over themselves in dark emerald ridges. Between them lie broad tracts of bare mineral ground. Where a seam of moisture runs, low forests spread: trees no taller than a person, with dense black branches and leaves so closely packed that snow rests on them as on a roof. Their roots enter the stone through joints too fine for a blade. In summer—the name used for a somewhat brighter season—their canopies open by a fraction, and condensation falls from them in a steady, nearly inaudible drip.

There are no animals to move through these forests. Nothing nests in them or grazes at their edges. Spores drift on the wind, and pale runners creep from one sheltered hollow toward another. When a patch reaches a favorable place, it expands rapidly; when the moisture shifts, its center gradually dies and is covered by new growth. From a distance the beds seem stationary, but old paths have been swallowed and whole groves have migrated beyond their walls.

A city crosses the plateau, though its districts are separated by distances that would elsewhere be called wilderness. Low houses of fitted stone stand in clusters around cisterns. Causeways run between them, marked by upright slabs, and every few miles there is a shelter with a hearth, a bench, and a roof of layered turf. The stonework is careful rather than monumental: lintels worn smooth by hands, drainage channels cut with repeated blows, walls packed with moss to keep out the cold. No chimney smokes now. Doors hang open or have fallen inward, and the hearths contain only fine gray silt.

The inhabitants left behind tools of chipped stone, bone-colored ceramic, and soft hammered metal. They made gutters to catch the brief rains and broad condensation screens from woven reeds. Many of these still work. At night the screens gather moisture; by morning it has run into covered jars or sunk through the floors of deserted houses. Plants crowd the spillways and grow in thick rings around the cisterns. The city’s engineering continues to feed them long after anyone has tended it.

The causeways do not always lead to the same places. Walk one in clear weather and a settlement may appear ahead, its walls distinct against the plain. Walk it under low cloud and the same stones pass a grove, a dry reservoir, then continue into country with no sign of habitation. The distances are not merely mistaken: travelers have laid out cords, placed markers within sight of one another, and returned to find extra stretches of pavement between them. In some districts, the houses seem to share more interior rooms than their outer walls could contain. A doorway may open onto a courtyard filled with snow; the next morning it opens onto a stair going down.

The old builders worked with this uncertainty rather than trying to eliminate it. At junctions they set shallow bowls of polished stone. Each holds a little water when the air is damp, and the water reflects not the sky overhead but the sky above the road that can presently be reached. A dark reflection means cloud somewhere ahead; a white one means open frost. The bowls have cracked and been repaired many times. Some still fill on mornings when there has been no rain or dew.

Weather moves actively across the plateau. Cold fronts gather their own banks of cloud, which cast shadows the size of provinces. Frost advances over the black-leaved groves and retreats again. Thin rain wakes the moss beds: their surfaces swell, seams close, and trapped air escapes in slow bubbles from pools underneath. Far away, a line of stones darkens as water passes through an old channel, and a green district brightens over the course of an afternoon.

Most of this happens quietly. There is the faint brushing of wind over leaves, an occasional drip within a house, the soft settling of frost in a basin. Sometimes a stone shifts in a wall, followed by a long interval in which nothing else moves. The silence carries across the immense open ground until even a nearby sound seems to come from another part of the city.

Toward evening, light remains on the plateau after the clouds below have gone dark. It lies in the pale channels and along the edges of the moss beds. The scattered settlements become difficult to distinguish from the outcrops around them; both are low, weathered, and threaded with green. Then the condensation screens begin to wet, the cisterns receive their first drops, and somewhere along a causeway a bowl slowly fills with the reflection of a sky not yet overhead.
