The chamber is completely underwater, a little larger than a railway station and much older than the machinery still working inside it. Its floor, walls and low vault were cast in sections of dark concrete, then lined with iron plates. Water has worn the joints almost smooth. The corners are rounded, the bolt heads flattened, and the floor slopes so gently toward the drains that the slope is visible only when loose material begins to move.

A row of electric lamps burns behind thick glass along one wall. Most are dimmed by mineral deposits; one flashes intermittently when the current falters. Their light reaches only partway across the chamber, picking out suspended grit and the slow sweep of fish. The water is cool and faintly brown, clear enough near the lamps to see the opposite wall, but cloudy wherever the pumps disturb the floor.

Everything here was made. Even the pale gravel gathered in the seams is concrete worn from the vault and iron scale shed by the pipes. There is no exposed earth. Broad ducts enter through the ceiling and leave beneath the floor, carrying water through this small chamber on its way between larger flooded works. At one end, a bank of pumps sits inside a corroded cage. Two run continuously. A third starts at intervals with a hard mechanical clatter, sending a pulse through the water and stirring old debris from the grilles.

The noise fills the chamber. The pumps rumble through the floor, loose fittings knock, and water rasps over the teeth of the intake screens. The sounds travel far through the flooded structure: a fish feels them before a diver can distinguish one machine from another. When the third pump stops, the remaining noise seems briefly hollow, as if a large object has been removed from the room.

Life gathers around the moving water. Small silver fish hold position downstream of the screens, turning together when the flow changes. Eels shelter in the open ends of abandoned pipes. Mussels coat the undersides of the intake ledges, where food arrives steadily, and pale crustaceans pick through the material that settles around them. Larger fish pass through the ducts occasionally, scattering the smaller ones into the machinery cage. There is little vegetation: a few dark tufts cling near the lamps, and thin patches of algae grow where the light is strongest. Most of the chamber’s food comes in with the water.

The machines have been repaired many times. Newer lengths of pipe cross older ones; replacement brackets sit against walls worn almost featureless. A maintenance hatch high in the vault is clean around its rim, though the ladder beneath it has lost most of its rungs. Somewhere beyond the chamber, people or automatic systems still keep the pumps supplied with power.

For long stretches the place changes only by accumulation: a little more silt behind a bolt, another layer on a lamp, another shell fixed to the iron. Then a pump starts, the current lifts, and the loose floor of debris travels several metres before settling again. The fish return to their places, facing the flow.
