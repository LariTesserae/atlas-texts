The basin lies only a little above the water that surrounds it. From its rim you can see across several miles of low ground: a dark plain crowded with stone fins, shallow pools and ridges of pale mineral crust. Nothing grows here. The surfaces that look furred from a distance are crystals, fine as frost and hard enough to cut a fingertip.

The rock is young and sharp. Broad plates have risen at slight angles, their edges still unrounded, and between them the ground has sunk into narrow troughs. Water stands in most of these, black where it is deep and silver-gray where a skim of silt covers the bottom. After rain, the troughs join into a single, intricate sheet. Even in dry weather, beads gather on the undersides of the rocks and fall back into it.

The light is poor. A low ceiling of cloud diffuses what daylight reaches the basin, and mist sits among the fins long after the rain has stopped. Close at hand there is plenty to see: overlapping seams of stone, salt needles, the round mouths of bubbles trapped beneath clear films of water. Farther away, everything is compressed into bands of gray. The basin feels open because there is no wall enclosing it, though a person walking through it would seldom find a clear path for more than a few steps.

The noise is immense. Water enters the basin through hundreds of fissures, most too narrow to see, and forces air through chambers in the rock. The result is a continuous roar with a low, uneven note beneath it, like surf heard from inside a cave. Here and there a slit gives a thin whistle. The sounds change very slowly: a tone may deepen over the course of a day as water fills a hidden space, then hold for weeks. In one part of the plain, a hollow booming seems to come from underfoot. The stone there trembles faintly against the soles.

Animals are scarce. Occasionally a pale, many-jointed scavenger can be found beneath an overhanging slab, pressed into a seam with its limbs folded close. It ventures out when the mist is thickest, moving over wet rock in long pauses. It leaves small crescent-shaped marks in the mineral film and sometimes carries away the soft remains of another creature. Most pools appear empty, though something will now and then disturb the surface from below.

There are a few low piles of stone on the higher ridges, each made of four or five flat pieces laid carefully one over another. Some have tilted; others have mineral deposits binding their edges. They are too widely spaced to suggest a settlement. In the dimness they can be difficult to distinguish from the layered rock, until the regular gaps beneath them catch the light.

Toward evening, condensation gathers more quickly. The mineral needles thicken almost imperceptibly, and the pools take on a blurred sheen as mist settles across them. The roar continues without a break, filling the spaces between the rocks while the visible world remains nearly still.
