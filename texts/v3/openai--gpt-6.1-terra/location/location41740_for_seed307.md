High on a mountain that has only lately risen, there is a hollow in a fresh break of black rock. It is small enough to cover with both hands. From the ridge above, nothing distinguishes it from the other dark marks in the stone.

The hollow is hot. Water wells slowly through its floor, gathers in a shallow, trembling lens, and slips away through a crack too narrow to follow. The rock around it sweats. Droplets swell on the ceiling, fall, and are replaced. Beyond the opening, ordinary daylight lies across the cliff; inside, it is softened by steam but bright enough to show the edges of things.

The stone is sharp and unworn. Thin flakes stand upright around the pool, translucent brown at their tips, and a new fracture runs through one wall like a frozen spark. There is no soil, no moss, not even a green stain. A pale crust forms where the water reaches the hottest rock, then loosens and drifts away in tiny plates.

The plates are crowded with animals.

Some are almost transparent, jointed things with dark grains for eyes. They stand packed together on the crust and descend whenever it sinks, holding to its edges until another plate rises beneath them. Others live at the waterline: pale-bodied crawlers whose backs open and close in a slow rhythm, taking in moisture. In the wet fracture there are long, nearly threadlike creatures, many laid side by side and moving independently, though at first they look like one gray root.

Nothing grows here that could feed them in a familiar way. The smallest animals gather at the places where hot water emerges, pressing their mouths to bubbles before they burst. Larger ones pick them from the stone. Still larger ones wait half-submerged, indistinguishable from droplets until a cluster passes close enough to disturb their outlines.

The hollow is almost silent. A falling drop makes a soft tick. The crust shifts with a dry, delicate scrape. Occasionally one of the larger animals closes around its prey, and the sound is no louder than a wet fingertip lifted from glass.

The animals carry loose grains of stone, though they do not make anything with them. One wedges a grain beneath a sinking plate and holds it there until several smaller creatures have crossed. Another uses a sharp flake to open a bubble against the wall, then leaves the flake where it falls. These gestures recur, but never quite in the same order.

The order is difficult to be certain of. A drop may hang beneath the ceiling while its ripples are already widening across the pool. A creature can disappear into the fracture and emerge from it a few breaths later carrying the shadow of an animal still approaching the entrance. The shadows here are not always obedient to their owners: sometimes they remain on the stone after the animals have moved, fading only when a new droplet passes over them.

The creatures seem accustomed to this. When a ripple arrives before its drop, those nearest it brace their legs. When a shadow lingers, a predator may strike at it once, then turn toward the animal that cast it.

By midday the lip of the hollow is too hot to touch. Steam gathers until the far wall vanishes, then thins as water drains into the crack. The exposed animals press close against one another in the cooling damp. A fresh bead forms overhead, catching the light. Beneath it, the pool is already beginning to tremble.
