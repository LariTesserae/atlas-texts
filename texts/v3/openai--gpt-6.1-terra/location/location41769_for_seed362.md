The plain is made of leaves laid edge to edge, though at first they look like paving stones. Each is a pale, rigid plate, jointed to its neighbors by seams of dark green tissue. They extend to the horizon without a break for soil or exposed rock. When a plate wears thin, the leaves beneath it lift and press together, closing the gap before the old one has finished turning to dust.

The country is low and almost level. Its shallow basins are so wide that their rims cannot be seen from the center. Across them stand forests of fabricated stems: columns ribbed like factory chimneys, branching into trusses, galleries, and great suspended fans of foliage. Some are planted in regular rows; others have grown around older structures until it is impossible to tell which was the scaffold and which was the crop. All of them are alive. The bolts are seed cases. The girders thicken slowly under strain.

It is cold enough that the air feels clean against the teeth. There is no water here, not in the ground or in the plants. Their sap is a fine circulating powder, drawn through hollow fibers by electrical differences between leaf and root. In the brightest hours the powder moves quickly, and the columns grow warm to the touch. At other times it settles into chambers inside the stems, where it is sorted, altered, and sent out again.

Light comes from a ceiling too high and too bright to have a visible surface. It has no sun in it. Every leaf casts a sharp shadow, but the shadows do not always belong to the objects above them. A narrow stem may cast the outline of an enormous arch. A person crossing the plain may find their shadow already waiting several paces ahead, lying beneath a structure that has not yet grown.

The old workers learned to build from those shadows. They laid down toothed frames around them and fed the frames powdered leaf. Given enough time, a shadow would harden into a root, then a wall, then the thing whose outline it had first described. Some of the factories still use this method. Their floors are crowded with shadows held in metal lattices, and overhead, mechanical arms turn young leaves toward the light.

The factories are vast and nearly silent. Belts advance without audible motors. Rollers press veins into sheets of living material; rows of blades part them cleanly into panels. The machinery is insulated by thick growth, and what little sound escapes is absorbed by the miles of foliage beyond. Standing beside a working press, one hears only a soft, repeated click, like a seed opening.

Farther out, the older constructions have weathered. Their edges are rounded by the passage of airborne grit, and their upper galleries lean in long, graceful curves. Nothing collapses quickly. A weakened beam puts out tendrils, finds another beam, and draws it close. Sometimes the repair closes a doorway or lowers a roof. Whole districts have become too dense to enter, though their interiors remain busy: leaves unfolding in darkness, roots exchanging powder, abandoned machines held gently in place.

The plain makes its own weather. In the coldest reaches, the leaf plates curl upward together, exposing their silver undersides. A front of reflected light travels across the basin faster than any wind, whitening the columns as it passes. Behind it, millions of leaves relax with a faint papery rustle. On some days the bright front returns from the horizon before it has reached it, passing through itself and leaving a band of country where distances are briefly uncertain. A tower seen there may be close enough to touch from one side and several days’ walk away from the other.

Animals are scarce. Small, dark things with hinged shells live inside the oldest columns, eating dead fibers and carrying fresh powder between chambers that have lost their connections. They emerge only when the light changes. Occasionally a larger creature crosses an open floor on many careful legs, pausing at each seam to test whether the next leaf is firm. The plants do not seem to pursue it. Once it has passed, the plates it touched remember its weight for a while, bowing slightly whenever its shadow comes back.

At the edge of one basin, a row of unfinished arches stands in the light. Their foundations are old, abraded almost smooth, but their upper branches are still lengthening. Beneath them lie the shadows of rooms that have no walls yet: long bright-edged chambers, each furnished with the outlines of leaves. The presses continue to work. Powder circulates through the floor. Somewhere beyond sight, another silver front is beginning to cross the plain.
