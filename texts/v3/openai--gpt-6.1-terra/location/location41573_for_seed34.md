The plateau is high enough that the clouds often come down among its roofs. It stretches beyond sight in every direction, a cool expanse of pale stone, shallow water and closely gathered walls. From a ridge, the city looks less like a single settlement than a pattern repeated across the land: courts within courts, long terraces, dark seams where water runs. The pattern grows faint in the distance, disappears under rain, then emerges again on another slope.

The ground is never quite dry. Rain collects in the joints of the stone and rises through them days later, beading on walls beneath a clear sky. Shallow pools occupy the floors of abandoned courts. In the inhabited parts, narrow channels pass between houses and join broader, stone-lined courses that wind through the city. Their banks are worn smooth by water and by generations of feet.

The buildings are made from the stone that lies everywhere at the surface. People gather slabs by hand and settle them against one another; smaller stones fill the gaps. The walls are thick, and most roofs are low, held down by their own weight. Nothing rises very high, but the buildings crowd together, climbing the folds of the plateau and descending into its basins. In some places passages run beneath old rooms, and newer rooms stand on walls whose first courses have sunk into wet ground.

There are no engines, no wires, no bright signs. The city’s order is in its channels, its shared courtyards and the broad ways kept open between districts. People know which stones can be lifted and which hold a wall in place. After heavy rain they move from passage to passage, clearing silt with their hands, opening one course and closing another with a slab. Water is allowed to spread over certain empty courts before it reaches the houses. These places are recognizable by their clean floors and the watermarks on the surrounding walls.

The plateau has been weathering for a very long time. Its ridges are rounded, its gullies broad and shallow, and even the steepest exposed faces have shelves where mist gathers. The city follows these older forms rather than overcoming them. Streets bend around humps of bedrock. Some neighborhoods occupy natural hollows; others line the edges of long terraces, looking across miles of roofs and wet stone to the next rise.

Vegetation is sparse but persistent. Low, wiry plants grow on the better-drained slopes, and moss makes dark patches in the shelter of walls. Near the channels, small rushes lift their tips above the water. There are long reaches where almost nothing green can find purchase. Then, in a protected basin, a thin meadow appears, closely cropped by the few grazing animals that move through the outskirts.

Animal life is easy to miss. A small waterbird may stand for an hour beside a pool, then pick its way into a covered drain. Insects gather briefly over warmer stones when the sun comes out. Sometimes, well beyond the inhabited districts, an animal crosses an open terrace with its head low and vanishes among the walls before it can be clearly seen.

The air remains cool even in full daylight. Weather travels slowly across the plateau and changes as it goes. A rain bank may cover the western quarters while the eastern courts lie bright and steaming. Clouds form above the wetter basins, drift toward higher ground and dissolve there. After a long shower, thousands of little flows begin at once: water slips from roofs, threads down steps, fills channels and seeps through old foundations. By afternoon the exposed stone is pale again, though water continues to move beneath it.

For such a large city, it is quiet. Voices carry only as far as the next court. Footsteps are softened by damp passages; water makes a faint sound where it falls from one level to another. Wind passes over the roofs more audibly than through the streets. At night the channels can be heard from inside the houses, and in the morning the people come out to find which pools have filled, which passages have cleared, and where the water has chosen a new way through.
