The place is a shallow bowl high on the flank of a mountain, no wider than a village square. Its rim is worn smooth in long, overlapping strokes, as though a great hand had turned it for centuries against a harder surface. The stone is pale and cool, threaded with metallic seams. Beyond the rim the slope falls away so steeply that the far country is hidden.

There is no water here. The pale dust that gathers in cracks is salt, but it has never been dissolved. The air is dry enough to draw the oil from exposed skin; a visitor’s lips stiffen within minutes.

Vegetation fills much of the bowl without making it feel crowded. Long, dark fronds grow from a few widely spaced roots, each root anchored deep in the rock. The fronds divide and divide again until their tips are finer than hair, spreading into enormous loose crowns. Between the crowns there is mostly empty air. Seen from the rim, the grove looks lush; walking through it, one can travel several paces at a time without brushing a stem.

The plants feed on the mountain. Their roots loosen mineral grains and lift them into the fronds, where daylight sorts them into useful substances. Every few seconds a crown sheds a faint glitter of waste. Before the particles can settle, the wind takes them up.

The wind is almost continuous, though it does not come from any one direction. It rises from the floor in narrow columns, strikes the rim, folds back, and meets itself. Fronds whip flat and spring upright; whole crowns turn inside out, exposing their silver undersides. The noise is immense. Each filament has its own thin voice, and millions sound at once: a metallic roaring, full of shrieks and deep, wavering notes that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Close to a root, the stone hums against a hand.

Animals are everywhere in the grove and hard to see at first. Most are nearly transparent, with bodies stretched over light frameworks. They ride the violent air between the crowns, hundreds at a time, opening and closing jointed sails no larger than fingernails. Some graze on the filament tips. Some catch the grazers. Others drift in great, loose swarms that break apart whenever a gust passes through and gather again on the other side.

Their numbers make the emptiness more apparent. A swarm may contain thousands, yet there is room between each animal for several of its own kind. They rarely touch except to feed or mate. When they die, their frameworks tumble down among the roots, where smaller creatures reduce them to bright dust.

A few structures stand around the bowl. One is a low arch made from a material that looks like black glass until a shadow passes over it, when a pattern of joints becomes visible inside. Another is a narrow platform, tilted and half-buried in the stone. It remains perfectly still even when the air above it is thrashing; loose grains that blow onto its surface pause there, then lift away in a straight vertical line.

No one has repaired these objects for a very long time, if repair is what they would need. The arch has been worn down along one side by the same force that polished the rim, but the exposed interior continues to rearrange itself. At intervals a blue line travels through it, and the nearest fronds draw together as if caught by a passing hand. Then they fly apart again.

The bowl itself is old and settled, but almost nothing within it holds a shape for long. Openings appear in the grove and vanish. Animals are flung upward out of sight and return in dense spirals. The sound swells until individual notes disappear, then drops just enough for a dry tapping to emerge: thousands of tiny feet finding purchase on the moving leaves.

In the full daylight the crowns cast very little shade. Their shadows race over the pale floor, crossing and recrossing faster than the eye can follow, while the mountain beneath them stays cold and still.
