The plateau begins above the cloud line and keeps climbing. From its southern edge, a person can look down through miles of clear air to a band of white weather caught against the mountains. Here the sun falls without much filtering, and heat rises through the rock to meet it. Even before noon the dark ground is too hot to touch for long.

The country is open for a great distance, but not empty. Ridges of black and russet stone lie close together, their slopes crowded with fins, fallen slabs and columns rounded by a very old weather. Narrow gullies divide them, each with its own small traffic of heat and moisture. In some places the rock has cracked into tiles no larger than a hand; in others it stands in smooth, immense shoulders, polished by centuries of windblown grit.

There is little standing water. Rain arrives in brief, hard bursts, sometimes falling from clouds that never quite descend to the plateau. Most of it vanishes into the hot stone. At dawn, however, condensation gathers beneath overhangs and along the shaded sides of fissures. Beads run together there, finding the same routes they have followed for years. The damp places are marked by pale mineral rims and by the animals waiting nearby.

Small lizards crowd the ledges, flattened against the rock with their tails lifted clear of the hottest surfaces. At the first glint of water they move in quick, economical rushes, drinking from the cracks. Larger animals, narrow-bodied and long-legged, travel between the gullies in loose herds. Their feet have broad, layered pads, and they pause often to let heat escape through the thin skin of their ears. Carrion beetles occupy almost every sheltered seam. High above them all, dark birds hang in the rising air, scarcely moving their wings.

Plants are harder to find. A few thorny shrubs grow in hollows deep enough to keep a little moisture below the surface. Their leaves are small and grey, and their roots follow fractures far into the stone. Near the warmest vents, mats of reddish growth spread over damp mineral deposits. In the morning they are soft and dark; by afternoon they have dried almost to dust, though they recover whenever the next water comes.

The quiet is full of slight sounds: claws ticking on stone, grit settling down a slope, the occasional dry flutter of a bird changing position. Steam issues from cracks with a low, steady breath. Much farther across the plateau, an engine starts and runs for a few minutes before stopping again.

The engine belongs to one of several pumping stations scattered along the high basin. Each is a small metal building beside a borehole, with pipes sunk into the ground and a condenser mounted above it. They draw hot vapour from below and collect what water they can. There are no roads in the ordinary sense, only routes marked by short iron posts; between them, maintenance vehicles pick their way over exposed rock. The machines are industrial and heavily built, but the distance between stations is large enough that one is usually out of sight of the next.

Late in the day the light turns copper, and long shadows join across the gullies. The animals become bolder as the surface cools, though warmth continues to leak from the fissures through the night. Clouds sometimes climb the escarpment after sunset. They move among the rocks without rain, leaving a fine wetness on the iron posts, the thorn tips and the backs of the waiting lizards.
