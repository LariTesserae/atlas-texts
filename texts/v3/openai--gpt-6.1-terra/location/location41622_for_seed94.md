The upland begins above the cloud line and goes on beyond sight: a vast, cool table of folded stone, its ridges worn down to broad backs and its valleys filled almost to their rims. From a distance the slopes look smooth, but there is hardly a bare handspan among the mosses, low trees, ferns, and close-set flowering plants. Water glints everywhere between them. Even the exposed rock is dark with it, and beads gather beneath overhangs before dropping into the growth below.

The stone has been shaped slowly. Long shelves step down the mountainsides, their edges rounded and undercut by centuries of seepage. Shallow basins lie in the hollows, linked by channels so narrow that the vegetation nearly closes over them. The water moves through root and gravel for much of its journey; only where the ground falls sharply does it show itself as a stream. Far out on the plateau, rain can be falling in one valley while another remains clear, the clouds forming and dissolving above the wet ground in the ordinary light of day.

The trees are short for their age, thick-trunked and bent by cold winds. Their branches interlock over the slopes, holding rafts of moss that continue to grow high above the soil. In those suspended gardens live pale insects, snails, and small climbing mammals whose coats collect seeds. Larger animals move underneath: broad-footed grazers parting the fern beds, solitary hunters following them through the mist, and flocks of ground birds that lift only when almost stepped upon. Their paths appear briefly in the plants and then fill again.

Much of the plateau’s work takes place out of sight. Beneath the saturated turf are layers of fibrous root and dark mineral nodules. Water pressing through them separates charge, and during wet weather faint currents travel for miles through the soil. Certain plants tap into the currents with metallic threads in their roots. They grow remarkably fast for such a cold place, drawing minerals upward and depositing hard, rust-colored grains along their leaves. When the leaves fall, the grains wash back into the earth, where fungi take them apart and return them to the nodules.

The process runs on an immense scale. Whole hillsides redden, shed, and turn green again as the current rises and fades. At dusk, after heavy rain, a fine blue light sometimes flickers along the margins of pools. Animals seem to feel it coming: the grazers withdraw from the wettest ground, while the small mammals climb down from the trees to lick minerals from the stones. Nothing has been built here, yet the land sorts and carries and remakes its materials with the steady capacity of an industry.

It is a quiet place to stand in. The packed vegetation softens the wind, and the water is usually too dispersed to roar. There is the ticking of drops, the brush of a bird passing through leaves, the distant, muffled crack of rock opening under frost. All around, things are moving and feeding and being carried downhill. From one ridge the plateau seems almost motionless; from the next, under a passing cloud, its innumerable wet surfaces brighten and dim in sequence, as though a slow current were crossing the whole height of the world.
