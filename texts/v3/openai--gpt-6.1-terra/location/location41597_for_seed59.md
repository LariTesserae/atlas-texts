The settlement occupies a shallow basin high on a mountainside, no more than a few hundred paces across. Rock walls rise behind it, their faces softened by moss and the dangling roots of trees. Below, the land drops away through cloud. The basin holds water the way a cupped hand does: loosely, with spills from every side.

Streams run down the walls in thin sheets, gather in the hollows, and vanish beneath mats of sedge. After rain, which is frequent, the footpaths become little channels. The ground is cool and yielding even in dry weather. Push a walking stick into it and water wells up around the shaft.

Nearly every surface is green. Moss covers fallen trunks and the low stone foundations of houses; ferns grow between the split planks of walkways. Alder and willow crowd the wetter edges, their branches woven into fences that have taken root and put out leaves. Roofs are thatched with reeds and weighted with poles. In places, grass has established itself in the old thatch, and the houses seem to rise out of the same dense growth as the banks around them.

The paths are narrow, raised on stones or laid with short lengths of timber. They link storehouses, hearth shelters, animal pens and a communal yard around a spring. People have built small drains beside them, using flat stones and hollowed wood, but the water regularly finds another route. There is always a section being lifted, cleared or replaced. Poles are stacked under the eaves for repairs; bundles of reeds hang there too, drying whenever the air permits.

The basin is loud from early morning. Water rattles down stone runs and splashes from roof edges. Ducks push through the flooded sedge, quacking and beating their wings; a flock of small birds moves continually through the alders, chattering and shaking droplets from the leaves. Goats call from the higher, firmer terraces, where their browsing keeps a few patches of grass open. Chickens scratch beneath the houses and quarrel over insects turned up by passing feet.

There are animals in almost every layer of the place. Frogs crowd the pools and fall abruptly silent when someone approaches. Shrews tunnel through the mossy banks. Beetles work under the wet bark of the path timbers, and clouds of midges gather wherever the air is still. Swallows sweep the open yard when the weather clears; when a hawk passes over, they scatter into the branches along with half the smaller birds in the basin.

The slopes above bear the marks of long weathering. Old slips have settled into rounded shelves, now thick with shrubs, and the stones in the streambeds are smooth enough to shine through the water. Yet the surface is always shifting in small ways. A bank slumps after a hard rain, blocking a drain. A goat gets through a fence and strips the leaves from a young willow. Someone cuts back the willow, lays its branches across a muddy gap, and by the next wet season several have begun to grow.

On an ordinary afternoon, daylight filters through moving cloud and the whole basin stays busy: water running, wings flickering, people calling to one another over the noise. The air smells of wet wood, bruised fern and hearth smoke. Nothing here is very far away, though the growth is thick enough that a person can disappear from sight a few steps off the path.
