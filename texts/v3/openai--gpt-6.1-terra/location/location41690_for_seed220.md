The town is built inside a chamber no larger than a modest cathedral, far below the surface. Its streets are narrow bands inset into the floor and walls, arranged in blocks around a central square. There are doorways, platforms, crossings, and tiers of little balconies, but almost no buildings project into the chamber itself. The settlement leaves its air open. From one end to the other there is a long, dark distance in which nothing hangs.

The stone is warm to the touch. It was once cut sharply, then wore down over a very long occupation: stair edges have gone shallow and smooth, gutters have widened into scalloped channels, and the corners of the square have acquired the softness of water-worn rock. Newer work follows the old curves rather than correcting them. Thin seams of engineered material run through the stone, branching at junctions and disappearing beneath thresholds. They carry power and messages through the town, though they give off no light.

Moisture arrives irregularly. Sometimes the walls are dry for days; then a change of pressure sends a fine condensation over the upper tiers. Drops gather along the undersides of balconies and fall into the street. The gutters carry them to three shallow basins, each no wider than a handspan. Around these basins grow the only obvious plants: pale, wiry stems with small waxed leaves, rooted in cracks where mineral dust has collected. They lean away from the water until the basin fills, then slowly turn toward it.

It is very dark. The little light there is comes from the seams when they are under strain, a dull red that passes from one junction to another and vanishes. In that moment a section of street may appear, or the rim of a basin, or a door standing open. The rest of the town remains a matter of remembered distances.

The noise is much greater than the size of the place suggests. Air is driven through shafts behind the walls in heavy pulses, and the chamber answers with a rolling, metallic roar. Water striking the basins makes sharp notes that carry beneath it. At intervals the whole sound changes pitch as mechanisms deep in the stone adjust the pressure; loose grit skitters across the square, and the sparse plants tremble. Between pulses, smaller noises become audible: the scrape of something passing through a gutter, the tick of a cooling seam, a door settling into its frame.

There are animals, though a person could spend a long time here without seeing one. Flat-bodied insects shelter behind the dampest thresholds and come out when condensation begins. A blind, many-jointed hunter follows them along the gutters, feeling ahead with two thin limbs. Once, during a pressure shift, a winged creature may cross the empty chamber from wall to wall, its dry flutter briefly distinct from the machinery.

The town still works. Its doors open when approached; its crossings alter their shallow ridges to guide feet toward occupied rooms. The machinery seems able to anticipate a movement before it is completed, though perhaps it is reading subtler signs: a shift of weight, a change in the air. No lamps come on. The inhabitants, if any are near, make their way by touch, memory, and the town’s quiet responses beneath the roar.
