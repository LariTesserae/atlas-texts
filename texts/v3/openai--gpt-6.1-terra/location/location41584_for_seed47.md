The plateau begins above the last visible slopes. From its edge the country below is lost in a pale haze, but the plateau itself extends for hundreds of kilometers: a high, nearly level expanse of dark stone, old enough that even its mountains have been worn down to broad rises. Here and there a ridge remains, no higher than a few meters, although its exposed layers suggest it was once the root of something immense.

There is no standing water. The ground is a close mosaic of black basalt, gray salt and a finer material that looks like ash until it catches the light. That dust is metallic, and it is almost always moving. Long bands of it sweep over the surface in opposite directions, flowing around stones and through shallow channels with no apparent agreement about which way the wind is blowing.

The air is cool and very thin. At midday the light is clear and ordinary, revealing distant features with deceptive sharpness. A low ruin that appears an hour’s walk away may take a day to reach. Above it the sky is a washed, hard blue, crossed by fast-moving curtains of dust so high that their shadows arrive several minutes before the curtains themselves can be seen.

The great changes are mostly silent. A storm can fill half the horizon without thunder. Its leading edge rises in folding walls; columns lift out of it, break apart and descend again; the whole mass may change direction within minutes. Close by, grains tick against stone or whisper along a salt crust. Farther away there is only the sight of furious motion in a place too thinly filled with air to carry much sound.

The surface is not as settled as its age suggests. At intervals the dark ground opens along seams too straight to be natural, and plates of stone drift a handspan apart. Dust pours into the gaps. By evening the seams may have closed, leaving a new arrangement of pale lines across the plain. Some travelers mark their route with stacked stones, then find the stacks intact but separated from one another by a freshly formed ridge. The oldest maps show the same broad contours as the newest; the details between them are never quite the same.

Life occupies the sheltered undersides of things. Sparse growths cling to the lee of stones: flat, branching patches the color of tarnished copper, with a texture between lichen and foil. They spread slowly through the mineral surface, drawing what they need from salts and trace gases. When a storm approaches, their outer branches curl tightly inward. Afterward they unroll, often partly buried, and continue growing.

Animals are difficult to find. A few small, jointed creatures move through the dust at dusk, too low to cast much shadow. They leave tracks like a row of pinpricks and feed on the copper growths. Much more rarely, a pale glider passes overhead, riding the violent high currents with its wings held rigid. It may remain visible for an hour without once coming closer to the ground.

The structures are widely spaced and easy to mistake for rocks. One is a shallow dome, its surface pitted and dark, with an entrance no wider than a person’s shoulders. Inside, the air is noticeably denser. The chamber has no visible machinery, but a small patch of its floor remains clean while dust swirls everywhere outside. Another structure stands far across the plain: three narrow slabs leaning inward, suspended just short of touching. Grains passing between them slow, hang briefly in the air, and fall. No one watching can see what holds the slabs in place.

The makers left little else that can be recognized. There are occasional lines of fitted stone, fragments of a material that bends without creasing, and areas where the plateau’s shifting seams seem to avoid a precise circle of ground. These works endure among changes much larger and faster than themselves. A dust wall may pass over a dome and scour the salt from every stone around it; when the air clears, the dome remains, with a new crescent of dark grit at its door.

Toward evening the light warms the upper dust while the plain below turns slate-gray. Storms continue to build and collapse across the plateau. Their shadows move over ancient ground, over the tiny closed branches of the growths and the long, empty distances between shelters. From a still place among the stones, the only sound is the faint tapping of grains arriving ahead of the next storm.
