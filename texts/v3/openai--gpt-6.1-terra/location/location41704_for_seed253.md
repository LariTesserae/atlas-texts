The water covers even the highest ground. Here, near the drowned summit of an immense plateau, it is cold enough to numb a hand and black enough that distance has to be judged by sound. The plateau’s sides fall away for miles, though its upper surface has been worn into broad, shallow folds. Nothing remains of whatever sharp peaks it once had.

The ground is made of living roots. They have grown together into a continuous pale felt, dense enough to bear weight, soft enough to yield under it. Within the felt are older layers: stems flattened and sealed by newer stems, hollows lined with a smooth skin of leaf, walls laid down a thread at a time. Generations of animals have shaped it by grazing, burrowing and binding loose strands into place. There is no exposed stone. Even the long terraces along the plateau edge are living construction, repaired as steadily as they are worn.

Above the ground the water is open. Great fronds rise from widely spaced anchors and spread into thin, dark canopies, leaving room between them for long crossings. Their surfaces draw nourishment from the water and from the slow chemical seepage in the root-bed below; little light reaches them, and they need almost none. Once in a while, a frond’s edge gives off a dull green glimmer, revealing a curtain of suspended fibers and a few animals sheltering underneath.

There are many animals, though they seldom gather in one place. Armored grazers move across the terraces, rasping at the living floor. Narrow-bodied swimmers travel between the canopies in loose ranks. Burrowers work inside the walls, and small blind creatures cling to their backs, feeding on what their hosts loosen. Farther down the plateau’s flanks, larger bodies pass without coming into view; their calls arrive first as a pressure in the chest.

The place is loud. Grazing makes a ceaseless grinding, layered with the knocks of shells, the creak of bent stems and the deep, carrying voices from below. Water bears every sound a long way. Yet the landscape keeps its shape. Fronds tear and regrow to the same reach; tunnels close behind their makers and are opened again. The animals feed, the root-bed thickens, and the cold black water remains full of their work.
