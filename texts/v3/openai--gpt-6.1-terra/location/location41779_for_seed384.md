The town occupies the bottom of a shallow basin beneath the sea. From one edge to the other it is no farther than a person could walk in ten minutes, though there is nowhere left to walk. Water fills the streets, the stairwells, the machine halls, and the narrow spaces between buildings. Above the roofs, a dark ceiling of ice has grown down around the chimneys.

The water is so salty that it remains liquid below the freezing point. It moves slowly through the basin in layers: the coldest and heaviest among the foundations, a slightly warmer current along the roofs. Where the layers meet, they make a wavering surface like another sea, suspended inside the first.

The buildings stand close together. Their lower stories have been scoured smooth by centuries of grit carried in the current. Corners have become rounded; iron railings have narrowed to dark ribs; the stone steps of a municipal building have worn into a continuous slope. Fine sediment lies in every sheltered place, but the exposed walls are clean. The basin has been shaped into a form the water can pass through without much resistance.

There was industry here before the flooding, and some of it continues. A row of low factories faces a central channel, their windows blind with mineral crust. Inside, broad wheels turn almost imperceptibly. They draw water through pipes, separate salts on chilled plates, and send the dense brine back into the deepest part of the basin. No furnaces burn. The machinery takes its power from the difference between the moving layers of water, and its parts have been fitted so carefully that the whole works with very little sound.

Now and then a plate releases its growth of salt. The pale sheets drift down through the factory rooms, striking beams and splitting into smaller pieces. In the streets they catch on wires, window ledges, and the branches of old streetlamps. The lamps still show a faint blue at their cores, just enough to reveal the flakes passing through their light.

Life is sparse but persistent. A few stiff fronds grow in the lee of a pumping station, dark red where the lamps reach them and nearly black elsewhere. They collect the fine material that settles from above. Small translucent animals shelter beneath the fronds and venture out when a fresh sheet of salt falls, picking from it whatever has been trapped during its growth. Larger creatures visit from beyond the basin, though rarely. Their passage is usually known afterward, by a line swept clean across the sediment or a frond bent flat against the wall.

There is an old rule to the water here: anything left still for long enough develops a second outline. A pipe acquires the faint shape of a wider pipe around it; a doorway seems to open a little beyond its frame. These borders are made visible by the different densities of water gathering around each object. When a pump changes speed, they loosen and drift away. For a few minutes the town looks crowded with transparent copies of its own machinery, all slowly coming apart.

Above, cracks travel across the underside of the ice. Below, the wheels continue to turn. Between them the basin remains almost silent: a soft settling of salt, an occasional tick from a contracting beam, and the close, patient movement of water through the streets.
