Deep below the floor of a flooded basin, a crack opens into a chamber no larger than a cupped hand. It is entirely full of cold water. The only way in is a seam so narrow that the water passing through it has to leave some of its darkness behind.

Inside, the rock is black and newly broken. Thin flakes stand edgewise from the walls, their tips still sharp, though a soft gray silt has begun to gather at their bases. The floor slopes almost imperceptibly toward one corner, where the silt is deeper than the chamber itself should allow. A grain settling there takes a long time to arrive.

Pale growth crowds every surface. Filaments spread over the rock in overlapping fans; translucent fronds fold against one another and rise again, too slowly to see. There is no open space between them, only narrow passages of water threaded through the living mass. Some growth is fine as fur, some broad and rubbery, some arranged in small white cups that hold no air. It feeds on minerals seeping from the fresh stone and on the faint remains of things that sink through the basin above.

Almost nothing enters. Now and then a colorless creature works its way through the seam: a soft body with a fringe of legs, feeling ahead with two delicate threads. It may stay for years. When it moves, the fronds close gently behind it, and the path it made seems longer than the animal itself.

A few curved grains are caught near the ceiling, fitted together into a low ring. They might be the remains of a shelter, or simply pieces of shell that settled beside one another. The growth has covered most of them. Beneath it, the ring keeps its shape, though the distance across its center changes with each slow stirring of the water.

There is no daylight here and almost no sound. Occasionally the rock gives a minute click as the new fracture adjusts. The click reaches one wall, then the other; much later, something in the packed vegetation draws tight as though it has only just heard it.

The seam outside admits a little fresh water and, with it, traces of the basin’s seasons. In the coldest months the fronds become rigid and the chamber seems to narrow. When warmth returns, it opens again, not quite in the same places. The white cups multiply, the silt corner deepens, and the sharp stone slowly grows a skin. Nothing happens quickly enough to make the water cloudy.
