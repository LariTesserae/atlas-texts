The grove occupies a shallow hollow near the top of a high plateau, no larger than a meeting hall. Its rim is broken into teeth of dark stone. Beyond them, the land falls away into a dimness filled with drifting mineral dust.

The hollow is warm. Heat seeps through the floor in slow pulses, and the stone gives off a faint smell of hot flint. There is no water here, not even frost in the cracks. The soil is a loose, glittering powder that runs between the fingers and leaves the skin dry.

Plants grow in seven or eight widely spaced clumps. Each clump is dense with foliage: broad, pleated fronds, pale underneath and almost black on top, and smaller leaves curled like shavings around their stems. The plants draw a thick amber oil from nodules beneath the ground. When a leaf tears, it bleeds a thread of this oil, which hardens almost at once into a translucent bead. Animals prize the beads and will fight over a fresh one.

Between the clumps there is plenty of bare ground. It is never still for long. Gusts descend unpredictably through the broken rim, sweeping dust into high, twisting pillars. Fronds thrash and slap against one another. The ground is marked with countless overlapping tracks, most of them erased within minutes.

The animals are small and numerous. Jointed runners shelter beneath the leaves, coming out in bursts to collect hardened sap and carry it underground. Soft-bodied creatures cling to the undersides of the fronds, their ridged backs trembling as they rasp at the oil-bearing veins. Others ride the gusts: flat, many-limbed things that lift from the stone, wheel across the hollow and catch themselves on whatever plant they strike. Every landing starts a commotion among the animals already there.

The noise is continuous and surprisingly loud. Leaves crack like loose boards; runners strike pebbles together as they dig; the flying creatures produce a coarse buzzing that rises and falls with the wind. Sometimes a gust makes the entire grove roar. Then it passes, and the individual sounds can be heard again—the tapping, scraping and thin calls from beneath the soil.

The stone rim has worn into its present shape over a very long time. Its hollows are smooth, and old fractures are packed with compacted dust. Within it, change is incessant. Fronds are stripped away, and new ones unfold from protected knots at the plants’ bases. A dust pillar may bury a burrow entrance; its inhabitants open another before the gusts have moved on.

Near the southern edge stand three low shelters, built from stacked stone and roofed with overlapping plates of dried frond. One has partly collapsed. Inside another is a shallow bowl, a flaking stone knife and a cord threaded with amber beads. The cord is tied to the roof, where it knocks against the stone in the wind.

Whoever left it knew where to build: the shelters sit behind a tooth of the rim, in the one patch of the hollow where the dust settles instead of rising.
