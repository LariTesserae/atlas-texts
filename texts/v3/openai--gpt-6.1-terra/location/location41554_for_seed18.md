At the summit of a narrow industrial tower is a room open to the sky. It is no larger than a village chapel, though the drop beyond its walls makes it feel much higher than it is wide. Far below, the tower disappears into haze. There is no mountain beneath it: the height has been assembled, section by section, from bolted iron and kiln-fired blocks.

The room is almost entirely empty. Its floor is a single shallow bowl, its walls a ring of slender ribs. Between them is warm air, and nothing to cross it but light. The ribs are thickly furred with ferns, waxy leaves and pale flowering vines, all rooted in felt sleeves wrapped around the metal. Their growth follows the structure closely; hardly a tendril ventures into the open space. From the center of the bowl one can look through a green colonnade to the hazy distance on every side.

The plants live on little water. At night, condensation gathers on the cold upper edges of the ribs and runs down concealed channels into the felt. Sometimes a brief rain reaches the room, striking the floor and collecting in its center. By afternoon only a dark patch remains. The rest of the time the air is dry and warm, scented with hot metal and bruised leaves.

The bowl has been worn smooth by weather and by the slow movement of grit. Its lowest point is polished almost black. The iron ribs are scalloped where centuries of rain have worked at their cast surfaces; their old seams show through the rust in delicate, regular lines. Nothing here was laid down by a river or lifted by a mountain, but the place has acquired the patience of both.

There are many animals, though they occupy very little of the room. Tiny lizards sleep along the backs of leaves. Moths crowd into the felt sleeves by day, and beetles move in the narrow damp beneath them. Near dusk, dozens of small bats emerge from slots in the outer wall and circle once through the open center before dropping away into the haze. Their passage barely disturbs the vines.

The noise is tremendous. Inside the tower, a bank of engines turns a great fan whose purpose is no longer clear. Its roar rises through the floor, joined by the ringing of the ribs and the long, hornlike notes made when wind crosses their hollow ends. The sound is so continuous that, after a while, individual events become visible before they are audible: a leaf tears loose and tumbles; a lizard opens its mouth; the bowl receives a drop of water.

The flowering vines open in response to the engines. Their buds loosen when the deepest note swells, a change that takes several days, and close again as it recedes. The bats seem to follow the same cycle, growing restless at the threshold of a new note. No one has seen the engines stop. The plants have grown around their rhythm for so long that even their seed cases are shaped to rattle in tune.
