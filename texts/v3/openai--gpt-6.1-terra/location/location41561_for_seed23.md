The settlement occupies a hollow near the top of the ridge, small enough to see across from any of its upper roofs. Beyond it, white slopes fall away into cloud. The hollow itself is crowded: stone dwellings stand shoulder to shoulder, their walls rising from one another’s foundations, their roofs weighted with flat rocks. Narrow lanes thread between them and disappear beneath bridges no wider than a handcart.

Water is everywhere, though the air is bitterly cold. It comes through the rock in clear threads and gathers in the lanes, where it runs beneath thin, shifting plates of ice. The water is too salty to freeze easily; at the edges it leaves delicate white rims on the stone. A person can cross the hollow with dry feet only by knowing which stepping-stones are fixed and which are being carried slowly along.

In the bright hours, light enters from every direction. It shines down through a pale sky and comes back from snow, water, ice and the wet faces of the buildings. The settlement has few shadows deep enough to rest the eyes. People hang curtains of scraped hide in their doorways and wear narrow slotted shades over their eyes, carved from driftwood brought up the mountain long ago.

The rock around the hollow has been worn into rounded ribs and pockets. Some pockets hold black water; others contain colonies of small animals pressed so closely together that their bodies make a single rippling surface. They have translucent shells and blunt, jointed legs, and spend much of their lives grazing on the faint growth inside the wet stone. When a crack fills with fresh meltwater, they pour into it. When it drains, they retreat together, leaving the rock polished.

Larger creatures move among the houses: pale, low-bodied scavengers with broad feet that spread on the ice. They shelter beneath the bridges and emerge whenever someone cleans a fish or opens a drain. Birds nest in the masonry, in every gap not already occupied by a neighbor. There are many of them, though little birdsong. They communicate with tiny movements of their throats and wings; the scavengers watch them closely, and the people watch both.

Plants are scarce. A few dark cushions cling to ledges where the water cannot reach them, and a wiry grass grows around the warmest seep at the center of town. Its blades are short, bent almost flat by frost. The people protect it with stones because its roots hold that bank together.

The quiet is remarkable for such a busy place. Water slides rather than splashes, and the snow beyond the walls receives what little sound escapes. Tools make dull clicks against stone. A bird’s wings brush another bird’s wings. Underfoot, ice occasionally gives a soft, prolonged sigh.

Yet nothing stays still. Channels open and close within an hour. One lane floods while the next empties; a roof begins to drip from underneath; a crowded crack in the wall sends its inhabitants streaming across a doorstep. Families spend much of each day moving stones, lifting woven gates from one channel and setting them into another, making paths for the water without allowing it to take the houses. The gates are made of willow roots and bone, the walls fitted by hand. In several places old walls have been left standing inside newer ones, because the settlement has learned not to remove a stone merely because it seems unnecessary.

Sometimes, in the glare, the flow reverses. Water climbs the shallow steps toward the ridge, carrying loose ice and the little shelled grazers with it. No one interrupts their work when this happens. The people turn their gates around, the scavengers face uphill, and the birds tighten their wings against their bodies. By evening the water is generally moving downhill again, and the lanes are laid out differently.
