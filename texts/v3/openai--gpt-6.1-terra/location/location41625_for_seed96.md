The high basin is bright enough to make you narrow your eyes. A cool white light falls across a country of fitted stone, clay and shallow water, with no trees or dark soil to soften it. The basin is miles across. From its rim you can see nearly all of it, though the ground below is so closely built that the eye has trouble finding a place to rest.

Every surface has been made. Even the slopes are assemblies: pale blocks laid against one another, their seams sealed with a darker clay. Some blocks are as long as a person; others are no larger than a thumb. Their edges are sharp and recent-looking, and the marks of pecking tools remain on the broad faces. There are no old gullies cutting through them, no rounded heaps of rubble. The basin looks less worn than unfinished.

Water fills almost every joint. It lies in narrow channels between the blocks, gathers in square pans, and stands in thin clear sheets on the terraces. A few channels carry water slowly downhill, though their movement is difficult to see except where a floating grain of grit passes a corner. Elsewhere the water is perfectly still, holding the pale sky and the undersides of stone lips.

The terraces crowd together in no obvious order. Some are only a handspan high; others rise above the basin floor in steep flights. Low walls divide them into cells, and within the cells are smaller walls, bowls, ridges and upright slabs. There are passages through the work, but they turn often and narrow without warning. A dry route may end at a pan of water too wide to step across. A wet route may continue for a long way on submerged stones whose tops sit just below the surface.

Nothing grows along the margins. The dark lines in the cracks are wet clay, not roots. The greenish cast beneath some of the deeper pools comes from the stone itself. There are no insects on the water, no birds crossing the light, no small movements in the sheltered places. The only loose things are chips from the stonework and occasional flakes of pale clay, softened at their edges.

The construction changes as you cross the basin. Near the rim the blocks are large and fitted tightly, with small drainage holes bored through them. Farther in, the work becomes more intricate: overlapping tiles, thumb-pressed troughs, rows of shallow cups. Several broad towers stand among the terraces. They are solid for most of their height, built from hand-sized stones packed around clay cores. At their tops, flat stones project outward and collect condensation. Drops gather beneath these overhangs and fall into basins set directly below.

The air is cool and damp. Even in the brightest hours, exposed stone feels cold in the shade. Moisture beads on the vertical faces, joins into threads and descends through grooves that were cut for it. Some grooves are crowded with others, as though each generation of makers found another way to delay the water before letting it reach the floor. One thread is led around three sides of a block, under a projecting tile and through a row of seven cups before it rejoins a channel only a little lower than where it began.

Most of the time the place is nearly silent. There is a soft, isolated drip from the towers and, in a few covered runs, a faint rubbing sound where water moves over grit. When the wind reaches the basin it passes over the rims of empty vessels and produces low, brief tones. The tones do not travel far. Beyond them, the wet stone seems to hold the quiet close.

The makers are absent. Their methods are visible everywhere: flakes struck from harder stone, clay pressed into seams by hand, holes patiently ground through slabs. No beam spans the larger gaps; instead, the walls lean inward course by course until their tops meet. There are no wheels left in the passages, and many of the turns would be too tight for one. Whatever was brought into the basin appears to have been carried piece by piece, or made here from earlier work taken apart.

At the center is the largest expanse of water, a shallow polygon hemmed in by terraces. Its bottom has been paved with pale tiles, each slightly different in thickness. In the bright light the high corners emerge as a dense scatter of dry points. Water creeps between them, rises over a few, then recedes so slowly that the change can be noticed only by returning later. Along the edge, thin bands of deposited mineral show where the water has stood before.

The basin gives the impression of being complete from a distance and still under construction from within. Every level surface has been allotted some use: to hold water, guide it, divide it, catch its next drop. Yet one wall ends in a row of exposed joint sockets, and a flight of steps stops abruptly at a blank face of stone. Water has already found these places. It rests in the sockets and pools on the last step, clear and cold beneath the wide white sky.
