The basin is no larger than a hamlet, though it is difficult to see from one side to the other. The ground rises gently around it, closing off the view with banks of fern and broad, overlapping leaves. Everything within that rim is occupied. Roots cross the paths, moss cushions the roots, and smaller plants grow in the moss. Even the shallow pools have green skins, broken only where drops fall from above.

It is warm under the canopy. Rainwater lingers for days in the folded leaves and seeps slowly through the ground, so that the basin remains damp in clear weather. The paths are firm enough to walk, but each step presses a dark print into their woven surface. Here and there an old stone protrudes, rounded by long use and clasped so tightly by roots that it seems to have grown from them.

Three small structures stand among the plants. One is a shed of corrugated iron, nearly hidden beneath a vine whose leaves turn their pale undersides outward. Another is a brick pump house with no door. Inside it, an electric motor drives a wheel at an unhurried, steady pace. Belts run from the wheel into the vegetation, vanishing through collars of thickened stem. The machinery is loud enough to fill the basin: a metallic rattle, a low hum, and the periodic slap of a belt against its guard.

The plants around the pump house have adapted themselves to its motion. Stems lie in parallel channels where the belts pass. Tough leaves fold back before each rotation and relax afterward, with a soft collective hiss beneath the noise of the motor. No one seems to have trimmed them for many years. They keep their own clearance, narrow and exact, while shoots everywhere else press against the brickwork and creep across the roof.

At the lowest point of the basin is a settling tank, its concrete rim broken in two places. Water enters from somewhere beneath the roots and leaves through a pipe too overgrown to follow. The surface trembles with the motor’s vibration. Pale plants grow across it in interlocking plates, opening a hand’s width whenever the water rises and closing again as it falls. The change takes several hours. By evening, a plate that was open in the morning may have sealed around a floating leaf.

Animals are seldom seen. A small frog lives under the tank’s rim, and once in a while a moth settles on the pump house wall. Most of the movement belongs to the plants: tips inclining toward light, tendrils feeling along warm metal, the slow tightening of roots around anything left in one place. The motor continues through it all. At night its housing gives off enough warmth to bring a faint mist from the leaves, and the basin sounds much the same as it does by day.
