The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few streets. Its rim is an old ridge, worn so low that in places it is difficult to tell where the basin ends. Warm water stands just beneath the ground. After rain, which comes often and briefly, it rises through the walking surfaces in clear beads.

Almost everything underfoot is alive. The broad, pale avenues are made of a close-packed mat of leaf tips, flattened and woven together. Their roots descend through older mats, dead but still useful, and farther down through the softened remains of buildings. The avenues cross a great deal of open space. Between them lie wide courts of cropped green, with here and there a tree or a narrow tower of intertwined stems. From a distance the town looks sparsely inhabited. Up close, its surfaces are crowded with small lives.

The towers are buildings, though they have no fixed number of floors. Their chambers grow and close according to the needs of the residents: a balcony may uncurl in the morning and be drawn back into the wall by evening. Water moves upward inside the stems without pressure or pumps. When a room is needed somewhere else, its doorway is marked with a dark resin, and by the next day the room can be entered from another tower. People carry baskets and furniture through these passages while the leaves around the frames tremble.

The courts are never still. Wind travels along them in sudden, narrow currents, bending the green one way while the neighboring court bends another. Flocks of small, long-legged animals hurry across the open ground, stopping all at once to probe for larvae. Larger creatures shelter under the trees: slick-backed browsers with translucent ears, and patient predators that resemble bundles of cut reeds until they rise. In the water beneath the avenues, silver bodies flash whenever a footstep parts the leaves.

There is a constant sound of water being moved: trickling in the towers, slapping under bridges, pattering down from leaves long after the rain has passed. Calls carry clearly across the open courts. Occasionally a passage changes places with a noise like a sail snapping, and every animal nearby falls quiet for a breath.

The basin is very old. Whatever mountains once fed its streams have been reduced to clay and fine mineral dust; the streams themselves now wander only a few handspans above the level of the marsh beyond the ridge. The town has survived by changing quickly within that ancient, level shape. After each storm its inhabitants go out to see what has opened, what has drowned, and which familiar route has grown shut. They lay their hands on the warm living pavement, and the pavement slowly makes room for them.
