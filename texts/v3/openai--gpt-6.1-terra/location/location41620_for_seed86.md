The land lies under a roof of limestone, in a hollow so broad that the far wall is often lost in the dimness. Low openings along its margins admit a little daylight, strained through cracks and steep clefts in the rock. At noon, several pale patches appear on the floor; beyond them the cavern remains gray, and its distances have to be judged by sound.

The floor is young-looking and uneven. Ribs of stone stand at sharp angles where slabs have fallen from the ceiling. Fresh breaks show almost white beneath the darker, weathered surfaces. Between them lie long stretches of gravel, clay and loose chips of limestone, with room to walk for hundreds of paces without coming to a wall. Here and there the ground dips into shallow basins. Most are dry, though the clay at their bottoms keeps the imprint of feet.

Water enters slowly. It gathers on the roof and falls in widely spaced drops, darkening small circles of stone. After rain aboveground, a few narrow runnels appear along the cavern’s edges and sink again into the gravel. In hollows where the drips persist, there are cold pools no larger than a hand, with smooth pebbles visible at the bottom. The air is cool and damp enough to feel on the face, but much of the ground is dry to the touch.

Near the openings, sparse ferns grow from cracks, and moss holds to the shaded sides of fallen rocks. Farther in, vegetation gives way to pale films and tiny fungal growths on damp stone and accumulated droppings. Fine roots descend through some of the ceiling cracks. Their tips hang among the bats and occasionally reach the floor.

The animals are everywhere. Bats pack the high ledges in immense colonies, their bodies pressed so closely together that the rock beneath them is hidden. Cave crickets cover the undersides of slabs and crowd into the loose gravel. Beetles move through the thick deposits below the roosts; small pale mites move over the beetles. Spiders set their webs in sheltered notches, and salamanders wait beside the wetter cracks. Wherever a pool lasts long enough, blind crustaceans pick through the sediment.

The abundance is sustained from beyond the cavern. The bats fly out through the clefts to feed, bringing back what becomes food for much of the life below them. Floodwater occasionally carries in leaf litter, seeds and drowned insects. At the wettest margins, bacteria grow on minerals in the seeping water, adding another thin source of nourishment. There is little greenery, but there is almost always something feeding.

The noise is continuous and surprisingly varied: the dry rasp of crickets, the close squeaking of bats, the papery rush of wings when part of a colony stirs. Drops strike pools with small, clear notes that can be heard between the louder sounds. The broad chamber gathers everything and sends it back softened and prolonged, until even a brief commotion travels a long way.

There are no paths, walls or signs of habitation. A few stones have been shifted by animals; some have been chipped or knocked against the floor, exposing clean edges. Nothing has been built from them. The creatures follow the hollows and ledges that are already there, finding shelter beneath stone, moisture in the basins, and food in what falls from above.

The cavern changes little in any season. The light patches move across the floor each day, and rain makes the wet places spread for a time, but the same roosts remain occupied and the same chorus fills the open ground. In the dimness, with the far wall out of sight and animals moving over almost every surface, the place feels both spacious and densely inhabited.
