The plateau is made of slag, brick, and crushed concrete, laid down over generations until the old ground is far beneath it. From the northern rim, the constructed country spreads for miles: terraces stepping down into a broad basin, factory roofs fitted edge to edge, viaducts crossing over viaducts. A low ceiling of cloud hides the higher stacks. Even at midday the light is the color of unpolished steel.

Vegetation covers nearly every level. Moss thickens in the seams of retaining walls; ferns crowd the grated walkways; alder and willow grow from deep beds built onto the roofs. The trees lean inland, shaped by the prevailing wind, and their roots are held in wire cages so they do not break through the waterproof layers. Between the factories are long belts of reed beds, planted to filter the water that drains from the workshops. Their brown heads shake together whenever the wind comes up.

Rain is frequent but seldom heavy. Water gathers on the cold metal and runs in narrow threads down the masonry, through open channels, under bridges and back into storage tanks. The basin floor is a network of shallow settling ponds, dark except where leaves or a skin of oil have collected. Some mornings the whole place is wrapped in condensation, and the nearest roofs vanish while the red warning lights on their corners remain visible.

Nothing here is quite still. On the eastern terraces, an automated gantry lifts sections of a dismantled warehouse and carries them away along an overhead track. New frames are rising beside it. Farther down, a slope of old fill has begun to creep; its retaining wall is being taken apart course by course while a temporary one grows behind it. Conveyor belts move soil, aggregate, scrap metal and cut vegetation between the work sites. Every few days a channel is diverted, a walkway closed, a stand of willows cut back to make room for a machine.

The changes happen with surprisingly little noise at a distance. Motors hum behind insulated housings. Electric haulers pass on rubber tires. Wind in the reeds often covers the sound of work, though now and then a dropped load sends a hard clang across the basin. Near the pumps there is a continuous low vibration that can be felt through the soles of boots.

The older structures have acquired the rounded look of things that have been wet and cold for a long time. Mortar has washed from the joints, iron has swollen under paint, and mineral deposits hang in pale ridges beneath the drains. These surfaces are repaired, then weather again. The people who maintain the plateau leave the useful moss alone and scrape it away where it holds water against a joint.

Animals are scarce. A few gulls follow the waste lifts, and small birds shelter in the alders. On quiet stretches of roof, insects gather around the warm vents, but there are long intervals when nothing moves among the leaves except water and wind. Beyond them, almost everywhere one looks, some part of the built ground is being opened, shifted or put back together.
