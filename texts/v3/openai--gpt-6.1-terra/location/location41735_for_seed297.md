The plain lies low and warm beneath a clear white sky. It runs farther than the eye can comfortably measure, though the farthest things appear almost as sharp as those nearby: a crowded expanse of foliage rising from pale mineral ground, with occasional ridges of black glass between. The ridges have keen, unworn edges. Fragments at their feet fit the breaks above them exactly.

There is no water here, not even frost in the sheltered places. The plants draw what they need from the air and from a slow chemistry in the stone. Their broad leaves are thick and faintly translucent, green at the center and copper along the veins. Beneath them grow fans of blue needles, soft-looking cushions with crystalline tips, and low, branching forms that fold around every exposed patch of mineral. Their tissues are filled with an oily, colorless medium that does not evaporate in the heat. When a leaf is broken, the cut seals before a drop can fall.

The growth is so close that the plain seems almost solid from a distance. Up close, narrow passages remain between the stems. They are not paths; they bend back into the thicket or stop at a cluster of leaves. A small animal sometimes occupies one of these clearings: a flat, many-legged creature the color of ash, difficult to distinguish from the ground until it lifts its head. It can be watched for a long time without seeing it take another step.

The place is quiet enough that the smallest noises carry: a dry click as a leaf closes around a grain of dust, a fine crackle from somewhere inside the glass ridges. There is no wind to speak of. Warmth falls evenly across the plain, and shadows keep their places.

Nothing here has been built in any familiar sense. Yet the vegetation performs work no ordinary plant could do. Around each stem, distance is held under a delicate constraint: two leaves that appear to touch may be separated by a handspan when approached, while a gap that looks wide enough to enter can narrow without anything moving. The effect is not illusion. A fallen seed can rest in one of those gaps, impossibly far from either side, and remain visible from both.

The plants maintain these arrangements together, exchanging instructions through changes in the ground too small to see. Their roots alter the relationships between neighboring points with a precision beyond any instrument left among them. Why they do this is not clear. Perhaps it gives their crowded leaves more room; perhaps it is simply how they grow.

At the edge of a black ridge, the foliage parts around a patch of bare ground. Its surface bears the impression of a leaf that still hangs overhead. The leaf has never fallen, and the impression is just as fresh as the fracture in the glass. Farther out, the same leaf appears again, attached to another plant, then another, each copy lit from the same direction. Between them the plain continues, dense and bright, with enough differences to make it impossible to say where a repetition begins.
