The land is nearly level, but never quite dry. Shallow water lies in long gray sheets across a plain of dark stone, broken by gravel bars and low, rounded rises. The rises were once higher; their exposed cores have been worn smooth, then split by frost, then worn smooth again. In places the stone has been reduced to a fine pale grit that clouds the water when anything passes through it.

There are no plants. Nothing green fringes the pools or softens the edges of the channels. The damp surfaces carry stains of iron and manganese, and thin films of living cells too small to see individually. These films gather most thickly where water seeps from the rock, making a faint bronze sheen. Small animals graze them away, leaving clean tracks that slowly darken again.

The plain extends for several miles between low escarpments. Even those are old and blunt, their ledges collapsed into slopes. Water moves through the basin by a complicated, patient route: it wells up through cracks, spreads over the stone, gathers in shallow runs, and disappears into gravel farther down. Rain is frequent but rarely heavy. It arrives as a cold haze, or as droplets so fine that they drift sideways for a long time before touching the ground.

The noise begins at the water. Thousands of small hard-shelled creatures occupy the submerged stone, each fixed in place by a muscular foot. They feed by drawing water through their shells. At intervals, in waves that pass from pool to pool, they snap shut. The sound of one shell is a sharp click; the sound of an entire shoal closing is like a great sheet of gravel being poured onto a roof. A moment later the shells open with a softer rattling hiss, and the sequence begins again somewhere else.

Among them move larger animals, pale-backed and many-legged, with flattened bodies that let them slip beneath the water without raising more than a ripple. They pry the shell creatures loose and carry them to the gravel bars, where heaps of broken white casings mark their feeding places. Farther out, in the dimmer pools, long blind swimmers hunt the grazers. Their tails strike the surface when they turn, adding deep, irregular blows to the clatter.

The animals are abundant, though the landscape looks sparse at first. Most remain underwater or pressed against stone, and the sound makes them seem farther away than they are. When a shadow crosses a pool, the shell creatures close in a widening ring. The grazers freeze. For a few seconds the ring travels outward through the basin, carrying news of the shadow well beyond the place where it fell.

Scattered across the rises are small shelters built from slabs of the local stone. Some still have roofs weighted with gravel; others have settled inward until only a low circle remains. Their entrances face away from the prevailing rain. Beside several are shallow pits lined with carefully fitted pebbles, and stones worn smooth by repeated use as hammers or scrapers. The people who made them, if they still come here, leave little else. On one sheltered wall, parallel grooves have been cut into the rock in groups of unequal length. Rainwater gathers in them and trembles with every wave of closing shells.

The light is weak even at midday. Clouds hang low over the basin, and the water reflects them without much distinction, so that the far pools appear to merge with the air. At night the temperature falls close to freezing. A skin of ice forms in the stillest places, only to be broken from below when the animals begin to feed. By morning the fragments are scattered along the edges, knocking together in the seepage.

There is almost nowhere in the basin that could be called quiet. The clatter builds and recedes, joins with the hiss of rain, echoes from the escarpments and returns as a low continuous roar. Close to a shelter, a person can hear water ticking from its roof and grit shifting underfoot, but only when the pools pause. Those pauses are brief. The first clicks start up again at a distance, and the sound comes rolling over the flat stone.
