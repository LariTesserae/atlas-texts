The plateau begins above the last continuous slopes, where the mountains have been worn down to a vast, uneven table of pale stone. It is so high that distant ranges show beneath its rim, their summits separated by blue depth. From the center of the plateau, however, the ground feels almost level. Shallow basins, stone ribs, and long gravel fans extend for miles before the land breaks away again.

The rock has the softened forms of great age. Some ridges are rounded like the backs of resting animals; others have been pared into thin fins, each fin leaning at the same slight angle. Loose fragments lie sorted around them by size: coarse blocks near the outcrops, then chips, then a fine, bright grit that collects in the hollows. There is very little soil. Where it does gather, it holds a few dark cushions of vegetation, flattened close to the ground and threaded through with tiny silver leaves.

Water appears only in sheltered places. Condensation gathers under the cold overhangs and falls into pits no wider than a hand. After rain, narrow seams in the stone remain wet for a while, outlined in black. Most of the plateau is dry to the touch, although the air has a cool mineral dampness that clings to the inside of the nose.

The light is almost without shade. It pours across the bare surface and returns from it, whitening the undersides of stones and picking out every grain in the gravel. Shadows are short, hard-edged, and blue. Even at the bottoms of the deeper basins there is a clear, reflected brightness, so that nothing seems entirely hidden.

The sound reaches the plateau before its source can be seen. It is a continuous, tremendous roaring, with a higher, rattling note inside it. The wind passes through miles of narrow clefts in the rock, and each opening adds its own pitch. Some make a steady whistle; others produce a rapid clacking as small stones quiver against the walls. Together they make a noise so loud that it seems to press against the face. A cry from an animal nearby can disappear into it, while a low vibration carries through the soles of the feet.

There are animals everywhere, though it takes time to notice them. Small pale grazers lie among the stone cushions, spaced so widely apart that each seems alone. Farther out, long-legged creatures move in slow lines between the damp hollows, lowering their heads to drink. Their coats are close and dull-colored, almost the same shade as the gravel. Insects shelter beneath the vegetation and emerge into the bright air in thin clouds, so dispersed that their abundance is visible only when they pass across a shadow.

Above them, birds wheel in great numbers. They use the rising air along the plateau’s edge and travel far over the lower country to feed, returning to rest on the natural ledges. From below, those ledges must appear dark with bodies. On the open surface there may be half a mile between one living thing and the next.

The animals have ways of making themselves heard. The grazers lift their heads and open their mouths toward one another, but much of their communication is made through the ground: a forefoot pressed down, a short drumming against stone. The birds answer with gestures of wings and tail. Now and then one takes a loose pebble in its beak and knocks it repeatedly against a resonant slab, producing a sharp note that can be distinguished from the wind. When it is finished, it leaves the pebble where it falls.

Nothing here appears newly made. The damp hollows hold their water for as long as they always have; the plants occupy the same protected cracks; the animals follow paths that leave no visible track on the hard ground. Wind and birds and the trembling stones maintain the immense noise, and the plateau remains open and bright beneath it.
