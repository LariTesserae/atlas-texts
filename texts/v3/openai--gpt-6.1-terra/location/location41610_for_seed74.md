The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few streets. From its rim you can see nearly all of it: a square, a narrow works building, several low houses, and a road that enters through a gap in the bank and ends at the far side. There are no neighboring fields. Beyond the rim, the ground rises gently into a mass of pale, interwoven stems.

Almost every surface in town is alive. The walls are made from thickened roots trained around old iron frames; their outer layers have weathered into a gray, fibrous hide. The road is a close, springy mat of leafless runners, worn smooth by years of feet and small wheels. Between buildings lie broad open spaces where nothing rises above ankle height. The vegetation fills the town without crowding it: it makes the ground and the structures, then leaves the air clear.

The works building still runs. Its roof lifts on slow hinges each morning to admit light, and belts turn inside, powered by an engine somewhere below the square. The machinery clips lengths of tough vine, draws them through rollers and winds them onto spools. The spools stand in neat rows under the eaves, gradually changing from green to the color of old rope. There is little noise from the works at a distance, only an occasional knock followed by the soft, regular ticking of the belts.

The air is cool. Rain comes briefly and tends to miss the basin altogether, though at night moisture gathers on the metal frames and travels down into pockets in the living walls. By morning, small leaves have opened along those damp seams. They close again as the day dries, and the pale stems on the roofs turn a few degrees toward the light. In the square, cracks in the mat widen and narrow so slowly that a dropped washer may be visible for hours before the runners draw it underneath.

Everything has the rounded look of a place long in use. Corners have softened; rooflines sag and then lift where fresh growth has been bound into them. The people who maintain the town cut doorways back to size and train new shoots over the oldest frames. They leave the open spaces alone except for the paths across them.

Animals are scarce. Now and then a small gray moth rests beneath a roller, or something with too many legs slips into a seam when a door opens. Most of the movement belongs to the town itself: leaves unfolding, water descending through hidden channels, vines being drawn through the quiet works. By evening the roof closes, the belts stop, and the last drops fall from the iron into the roots waiting below.
