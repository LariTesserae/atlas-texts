The city occupies a high shelf between two rims of newly raised rock. Beyond the outer rim, the land falls away into cloud; beyond the inner one, black peaks rise again, their slopes so sharp and unweathered that they look folded rather than worn. The shelf is broad enough to hold several districts, yards, and a railway, though from its edges they seem pressed together by the mountains.

Heat gathers here. It comes through the soles of boots from dark pavement and radiates from the cliff faces long after the sun has moved behind them. The air is thin enough that a short climb can leave a person breathless, but it is never cool. On some mornings a low cloud crosses the shelf, wetting the upper walls while the streets remain dry. Its moisture beads on metal and runs into channels cut for that purpose.

The buildings stand close, many sharing walls, their roofs crowded with tanks, ducts, and ranks of ceramic chimneys. Most are made from the local stone: dense, dark blocks fitted around steel frames. In the industrial quarter, long workshops have been built directly against the exposed rock, so that one wall of each room is still the mountain. Narrow passages lead between them into courtyards full of pipes and idle machinery. The newest foundations sit on pale gravel that has scarcely begun to settle.

There is no greenery in the courtyards or along the railway. The colors come from minerals: rust-red seams in the cliffs, white salt along the drains, a dull green sheen where water has touched copper. Small pools collect beneath the condensers, warm at the edges and cool in the center. Their surfaces are sometimes marked by fine, branching films, which dissolve when disturbed and return several days later.

The machinery runs slowly and with very little noise. Heavy flywheels turn behind screens of woven metal. Water moves through insulated pipes under the streets, carrying heat to the works that shape stone and draw wire. Most of the city’s sound comes from these pipes: an occasional knock, a soft hiss at a joint, a low vibration felt through a hand on a wall. Voices carry a long way across the yards. Even the trains enter quietly, their motors laboring almost below hearing as they bring up ore and take away finished lengths of rail.

Animals are uncommon. A few pale, jointed creatures shelter in the cool spaces beneath the tanks; at night they venture out to drink along the drains. They move with great care over the hot stone and vanish at the first footfall. Far above the city, something winged is sometimes visible circling a peak, though it may remain so long in one place that it is difficult to tell whether it is flying or caught in an updraft.

The people who built the city leave gaps in their walls at regular intervals, narrow seams packed with a soft black compound. They say the shelf is still rising, and the seams allow the buildings to rise with it. Survey marks on the cliffs confirm a little movement each year, though not always in the expected direction. A mark placed on the eastern rim may be found a handspan higher the following season, while the neighboring mark has stayed where it was.

Above the highest workshops are several unfinished streets. Their lamps have been installed, their drains connected, but the buildings stand only one or two stories high. Work continues there without hurry. Blocks arrive on the railway, are stacked beside the foundations, and wait through the hot afternoons for crews to place them. From those streets the whole shelf is visible: roofs and chimneys gathered in the basin, silver lines of pipe running between them, and the occasional patch of cloud passing over, leaving a brief darkening on stone.
