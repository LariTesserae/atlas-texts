The town stands on a high basin of pale stone, where the heat rises from below as well as falling from the sun. From the rim, its streets appear widely spaced: long white causeways crossing flats of shallow water, with workshops and dwellings gathered at their intersections. Beyond them the basin opens into miles of wet, bare ground, broken by low ridges and pools that shine too brightly to look into.

There are no trees. Nothing green fringes the water or takes hold in the cracks. The exposed stone has been worn into rounded shelves by a long traffic of runoff, and mineral crusts lie over it in colors from chalk-white to rust. Water seeps down the faces of the ridges even in the hottest hours. It collects in narrow channels, warms, spreads over the flats, and vanishes again through seams in the rock. After rain the whole basin holds a skin of water; several days later, there is still enough to wet the soles of a walker’s feet.

Animals are everywhere, though at first they can be mistaken for features of the ground. Broad, flat-bodied grazers settle in the shallows, their backs the same dull cream as the silt. They feed on the thin bacterial films that grow around mineral seeps, shifting slowly from pool to pool as the films are consumed. Smaller creatures move beneath them: jointed, translucent things that comb the sediment for the same nourishment. When a shadow crosses the water, hundreds of these vanish into it at once, leaving the surface almost undisturbed.

Long-legged hunters wait along the causeways. They stand so still in the heat that people use them as markers when giving directions. At dusk they step down into the water and lift out the smaller grazers with narrow, closing mouths. The larger animals can defend themselves by pressing tight against the stone; sometimes one bears a pale scar where a hunter has managed to pull it loose.

The town draws its water from deep wells and returns it through cooling channels. Its factories separate salts and metals from the brine, leaving neat beds of mineral waste beyond the last houses. Pumps work in sunken chambers, and the engines are enclosed behind thick walls to keep their heat out of the streets. Their vibration can be felt through a hand laid on the masonry, but little of it reaches the open air. Workers communicate across the yards with shutters, flags and hand signs. In the middle of the day, when the light is strongest, a lifted hand can be seen from one side of town to the other.

The quiet makes small sounds conspicuous: the click of cooling metal, the soft release of a valve, the scrape of an animal turning on stone. Rain arrives with more motion than noise, whitening the distance as it crosses the basin. For a while afterward, warm vapor rises from the streets and the town seems to float above its own reflection.

At night the water remains warm. The factory windows glow behind their screens, and the shallow flats fill with moving shapes. By morning, fresh feeding tracks cross the mineral dust on the causeways, and beads of water hang from every shaded edge.
