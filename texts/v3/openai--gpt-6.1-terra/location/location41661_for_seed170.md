The plateau begins above the reach of any visible lowland. It runs for hundreds of miles beneath a pale, ordinary sun, its surface worn into broad shelves of rust-red stone and black glass. Long escarpments have softened at their edges. The hollows between them hold fine gray grit, dry enough to flow when the ground tilts, though it has not tilted noticeably in anyone’s lifetime.

Heat gathers close to the stone. At midday the air shimmers, and the far ridges appear to lift and settle, but the ridges are moving only in the eye. There is no shade except what the animals make.

At first they look like more escarpments. One lies along the horizon, a low-backed shape the length of a valley, its skin plated in the same colors as the ground. Another stands far beyond it on legs so widely spaced that each could be mistaken for an isolated pillar. Between them is a great deal of empty air. Yet wherever the view opens, there are more: backs, jointed limbs, slow-turning heads. Their bodies occupy nearly every horizon, and the plateau bears the broad polished tracks of many others that have passed through.

They graze on stone. A head lowers by degrees until its mouth meets a ridge; then the animal remains there for years, drawing the rock inward. Heat glows faintly through seams in its jaw. Deep inside, crushing surfaces turn against one another, and long metallic tones travel out across the plain. When the head eventually rises, a clean-sided trench remains.

The animals are full of mechanisms. Rings rotate beneath their translucent hides; pistonlike bones move in sleeves of living tissue; electrical arcs flicker between teeth. Each creature sorts the minerals it takes in, carrying threads of copper and plates of iron into different parts of its body. These are not machines fitted into animals, nor animals that have learned to use machines. They grow their own bearings, replace their own worn surfaces, and pass the patterns of their immense internal works to their young. Nothing is manufactured apart from them.

The noise is tremendous and almost unchanging. Grinding hums overlap from one end of the plateau to the other. A distant animal calls with a note too low to separate into sound and pressure; it continues for several days, and the grit creeps toward the bases of cliffs. Nearer at hand, a smaller creature—small enough to fit inside a canyon—clicks its joints as it turns. The intervals between clicks are long, but each one rings through the stone.

Space behaves differently in the presence of the herds. A ridge can be seen between the legs of an animal and also beyond its back, though the two views show opposite sides of the ridge. Tracks sometimes emerge from a footprint before arriving at it. The creatures seem undisturbed by this. One may spend a century crossing its own shadow and come out farther away than when it entered.

At evening the light becomes copper-colored. The plated bodies cool slowly, ticking across the plateau. A young animal pushes its way out from beneath the shoulder of an older one, unfolding three bright limbs. For a while both remain still. Then, almost too gradually to follow, the smaller head turns toward a distant ridge, and the grinding begins there too.
