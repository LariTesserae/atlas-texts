At the top of the range, the land is still coming apart. A long ridge of dark, newly exposed rock rises above a high basin, its flanks broken into sharp plates and blocks. Little soil has gathered between them. The hollows are not yet rounded, and the streams have cut only shallow, uncertain courses through the stone.

The basin is open enough that weather moves visibly across it. Low cloud catches on one side while, several miles away, a strip of pale sky hangs over the opposite rim. The elevation makes every distance look spare: rock, snow, water, then more rock, with great stretches of air between. In the dim light the wet surfaces show first, each fissure picked out in dull silver.

Water is common but seldom deep. It seeps from beneath old snowfields, spreads into thin sheets over the flatter stone and collects in cold pools at the foot of the ridge. Around their edges, mud remains soft even after a freezing night. Frost lifts it in small scales; by afternoon, the surface loosens again. In sheltered cracks there are cushions of moss, a few wiry grasses and low plants no taller than a thumb. They grow far apart, wherever a little grit has stayed long enough to hold their roots.

Animals are easier to find than plants, though they too leave much of the basin empty. Birds arrive in flocks from lower valleys and settle beside the water, feeding on midges and other small insects carried upward on the wind. Their nests occupy ledges on the warmer face of the ridge. Beneath loose stones, beetles and spiders shelter from the cold; small mammals come out briefly among the rockfalls. When a hawk passes over, the feeding birds lift together, circle without much sound and resettle farther along the shore.

The mountain has an industrial intensity without anything having been built. Deep below, heat drives water through fractures; at one seep it emerges warm enough to steam. Elsewhere, pressure splits stone with a single hard report. Wind forces a steady current through gaps in the ridge, and snowmelt carries fine mineral grains into the pools, where they settle in separate bands. These processes work at different speeds, often out of sight. Their signs are everywhere: a fresh crack, a cloudy trickle, a little fan of grit laid down overnight.

Most of the time the basin is quiet. There is the thin hiss of water over rock, an occasional wingbeat, the faint clicking of loose stones as they cool. Toward evening the cloud lowers, the birds draw closer to their ledges, and the pools begin to gather ice around their margins. Water continues to move beneath it.
