The chamber lies below a low mound of brick, entered by a stair so worn that its middle steps have become a single shallow ramp. It is not much larger than a house, but very little of it is empty. Clay tubes crowd the walls from floor to vault; baskets hang between them, and narrow shelves project over the paths. Even the floor is made in layers: old tiles, broken tiles, a packing of fired fragments, and newer tiles laid wherever a foot is likely to fall.

Daylight comes through a row of openings along the crown of the vault. Each has a hood of baked clay to keep out most of the rain. The light is ordinary and sufficient, though it falls in strips, so that a person crossing the chamber moves repeatedly from warmth into shade. The air is warm throughout, with the dry smell of pottery and a sharper animal scent around the tubes.

Most of the tubes are occupied. Small brown swifts nest in the upper ones, bringing in insects from the country above. Below them live several kinds of gecko, some so pale they are difficult to distinguish from the clay until they move. Moths settle among the baskets, where spiders have stretched dense webs from rim to rim. Beetles work through the sweepings in the corners. There are always wings opening somewhere, claws ticking on tile, and small bodies slipping through gaps too narrow to see into.

The noise is much greater than the size of the place suggests. The swifts call continuously when they are feeding young, and their voices bounce from the hard vault. Loose shutters knock at the light openings. A row of hanging pots rattles whenever someone climbs the stair or a strong gust passes over the mound. At times the sounds merge into a dry roar; then a flock leaves through the openings and individual noises become audible again.

Rain reaches the chamber only occasionally. Water runs down grooves in the hoods and gathers in a few shallow bowls set into the walls. After a storm, drops continue to fall from the roof joints for a day or two. The geckos drink there, and the birds crowd the rims, jostling one another into the basins. A little moss survives on the dampest joints. In one basket a wiry plant grows from a handful of old nesting material, its roots caught in the weave; elsewhere there is little green.

The chamber has been repaired for longer than anyone can readily count. New clay was pressed around old cracks, and that clay has cracked in turn. The rims of the most-used tubes have been polished smooth by generations of birds. Water has rounded the painted designs on the walls until only bands and faint thumb marks remain.

People still come down to tend it. They replace a fallen basket, clear enough debris from the stair to keep it passable, or set a fresh bowl beneath a persistent drip. They leave the occupied tubes alone. By late afternoon, the strips of light have shifted to the opposite wall, the bowls are busy with drinking animals, and the chamber is loud again with birds returning.
