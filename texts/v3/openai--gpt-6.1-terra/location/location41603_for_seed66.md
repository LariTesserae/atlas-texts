The city occupies a high basin so broad that its far walls are usually hidden by weather. From the upper ridges it looks less like a single settlement than a pale, continuous working of the ground: roofs, reservoirs, freight yards and old excavations spread across the stone, interrupted by long reaches of exposed bedrock. Beyond the last visible buildings, the same chimneys rise again through the haze.

The basin is warm. Rain falls often, though rarely with much force, and the paved surfaces are seldom entirely dry. Water gathers in shallow seams beneath the streets and emerges through retaining walls in clear, patient threads. It runs along the gutters, under the loading platforms and into channels cut so long ago that their edges have rounded to the shape of the flow. In the oldest districts the water has deposited a cream-colored crust over steps, bolts and the lower courses of masonry.

There are no trees or grasses. The slopes above the city are bare, except for gravel fans and the mouths of drains. In sheltered places the rock carries a thin dark film, and there are soft fungal growths in some of the damp service tunnels, but from a distance the whole basin is mineral: gray limestone, black industrial brick, rusted iron, standing water. Even the open squares are surfaced with worn stone. The absence of vegetation leaves every contour exposed.

Animals occupy nearly every other space.

Broad-backed grazers move slowly through the drainage fields, feeding on the dark film that grows over wet stone. They have blunt, pale mouths and leave clean arcs behind them. Smaller creatures follow to pick at the scraps lifted by their feet. At the reservoirs, heavy flocks crowd the retaining walls, some standing shoulder to shoulder, others floating so thickly that the water shows only in narrow strips between their bodies. Their calls travel a long way across the basin: low clattering sounds, interrupted now and then by a sharp collective cry.

The animals are not confined to the outskirts. They sleep beneath elevated railways, in the lee of warm factory walls and along the broad landings of public stairs. Nesting colonies fill disused warehouses. Herds pass through certain streets at established hours, and the tram drivers know where to slow for them. The inhabitants have fitted gates to some doorways and raised the electrical equipment above reach, but much of the city is built around accommodation rather than exclusion.

The buildings are substantial and weathered: workshops with high windows, apartment blocks faced in stone, pump houses, foundries and long sheds with sawtooth roofs. Their foundations have been repaired many times. Walkways bridge the wetter streets, while larger bridges carry freight trains over canals and animal crossings. Pipes run openly between buildings, expanding at joints polished bright by years of slow movement. The factories produce a steady haze, though rain usually brings it down before it can rise far above the rooftops.

Sound accumulates in the basin. Engines hum behind walls; presses strike at measured intervals; water rattles through grilles. Hooves and claws scrape on pavement, wings beat heavily against the air, and calls echo between the buildings. It is a loud place without much hurry. Most of its noises continue for so long that they become part of the ground of hearing.

On the eastern side, the basin floor rises into terraces of ancient quarry cuts. They have been worn smooth by runoff, and the city has climbed into them in layers. Workshops occupy the lower ledges; housing stands above; tanks and pumping stations crown the highest accessible shelves. From there the scale becomes difficult to judge. A train crossing the floor of the basin can remain in view for an hour. Rain may be falling over one quarter of the city while another lies in clear afternoon light.

The weathered forms give the impression that the place has reached a durable arrangement, though it still requires work. Channels must be cleared of mineral deposits. Gates must be repaired after the larger animals lean against them. Workers scrape films from the reservoir walls in some places and cultivate them in others, where they provide feed. Buildings settle, pipes corrode, and new stone is fitted into old walls.

Toward evening, warmth rises from the pavement and meets the cooler air descending from the ridges. A low mist forms over the canals and drifts between the factories. The reservoir birds settle with a prolonged disturbance of wings. In the streets, the grazers continue along their familiar routes, their feet sounding on wet stone beneath the steady machinery. From the upper terraces, lights begin to appear across the basin, row after row, until distance and mist obscure them.
