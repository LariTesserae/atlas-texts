The plateau stands so high that its edges are often lost above the weather. From below, it looks like the remnant of a mountain range ground down to a single immense table: a level horizon upheld by cliffs, with long aprons of ancient stone spilling into the haze. From above, its surface is anything but flat. Shallow basins spread for miles between ridges worn smooth as knuckles. Here and there the ground opens into a hollow large enough to hold a town, its walls rounded by water that vanished ages ago.

The warmth is surprising at this height. Dark stone absorbs the sun, and warm air climbs continually from the lowlands, gathering speed as it passes through the cuts in the cliffs. By afternoon the wind has become a vast, uneven roar. It tears across the basins, strikes their far walls and wheels back on itself. Pebbles rattle. Loose sheets of bark rise and spin. The sound fills the hollows so completely that a person standing in one must shout to hear their own voice.

Yet the land under all this violence is very old. The ridges have been stripped almost to their roots; their stone shows layers bent and folded long before the present valleys took shape. Each storm rearranges the dust and breaks a little more from the cliff edges, but the great curves remain. The plateau seems to have settled into a form that weather can trouble without easily changing.

Vegetation follows the seams in the rock. Low, densely leafed trees grow in broad green bands along the basin floors, their crowns pressed nearly horizontal by the wind. Between the bands are long stretches of bare pavement, warm underfoot and empty except for drifting grit. In sheltered cracks, ferns grow higher than a person, and soft vines knot themselves around the trunks of the little trees. Their leaves are thick and glossy, equipped to catch what moisture comes their way.

Rain is brief and forceful. A cloud may drag itself over the rim, empty into one hollow and leave the next untouched. Water runs brown along the seams, gathers in stone cups and disappears underground within a day. On clear mornings, condensation beads on the colder rock before the sun reaches it. The plants have roots packed deep into fissures, where a little dampness remains after the surface has dried.

Life is difficult to see in the open. Now and then a broad-winged glider is lifted abruptly over a ridge, hangs against the rush of air and drops out of sight into a green basin. Small pale animals shelter beneath the fern roots. After rain, their tracks cross the mud between pools, already blurring as the water drains away.

Scattered around the plateau are low shelters made from slabs leaned together and weighted with stones. Some have collapsed; others still keep a dry patch beneath them. At their entrances lie chipped scrapers, lengths of twisted plant fibre and bowls cut from soft rock. The bowls have narrow grooves leading into them, apparently to collect condensation. A few contain water, though no one is there to drink it.

Toward evening the wind shifts. The roar continues, but underneath it comes a deep pulsing note, rising and fading through the stone. It seems to move from basin to basin, sometimes close enough to feel through the soles of the feet. Then the green bands begin to thrash again, the warm air fills with dust, and the note is lost.
