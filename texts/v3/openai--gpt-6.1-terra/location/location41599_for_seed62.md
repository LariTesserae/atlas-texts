The plateau is high enough that the light seems to arrive without passing through much air. Even in the cool of morning, every pale surface shines. Long ridges of newly raised stone cross the plateau in parallel folds, their edges still sharp, their slopes broken into slabs that fit together as though they had only recently been pulled apart.

Between the ridges, vegetation fills nearly every sheltered place. There are dense cushions of silver-green leaves, stands of low trees with black, waxy bark, and ferns so tightly packed that their fronds hold one another upright. Vines spread over the fresh rock in broad, overlapping fans. Their roots enter hairline cracks and widen them very slowly. From a distance the green looks continuous; up close it is a crowded arrangement of distinct, patient lives.

There is little open water. On some mornings a thin fog comes over the eastern rim and leaves droplets along the leaf edges. The plants collect these in folded stems and deep central cups. Beneath the mats of vegetation, the ground remains cool and faintly damp for weeks after a brief rain, while exposed stone dries almost at once. A few hollows contain dark water, motionless and clear enough to show the pale roots suspended in it.

The valley beyond the plateau is broad enough to take half a day to cross. Its floor rises in long terraces toward a line of bright cliffs, and each terrace has its own cover: thick moss on one, copper-colored shrubs on another, a miniature forest of straight white trunks farther down. No paths are visible from above. At ground level, narrow gaps wind through the growth, some following old fractures, others maintained by machines.

The machines are scattered. A low pumping house sits beneath a stone overhang, its metal walls freckled with mineral deposits. Two extraction towers stand a great distance apart among the ferns, joined by a cable that disappears and reappears in the foliage. Their surfaces are clean where the sun reaches them. Plants have grown around their feet and over their anchor bolts, but not over the small glass panels set into their sides.

The machinery works without an audible vibration. Wheels turn slowly behind sealed windows; indicator lights change from white to amber and back. Along the cable, little carriers move between the towers with baskets of pale, fibrous material. They pass through the treetops without disturbing a leaf. Occasionally one stops in full sunlight and remains there for hours before continuing.

The quiet extends beyond the machines. Rain can be seen striking the stone, but it makes no patter. A branch may settle against another without a creak. Near the eastern cliffs, the silence has a physical peculiarity: a person can feel their own words in the throat and chest, but beyond a few steps even a shout seems to lose its shape. The workers who once marked the towers left signs in raised metal lettering, large enough to be read across the clearings. Some of the signs have been partly surrounded by leaves.

Animals are scarce. There are occasional silver tracks in the dust beneath the shrubs, and once in a while a small, dark creature can be seen resting inside a flower cup, its narrow body almost indistinguishable from the folded petals. Near the water hollows, transparent insects cling to the undersides of leaves. They move only when the shadow of a cloud passes over them, and even then with such care that the droplets above remain intact.

By late afternoon the bright ridges cast long, cool shadows across the valley. Moisture begins to gather again on the shaded plants. The towers continue their slow work, carrying their harvest above the dense green, while the exposed edges of the plateau hold the last light. From one day to the next almost nothing appears to change. Over many seasons, the roots advance into the stone, the terraces thicken, and the narrow gaps through the vegetation take slightly different courses.
