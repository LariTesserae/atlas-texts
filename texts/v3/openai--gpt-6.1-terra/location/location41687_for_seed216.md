The high country is made of woven roots. They cross and recross beneath your feet in ropes as thick as a body, their bark worn smooth where generations have walked. Between them, finer roots have been threaded into a springy floor. There is no soil underneath. Through the small openings you can see other floors, far below, and between those more darkness than the eye can measure.

The land rises in terraces across an immense hollow. Each terrace is a living construction: trunks bent while young, lashed with vine, and persuaded over many years to grow into arches. Whole groves have been raised this way. Their crowns spread into broad, dim ceilings; their trunks brace the levels below. The work is old enough that nobody remembers its first makers. Where a limb fails, people cut and bind a replacement with stone blades and wooden wedges. Where no one tends it, the structure grows on without them, closing a doorway or lifting a path slowly out of reach.

There is room for weather inside. Warm mist gathers under the canopy and travels for days across open spans. Sometimes a bank of it catches in the upper branches and rains for an hour on one narrow terrace, leaving the next dry. Water beads along the woven paths, seeps into seams, and emerges lower down in strings of drops. The roots drink much of it. Shallow pools collect where old knots have cupped with age, and pale floating leaves cover them so completely that an animal may step onto water thinking it has found solid ground.

Everything the eye can rest on is plant: the walls, the bridges, the mats of leaf and moss, the enormous pillars vanishing overhead. Yet most of the place is open air. A single branch may run for miles without meeting another, bearing a thin green crust of gardens and resting platforms along its length. From one of these, the nearest occupied terrace can be a faint line across the hollow. You might watch a storm form between here and there without seeing what lies beneath it.

The light comes from above, strained through so many layers of foliage that even at its strongest it is the color of old green glass. At the edges of clearings, leaves turn their undersides toward it, exposing a dull silver. Some of the oldest terraces have grown smooth and rounded under centuries of dripping water. Others retain the marks of hands: peg holes, twisted bindings, patterns cut into bark that has since swollen around them.

Animals move everywhere, though they are rarely easy to see. Soft-footed grazers browse the hanging fronds. Small gliders fold themselves against the pillars, indistinguishable from dry leaves until they launch into the open. Long, many-jointed hunters wait beneath the paths, feeling for the pressure of feet through the roots. There are insects in the pools and in the hollows of stems, and pale creatures that spend their lives inside the living wood, widening its passages as they feed.

The place is remarkably quiet. Leaves rub together with a sound like breath; water taps once, then again much later. Even when a great limb breaks, the report travels only a short distance before the open air takes it. From far away you may see the falling foliage spread and turn without hearing anything at all.

Distances are not dependable here. A bridge can carry you across the same gap in an afternoon or through three warm nights, though its knots and handholds remain familiar throughout. Sometimes the terrace you left appears above you when you look back; sometimes it is below. The inhabitants mark their routes by the smell of certain flowers, by scars in the wood, by pools whose surfaces show the correct arrangement of leaves. They repair these landmarks as carefully as they repair the paths.

Toward evening, when the dimness deepens, the plants draw moisture from the air. Droplets form on every exposed edge. Animals come out to drink, and the terraces shift almost imperceptibly as leaves unfurl and roots tighten their hold. Across the hollow, isolated lights appear: bowls of resin burning on distant platforms, each one tended by someone living in the vast work of everyone who came before.
