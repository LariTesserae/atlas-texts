The land has sunk below the surrounding plain, leaving a broad, cold basin open to the sky. Its rim is a fresh-looking edge of dark earth and pale stone, not yet rounded into hills. Water seeps from it at every level, joining in thin streams that descend through curtains of moss. In daylight the basin is bright enough to see across, though mist often gathers over its farther reaches.

Almost none of the floor is dry. Shallow channels divide around hummocks of peat and low, dense willow. Sedges grow so closely that their stems rub together in the current. Beneath them, black water moves over clean gravel and freshly exposed clay. Here and there a low ridge of stone breaks the surface, sharp along its crest, with young ferns established in its cracks.

The basin has the crowded order of a settlement. Beaver lodges stand close together among the pools, their domes threaded with living shoots. Long dams run between them, holding terraces of water at slightly different heights; narrower channels lead from pool to pool and into the willow thickets. The beavers keep adding branches as the water finds new paths, and where a dam leaks, moss and sedge take root in the wet wood. No open space stays open for long.

Water voles travel beneath the overhanging grass. Frogs shelter in the warmer mud at the bottoms of the pools, while small fish move through the clear channels. Birds nest in the willow, and at the edge of a pond an otter may lie nearly hidden among the reeds. Life is abundant, but sound carries only a little way: a soft knock from within a lodge, the settling of water over a dam, the occasional brief call of a bird.

At night the upper pools sometimes freeze. By afternoon, sun has opened dark seams along the banks, and meltwater from the rim is moving through the terraces again. The cold slows the growth but does not stop it. New roots tighten around the raw edges of the basin, and each small rise in water sends a quiet adjustment through the crowded place.
