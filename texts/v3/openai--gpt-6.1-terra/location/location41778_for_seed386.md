The valley lies high among volcanic ridges, above the cloud deck that fills the lower country. Its floor is broad enough that the far wall looks blue through the haze, but the mountains rise abruptly on every side: dark, weathered slopes scored by old gullies and draped in vegetation wherever roots can hold.

It is hot even at this height. Warm water moves close beneath the ground, and in places it comes up through beds of black gravel, spreading into shallow pools before slipping away again. The air above them trembles. A pale mist gathers in the hollows at night and breaks apart after sunrise, leaving every leaf wet. Rain is frequent and heavy, though by afternoon the sun can be bright enough to draw steam from exposed rock.

The plants grow in layers. Ferns crowd the stream margins; broad-leaved shrubs rise behind them; farther upslope, small trees lean out from the cliffs, their branches burdened with moss and hanging roots. Some of the ground is firm and springy, made of roots woven through old volcanic ash. Elsewhere a foot would sink into warm mud. Water beads on waxy leaves and falls from one to another long after a shower has passed.

The valley has a quiet, continuous sound: trickling water, the soft shift of foliage, an occasional bird calling from somewhere hidden. Insects gather over the warm pools, and small frogs occupy the cooler seepage along their edges. There are larger animals too—dark shapes grazing among the ferns or picking their way across a scree slope—but the cover is thick enough that they are often heard before they are seen.

A low hum comes through the stone near the hottest springs. Water forced through narrow channels underground drives a steady pulse; minerals collect around the outlets in pale crusts, then crack as the ground warms and cools. The valley has something of a great works about it: heat and water moving through a system too extensive to see, making and depositing material day after day. Yet there are no buildings, tracks or machines here. On the ridges above, bare rock breaks slowly under rain and roots, and the loosened fragments descend to join the wet, fertile floor.
