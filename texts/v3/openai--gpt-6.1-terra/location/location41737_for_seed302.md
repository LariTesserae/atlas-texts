The high basin is white with light. It spreads for miles between blunt ridges, its floor crowded with pale stones, upright flakes of shale and close-set knobs of limestone. From a distance it looks almost smooth; underfoot there is hardly a place to put a boot that is not already occupied by rock or running water.

Water lies just below the surface and comes up everywhere. It wells through narrow seams, threads between the stones and disappears again into dark openings. Thin sheets slide down the exposed faces of the ridges, leaving them glossy. There are shallow pools, too, clear enough to show every grain on their bottoms, though their surfaces tremble so continuously that the grains seem to move.

The noise is immense. Water falls through cavities in the stone, and the whole basin answers with a deep, unbroken roar. Over it comes a finer rattling from thousands of small streams striking rock. The sound is loud enough to be felt in the chest; there is no quiet place behind a boulder or beside a pool. Yet it never builds or subsides. Each channel carries the same flow, each hollow holds its note.

No plants soften the ground. The colors belong to minerals: chalk white, wet silver, iron red in the deeper cracks. The air is cool and damp, and the bright sky whitens the mist caught close above the basin. Long weathering has rounded the ridges and widened the seams, but the forms now seem settled, neither fresh nor crumbling.

Life is scarce and easy to miss. In a few sheltered channels, tiny colorless crustaceans cling beneath stones. They graze on films of microbes that draw their energy from minerals in the water. There may be miles of bare rock between one such patch and the next.

From the rim, the basin appears still despite all that moving water. The pools keep their levels, the mist hangs in the same low hollows, and sunlight flashes on the same countless wet edges. The roar fills the open distance without interruption.
