The city lies in a low basin, broad enough that its far edge disappears into a dim, warm haze. From a distance it looks nearly empty. Its buildings stand miles apart: pale towers with sharp, unworn corners, thin bridges suspended between heights, and broad black foundations laid directly over the young stone. There are no streets in the usual sense. A person leaving one building may cross hours of open ground before reaching another.

The ground is new. Long ridges of dark mineral rise from it like folds in cooling wax, their edges still keen enough to cut leather. In some places the surface has split cleanly, exposing bands of blue and copper beneath. The basin has not been much shaped by rain. Most days are dry, and dust gathers in the sheltered sides of the ridges, though the air carries a faint dampness after nightfall. Water appears in small, dependable places: beads along the undersides of leaves, shallow basins at the feet of towers, a seam of condensation inside a crack in the rock.

Vegetation grows around these sources with extravagant patience. A single plant may occupy an acre, its trunk no wider than a wrist but its canopy spread on transparent ribs across a great circle of ground. The leaves hang far apart, each one thick and green, each collecting its own few drops. Between the canopies are long stretches of bare stone. From a tower, the basin seems patterned with enormous, widely spaced green hands.

Animals move through the open spaces in considerable numbers, though they are difficult to see all at once. Small, jointed creatures shelter beneath the plants by day and emerge in streams when the stone begins to cool. Pale gliders pass between the canopies, feeding on the insects that rise around them. There are grazing animals, too, long-legged and nearly translucent, with dark organs visible shifting inside their bodies. They travel in loose herds, so widely separated that a hunter might mistake one animal for an isolated shape and only later notice the others along the horizon.

The noise reaches a visitor before any of this can be properly seen. It is immense: a continuous metallic roar, crossed by deep pulses that shake the chest. The plants are partly responsible. Their hollow ribs resonate in the moving air, and the stone beneath them carries the vibration for miles. The city adds its own immense machinery to the sound. Somewhere below the basin, structures press against one another without touching; their passage can be heard as a rolling succession of impacts, though no one has found a joint to oil or a wheel to stop.

Conversation is conducted by touch, by signs, or in the sheltered interiors of the towers. Even there, cups tremble on tables. The animals seem untroubled. Many navigate by the sound, making minute adjustments to their calls and listening for what returns. Predators wait where the ridges interrupt it, in sudden pockets of relative quiet.

The towers are occupied, though their windows seldom brighten. Their inhabitants have built the city to gather what the basin offers: a little water, a great deal of vibration, and the slow heat stored in stone. Fine seams run down each tower into the ground. At intervals a seam opens, revealing a space deeper than the foundation should allow; things are lowered into it, or brought out, and the seam closes without leaving a mark. Some bridges connect towers that are too far apart to see from either end. People enter them and arrive before they ought to, carrying the basin’s dust on their shoes.

Near the end of the warm season, a brief rain sometimes crosses the city. It falls in narrow curtains, darkening one ridge while the next remains dry. The great leaves tip toward it. The gliders drop out of the air and cling beneath their ribs. For a few hours afterward, the scattered basins fill and the green canopies shine under the low light.

Then the rain moves on. Water continues to fall from the leaves, drop by drop, long after the ground between them has dried.
