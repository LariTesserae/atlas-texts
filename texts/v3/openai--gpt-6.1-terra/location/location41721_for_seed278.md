The plateau is high enough that the horizon has weather of its own. Long veils of cloud hang below its outer rim, while above it the sky remains a dim, metallic blue. The land falls away so gradually that its distances are hard to judge: a ridge that seems close enough to reach by noon may take several days, and beyond it are other ridges, worn to the same long curves.

Stone covers most of the ground. It has been planed and fluted by old ice, then broken down more slowly by frost. Pale gravel lies in the hollows; the exposed rock is dark, with a faint sheen where meltwater has frozen again. There are no streams on the open heights. Moisture gathers instead beneath stones, in seams of blue ice, and as a fine condensation on the downwind sides of boulders. Occasionally a thin rain drifts across the plateau and vanishes into the gravel without making puddles.

Plants keep to these small reserves. A little gray-green moss spreads under an overhang; a black, branching lichen follows a crack for several yards. In sheltered places, low mats of wiry growth have pushed themselves almost flat against the ground. Much of what looks like bare stone is lightly furred with them.

The animals are not apparent at first. They are the color of gravel and keep their bodies close to it. A broad-backed grazer may stand motionless on a slope for hours, its legs folded beneath its coat, while smaller creatures shelter in the warmth along its flanks. Farther away, another rests beside a patch of frost. The space between them seems empty until the light changes and picks out the same rounded shapes across the next ridge, and the next.

There are immense numbers of them. On the open ground they are widely scattered, but the plateau extends so far that even this sparse arrangement contains herds beyond counting. In the deeper folds of the land they gather more closely, covering the valley floors in a slow-moving gray. Each animal has a blunt, flexible mouth for lifting lichens from stone and a dense fringe along its underside that collects the day’s condensation. Young animals follow beneath the adults, almost invisible in their shadows.

The herds move without calls. Their feet are broad and padded, and the cold still air carries little sound. Even among them one hears mainly the soft brushing of coats, a faint grinding as an animal feeds, and, now and then, the delicate click of ice shedding from its fringe. Predators travel the same slopes: long, low creatures that wait beside narrow passages in the rock. They, too, are silent, and a herd parts around one with a deliberateness that makes the movement look like weather.

Beneath the gravel, life works on a smaller and more concentrated scale. Dark colonies fill the wet seams of the plateau, drawing minerals from the rock and passing electrical charge through threads finer than hair. In places the stone is warm to the touch, though no warmth reaches the air. These colonies fix metals into glittering crusts, break down old tissue and slowly renew the thin living film on which the grazers feed. Across the continent their work is vast, continuous and almost entirely hidden.

Seasons pass here as changes in hardness and light. During the colder months, the ground seals over and the herds settle into the lee of ridges. When the dim sun stays longer in the sky, moisture loosens beneath the surface. Animals rise, one after another, and begin to travel. From a height, their paths appear as broad, faint currents flowing around the stone. They take weeks to cross a single basin, leaving the gravel scarcely disturbed behind them.
