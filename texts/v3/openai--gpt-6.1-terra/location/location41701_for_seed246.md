High on a plateau, a town occupies the shallow bowl of a mountain that has almost finished wearing away. Its streets are broad and pale, separated by low, rounded walls. From above, their arrangement is unmistakable: a central square, branching lanes, a ring of little courtyards. But the walls are living things, grown from the joined roots of a cold-loving plant. No one seems to have laid them out.

There is room for a crowd in the square. Most days it holds only wind.

The root walls are thickly furred with green. In their sheltered hollows, moss rises in soft cushions, and dark leaves overlap so densely that rain cannot reach the stone beneath. Elsewhere the ground is nearly bare: gray gravel, a few flattened lichens, long spaces swept clean by weather. The greenery is lush where it occurs, but the patches are far apart, like islands in a shallow sea.

The cold is steady rather than fierce. Frost survives all day inside the deepest courtyards, while the upper surfaces thaw whenever the sun comes through. Water is scarce on the open ground, though never entirely absent. It gathers under the moss, beads on the walls and occasionally runs through the streets in brief, clear threads. After rain, the whole town darkens. For an hour or two, its lanes carry a network of little streams toward the square, where they sink through a crack no wider than a hand.

The sound is much larger than the place. Wind rushes over the rim of the old mountain and drops into the bowl, making the root walls hum and the hollow lanes roar. Water entering the crack adds a deep knocking, irregular and resonant. Sometimes the two sounds meet, and the gravel trembles. A visitor can stand in an empty street and feel as though something immense is passing just out of sight.

The town changes while it is being watched. Frost loosens from a wall and patters down. A bank of leaves slowly lifts as warmth reaches it, exposing a black seam of soil. Narrow runnels open and close as meltwater finds its way through the gravel. The living walls grow almost imperceptibly across their own streets; in places, opposing roots have met and fused, making doorways so low that the wind whistles through them.

Animals are rare. A pale, short-legged grazer visits from the higher ground, cropping the moss with careful bites before vanishing over the rim. Small insects shelter deep in the green cushions and emerge in the briefest warm spells. Their movement draws a few dark birds, which wait on the walls with their feathers blown flat.

By evening the frost begins to return. The wet lanes shine in the low light, and the walls cast long shadows across them. Wind continues through the empty town, while underneath it, for as long as the water lasts, the stone keeps knocking.
