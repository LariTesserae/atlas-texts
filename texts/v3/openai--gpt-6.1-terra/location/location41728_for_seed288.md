The land lies below the level of the surrounding desert, in a broad basin with walls so old they have been worn into long, low slopes. From its floor the rim is visible only in places: a dark line against a dim evening sky. Most of the basin is open to the air, though a shelf of rock runs along its northern side, sheltering a wide belt of deeper shadow.

There is no standing water. The floor is hard, pale earth, crusted with salt in some places and broken elsewhere into plates of clay. Fine grit has gathered in the hollows of the rock, and old windblown sand lies banked against the slopes. The air is cool and exceedingly dry. It carries the smell of dust, dry leaves, and the close, animal warmth of crowded nests.

The basin has the spaciousness of a place worn nearly to its final shape. Ridges project only a little way above the flats. Channels cross them, shallow and rounded, their edges softened by ages of wind and falling grit. Great blocks that once dropped from the rim have been reduced to low humps, or split into heaps of flat stone. In the fading light it is difficult to tell where one old formation ends and another begins.

Vegetation grows in broad, separated colonies. Low trees with thick trunks and small, dark leaves spread over the shallow channels; their branches interlock into dense roofs, while their roots run far beneath the hard ground. Between them stand cushions of thorn and silver-gray scrub. Some patches are lush enough to make deep shade, full of leaves and seed heads and a soft accumulation of fallen growth. Then there may be a long stretch of bare salt-colored earth before the next thicket begins.

Across the central flats rises a city of earth. Its oldest quarters are low and massive, their walls rounded by time, while newer towers stand in close ranks along their edges. Covered passages connect mound to mound. The largest structures are taller than a person, buttressed at the base and pierced near their summits by narrow openings. Around them are thousands of smaller chimneys, ridges, and galleries, all built from grains of soil and plant matter by termites. The settlement extends a long way across the basin, interrupted by open courts of bare ground and belts of scrub. There are no straight streets, but there are routes used so consistently that the surface has been polished smooth.

The termites work mostly out of sight. In the dimness beneath a broken wall, they move slowly along the packed passages, carrying fragments of dead leaves and wood. Lizards wait near the openings. Beetles shelter under the rims of the mounds. Small, long-eared mammals live in the cooler ground below, and at dusk they emerge among the towers to feed.

The shelf along the northern wall is crowded with nests. Birds occupy its ledges, and bats hang in the deeper cracks. As the light fades, birds settle and bats begin to leave, pouring out in loose, wavering streams above the basin. Insects rise from the thickets to meet them. The birds call continually from their ledges; the bats chatter at the mouths of the cracks; crickets and cicadas pulse from every patch of cover. The sound is immense, filling the open ground and returning from the rock walls until individual calls are hard to distinguish. Even close to the trees, leaves can be seen trembling without their rustle being heard.

The noise changes through the night, but it seldom stops. A chorus may fall quiet in one part of the basin while another continues farther away, and then begin again, spreading from thicket to thicket. Above it, wings pass through the cool air. Below, the city holds its warmth, and its inhabitants keep moving through the dark, enclosed ways. By morning the birds will be leaving their ledges, the bats returning, and the sun will touch the rim long before it reaches the basin floor.
