The plateau is so high that the dark above it has ceased to look like sky. It is a close, faintly luminous ceiling, ridged in places as though immense pressure has folded it. Below, the ground extends for miles in shallow terraces of black glass and pale, unweathered stone. The edges of the terraces are sharp enough to cast thin shadows in what little light there is. Nothing has rounded them yet.

At intervals, the stone opens into upward-facing seams. Long needles of mineral stand in these seams, still growing, so slowly that a person watching for a lifetime might see no change. Farther out, a range of peaks is only partly present. Their lower slopes are solid and cold; above them, outlines crowd together without agreeing on where the summits should be. From one terrace there seem to be seven peaks. From the next there are four, one of them behind you.

The air is cool and dry. There is no scent of rain in the stone, no dampness beneath it. In sheltered basins grow dense thickets of broad, dark fronds, glossy as lacquer and thickly layered. Each thicket is lush within its own boundaries: leaves overlap, twist around one another, and press new growth up through their older shadows. But the basins are far apart. Between them lie long stretches of bare mineral, with perhaps a single low rosette gripping a crack.

The plants draw a pale vapor from the rock and bind it into an oily sap. When a leaf breaks, the sap comes away in elastic threads that stiffen in the air. Fallen leaves do not rot. They gradually unfasten into thin plates, which remain beneath the living growth and give the next generation something to root around. There are no insects among them, no burrows or tracks, and nothing visits the thickets to feed. Their seeds, heavy as beads, are lifted by the noise.

The noise fills the whole plateau. It is a continuous, tremendous chord, low enough to shake the chest and high enough to make the edges of things tremble. It comes through the soles of the feet as force and through the air as sound; covering the ears scarcely diminishes it. The needles in the seams appear to produce part of it, although they do not visibly move. The distant peaks contribute a deeper note whenever their outlines coincide. Even in the pauses between these events, the ground itself continues to roar.

A few structures stand at the edges of the plant basins. They are small shelters made from slabs leaned together and tied with the long fibers of the fronds. Their doorways face away from the nearest seam. Beside one shelter lies a stone hammer with a handle worn smooth; beside another, a heap of black plates has been sorted by size. There are no signs of recent work, though a strip of fiber pulled loose from a roof is still descending toward the ground. It has been descending, perhaps, for a very long time.

Distances are unreliable here. The walk from one shelter to another may take the better part of a day, while the return can be made in a few dozen steps. A crack crossed in the morning sometimes lies ahead again by evening, bearing the same loose shard on its edge. The thickets remain where they are, but their shadows occasionally reach shelters too far away to receive them.

Almost everything changes patiently. A new terrace is easing itself out of the plateau, its sharp lip rising by a fraction beneath the permanent thunder. Around a basin, a fringe of young fronds has advanced onto bare stone. The partly made mountains continue to disagree with their surroundings. Whatever is making this place has not finished deciding its shape.
