The town occupies a high shelf in a range so old that its summits have lost their peaks. From below, the mountains rise almost sheer, tier upon tier of dark stone, but their upper surfaces are broad and gently worn. Shallow basins lie in them like thumbprints in old wax. The shelf is large enough for several districts, a belt of terraces, and a long view across the neighboring heights, though the drop at its edges is rarely far away.

The air is cool even at midday. Clouds move below the outer streets and occasionally rise through them, thinning the houses into silhouettes. Rain comes in brief, slanting bursts. More often, moisture gathers quietly on every exposed surface: on railings, on leaves, on the black cables stretched between buildings. The town has learned to live on these small deposits. Gutters feed cisterns under the streets, and the roofs are ridged to give condensation somewhere to collect. In dry weeks, people go out at dawn to empty the shallow pans set among the stones.

Vegetation fills almost every space that is not walked or worked. Low trees with dark, flexible branches grow against the buildings, their crowns clipped flat by wind. Ferns crowd the sheltered sides of walls, and mats of thick-leaved plants cover the old scree. Higher up, pale grasses grow in dense whorls around boulders, catching mist before it passes. The greenery looks lush from a distance, but up close its leaves are small and leathery, fitted tightly together to conserve what water they can. Gardens occupy balconies and the warmer interiors of ruined workshops; vines hang through broken floors, searching the dim rooms below.

The buildings are made mostly from the mountain itself: close-grained gray blocks, dark slate, and a softer green stone that powders slowly where water reaches it. They stand shoulder to shoulder along narrow streets, with bridges joining their upper floors. Some were built over older foundations whose original use is no longer known. A stair may descend into a cellar, pass a sealed doorway at an odd angle, and emerge into a courtyard a full level below the street.

At the town’s center are the works. Their long roofs are glazed in soot, and their chimneys release only occasional threads of smoke. Inside, slow engines turn rollers and pumps. Fine mineral grit, gathered from the slopes and sieved through several grades, is pressed into tiles and insulating plates. The machinery is maintained with care: replacement teeth are cast in small batches, belts are patched, and shafts are polished where generations of hands have checked their heat. Power comes partly from a wind station on the ridge and partly from water falling through pipes between the cisterns. Neither is reliable alone.

The engines make less noise than their size suggests. Most of the time the town is filled with a subdued tremor, felt in a cup left on a table or in a hand resting against a wall. Other sounds carry clearly through it: a latch closing, a cart wheel crossing a seam in the paving, water dripping into a tank. When the wind changes, the cables overhead sing a few thin notes, each line at its own pitch.

Few animals are seen. Small cliff birds nest beneath the bridges, and now and then a long-bodied grazer appears above the highest gardens, picking its way among the stone basins. Their paths are easier to find than the animals themselves. In sheltered places, people sometimes discover neat circles cleared of moss where the grazers have slept.

The mountain continues to wear down, though there are no torrents or great collapses to announce it. Moisture enters the rock and freezes; flakes detach and creep through the plants. Every morning, workers clear grit from the drains and brush it from the stairways. Every afternoon, wind brings more. The oldest streets have sunk slightly beneath the accumulation, so that the thresholds of some houses now sit below the paving.

In the evening the town’s lamps come on unevenly, following the power available from the ridge. Their light is amber and faint in the mist. Across the shelf, tiny glimmers appear in the wet seams of the stone as well. They last a moment after a lamp goes dark, as if the rock has held on to a little of its light. Children trace them with their fingers; adults use them to find the edges of steps. Beyond the last buildings, the same dim glimmers mark cracks in the open ground, extending a short way toward the cliffs before disappearing into cloud.
