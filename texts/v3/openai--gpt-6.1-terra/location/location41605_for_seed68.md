The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a few streets. Its buildings stand well apart: a brick pumping station, a row of pale workshops, a square of workers’ houses with their backs to a long, open yard. Between them are broad stretches of rough ground where grasses grow waist-high and willow saplings lean into the light. From above, the town might look unfinished. On the ground it feels spacious and deliberately made, with straight curbs, numbered doors and narrow rails laid through the green.

The basin floor is mostly dry. Rain comes in brief showers, and the water does not travel far. It collects in the hollows of the rails, in stone-lined sumps and in the upturned leaves of burdock. By morning there is condensation on the metal roofs and a dark fringe beneath every wall. The soil between these damp places is pale and firm. Plants root deeply there, returning each year in dense islands separated by bare paths and gravel.

The ground is young. A low bank at the town’s edge has the clean, angular face of a recent break, with sharp pieces of dark stone still lying where they fell. The brickworks was built to use that stone, and its old kilns remain, though only one is fired now. Chimneys rise above the willows, their mortar still square-edged. New grass grows in the seams of the loading platform, but the corners of the platform have yet to crumble.

The air is cool even in bright weather. Sunlight reaches the bottom of the basin for most of the day and warms the brick walls enough to draw out flies and small brown lizards. At night, cold settles quickly over the open ground. The workshop windows mist from within, and water beads along the fences.

There are animals everywhere, though they are not always easy to see. Sparrows crowd into the ivy on the pumping station. Rabbits live beneath the disused kilns, and swallows nest under the eaves of the houses, darting low across the yard. Beetles gather at the damp sumps; by dusk, bats are stitching through the spaces between the lamps. A fox sometimes sleeps on the warm brick above the working kiln, undisturbed by the people moving below.

The town is loud. The pump runs steadily behind thick walls, its beat felt through the soles of the feet before it is heard. Fans turn in the workshops, sheet metal rings when it is moved, and short trains of carts clatter along the rails. Birds add their calls to the machinery, particularly in the morning, when hundreds of them seem to be arguing from the roofs at once. Even so, little changes quickly. The carts travel at walking pace. The pump lifts water from one tank to another for hours. A person crossing the yard has time to notice a new shoot beside a rail, or a puddle shrinking under the sun.

At the far end of the basin stands a tall iron frame with no obvious function. It was once part of a crane; now it supports a great tangle of flowering vine. Each spring the town’s mechanics grease the old bearings, though the arm has not been used in years. They say it keeps the vine from pulling the frame down. On windy days the arm turns a few degrees, carrying its flowers slowly over the yard, while the machinery goes on sounding beneath it.
