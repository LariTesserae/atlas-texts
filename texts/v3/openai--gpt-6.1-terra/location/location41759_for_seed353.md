The plateau begins above the last escarpment and goes on for a day’s walk, though there is no path across it that lasts a day. In the darkness it feels level, worn nearly flat by an age of abrasion. The higher ridges have been rubbed down into long shoulders. Between them lie shallow basins packed with black growth.

Nothing here stands on bare ground. The plateau is made of plants: layer upon layer of compressed stalk, root cable, resin and the pale, stony husks of earlier growth. The oldest layers have been pressed so hard that they ring like metal when struck. Heat rises through them from buried machinery, and the air just above the surface is hot enough to make the exposed skin of a hand tighten.

The plants have been built as much as grown. Their trunks rise in square bundles, bound with collars of their own hardened fiber. Leaves overlap into ducts. Rootwork has been trained around axles, switching gear and great slow-turning drums. Some of the machines were installed so long ago that the living material has entered their joints and become part of the mechanism. Others appear newly assembled, their bright resin teeth moving against dark ones polished almost smooth.

There is very little water, even within the plants. They carry hot oils through narrow vessels and draw what they need from dust, mineral fumes and the breakdown of their own dead parts. Broad fronds spread beneath the ceiling of darkness to gather heat. Fine combs extend into the air, catch grit, then fold inward to grind it. Wherever a surface is exposed, something grows over it; wherever space opens, a shoot enters.

The landscape is never still. A stand of tall, ribbed stems slowly leans across a basin, pressing its crown into the stand opposite. Fibers tighten, fasten and pull. As the two masses join, a row of lower stems buckles and is swallowed beneath them. Farther off, an old factory mound splits along its seams. Its roof descends in sections, each section held together by the roots running through it, while new growth begins to bridge the gaps. By the time the mound has settled, the slope around it has changed direction.

All this movement makes surprisingly little noise. There are dry creaks, faint clicks from the buried works, and now and then a long rasp as two densely packed surfaces slide past one another. The plants muffle most of the engines. Standing among them, one can feel a steady tremor through the soles while hearing only an occasional tap from somewhere deep inside a trunk.

There are no calls or footsteps in the growth. A few pale, jointed shapes may be seen clinging to the hotter vents, but they do not move away when approached. They are seed structures, loosening grain by grain in the rising air.

On the highest shoulders, the covering thins enough for a dim red glow to show through: furnace light passing through translucent layers of leaf and resin. It reveals a region of low, crowded forms extending into darkness, ridges and hollows slowly being made and unmade. The plateau is ancient, but hardly anything visible on it is old. Its age lies deeper, in the flattened foundation and in the machinery that the growth has learned, over immense time, to keep running.
