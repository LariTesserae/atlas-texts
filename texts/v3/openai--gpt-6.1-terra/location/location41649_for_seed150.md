The settlement occupies a shallow notch high on a mountainside, no wider than a small village. From above it looks like a dark patch of forest caught between new slopes of pale stone. Only when the cloud thins can its streets be seen: narrow lanes laid out by the growth of trees, bending around crowded squares and disappearing beneath roofs of interlocked branches.

There are no inhabitants in the usual sense. The settlement is made and occupied by the same living mass. Trunks lean together to form walls; roots rise in smooth ribs around sheltered hollows; thick fans of evergreen needles overlap overhead, letting through a little green-grey light. Moss grows over moss, and small leathery plants take root in the accumulated needles on higher branches. Nothing is bare for long. Even the freshest stone wears a fringe of lichen or a scatter of minute, red-stemmed leaves.

The mountain here is young enough to remain angular. A recent seam of rock juts through the trees at one end of the notch, its broken faces sharp and faintly blue. Plants have already found its cracks. Their roots hold beads of water against the stone, and when those beads freeze, the expanding ice gives a quiet tick. A splinter sometimes falls into the greenery and vanishes without a sound.

It is bitterly cold, though the crowded growth shelters the lanes from wind. Snow collects on the outer canopy and slowly melts where the living wood gives off warmth. The water filters down through needles and moss, appearing at last as drops along the undersides of roots. Some fall into shallow, black pools; others freeze on the tips of leaves. The pools are never very deep. Their edges shift as moss swells with moisture, and their surfaces close each night beneath thin, wrinkled ice.

There are junctions where several lanes meet, and low chambers under the oldest trees. One chamber has a floor so densely woven with roots that it feels springy underfoot; another is filled almost to its ceiling with soft, pale growth. The passages have the order of streets without having been surveyed or built. Each plant reaches for the available light and braces itself against its neighbours. Over many seasons their mutual pressure keeps some ways open and closes others. A corridor that was broad enough last winter may now require a sideways turn.

Animals are scarce. Now and then a small dark bird moves through the upper branches, shedding a little powdery snow, or an insect stirs deep inside a pocket of unfrozen moss. Most of the sound belongs to water and wood: a drop finding a pool, the dry brushing of needles, the occasional creak as ice loosens its grip on a branch. When wind passes over the notch, the canopy shivers in sections, but very little of it reaches the lanes below.

The place is always changing at a pace just beyond easy notice. Frost gathers and retreats; droplets travel through the living roofs; roots thicken against stone. By the dim middle of the day, the walls glisten. Toward evening they harden again, and the narrow streets fill with the faint, close smell of wet bark and evergreen leaves.
