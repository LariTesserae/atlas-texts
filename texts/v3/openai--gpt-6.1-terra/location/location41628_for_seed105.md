The plateau lies under a cold inland sea. Its old ridges run for miles through the dark, rising here and there into blunted summits, then falling away into broad basins of pale silt. Above the highest stone there is still a great depth of water; no surface can be seen, only a faint grayness so far overhead that it might be another layer of rock.

The ground is weathered into long ribs and shallow bowls. Water moves steadily across it, carrying fine mineral dust that gathers behind every ridge. In the basins, the silt lifts and settles in slow clouds. On the exposed heights the rock is scoured clean, black and smooth except where harder veins stand proud like the seams of an old vessel.

Vegetation grows in widely spaced colonies. Each begins as a thick mat fixed to stone and spreads upward in hundreds of dark ribbons, some as long as a tree is tall. The ribbons fork and curl around one another, making loose towers with hollow centers. Farther downslope, pale branching growths cover the rock in dense cushions. Their surfaces open and close with the current, taking in dissolved minerals and releasing threads that drift away to root elsewhere. Between the colonies are long stretches of bare ground.

There is no animal life. Nothing swims through the water, grazes the growths, or shelters in them. When a tower of ribbons folds suddenly to one side, the cause is a current arriving before its cloud of silt. The colonies consume the dead edges of their own mats; loosened fragments drift until they decay or find stone on which to fasten.

The current has a powerful voice. It pours through gaps in the ridges with a continuous low roar, and loose stones knock together in the channels. At intervals a deeper sound passes through the whole plateau: a drawn-out note that makes the ribbon towers tremble from their roots upward. It comes from chambers beneath the rock, where water enters through narrow cracks and forces airless pockets of mineral gas to contract and expand. Sometimes several chambers answer one another for hours.

On a low shelf stands a scatter of small stone structures. Most are rings no wider than a room, built from slabs leaned together without mortar. A few retain roofs of overlapping rock, weighted by rounded boulders. Within them lie shallow clay bowls and lengths of cord preserved under mineral crust. The cord was braided from the same tough ribbons that grow across the plateau. No path joins the structures; silt has buried whatever marks once led between them.

Near one ring, a row of upright stones crosses the current. Their upstream faces are polished, while on the sheltered sides small colonies have taken hold. The stones were set at different heights, and when the deeper note comes through the ground, each casts a momentary shadow in the water. For an instant the shadows point against the flow. Then the sound fades, the water carries them downstream again, and the plateau returns to its long, dark movement.
