The water is black almost everywhere. Only near the upper surface, far above the plain, is there a weak blue thinning that comes and goes with the seasons. Down here the cold is steady, a degree or two above freezing, and the pressure has pressed every loose thing into place.

The bottom is nearly level. Over distances too great to see across, it rises and falls by only a few metres: long swells, shallow basins, the occasional low ridge. There are no bare rocks. The ground is a pavement of small fitted blocks, most of them rounded until their joints are difficult to find. Some are baked clay, some stone. Between them lies a soft dark grit made from the same materials, worn down and settled over an immense span of time.

The pavement continues beneath the silt, beneath the beds of growth, beneath the collapsed banks that divide one broad basin from another. Where a current has scoured a channel, the sides show courses of blocks laid one upon the next. There is no exposed earth under them. Even the ridges are built: long walls that have slumped outward until they resemble natural rises, their tops buried under generations of sediment.

The people who laid them left no sharp edges. Perhaps the edges were worn away long ago. The blocks were cut with simple tools, carried here, and fitted by hand; in sheltered places one can still feel the shallow parallel marks where a harder stone was dragged across a softer one. The walls run farther than any single settlement would have needed. They cross the submerged plain for hundreds of kilometres, bend around old depressions, meet and part again. Much of the pattern has been lost under deposits, though sometimes a straight line reappears after a long absence.

Growth covers nearly everything. Broad, dark fronds lie along the pavement and lift a little when the water moves. Fine mats fill the joints, and pale branching films spread over the undersides of the blocks. Near the low ridges, stems grow close together in groves, their flattened blades hanging limp in the dark water. They are fed not by sunlight here but by mineral-rich seepage and the work of microbes living in their tissues. The seepage is weak; the growth is slow. A stem may stand for centuries before it folds down and becomes part of the bed beneath it.

In a few places, where the floor rises closer to the faint light above, the vegetation is greener and more delicate. Long ribbons reach upward through the water, with other ribbons tangled around their bases. The uppermost tips catch what light there is. Below them, dead leaves collect in thick layers, slowly broken down by bacterial films.

There are almost no animals. Occasionally a tiny crustacean can be found in the folds of a leaf, or a few translucent worms among the grains of the pavement, but large stretches contain nothing that swims or crawls. The plants and their microbial companions occupy the space instead, layered from the joints of the masonry to the high, sparse canopy.

The water makes little sound. There is a faint rubbing where fronds touch, and sometimes a soft knock as a loosened block settles against another. Far away, a current moves along one of the old walls, carrying cold water from one basin to the next. It takes days for the current to strengthen, and longer for it to fall quiet again.

The walls are still coming apart. A grain leaves an edge; a thin slab slips into a joint; a mat grows over the exposed surface. In the shallow channels the grit travels a little way before settling. Elsewhere it remains undisturbed, gathering over the work of the builders until only the gentlest change in the level of the floor shows where a wall once stood.
