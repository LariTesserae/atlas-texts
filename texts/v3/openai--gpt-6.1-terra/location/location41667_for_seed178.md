The valley is low and broad enough that, from its northern rim, the far side is only a darker line against the clouds. Most of the year it lies below freezing. Tonight rain is falling into snow, and a wind off the inland ice drives both nearly horizontal.

The sound is immense. It comes first from the wind passing through millions of stiff reed stems, then from the reeds striking one another, then from the black water battering the banks beneath them. Loose ice plates collide in the channels with a noise like freight wagons being coupled. Every few minutes a bank gives way, and the rush of water into the new opening swells the rest into a roar. A person can shout at arm’s length and be heard only as a change in the air.

The marsh is lush despite the cold. During the long summer light, sedges and dwarf willows grow rapidly over the saturated ground; now their leaves are gone, but their red and brown stems stand shoulder-high, woven with moss and the dry heads of flowering plants. Thick mats of crowberry cover the firmer rises. Under the snow there are green rosettes pressed flat against the soil, and in sheltered hollows ferns remain soft beneath a crust of ice. The vegetation bends and springs back in great, uneven waves as gusts cross the valley.

Water is everywhere without forming a single lake. It runs in narrow black cuts, wells up around stones, spreads over frozen peat and disappears beneath it. The ground is young here: a glacier withdrew only recently, leaving angular ridges of gravel and raw faces of blue clay. Streams are still choosing their courses through them. One channel may be choked with broken ice by morning, sending its water across a willow thicket and tearing a fresh route to the sea.

Animals keep moving through all of it. Small pale deer pick their way along the gravel crests, heads down against the wind. Beneath the reeds, water voles use tunnels roofed with snow and vegetation; when a tunnel floods, they scramble through the stems to another. Flocks of dark birds rise suddenly from the marsh and vanish again a few yards away. Their calls are lost, but the frantic flicker of their wings is visible whenever lightning passes behind the cloud.

There are a few buildings, widely spaced: low shelters anchored into the gravel, their roofs steep enough to shed ice. Fine black cables run from them into the marsh and vanish under the roots. They connect to a flood-control system mostly buried beneath the valley, a network of tiny machines that can sense shifting pressure through the ground and alter the strength of its anchoring mesh before a bank collapses. It does not stop the marsh from changing. It keeps the changes away from the shelters and the narrow raised paths between them.

One shelter has a light on. Through its window, someone is fastening a hood and waiting for a lull. The walls tremble around them. Outside, the reeds thrash, water climbs over a frozen lip, and an entire stand of willow slowly tips into the new current.
