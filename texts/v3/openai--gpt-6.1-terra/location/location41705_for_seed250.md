The highest ground lies far below the surface.

It is a plateau still coming into being: a continent-wide rise of dark, warm mineral, its summits smooth and rounded because they have only just emerged from the deep. Long seams brighten across it, open, and seal again. New ridges lift slowly through the water, their crests pale as milk, then darken as their skins harden. Little has had time to wear away. There are no valleys cut by rivers, no ancient sediment beds, only folds and blisters and the occasional great dome of rock swelling under its own pressure.

Above the plateau is an immense, clear ocean. Daylight reaches surprisingly far into it, falling in broad, wavering bands. From the high ridges the surface is visible as a silver ceiling, although it is many kilometers away. Across the lower slopes it disappears, and the water becomes a deep blue distance, bright enough to see by but impossible to judge. There is very little in that distance. No fish pass through it, no drifting animals, no shells or bones. A current may carry a few pale grains from a fresh seam, but they soon settle or dissolve.

The water is cool. Near the newborn ridges, warmth rises in transparent columns that twist and break apart. Cold water pours down around them, gathering speed over distances too large to see at once. The currents meet, recoil, and meet again. They raise underwater storms: vast rotating masses whose edges flicker with sunlight. Sometimes a whole slope vanishes behind a moving wall of mineral haze; an hour later it is clear again, the haze drawn out into a ribbon hundreds of kilometers long.

The noise is continuous. Rock groans as it lifts. Seams crack with sharp reports that travel through the plateau and return from remote ridges. The currents make a low, enormous rushing sound, and where warm water meets cold there is a fine, persistent hiss. Occasionally a deep pulse passes through everything, strong enough to tremble in the bones of the place. For a few moments the smaller sounds seem to gather around it; then they scatter again.

Vegetation is scarce and stays close to the ground. Thin green films occupy sheltered hollows on some of the older domes. Elsewhere, single fronds rise from cracks, long and nearly transparent, bending flat when a current passes and slowly lifting afterward. They grow toward the brighter water, though the light moves too restlessly for them ever to face it for long. New rock often pushes up beneath them. A green patch may be carried upward intact, split across a widening seam, or buried by a fresh flow of mineral.

The water has an order that becomes apparent only at great distances. Pressure waves arriving from opposite sides of the ocean sometimes meet without making the expected collision. They pass through one another and leave behind a region of perfect stillness, bounded by a surface too thin to see. Inside it, grains hang motionless and the loud rush of the ocean drops away. The region can last for days, moving slowly across the plateau, before it opens and releases everything it held.

Similar arrangements occur in the light. Bright bands divide around certain empty volumes and rejoin on the other side, as if the water had learned to conduct them along chosen paths. Warm columns occasionally bend in concert, miles apart, responding to a change that has not yet reached them by any ordinary current. The sea seems to perform calculations in its own shifting substance, using pressures and temperatures so finely arranged that no apparatus can be found. Whether this was given to the water or emerged with it is impossible to tell. There are no ruins here, no instruments, no marks of a hand.

At the edge of the rising plateau, the ground falls away in broad terraces, each younger than the one above. The newest is still soft in places. Water wells through it, carrying a faint mineral brightness, and the terrace expands into the open ocean by degrees. Far below, something vast presses upward. The slope shudders; a line of pale beads appears along its face and joins into a glowing seam.

Then the seam opens. A slow cloud rolls out, lit from above, and the sound reaches the high ridges long before the new rock does.
