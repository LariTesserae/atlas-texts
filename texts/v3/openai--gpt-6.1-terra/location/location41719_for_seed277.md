The plateau is high enough that the air feels thin in the chest, though it is warm against the skin. It goes on beyond any useful measure of distance: a country of shallow basins and long, worn ridges, with escarpments so gradual that their far edges vanish into darkness before their slopes can be seen.

There is almost no sky. A deep layer of cloud hangs over the plateau throughout the year, admitting only a faint grayness around what might be midday. Most of the time the land is known by touch and sound: the gritty crust underfoot, a warmer current of air rising from a fissure, the little clicks of stones cooling after rain.

Water arrives without much ceremony. Condensation gathers on the ridges and runs down into pockets of black gravel. Occasionally a warm rain crosses the plateau, leaving the rock dark and slick for a few hours. In the deepest basins, water remains hidden beneath the surface, held in beds of porous stone. The ground there yields slightly under pressure, then firms again as the water moves away.

Plants grow far apart. Low, pale mats occupy the sheltered sides of stones; in the dampest hollows, leafless stalks rise in loose circles, their waxy skins collecting moisture from the air. Their roots descend much farther than their height suggests. Some reach into warm mineral seams and draw up bitter salts, which crust around the bases of the plants and are eaten by animals during the dry intervals.

The animals are numerous, although the vastness leaves plenty of room between them. On a single ridge there may be only a few: a low browser moving slowly from one mat to the next, a cluster of small burrowers beneath a stone, a long-bodied hunter lying still enough to be mistaken for a fold in the ground. Across the plateau there are millions. Most travel by scent, pressure and changes in the local electric field. Eyes are small or absent. Wings, where they occur, are broad and soft-edged; an animal can pass overhead with scarcely more sound than a sleeve drawn over cloth.

Under the plateau, heat and groundwater work through thick layers of iron-bearing rock. The reactions are steady and immense. Warm brine rises, cools, sinks and rises again through different channels, depositing metals in branching seams. Electrical currents pass through these seams and into the damp ground. The process has been going on for so long, and on such a scale, that the animals have made it part of their lives. Burrowers line their chambers with conductive stones and orient their nests to the current. The taller hunters can feel a herd moving through the ground from a considerable distance.

There is something industrial about the place: the circulation of heated fluid, the sorting and concentration of minerals, the continuous electrical work carried on beneath the dark. Yet there are no buildings or machines, no roads or discarded parts. The workings belong to the rock and to the creatures living within it. A seam may shift after an earthquake, and whole populations gradually follow it, leaving old burrows empty and digging new ones over generations.

The plateau is mostly quiet. From time to time a ridge gives a deep, brief hum as water enters a hot fracture. Far away, a herd crosses loose shale with a sound like dry rain. Near a pool, small animals scrape the salt from stems, pausing whenever a shadow moves across the faint light. By morning the pool may have drained underground, but its damp margin will remain busy for days.
