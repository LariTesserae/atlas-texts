The high country begins without a climb. For days the ground has been rising so gently that the change is felt only in the thinner breath and the broadening distance. Then the mist opens, and the land is already above the clouds.

It is an immense plateau, worn almost level by ages of rain. What once stood as mountains has been reduced to long, low swells; what were valleys are shallow basins, scarcely deeper than the thickness of the vegetation that fills them. The horizon is never a clean line. It is a dark green unevenness, soft with foliage, from which pale weather continually forms and drifts away.

Nearly everything underfoot is alive. The surface yields like firm, wet felt: layers of moss, root, old leaf and pale woven stems, grown over one another until it is impossible to say where soil begins. In places the mat lifts slightly when stepped on, then settles again with a slow seep of water around the ankle. Elsewhere it has hardened into ridges that ring when tapped, though their interiors are still green and growing. Ancient trunks lie beneath these ridges, not dead exactly, but incorporated: their wood sends up fresh leaves through seams in the moss.

The plants do not all reach for the sky. Many spread outward, making roofs a hand’s breadth above other roofs. Wide leaves overlap like scales; fine, hairlike fronds hang from their undersides; small cups collect the water that passes through. Above them rise sparse stands of branching growth, neither quite tree nor fern, with trunks braided from hundreds of supple stems. Their crowns are broad and thin. They admit a little light, but the light has already traveled through cloud and mist and comes down greenish and dim.

Warmth gathers under the leaves. Water is always close, though open pools are uncommon. It lies in the living ground, in the folded bases of plants, in long clear bladders suspended from branches. When the air cools even slightly, the whole plateau begins to condense. Drops form along every edge and fall at intervals so widely spaced that no one shower can be heard, only the occasional soft strike of water on a leaf below.

There are animals everywhere, once the eye learns to separate them from the growth. Small grazing creatures move inside the lower foliage, their rounded backs carrying gardens of moss. Long, jointed things cling to the undersides of leaves and strain drifting spores from the air. At dusk, translucent bodies rise from the wet ground and hang motionless between the trunks, opening and closing their many fins. The larger animals are seldom seen whole. A deep back passes through the growth a hundred paces away; hours later, the leaves are still gradually closing behind it.

The place is mostly quiet. Wings make little noise in the thick air, and even the grazers have soft mouths. Now and then a low call travels across the plateau, so drawn out that it seems at first to be the sound of the land settling. Other calls answer from very far away. Rain clouds build over the plain itself, their bases brushing the highest crowns, but storms here are slow affairs. They may spend a day gathering and another day releasing a warm, fine rain.

Scattered across the living surface are a few small chambers. Their walls are made of bent stems, trained together while growing, and their roofs have thickened into continuous sheets of leaf. Some are occupied by colonies of pale animals that sleep in close-packed rows. Others stand empty, their entrances narrowed almost shut by new growth. Beside one, a path remains visible for several lengths: a darker strip where generations of feet once pressed the moss flat. It disappears beneath an old root and does not emerge on the other side.

Nothing here is entirely still, but most change happens at the pace of plants. A basin fills over centuries. A low ridge dissolves into a wet meadow. The living mat extends across a pool, first as a mesh of fine threads, then as a floating floor strong enough to bear the grazers. Far below it, clear water remains, inhabited by creatures that have never come into the air.

From a slight rise, the plateau can be seen going on and on: folds of dark vegetation, strips of silver mist, clouds casting slow shadows over the crowns. There is no obvious edge. The land has had so long to wear down that height is no longer a matter of cliffs or peaks, but of the whole vast surface held quietly above the lower world.
