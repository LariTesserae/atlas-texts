The land is almost level. From one horizon to the other it has been worn down to a dark, warm pavement, interrupted by shallow basins and ridges so low that a person might walk for hours before realizing they have climbed one. There are no mountains visible, though some of the broad swells in the distance may once have been mountain ranges. Water stands everywhere: in black films on the stone, in closely packed pools, in seams that open underfoot and close again after the weight has passed.

The sky is low and dim, the color of tarnished metal. Rain falls from it, but not always downward. Long veils sweep sideways over the plain; droplets rise from the pools to meet them, and for a moment the air is crowded with water traveling in both directions. A warm mist forms wherever the currents cross. It carries the smell of wet clay and something sharper, like a stone split open.

The noise is immense. It comes from the rain striking water, from water striking stone, from a continuous grinding far below the surface. There is thunder too, though it seldom arrives as a distinct crack. It rolls through the ground and the air together, until it is difficult to tell whether one is hearing it or feeling it in the bones. Across the flat distances, sounds do not fade cleanly. A peal of thunder may return an hour later from the opposite horizon, worn thin but still loud enough to make the pools shiver.

Very little grows above the waterline. Here and there, a tuft of stiff gray blades grips a crack, leaning almost flat beneath the wind. Broad patches of pale crust cover the rock just below the water; when a pool drains, they contract into dark, wrinkled islands. Tiny bubbles emerge from them in the heat. A few jointed animals live among the cracks, flattened and nearly transparent, feeding on the crust and retreating beneath it when the water begins to move too quickly. Their trails are easy to find in the soft mineral film, but the animals themselves are not.

Everything else seems crowded. Rain fills the air, ripples cover the pools, and the rock is crossed by so many fine seams that there is hardly an unbroken span as wide as a hand. The water presses upward through them even while rain presses down. Sheets of runoff meet, buckle, and climb over one another. A shallow basin can become a furious whirlpool without acquiring any apparent depth. It spins itself dry, exposing a floor of smooth black nodules, then fills again before the nodules have lost their shine.

The oldest surfaces are also the most restless. A bank of soft stone may peel away in layers and stream across the plain like wet paper. A ridge may dissolve into gravel during a single storm. Yet when the weather clears enough to see, the long outlines of the land are still there. The same low swells rise from the same great flats; the same shallow basins hold their water. Nothing stands high enough to be broken for long.

At intervals there are structures, if that is what they are: rings of stone half submerged in the shallows, or a narrow wall running a short distance before it sinks beneath the water. Their stones are worn to the same smoothness as the ground around them. Some rings are large enough to stand inside. Others contain only a patch of bare rock where the rain seems reluctant to fall. There are no paths between them, and no signs of what brought the stones together.

The plain is large enough to make its own weather several times over. Far away, under a darker band of cloud, water can be seen lifting in immense columns. Elsewhere the rain has stopped and a dull light lies on thousands of pools at once. The light moves toward you across the flats, touching each surface in turn, but the cloud opening responsible for it is already behind you. If you wait, the bright water may arrive before the patch of clear sky, or the clear sky may pass overhead without bringing any brightness at all.

Distances are unreliable in smaller ways. A low ridge can remain the same size through a long walk toward it, then suddenly be close enough to touch. A pool crossed by a falling drop may show the splash twice: once where the drop lands and once near the far bank, a breath earlier. Such things happen often enough that the animals react to them. When a patch of water begins to show an extra set of ripples, the little creatures withdraw into their cracks. Soon afterward, whether a storm arrives or has just gone, the surface usually becomes violent.

At the edge of one basin, a thin stand of gray vegetation survives in the lee of a stone wall. The stems knock together in the wind, too softly to be heard beneath the roar. Water pours through the wall’s gaps, eroding narrow channels in the mud behind it. Later the channels may run the other way. The plants hold on through both, their roots gripping the old ground while the water continually rearranges everything loose upon it.
