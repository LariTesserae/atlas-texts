The plain is made of blocks. They run from the shallow basin at its center to the dark horizon, fitted so closely that in some places a person could walk for an hour without seeing a seam. Elsewhere the seams are wide enough to swallow a foot, and the blocks rise at different angles, as if the ground were still being assembled from below.

There is no soil under them. A broken edge reveals more blocks: pale ones packed with crushed shell, black ones baked from ash, soft gray ones that can still be marked with a thumbnail. The newest are warm for a little while after they are laid. Far out on the plain, gangs of small figures carry them on woven slings and set them into place with wooden levers. They work without visible fires. Now and then a whole section lifts, knocks against its neighbors, and settles with a noise like a door slammed in a cavern.

The basin is not deep, but the land around it rises enough to hold the weather. Most days are dark and cool. A dimness gathers overhead without showing where the sky ends, and moisture arrives as a fine, slanting rain that may last only a few minutes. It beads on the hard surfaces and runs into the joints. In hollows left for that purpose, water collects in shallow pans of hammered clay. There it stays for a day or two, trembling, until someone comes to bail it out or the blocks beneath it drink it away.

Nothing green grows here. The blocks wear no moss, and the water pans are clean except for grit. Dampness has its own inhabitants, though: thin colonies of translucent film that appear along sheltered joints, spread for a hand’s breadth, and vanish when the stone dries. They are not quite still. Given time, their edges gather around droplets and draw them in.

The noise is nearly constant. Blocks strike blocks. Mallets beat wedges into place. Slings creak, water ticks from ledges, and the many narrow passages between raised sections turn every gust into a low, crowded roar. Sounds travel farther than their sources can be seen. A single falling block may be heard three times, each report arriving from a different direction. The workers stop and listen when that happens; after the third report, one of them usually goes to inspect a seam.

There are animals, though it is possible to cross a large part of the plain without meeting one. The most often seen is a long, pale creature with six padded feet and a blunt head, which comes out after rain to drink from the pans. It moves carefully over the joints, testing each block before it puts its weight down. The workers leave it alone. Sometimes one turns an empty pan over for it, and the creature waits beside the pan until moisture condenses on the underside.

At the basin’s edge, the workmanship becomes crowded and elaborate. Low walls overlap without quite meeting. Ramps climb onto platforms too small to stand on, and rows of clay pegs mark lines that will later be filled with blocks. There are houses among these works, or structures with enough room inside for a few bodies to rest, though their thresholds are built high against water and their roofs are weighted with stones. A person passing through must turn sideways often, ducking under slings and stepping over tools laid ready for the next shift.

The plain gives an impression not of ruin but of incompleteness. Corners remain sharp. Fresh chisel marks catch what little light there is. Places where a wall has failed are not smoothed by weather; they are promptly crowded with hands, braces, and new material. Even the horizon changes slowly as another course is raised.

When the rain comes, work does not stop. The slings darken, the mallets grow slick, and every hollow begins to speak in drips. For a short time the pale drinking animals appear between the crews. Then the shower passes, the workers lift the brimming pans and pour them into the waiting joints, and the ground answers with a succession of close, heavy knocks.
