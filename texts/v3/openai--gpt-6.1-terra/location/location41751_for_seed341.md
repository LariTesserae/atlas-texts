The district occupies a shallow bowl no larger than a town square. Beyond its rim, warehouses stand wall to wall, but down here the buildings leave room between themselves: a pumping station, a row of brick workshops, a cooling tower with its upper half removed. Their foundations sit in black water. Narrow iron walkways connect their doors, and most afternoons the water rises high enough to lap through the grating.

It is hot under the low cloud. Steam drifts from the pumping station and catches beneath a canopy of broad-leaved trees growing out of the flooded streets. The trees are widely spaced, each with a dense crown; between them lie open stretches of water, tiled pavement and rust-colored mud. Vines hang from the factory windows. Ferns have filled the gutters to their edges, and thick green moss follows the seams of the brickwork wherever condensation runs.

The district is loud. Pumps hammer in uneven sequence below the waterline, and a loose sheet of metal on the cooling tower booms whenever the exhaust fans start. Rain adds a finer, continuous clatter, interrupted by heavy splashes from leaves that have held their fill. There are no birds in the trees, no insects at the lamps, no fish stirring the flooded streets. The leaves shake with falling water and the vibration of machinery.

The brick has been rounded by a long succession of floods. At the corners of the workshops, the mortar has worn away in scallops, and pale mineral bands mark levels the water no longer reaches. Every few hours the pumps draw it down to reveal the old paving: blue-gray blocks laid in careful fans around the drains. Then warm water seeps back through those same drains, and the pattern disappears.

At the center stands a small electrical substation behind a fence furred with moss. Its transformers give off a low, powerful hum. When the pumps stop, the hum rises, and drops of condensation lift from the metal cases and hang trembling in the air. They remain there until the pumps start again; then they fall all at once, tapping the roofs and water around the substation like a brief, heavy shower.

People tend the machinery from the covered walkways. Their lamps move dimly through the steam, pausing at gauges and valves, then passing on. They prune the vines back from the vents and clear the drains when fallen leaves collect over them. By the next shift, water is already threading down the exposed brick, and fresh tendrils have begun to curl around the railings.
