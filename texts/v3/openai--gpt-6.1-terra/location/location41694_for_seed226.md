At the top of a long ascent, a small city occupies a hollow in the plateau. It is no larger than a hamlet, though its streets, cisterns and terraces have been laid out with the care of a much greater place. From the rim you can see nearly all of it: pale walls stepping down toward an open square, dark green roofs, and beyond them the bare lip of the high country.

The hollow is new. Its stone has not yet had time to soften into rounded forms. Fresh slabs stand at slight angles, their edges sharp and their faces marked with the faint folds of their making. In places, seams in the ground slowly open and fill with a pale mineral paste. The builders have set wicker frames around these seams; after some years the paste hardens, and a wall is raised without being quarried. Several unfinished walls stand in this way, their tops still damp and granular.

There is water close beneath everything. It gathers in the seams, beads on the stone, and runs in shallow channels alongside the streets. After rain, the square becomes a single reflective sheet, though it is never deep enough to cover the paving. The air is cool even in full daylight. Clouds pass low over the rim, and droplets form on the undersides of leaves long after the rain has gone.

Plants grow densely wherever they can take hold. Moss cushions the joints between slabs. Ferns crowd the terraces, and small, tough trees spread over the roofs, their roots guided through clay pipes toward the cisterns. The vegetation leaves the streets remarkably clear. Standing in the square, one has a sense of open volume: walls and greenery around the edges, a great deal of unoccupied air between them.

The place is loud. Wind enters through notches in the plateau and sounds the long clay gutters, producing a layered, sustained call. Water drums inside covered drains, amplified by the hollow spaces beneath the paving. Neither sound changes quickly. A single note may hold for an hour, with only a slight shivering at its edges.

Few animals live here. Occasionally a pale moth rests beneath a fern, or a small bird comes down to drink from a channel and leaves at once. The residents are seldom visible either, though there are signs of their work: stone mallets under a shelter, bundles of reed, a fresh wicker frame waiting beside a seam. They build slowly, following the pace at which the ground offers its material. A street may end at a low wet ridge for a generation before the ridge becomes firm enough to carry the next row of houses.
