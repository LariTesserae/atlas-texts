The plateau begins above the weather. From its rim the land falls away in immense, softened steps, each ledge wider than a town, each cliff worn back to a long, patient slope. The heights are extreme, but the high country itself is nearly level: an ancient surface lifted so far into the sky that distant clouds drift below its edge. In the afternoon the sun warms it gently. At night, moisture gathers on everything and is gone again soon after dawn.

Almost the whole plateau is plant. A springy mat of intertwined roots and stems covers the old stone, interrupted by shallow troughs where pale sedges grow and by low ridges of densely packed, woody moss. Some of the ridges are hollow. A person can crawl into them and find an interior like a vaulted cellar, cool and dry under the green roof. Elsewhere the mat bridges deep fissures without quite concealing them. Its surface dips toward the darkness; fine roots hang through the gaps, moving in air that rises from far below.

There are few trees. Those that survive grow flat against the ground, their trunks branching sideways into broad, leaf-covered fans. Between them the plateau feels extraordinarily open. You can see for miles over a living floor no higher than your knee, with only the occasional standing rock or woven shelter breaking the horizon. The air above it is mostly empty, except at certain hours when vast swarms rise.

The animals are everywhere else. Beetles press through the root mat in glittering streams; small grazing creatures travel beneath its surface, lifting it in long, rolling seams. Birds nest in the fissures, and pale, many-jointed hunters wait at their mouths. At dusk, moths come up from the sedges in such numbers that their wings throw a moving shadow over the ground. The hunters climb out after them. Birds burst upward, turn, and plunge back among the moths, while far below the plateau edge, larger shapes wheel in the warm air.

The noise begins before sunrise and seldom stops. Countless insects rasp against the dry stems. Nesting birds shriek from the cracks, and the hollows beneath the vegetation give their calls a deep, booming resonance. When a swarm rises, the sound changes to a sustained roar. During a rain shower it becomes louder still: drops strike millions of taut leaves, and the animals answer from under cover. Two people standing together must put their mouths close to each other’s ears to speak.

Rain comes briefly, usually from clouds climbing the plateau walls. Water runs along the roots into stone basins and narrow fissures; little remains on the surface by noon. Around the basins are the scattered signs of visitors: circles of laid stone, peeled poles propped into small shelters, a few clay vessels plugged with grass. The shelters have low entrances and thick roofs of cut sod, perhaps to keep out the sound as much as the weather.

By evening the plant mat has dried to a soft silver-green. Animals continue moving beneath it, and now and then a patch of ground trembles for several minutes before settling. Along the rim, the last sunlight reaches down through the haze to the country below, while the plateau goes on rattling and calling above it.
