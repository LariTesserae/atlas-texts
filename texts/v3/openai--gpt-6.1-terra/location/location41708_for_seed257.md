High on a drowned plateau is a chamber no larger than a village square. It has no roof, though the water above it is so deep and dark that the distinction is hard to feel. Its walls are made of fitted stone, curving inward in slow, uneven steps. The floor is paved too. Even the low ridges crossing it were laid by hand, each one a long row of blocks worn almost flat.

The water is warm enough that a body could remain in it without shivering. It passes through the chamber so slowly that the finest grains take days to settle. Over the paving lie drifts of pale silt, softening the corners of the stones. No leaves or roots have ever caught between them. The walls carry only a thin, colorless film of microbes, fed by minerals seeping from the old mortar.

Animals gather wherever the blocks meet. Small blind crustaceans crowd the seams, their jointed legs working over one another with extraordinary patience. Snails with thick, chalky shells graze the microbial film. In the deeper joints, worms extend translucent crowns into the water and draw them back when a neighbor brushes past. Larger creatures rest in the hollows: broad, flattened animals with folded limbs and mouths underneath, so still that the silt settles on them. Every so often one lifts itself and advances a few inches, leaving a clean impression of its body behind.

There is almost no light. Now and then a faint glimmer travels through the water as a disturbed animal closes its luminous organs, and for a moment the nearest stones appear: their rounded edges, their tool marks, the small shells packed into their seams. Then the glimmer is gone. Sound travels no farther than a delicate scraping, a shell touching stone, the muted crack of a crustacean working at its food.

The chamber was made to hold something. Along one wall are the remains of a gate, its wooden bars long since gone, its stone sockets enlarged by ages of gentle movement. Beyond it, a passage slopes upward and narrows until even the larger animals cannot enter. Fine particles continue to emerge from that opening, bringing food from some unseen place higher in the drowned structure.

The stones are old beyond any surviving purpose. Their surfaces have been rubbed smooth by water, silt, and generations of grazing mouths. Yet their joints still hold. In the darkness, the animals move among them, and the chamber remains full.
