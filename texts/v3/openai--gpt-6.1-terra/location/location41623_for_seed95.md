The highland is broad enough that its far edge is usually lost in haze. From below it must appear mountainous, but on top there are no peaks: only a warm, ancient surface worn almost level, with shallow rises of dark stone and long basins whose rims have softened into the surrounding ground. Even the fractures are rounded. The rock seems to have spent so long exposed that it has forgotten how to break sharply.

Moisture arrives without much weather. At dawn, condensation gathers beneath the lips of stones and in the hollows of old erosion channels. Some mornings a thin rain passes over the plateau, leaving the ground dark for an hour. The basins hold water longer, though most are no more than damp at their centers, where a mineral crust gives slightly underfoot.

Plants grow far apart: low gray cushions rooted in cracks, a few thorny stems, mats of tiny leaves that close against the heat. The distances between them look barren until they are examined closely.

The animals are everywhere. Small, pale creatures lie packed beneath the loose flakes of rock, their bodies pressed together so neatly that a lifted slab reveals what looks at first like a single quilted surface. Minute shelled things occupy the damp seams. Broader, soft-bodied grazers rest in the shade of the plant cushions, scarcely moving, while translucent fliers cling in layers to the undersides of overhangs.

They make very little sound. Now and then comes a dry ticking as a shell turns against stone, or the faint brushing of wings settling after a short flight. When a shadow passes over a crowded patch, thousands of bodies contract. The movement travels across the ground as a darkening, then stops. Nothing scatters.

The largest animals are flattened and many-lobed, the color of the plateau. At a distance they resemble patches of weathered stone. They spend much of their lives still, taking in warmth and grazing on the thin films that grow across the rock. Smaller creatures shelter beneath their edges. After a rain, the lobes lift just enough to let the water run inward, and a dark stain spreads slowly through each body.

Here and there, low domes interrupt the open ground. They are made of shed skins and mineral dust, bound together by colonies of burrowing animals. Some are abandoned and have slumped into rings; others are warm and faintly breathing, their narrow entrances crowded with waiting bodies. They are the nearest things to buildings on the plateau, though they rise and settle over generations without ever becoming permanent.

The air smells of warm stone and, in the damp places, something faintly sweet. Wind crosses the highland often enough, but it has little to catch. It moves a thorn branch, passes over the domes, and leaves the packed creatures almost undisturbed.

Toward evening, the light brings out shallow paths in the rock: not tracks, exactly, but routes worn by countless slow crossings between shelter and moisture. They join and divide across the basins and continue beyond sight. By morning, many are dark with animals making their way along them, so gradually that the plateau appears still unless one chooses a single creature and waits.
