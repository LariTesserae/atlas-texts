The plateau is warm even in the thin air. It lies above a ring of lower valleys, a broad expanse of pale stone worn into shallow basins and long, sloping shelves. From a distance it looks almost level; walking across it, you are continually descending into old channels and climbing out again. Their sides are rounded and fluted by weather that has had a very long time to work.

Rain reaches the plateau only in passing. It gathers for an hour against the higher rim, drops in widely spaced, heavy beads, and moves on. Much of the water disappears into seams in the rock. Some remains in polished hollows, where a dark skin of mineral dust breaks apart to reveal clear water beneath. At dawn, condensation gathers on the undersides of stones and along the edges of old metal. By noon the exposed surfaces are dry and bright enough to make the eyes ache.

Plants grow far apart. There are low grey shrubs with leaves no larger than grains of rice, mats of red lichen in the shelter of ledges, and here and there a pale, jointed stem rising from a crack. After rain, tiny yellow flowers open along the stems. They last until the next hot afternoon, though their seeds stay lodged in the stone for years.

Animals are easier to find than plants. Small, long-legged grazers move between the basins in loose bands, browsing on lichen and whatever has emerged after rain. Their coats are the colour of dust, but their ears are almost translucent, and when they turn toward the sun their heads seem briefly edged in amber. They feed without hurry, stopping often to watch one another. If one starts to run, the others follow with very little noise: their broad feet settle softly, then lift a faint powder from the stone.

There are thousands of smaller lives in the cracks. Scale-backed insects come out to drink from the shaded sides of pebbles. Narrow, bright-eyed hunters wait at the mouths of holes. Flocks of winged creatures cross the open ground close to the surface, their wings flexing so slowly that they seem to be swimming through the air. They make no calls that carry. Once in a while a shadow passes over a basin and all the grazers lower themselves at once. Something high above is hunting, though against the brightness it is difficult to see.

The quiet is not stillness. Water travels underground. Insects gather and scatter as the shadow of a stone moves. A bank of cloud builds over the rim and dissolves before reaching the centre. The loudest sound for long stretches may be the tick of a cooling rock, or the soft scrape of an animal turning it over to find moisture beneath.

Scattered across the plateau are structures too slight to serve as ordinary shelters: a curved wall, a pair of white posts, a low ring set flush with the ground. Their surfaces have weathered along with the surrounding stone, and lichen grows on some of them. Others remain perfectly smooth, though sand has drifted against them and been blown away again many times.

Near each ring, the air holds water a little longer. On clear mornings, threads of dew hang unsupported above the rock, joining and separating as the temperature changes. When enough has gathered, the threads draw together into a bead and fall. Animals visit these places but do not linger around them; water is found elsewhere too, in the shaded hollows and deep fissures.

What powers the rings is not visible. A hand placed inside one feels the same dry heat as the surrounding ground, yet the light beyond it arrives a fraction late. A moving creature seems to pause there, for less than a blink, while its body continues onward. No boundary can be touched, and a tossed stone passes through without changing course.

Toward evening the plateau turns gold, then a muted violet. The grazers move into the channels, where the last warmth is held in the walls. Small creatures emerge around them, picking through their tracks. High on the rim, clouds begin to gather again, and for a while their shadows travel slowly across the whole open basin.
