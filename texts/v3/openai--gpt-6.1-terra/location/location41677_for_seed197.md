The town occupies a hollow no wider than a small grove. From above, it looks like a shallow green bowl set in a low, warm plain. Its rim is a continuous growth of leaves, stems and knotted root. There is no soil visible, and almost nothing rises from the floor of the hollow. You can see all the way across it.

The floor is a single living surface, pale where it is stretched thin, dark where old folds have thickened. It feels springy underfoot. Long ago, running water cut branching channels into it; now the channels have healed into grooves, with soft lips that close and open as the surface swells. Most are dry. After rain, a little water travels through them, dividing and rejoining until it collects in shallow pockets along the rim. By the following afternoon the pockets have usually vanished, taken in through the floor or lifted into the warm air.

The town’s buildings grow from the rim, facing inward. They have fronts, entrances and upper rooms, but no joints or seams. Each is part of the next: one enormous, branching organism has folded itself into a street of separate houses. Doorways are puckered openings that remain wide during the day and narrow after dusk. Inside, living partitions curve around empty chambers. Old passages run between them, polished smooth by generations of small bodies. Roofs have slumped and healed and slumped again, so that their outlines are rounded and settled, as if the whole town had been worn by weather.

Nothing has been built here with tools. The streets and rooms are nevertheless exact. A root crossing the wrong passage will draw back over the course of a season; a doorway that receives too little traffic gradually closes. The town keeps the form of a town by responding to its inhabitants.

The inhabitants are extraordinarily numerous, though the hollow itself seems empty. They live in the walls: pale climbing creatures with jointed feet, plump leaf-coloured grazers, thin larvae packed into channels, and small warm-bodied animals that sleep in chambers just beneath the surface. They come out at different hours. Sometimes a whole façade ripples as thousands move behind it, while only one or two are visible in the open.

All of them make noise.

The sound is deafening even before the first creatures emerge. It comes through the floor and down from the leaves: shrieks, rattles, booming pulses, the scraping of innumerable mouths. The air has a continuous edge of vibration. A person crossing the hollow would feel it in the teeth and ribs; speech would be impossible, and even a cry close to the ear would disappear. When a large predator enters one of the wall passages, the noise around it changes pitch. That change races through the town far faster than the predator can move, and every exit ahead of it fills with fleeing bodies.

At certain intervals the whole green bowl gives a deep, resonant call. The animals continue their own noise through it. The call presses moisture out of the inner walls, so that the houses briefly shine with condensation. Droplets run down their faces and gather in the grooves. On dry days this is enough to feed the shallow pools; on rainy days the grooves brim and the entrances nearest the floor tighten shut.

The sound also alters distance. During the deep call, a creature can cross the open floor in three steps and return by the same path in thirty. A doorway may appear close enough to touch while the wall around it remains on the far side of the hollow. The animals seem accustomed to this. They pause when the call begins, feel the floor with their feet, and choose their direction only after the distance has settled.

There is ordinary daylight above the rim: moving clouds, broad leaves, the occasional brief rain. Down in the town, shadows arrive a little before the things that cast them. In late afternoon the empty floor darkens with the silhouettes of creatures still hidden in their rooms. Then, one after another, they emerge into the places already marked for them.

By evening the walls are busy. Grazers pass along the living street, predators work through the higher chambers, and winged animals pour out over the rim to feed. The floor remains almost bare. Its grooves close slowly as the remaining water is absorbed, and the houses draw their entrances inward. Through all of it the noise goes on, filling the hollow so completely that there is no sense of silence waiting underneath.
