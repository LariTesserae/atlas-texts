Below the surface, the water opens into an immense constructed basin. Its floor is a skin of poured concrete laid over sealed steel chambers, so broad that from most places it cannot be seen at all. The basin extends beyond the range of the lamps and beyond the reach of ordinary daylight; its walls are days away by slow inspection vessel. There is no exposed rock or natural seabed.

The water is cool and clear enough that the upper reaches are bright. Sunlight comes down through the moving surface in long, wavering bands, illuminating suspended inspection cables and the occasional fish. Farther below, rows of electric lamps follow the floor and the major conduits. They are widely spaced, but each throws a pale circle over the fresh concrete. Between them the basin falls into blue-green distance.

Most of that distance is empty water. The structures are enormous, but their scale is measured in kilometers: a gantry can vanish into haze before its opposite support comes into view. Pipes as wide as railway tunnels run along the floor, turning at sharply cast elbows and rising into open-mouthed towers. Some carry water in; others draw it away. At intervals, lattices of beams surround the intakes, keeping large animals clear of the blades.

The place is young. Formwork marks remain on the concrete, and the corners of the great foundations are still crisp. Steel plates show clean welds and only the first blooms of rust around their bolts. There has been little time for sediment to gather. Where the pumps have dropped a load of fine material, it lies in thin, rippled fans rather than deep banks.

Plant life is sparse. Narrow ribbons of algae grip the sunny sides of the upper supports, and a few tufts grow in seams where grit has lodged. They bend steadily toward the outflow. Small grazing animals work over these patches, leaving pale tracks. There is more life in the water itself: drifting clouds of plankton, quick silver fish that gather at the edge of a lamp’s reach, and larger hunters that pass through them with sudden, economical movements. The animals are abundant in places, though a swimmer may travel a long way between one congregation and the next.

The machinery gives the basin its weather. When an intake changes speed, a current begins to build across the floor, lifting loose silt and drawing long trails from the algae. Warm water released from a pipe rises in a trembling column, then spreads beneath the cooler surface. Fish hold position behind the columns and braces, sheltered from the strongest flow; when the pumps reverse, they turn together and find the other side.

The sound travels everywhere. Motors throb through the water, joints knock as pressure changes, and loose chains rattle against their housings. Near a pumping station the noise is a continuous pounding felt in the chest. Farther out, it becomes a layered hum, crossed by the sharper calls of animals and the distant clatter of maintenance equipment.

Occasionally an inspection vessel moves through the open water, its lamps sweeping over concrete and steel. It looks small against the work around it. Its passage stirs a few fish and leaves a wake of disturbed particles, which drift for a time before the larger current takes them.
