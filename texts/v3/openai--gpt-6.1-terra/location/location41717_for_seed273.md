The valley is high enough that the horizon appears below its rim. From the upper walkways, one looks down across a whole district of terraces, bridges, and steep-sided towers, then farther down through gaps in the structures to another set of terraces, hazy with heat. The valley was built rather than excavated: its walls are stacked courses of fired block, slag concrete, and old ironwork, rising in great buttressed steps. Their age has softened them into slopes. Corners have crumbled, mortar has washed out, and generations of repair have left bulges and seams where the newer work meets the old.

It is warm in the sun. The masonry stores heat through the day and gives it back slowly after dark, so that even the highest gardens rarely cool for long. Light enters from almost directly overhead and strikes the pale terraces with a force that makes their shadows look unusually blue. Metal roofs flash between the trees. Across the valley, tall chimneys stand in the glare, their outlines wavering above the warm air.

Plants cover much of the construction, though there is no ground beneath them as one might expect. They grow in troughs, on roofs, and in deep beds held behind retaining walls. Elsewhere they have found purchase in accumulated dust and leaf mould: ferns spread from cracks in the blockwork, figs grip abandoned staircases, and thick vines hang from bridge to bridge. On the broadest levels there are dense orchards, with paths kept open beneath them by pruning and traffic. Palms and climbing plants crowd the spaces between workshops. Even the more exposed towers carry gardens in sheltered pockets, their roots bound into old netting and layers of compost.

Water is carefully distributed. Rain comes now and then, hard enough to make the drains roar, but much of the year the green depends on cisterns, condensation screens, and narrow pipes running along the walls. At night, moisture gathers on tall ranks of corrugated metal; by morning it has run into channels below. Small pumps lift it from one terrace to the next. The beds nearest the outlets are dark and wet, while a few steps away the surface may be pale and powdery. Shallow pools stand under the outlets, crowded with insects and the tracks of birds.

Animals are everywhere, often heard before they are seen. Swifts work the bright air above the chimneys; pigeons and larger fruit-eating birds shelter in the orchards. Lizards lie along hot rails and scatter into the vines when someone approaches. Moths collect under lamps and in the shade of broad leaves, and their caterpillars have worn lacework through some of the gardens. Small mammals move through the cable ducts and nesting boxes. On the quieter terraces, their paths are visible as narrow, polished runs through the leaf litter.

The machinery is old, heavy, and still useful. A lift cage travels the height of the valley along iron guides, carrying workers, produce, and replacement parts. Fans draw air through the deeper levels, and slow conveyor belts bring up compost and broken material for sorting. The sound of it all fills the valley: motors humming, chains rattling, air moving through ducts, birds calling over the machinery. The walls return the noise in softened layers, so it is difficult to tell how far away a particular sound began.

Change is slow here. A damaged stair may remain closed for years while vines grow through it; a retaining wall may acquire several generations of patches before it is rebuilt. Dust settles in the gardens and is turned into soil. Pipes are moved a little as roots thicken around them. From season to season the place looks nearly fixed, but an old photograph shows a different arrangement of shade and foliage, and a different line where the white blocks meet the green.
