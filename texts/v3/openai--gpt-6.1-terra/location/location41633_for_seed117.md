The basin is small enough to walk around in ten minutes, though the ground makes it a slow circuit. It lies below the surrounding flats, cupped in grey stone that has worn into overlapping shelves. Water occupies almost every seam. It beads on the stone faces, slips through narrow channels, and gathers in dark, shallow pools whose edges are difficult to distinguish from the wet rock.

Nothing green grows here. The shelves are crowded instead with pale mineral knobs, translucent crusts, and soft-looking films that contract when touched by water. Some films are colonies of minute animals; others are only deposits, laid down in nearly identical shapes. Even close up, it is not always possible to tell them apart.

The air is cool and dim beneath a low bank of cloud. Condensation hangs in the hollows and gathers on any surface that stays still. There is a faint smell of iron, cold mud and something saline. The basin has been wet for a very long time: its corners are rounded, its channels deepened and then softened again, its hardest stones standing among the lesser ones like worn teeth.

Small creatures are everywhere. Jointed swimmers work through the pools, scraping the mineral films with transparent mouthparts. Flat, dark animals cling beneath the overhangs, where they wait for the swimmers to pass. A few pale, many-legged things move over the exposed shelves, keeping their bodies pressed to the water that coats them. When a shadow crosses a pool, dozens of tiny shells close at once, each with a sound too slight to hear.

Near the centre stands a short iron frame, set into the rock and leaning toward the largest pool. Its gears are red with corrosion, but one shaft still turns intermittently, driven by water falling through a pipe from a higher shelf. The pipe has split in several places. Every turn sends a small pulse through it, and droplets jump from the breaks in a fine, regular spray. Below it, a trough once carried water somewhere beyond the basin; now it ends in a collapsed wall and feeds another pool.

The machinery makes little noise. There is the soft knock of the shaft, a thin hiss from the pipe, and the constant, close sound of water moving through stone. Occasionally a creature disturbs the surface with a rapid patter, followed by a longer stillness.

At dusk—or at the hour that passes for it under the cloud—the pools begin to show a weak light. It comes from the animals in the deepest crevices, which brighten when the water above them is disturbed. A ripple crossing one pool can set off a chain of small gleams along the shelf. The light reveals the crowded spaces beneath the surface: bodies edging past one another, droplets forming on the undersides of stones, and fine grains of mineral drifting down through the clear water.
