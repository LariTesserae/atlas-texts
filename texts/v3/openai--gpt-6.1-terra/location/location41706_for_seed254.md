The settlement lies below street level, in a shallow basin roofed over by old factory floors. A stair comes down through warm air and ends at a narrow passage between brick walls. Water stands in every worn joint of the steps. The walls are green from floor to ceiling, not with a single coat of moss but with layers: moss beneath liverworts, fern roots over those, and trailing stems that have found their way into the mortar.

There are several streets, though none is much wider than a doorway. They meet around a pump house and continue beneath low arches into rooms once used for storage and work. Pipes run along the ceilings in bundles, their brackets thick with roots. The houses are small, built close against the retaining walls; their windows look into other windows across a few feet of leaves. Grates cover the drains, but even these have become growing places, packed with wet sediment and fine roots.

The basin has been wet for a long time. Water seeps through the stone above and gathers in channels worn smooth by years of flow. The brick corners are rounded; lime has crusted around the pipe joints. Nothing is collapsing quickly. The roots hold some surfaces together while gradually prising others apart, and the old drains carry off just enough water to keep the passages from flooding.

Electric lamps burn behind wire cages along the streets and in the broader rooms. Their light is weak by the time it passes through the foliage, but near each lamp the leaves grow thick and pale green. Farther back, ferns and moss occupy the damp shade. The air smells of wet brick, warmed metal and crushed stems. Droplets gather on every cool surface and fall at intervals into the water below.

The pump is the loudest thing in the settlement. Its motor fills the chamber with a steady mechanical roar, and its pipes tremble against the walls. Ventilation fans add a lower, uneven hum; water rattles through a return pipe and pours continuously into a covered sump. The sounds leave little quiet, although very little seems to move. Leaves hang almost still. A film of water creeps along the pavement. No insects stir in the vegetation, and no birds or other animals occupy the rooms.

At the far end of the basin, the last passage terminates at a concrete wall. A row of lamps lights a bank of ferns there, each frond pressing against the next. Water runs behind them in thin threads, feeding the roots before it reaches the drain.
