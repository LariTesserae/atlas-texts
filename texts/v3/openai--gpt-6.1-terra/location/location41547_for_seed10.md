The high country has been worn almost flat. It lies above the clouds in a succession of broad, shallow basins, each one edged by a ridge so old and rounded that it is difficult to say where the ridge begins. In clear weather the basins extend for many miles. Today, low cloud moves through them, leaving long strips of ground visible between its folds.

The ground is plant. A pale, close-grown mat covers the ancient stone, following every worn contour and filling the cracks. It is springy underfoot in some places and hard as frozen hide in others. Water lies just beneath its surface. A step sends it seeping up around the boot, and for several seconds afterward the depression gives off a thin, fern-shaped plume of mist.

There are few tall things. Great distances separate the scattered stands of white reeds and the low, black shrubs that lean with the prevailing wind. The emptiness between them is occupied by the mat: living root, leaf and stem packed so tightly together that no individual plant can be found. In places it peels back from itself in slow green scrolls, revealing older layers, each wet and cold and still faintly growing.

The basins are full of animals. From a distance they seem sparsely populated, because the herds spread themselves over such enormous areas: dark grazing shapes a hundred yards apart, long-legged birds standing alone in the shallows, pale burrowing creatures surfacing and disappearing again. Up close, there are tracks everywhere, crossing and recrossing until the vegetation heals over them. Small animals travel inside the plant mass, raising ripples as they pass. Larger ones graze with their muzzles submerged, pulling up lengths of root that tremble and withdraw if dropped.

Wind drives rain almost horizontally across the plateau. It also drives the shallow water in sheets, exposing the mat in one basin and flooding another. The weather changes so quickly that sunshine and sleet can occupy the same slope. When the cloud lifts, distant herds may be visible under clear sky while ice is forming on the backs of the animals nearby.

A heavy mechanical throb carries across the open ground. It comes and goes with the wind, sometimes accompanied by a higher metallic chatter. There are no engines to be seen, nor tracks where engines might have passed. Beneath certain reed stands, thick root cords contract in sequence, forcing water through chambers in the peat. The chambers knock, the reeds vibrate, and the sound travels for miles. At intervals every stand takes up the rhythm, until the whole basin seems to be running.

During these episodes the landscape becomes difficult to measure. A herd moving toward a ridge will gradually diminish in size, then return to its proper proportions once it crosses. Pools appear farther away while you walk toward them; a bird can fly across one in a few wingbeats, though its reflection takes much longer. The animals pay little attention. They continue grazing and calling to one another through the pounding.

The exposed surface is constantly torn open and repaired. Wind lifts sheets of vegetation; water fills the openings; the living edges knit together again, sometimes around an animal caught too deeply in the mat. Storms uproot the reed stands and carry them across the basin with their root chambers still beating. By the time the weather clears, some have anchored themselves in new ground and begun to grow.

Toward evening the light becomes a dim green-grey. The knocking slows. Calls pass between animals hidden by the cloud, answered from farther away than the basin seemed to reach. Water settles into the worn hollows, and the high ground holds still for a while, except for the small movements traveling beneath its surface.
