The entrance is a low cleft in the floor of a dry quarry. Warm air rises through it with a smell of hot stone and crushed leaves. A few steps down, the daylight falls away, and the cleft opens into a chamber about the size of a small railway station.

The chamber has been underground for a very long time. Its walls are folded and hollowed into smooth, overlapping shelves, with no sharp edges except where a more recent fracture has split the rock. The floor slopes gently toward a central basin. Most of it is dry, covered in rust-red grit and scales of mineral deposit, but the basin holds a shallow dark pool. Its water is hot to the touch and so still that the ceiling seems much farther down than it is.

Vegetation occupies nearly every surface above the floor. Thick, strap-shaped leaves grow from seams in the stone, hanging in dense curtains or lying flat against the walls. Behind them are mats of pale moss, woody roots, and a fernlike growth whose fronds have hardened into fans. There is not quite enough water for any of it to look tender. The leaves are glossy in some places, brittle at their tips in others, and many have folded along their length to expose less of themselves to the heat.

Condensation gathers only intermittently. When it does, it forms on the cool mineral ribs high above, then travels down grooves worn into the rock. The plants crowd these routes. Some send thin roots across bare stone for several metres to reach them; others have grown broad, cupped leaves beneath the places where drops tend to fall. The driest parts of the chamber are crowded too, but with old growth: grey stems, empty root sheaths, and the persistent shapes of plants that died without quite coming loose.

A machine stands near the pool. It is a squat iron pump mounted on a concrete plinth, with two pipes entering the ground and a third ending abruptly in midair. Its casing is furred with mineral salts. A belt runs from it to a flywheel set into the wall, and the wheel turns so slowly that its movement is difficult to see until a bolt on the rim has shifted past a fixed mark.

The pump makes most of the noise. Each stroke begins with a deep mechanical knock, followed by a long scraping exhalation through the pipes. The sound fills the chamber and returns from its many alcoves in layers, until it is hard to tell which knock is new. Loose leaves tremble, though the air itself barely moves. Now and then a drop falls into the pool with a small, clear note that can be heard even over the machinery.

There are other signs of work: a short rail half buried in the floor, an insulated cable disappearing into the rock, a metal platform reached by three corroded steps. No tool lies where it was last put down. The plants have grown through the rail joints and around the platform legs, holding them in place as firmly as the stone does.

No animals live here. There are no insects at the pool, no nests in the leaves, no small tracks in the grit. The flowers, where they appear, open against the rock itself: flat white structures with their reproductive surfaces pressed to the stone. Their pollen moves through the chamber in faint pulses whenever the pump exhales. Over years, it settles into the seams occupied by other plants, and new growth begins there, close enough for roots to find one another.

The strangest feature is the light. The pool gives off a dull green illumination, strongest along its edge, where the mineral crust has grown in thin translucent ridges. It is enough to make the lower leaves visible and to put a glint on the pump’s flywheel, but the upper chamber remains obscure. When the pump stops between strokes, the light slowly brightens. As it starts again, the glow contracts toward the basin and dims. The cycle takes several minutes. Nothing in the chamber reacts quickly to it; even the opening and closing of the white flowers appears to happen over days.

The heat stays constant. The pool shrinks by a little during long dry intervals, leaving a dark line around the basin. Eventually moisture gathers overhead again, and drops begin to travel down the worn channels. The wheel turns. The old leaves accumulate under the new ones. From the quarry above, only the sound comes out reliably: a muffled knock, a pause, and the long breath of the pump rising through the cleft.
