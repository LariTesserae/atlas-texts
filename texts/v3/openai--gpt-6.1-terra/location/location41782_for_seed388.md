The settlement occupies a hollow scarcely larger than a city block. Its outer wall is the rim of the ground itself: a low, worn escarpment of dark stone, scalloped by water and patched with brick where the rock has crumbled. From above, the place might be mistaken for a drowned courtyard. From within, it has streets, workshops, culverts, public stairs, and a square with a clock that no longer tells the hour.

Water lies close beneath everything. It fills the seams between paving stones and gathers in shallow basins worn into the floors of the oldest buildings. Several streets end at channels too narrow to be called canals and too deep to step across. Their surfaces are brown and almost still, except where the outfalls enter. There, water folds over itself with a steady, heavy sound.

The buildings lean close without quite touching. Their lower stories are stone, rounded at the corners by generations of damp and passing water; above them rise brick walls, iron galleries, and roofs of corrugated metal. Pipes travel along the façades in bundles, disappearing into walls and emerging elsewhere, thick with condensation. Some carry warm water. Others carry steam at a pressure so low that their joints merely breathe, releasing a fine mist that hangs under the eaves.

It is warm in the hollow, though the light is dim. The escarpment blocks much of the sky, and a canopy of broad-leaved plants has spread across the upper galleries. Their leaves overlap the roofs and sag under accumulated moisture. Ferns grow in the mortar, on disused machinery, and around the bases of the streetlamps. Pale moss occupies the places where neither sun nor rain quite reaches. The vegetation is abundant but carefully accommodated: pipes have been rerouted around old roots, and iron grilles keep the larger stems from entering the drains.

The lamps burn throughout the day. They give a dull amber light, enough to pick out a wet stair or the edge of a channel, but not enough to reach the upper windows. In the square, the clock face is luminous with a greenish coating. Its hands move only when water reaches a certain height in the cistern below it. They may remain still for months, then advance together through several hours in an afternoon. The people who built it marked no numbers on its face.

A pumping station stands at one end of the square. Its engines work continuously and slowly, turning great flywheels behind a screen of riveted mesh. Every rotation produces a long metallic complaint, followed by a thud transmitted through the paving. The noise fills the hollow; it mingles with falling water, the hiss of valves, and the deep resonance of the channels beneath the streets. Conversation, if there is any, would have to take place close to the ear.

No animals occupy the place. The water is clear of darting shapes, the foliage holds no nests, and nothing stirs in the warm mud at the channel edges. The plants shed their leaves into iron catchments, where they darken and soften until someone—or some old arrangement of machinery—draws them away. In a workshop beside the pumping station, belts still turn rollers that press the fibrous material into dense sheets. Stacks of these sheets dry beneath the galleries, though in the damp air drying can take a very long time.

There are doors throughout the settlement, some painted and some bare, with thresholds polished by water. Many open into rooms furnished for work: benches under lamps, tools hung in rows, ledgers swollen at their bindings. A few rooms are entirely given over to roots. In others, shallow water covers the floor and reflects the ceiling so cleanly that the room appears to continue downward.

At the lowest point of the hollow is a covered reservoir. Its iron lid has warped over time, leaving a narrow gap through which a cool draft rises. Condensation gathers on the underside and falls back inside. The drops do not land at once: for a few seconds they hang in the gap, trembling in the draft, before returning to the dark water. The workers who fitted the lid left a ring of clear space around it, and no plant has crossed that boundary.

The machinery keeps the water at roughly the same height. Brick wears away, roots thicken, and sheets of plant fiber accumulate in their stacks. The engines continue their slow rotations, loud enough to make the railings hum. Above them, the leaves gather another weight of moisture and release it, drop by drop, onto the roofs.
