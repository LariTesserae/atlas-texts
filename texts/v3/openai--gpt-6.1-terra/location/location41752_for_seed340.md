Below the salt plain is a country of leaves.

It lies in a long, shallow chamber, scarcely deeper than the roots of the mountains around it. The roof has not fully closed. Through miles of narrow seams, daylight comes down in pale bars, shifting with the sun and with the slow movement of the stone. Between the bars there is enough light to see by: the plants themselves give off a dull amber glow, strongest where they have been bruised.

The floor is warm and springy. It looks, at first, like a bed of broad green plates laid edge to edge. Each plate is the back of a leaf, thick as a hand and furred with fine white hooks. Underfoot the plates shift and overlap, revealing further leaves below, and stems packed so tightly that no bare ground can be seen. The plants climb the walls, hang in long curtains from the ceiling and grow out across the open chamber in arches. Some arches have stood long enough to support whole thickets; others form and buckle in the space of an afternoon.

There is almost no water here. The plants draw their nourishment from the mineral vapors rising through cracks in the floor, and carry it in slow, oily sap. Their leaves are waxed against loss. When one splits, the exposed flesh gives off a smell of hot resin and crushed pepper, and within minutes a skin forms over the wound.

Nothing stays wounded for long. A torn stem sends out a fringe of red fingers, each seeking a surface to grip. A fallen leaf takes root along its veins. Across the wider spaces, great pale shoots push upward from the living floor, opening as they rise; by evening they may be taller than a house, with animals already nesting in their folds. The chamber is still being made. Its stone walls are sharp and new, thrust out in ribs and shelves where the floor has lifted. Little has yet worn them smooth.

The animals are everywhere. Flat, many-legged grazers move over the leaf plates in herds so close-packed that their backs make a second, rippling surface. Small winged things pour from the plant curtains whenever the curtains shudder. Larger creatures, blind and velvet-skinned, travel beneath the floor, their progress marked by long ridges that rise and collapse. When two meet, the leaves above them burst apart and hundreds of smaller animals scatter into the air.

The noise is immense. Grazers scrape at the tough leaves with rasping mouths. Wing cases strike together like handfuls of dry seeds shaken in a jar. Somewhere deeper in the chamber, unseen animals make a continuous hammering call, answered from several directions at once. The plants add their own sounds: stems snapping upright, seedpods cracking, sheets of leaf rubbing together. Every so often a mature arch gives way. Its fall is a drawn-out roar, followed by a sharper uproar as everything living on it escapes or begins to feed.

Movement passes through the country in waves. A swarm settles on a stand of young shoots and strips it to white stalks. The stalks bend, release clouds of powder and grow again. A herd turns for no visible reason, carrying a whole hillside of leaves with it. Warm air rushes down through the roof seams, meets hotter air rising from below, and twists loose seeds and insects into dense columns. From a distance these columns look solid. They drift between the glowing trees, break apart against the ceiling and rain life back onto the floor.

There are people here, though their marks are easy to miss. On a shelf of bare stone stands a low shelter woven from stiff leaf ribs, its entrance weighted with carefully chosen rocks. Nearby, a row of shallow cups has been chipped into the stone to collect resin. Paths are kept open by cutting and trampling, but they need tending every day. If abandoned, they narrow first, then lift, and finally disappear into the general green.

The people wear thick wrappings against the hooked leaves and carry poles to test the floor ahead. They know which glowing stems will bear weight and which will fold around a foot. They also know when to stand still. At intervals the whole chamber gives a slow heave, as if something beneath it were taking a breath. The grazing herds bunch together, the hanging plants swing, and the stone seams overhead shed a fine white dust. Then the floor settles in a slightly different shape, and the paths must be found again.
