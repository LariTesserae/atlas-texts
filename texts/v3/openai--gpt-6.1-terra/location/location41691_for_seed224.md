High in a mountain of dark stone, there is a chamber no larger than a coat pocket. Its entrance is a seam in the rock, narrow enough to be missed even by someone looking directly at it. The chamber is much higher than the surrounding ridges, though no view of them is possible from inside.

The floor slopes gently toward a shallow hollow. It is covered in pale mineral grains, each with a flat, frosted face. A few larger stones stand out of the grit. Their edges have been worn smooth, not by water or wind, but by a slow disagreement between their surfaces and the space around them. Over centuries, thin layers have ceased to belong to the stones and settled nearby as dust.

It is cold enough that metal touched here would draw warmth from a finger almost at once. There is no frost. Nothing has grown in the chamber, and nothing moves through it looking for food. The air, if it is air, holds no scent.

A dim blue light comes from a line in the ceiling. It does not illuminate the chamber evenly. The hollow receives enough light to show the texture of each grain, while the floor immediately beside it remains almost black. When a stone is moved into the bright patch, its shadow appears on the side nearest the line. The shadow stays there for a while after the stone is returned.

Near one wall stands a structure made from three hair-thin struts. It is smaller than a fingernail. The struts hold a square frame around a piece of empty space, and the space inside the frame has a faint silver depth, as if a great distance had been folded into it. The frame was placed here deliberately, though it has sunk at one corner and accumulated dust. Beside it lies a second, simpler object: a dark disk with a hole through its center. From certain positions, the hole shows the floor beneath it. From others, it shows the ceiling.

These objects are remnants of an engineering that did not need large machines. The square frame seems to keep the chamber at its present size. Once, perhaps, other frames stood around it. There are four small indentations in the floor where something rested for a very long time.

The chamber is almost silent. Occasionally a grain shifts with a tiny click. Much later, the same click can be heard again from a different part of the hollow, without any grain moving.

The slope of the floor changes very slowly. A mark made in the dust will, over years, drift uphill toward the wall. The dust itself does not travel with it. The mark simply comes to describe a different arrangement of grains.

At the narrow seam, the chamber and the mountain are not quite in agreement about distance. A probe inserted a few centimeters may find the far wall; withdrawn and inserted again, it may pass farther before touching anything. This variation is slight and has remained slight for as long as the chamber has existed. The little frame leans, the hollow deepens, and the blue line grows dimmer so gradually that none of these changes can be watched. They become evident only when something left here is found again.
