The sea covers a high plateau so broad that its far edge has never been seen. The plateau rises from the drowned floor in terraces of pale stone, their rims rounded by an ancient erosion. Above it lies a depth of cold, clear water. Light reaches the heights in wide silver sheets, bright enough to show the green of the living ground and the blue distance between the terraces.

Plants cover every surface. Their roots have entered the stone so completely that, where a cliff has worn away, its interior shows green fibers running through the mineral. The plateau is firm under its mantle of leaves, but the leaves also make much of its bulk: layer upon layer of compressed, living tissue, veined with water and hard as wood. Vast fronds rise from it, widely spaced. Each spreads a dark canopy hundreds of feet across, with long bare reaches of water between one and the next.

Fish hang in those open reaches: silver schools held in loose crescents, flat-bodied grazers against the undersides of the fronds, translucent hunters among their shadows. Soft creatures cling to the trunks in pale whorls. Farther down, immense jointed animals stand on the terrace edges, their legs embedded in the plant mass and their delicate feeding fans extended into the water.

Nothing moves. The feeding fans are open; the grazers’ mouths rest against the leaves; the shadows fall in fixed bands. Every detail remains as it is, without growth, decay, arrival, or departure. This is not a place waiting to thaw. Its animals and plants are fully alive in a condition that does not require an instant to follow another.

The plant mass contains a natural machinery on an industrial scale. Beneath the terraces, chambers of green tissue are packed with interlocking disks, fine valves, and filaments that carry a steady blue charge. Long channels bring cold water through the living material; broad sheets of membrane separate its salts and metals into intricate patterns. Along the cliff faces, these tissues appear in exposed cross-section, repeating in ranks that extend for miles. They were grown, not assembled. There are no buildings here, no tools, no discarded things.

The noise is immense. A low mechanical roar fills the water, layered with the ringing of the fronds and the deep, brassy calls of the great animals. Sound has a fixed presence here: each note occupies its own place in the sea, as visible light occupies a place in the water. A listener among the terraces would hear the whole landscape at once, with the loudest tones pressing close around the exposed living machinery.

Above the highest fronds, the water remains clear and bright. Below them, the planted plateau drops through blue distances into darker terraces, each with its rooted canopies, suspended schools, and exposed bands of green machinery. The same scene continues as far as the light allows it to be seen.
