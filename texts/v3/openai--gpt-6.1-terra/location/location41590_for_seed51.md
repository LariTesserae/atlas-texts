The low country begins where the hills have been worn down to long, blunt shoulders. From there it spreads outward in shallow basins, a whole region of dark water and vegetation under a ceiling of cloud. The horizon is difficult to keep in view. Rain moves across it in broad gray curtains, and between showers a low mist gathers over the ground.

Water lies almost everywhere, though rarely very deep. It fills the runnels between ridges of black stone, spreads beneath mats of moss, and beads on the undersides of leaves. In the open basins it makes pale, trembling sheets that look firm enough to walk on until a gust passes over them. The air is cool and wet against the face. Even the exposed rock gives off moisture: thin threads emerge from its seams, merge, and run into the marsh.

The stone has been weathered into an intricate surface. Some beds are combed into parallel ribs; others have collapsed into rounded hollows packed with peat. Along the edges of the basins, old waterlines stand one above another, each fringed with small fernlike plants. The land has held roughly this shape for a very long time, though water is still moving grains of it away.

Vegetation crowds the available ground. Rust-colored reeds grow among broad leaves with silver undersides. Moss covers the higher stones, and low branching shrubs lean over the pools, their twigs interlaced so closely that fallen rain takes time to work through them. Here and there, groves of pale trunks rise from standing water. Their crowns are sparse, but what hangs beneath them is dense: streamers of lichen, droplets, new shoots, and long translucent growths that pulse faintly when the weather changes.

The place is loud. Water pours through unseen openings with a deep, steady roar, as if the marsh were suspended over a much larger river. Wind draws a high note from the reed beds. Stones knock together beneath the pools, sometimes singly, sometimes in rolling sequences that travel across the basin. During a shower, the impacts of the drops seem to come from all directions, including from below.

Animals are uncommon. A narrow-bodied creature may be glimpsed moving under a film of water, parting the reflected clouds as it goes. Occasionally a dark bird crosses the open ground, flying low and without calling, then disappears into the mist before it reaches the far shore. Most of the movement belongs to the plants and water: leaves turning, fronds uncurling, shallow pools lifting at their edges and draining away.

There are no paths through the country, no walls, no ruins, nothing set apart from the ground as something made. Yet the place is full of processes that work with a precision difficult to distinguish from design. The moss draws dissolved metals from the water and arranges them in threads finer than a hair. The threads branch through the soil and stone, carrying patterns of pressure and light across miles of wet ground. When a bank gives way in one basin, leaves in another may close a moment beforehand. When the wind changes over the western pools, the lichen on the eastern groves tightens around its drops.

These responses are not always confined to the distance or time between them. A sound can arrive before the stone that made it falls. A reed bed seen through rain may show a different season from the ground beneath one’s feet: seed heads where there are only young green blades, or a patch of frost that vanishes when approached. Sometimes a pool reflects a stretch of marsh that lies several miles away. The reflection is clear enough to show individual leaves moving in the wind, though the surface of the pool itself remains still.

The effects are strongest after rain. Water gathers along the mineral threads and briefly joins places that were separate. A channel may empty into a hollow higher than its source; for a few minutes, fishlike shapes can be seen passing beneath stones where there is no space for them. Then the connection loosens. The channel resumes its ordinary fall, the shapes are gone, and the reeds gradually lift their heads.

Across the open country these changes overlap. A rain front advances while an earlier shower is still draining from the rocks. Mist rises from a pool and settles into the grove beside it. Somewhere underground, a long series of impacts grows louder, pauses, and starts again farther away. The marsh absorbs each disturbance without becoming quiet. By the time the clouds thin enough to admit a little light, beads of water have already formed on the leaves again, and the next dark curtain is gathering at the horizon.
