The chamber lies below the water table, though it is not quite flooded. Its floor is a shallow basin of pale stone, perhaps sixty paces across, bounded by walls that lean inward and then flare into a low, uneven vault. Water covers most of the floor to the depth of a hand. In the hollows it is deeper and dark; over the stone ribs it runs thin and bright, breaking into a thousand quick surfaces.

Light comes from the water itself. It gathers in the falling sheets along the walls and spreads across the pool in hard white bands. When the flow thickens, the chamber brightens enough to show every mineral seam overhead. When it thins, the light retreats into the channels, leaving the higher rock briefly amber and indistinct. There is no regular cycle. A brightening may last an hour or end as soon as it begins.

The noise is continuous and large for the size of the place. Water descends through cracks in the vault, strikes the basin, runs under shelves, and is forced back out through narrow vents with a hollow, fluting roar. Some vents breathe steam. Others throw a spray so fine that it hangs in the warm air and turns the light milky. In the loudest moments, the stone underfoot vibrates; then a passage somewhere shifts or clears, and one sound drops away to reveal another that has been going on beneath it all along.

The rock is old limestone, deeply worked by water. Its edges are rounded except where a recent fall has opened a fresh white face. Scallops overlap on the walls, recording currents that ran in different directions long before the present ones. Smooth columns stand apart from one another in the basin, too widely spaced to form a forest. Their bases are submerged, and their upper ends have been worn into shallow cups. Water leaps between them when the pressure rises, then drains away, leaving the cups full.

Life keeps to places where the flow gives it a little purchase. A rust-red growth clings in narrow patches above the waterline; it lies flat when soaked and lifts into tiny branching fans in the drier intervals. A few pale, wiry plants have rooted in grit caught on a high ledge. They bear no visible flowers, but their stems tremble with small translucent insects that shelter there whenever the spray reaches them. In the quieter pools, blind, dark-bodied swimmers hover close to the bottom. They are seldom seen: a passing shadow, a flick of silt, then the water closes over the sign of them.

Near one wall stands a structure no larger than a doorway, made of three slender pieces of black material joined without visible seams. It has settled into the stone at a slight angle. Water passes through its opening, sometimes as a thin stream and sometimes with enough force to bow the surface outward. The doorway remains dry. Droplets turn aside just before reaching it and rejoin behind it, though the air moving through the opening is warm and damp.

A second artifact rests farther out on a stone rib: a shallow disk, partly buried under mineral deposits. From time to time the disk releases a clear bubble that does not rise. It travels instead along the brightest band of water until it reaches a crack, where it vanishes with a sharp report. The cracks change after these reports. Some narrow; others begin to pour. Whether the disk is controlling the flow or responding to it is difficult to tell. The chamber has been wearing down around both objects for a very long time.

Nothing here is still, but the place has a settled shape. The water makes sudden channels through the grit and erases them again. Sheets break loose from the ceiling, pools surge, spray sweeps from one side of the basin to the other. Through all of it, the old hollows receive the water, the stone ribs divide it, and the bright currents find their way back into the rock.
