The city occupies a high basin, open for miles between two dark escarpments. From the rim it is difficult to see at first. Its districts are marked not by clusters of buildings but by long, straight embankments and the pale lines of covered drains. Here and there a tower rises alone from the basin floor. Between them lie great distances of wet stone.

The heat is immediate and close. Water stands just beneath the surface, and a footstep on the porous ground can bring a slow seep into its print. Rain falls often from a low ceiling of vapor, though it seldom feels cool. It gathers in shallow channels, crosses the streets in thin sheets, and disappears through iron grilles. By the time it reaches the lower cisterns, some of it has already become vapor again.

There is very little light. During the day the cloud cover glows a dull pewter, enough to show the long grades of the basin and the polished tops of walls. At night a few lamps burn inside the towers, their light descending through shafts to the machinery below. The shafts are narrow; most of the city remains in shadow.

The structures are old and carefully spaced. There are pumping stations, exchange halls, reservoirs roofed with ribbed metal, and chimneys too broad to have ever drawn an ordinary fire. All have been softened by weather. Their corners are rounded, their foundations undercut, their metal surfaces furred with mineral deposits. The roads between them are almost empty: wide causeways of black aggregate, interrupted at intervals by expansion joints filled with warm water.

The city was built to accommodate movement that is difficult to see. As the ground heats and cools, the basin expands against the escarpments. Its buildings travel a little on their foundations; their pipes lengthen, bow, and settle into different alignments. Hinged bridges lower themselves across gaps that open in the streets. At several pumping stations, enormous flywheels turn only when the stone beneath them is in motion. They may go still for days, then begin again without a human hand.

The sound carries poorly in the vapor. Near a station there may be a low hum, a drip striking the same metal plate, the occasional heavy click of a gate changing position. Farther away, even these disappear. Rain makes the broadest sound, and when it stops the silence seems to spread outward from the listener.

No trees grow on the slopes. The only visible color comes from mineral films: rust-red around the grilles, chalk white where hot water has dried, and a faint violet sheen on some of the older walls. In the covered drains live small pale creatures with jointed bodies and delicate feelers. They emerge rarely, usually after a long rain, to graze on the soft deposits left where warm and cool waters meet. A passing tremor sends them back through the grilles.

At the basin’s lowest point, the drains feed an open reservoir. Its surface is nearly level with the surrounding stone, and in dim weather the edge can be hard to distinguish. Now and then a line travels across the water, straight as a ruled mark, followed by another farther off. These are the submerged walls shifting with the heat. A few minutes later a distant bridge settles into place, and somewhere beneath the quiet streets a pump begins to turn.
