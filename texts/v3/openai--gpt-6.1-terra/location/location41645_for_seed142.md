The city occupies a high plateau whose edges have worn back into long, shallow slopes. Above it there are higher ridges, but they are difficult to distinguish from the sky. Both are black, and the air between them is dry and clear.

Underfoot lies a fine grit of ground basalt. It is warm through the soles of a boot, though there is no visible sun. The warmth comes evenly from the plateau itself, from stone that has held its heat for longer than the city has stood. There are no dunes: the grit gathers in the same hollows year after year, too heavy for the faint movements of air to carry it far.

The streets are broad and nearly empty. Their paving blocks have softened at the corners until one joins the next like a field of low, dark knuckles. Buildings stand well apart: machine halls, storage towers, rows of workshops with their roofs intact. Most are only a few stories high, their upper portions having been worn down or taken apart and reused. From the eastern edge of the plateau to the western escarpment, the same construction repeats with small variations in the stone, in the spacing of doors, in the shapes of vents.

There are lamps, but few are lit. Their filaments glow a deep red behind thick glass, enough to disclose a gate or a flight of steps at close range. Beyond each lamp the streets disappear again. A person walking through the city would find it by touch as much as sight: a railing polished smooth, the warm wall of a factory, the sudden absence of stone at a cross street.

The machinery is still running. Beneath the paving, shafts turn in bearings packed with mineral powder. In the larger halls, slow presses descend onto beds of black dust and lift again, leaving thin, dense tiles. Conveyors carry the tiles away in the dark. The engines are so well balanced, and move with such deliberation, that their sound seldom rises above a low pressure felt in the jaw. Once in a while there is a clean metallic click, followed by a silence long enough to make it seem accidental.

The tiles have been stacked in vacant buildings throughout the city. Some stacks fill entire floors; others stand alone in the middle of a room. Each tile bears the shallow impression of a street plan. The plans are not all identical. In some, a courtyard has become a road, or a row of towers has shifted to make space for a broad square. These are not records of the city as it was. Visitors who have compared them with the streets outside have found places that do not yet exist, then returned much later to find a wall removed, a new pavement laid, the square exactly where the tile said it would be. No one has seen the work being done.

Life is scarce. A few pale, jointed animals shelter in the housings of warm motors. They are no larger than a hand, with hard surfaces that collect the black dust until they are almost invisible. They feed on the residues left in the bearings and move only when a machine stops for maintenance. Then one may cross a floor with a dry, rapid tapping and disappear into another casing. There is nothing green, and no smell of growth; the air carries only warm stone, metal and the faint sharp scent of electrical insulation.

At the plateau’s rim, the last buildings give way to old retaining walls. Below them the land falls in immense steps, each one worn so low and broad that its far edge is lost in darkness. The city’s heat reaches a little way down these slopes. Farther out, the stone is cooler to the touch, though never cold.

From here the city cannot be seen as a whole. A red lamp marks one street, another glows farther away, and between them there is mostly darkness. The quiet engines continue under the ground, turning out plans for alterations so gradual that the place seems, for any ordinary span of attention, never to change.
