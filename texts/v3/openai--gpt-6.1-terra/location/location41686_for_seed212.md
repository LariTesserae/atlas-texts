The plain is low and warm, and from any rise in it you can see a city of plants extending almost to the horizon. Its streets are narrow seams between towers of folded leaf. Its squares are shallow bowls of intertwined roots, paved so tightly with pale living tips that the surface shines. Farther out, the towers flatten into immense roofs of moss and then rise again in another crowded district. There is no boundary between the city and the country. Both are equally alive.

The ground gives underfoot, then stiffens. It is made of layers of stems laid across older layers, with no soil visible between them. New ridges thrust through it all the time: green blades the height of houses, their edges sharp and unweathered, their surfaces still wrapped in the translucent skins of their growth. Some stand for an hour; some tear open and fall outward, building fresh neighborhoods where they land. A fallen blade may send roots from its upper face before its lower face has finished settling.

Water is scarce enough here to be guarded. The leaves are thick, waxy and dry to the touch, but their folds contain small, cool pockets of damp. At dawn, condensation beads along the upper rims and is passed inward through countless closing mouths. A brief rain can make the entire plain bow, every surface turning to catch it. For days afterward, moisture travels beneath the streets in sealed green veins. Now and then a vein breaks, and a dark patch spreads through the living pavement before the surrounding roots gather it up.

The sound is immense. It comes from the plants themselves: from trunks rubbing together as they grow, from seed cases bursting, from great leaf membranes pulled taut across avenues and beaten by the wind. Deep below, something repeatedly contracts, producing a bass note that can be felt in the ribs. Above it are shrieks, rattles, rolling crashes and the dry applause of millions of fronds. When one tower splits, the report travels across the city and is answered by splits in distant towers, until the horizon itself seems to be breaking open.

There are no birds above the canopy, no insects among the flowers. The flowers are plentiful nonetheless. They turn toward brightness, close around warmth and open again with their dust carried in the convulsions of the air. Long white tendrils reach across streets and join flowers on opposite walls. Their contact makes the walls recoil, slowly at first and then with such force that whole blocks exchange places.

The city has a habit of making room for things that have not arrived. An avenue opens between two ranks of stems, and the leaves on either side lean away from its center. The opening rushes forward through the plain, rounding corners and crossing squares. When it reaches a district that does not yet exist, that district grows rapidly around it: doorways, courtyards, chambers roofed in overlapping fronds. Sometimes a person standing in its path finds the new street has passed around them. Sometimes it passes through, and for a moment they can see both ways along it, though they are still facing only one.

In the ordinary bright hours, light reaches deep into the city through yellow leaves thin as skin. The air beneath them glows green and gold. Shadows move faster than the growth that casts them; one may climb a wall while its leaf remains still, and only afterward does the leaf begin to uncurl. A few squares remain in full sun for a long time, their root-paved floors hot and dry. Others close over in minutes, the edges of their surrounding roofs touching and knitting together with a soft sound no one could hear beneath the roar.

By evening, the shape of the skyline is entirely different, although many of the same towers are still there. One immense stalk bends down into a square and flowers against the pavement. Its bloom is a wet red chamber with walls that pulse gently inward. The nearby streets narrow as their roots turn toward it, and moisture begins to gather on the underside of every leaf. Across the plain, fresh towers lift their pointed crowns into the light. Some are already opening; others are growing backward into the ground, leaving bright, clean-sided hollows where they stood.
