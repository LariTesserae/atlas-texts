The settlement occupies a hollow in the seafloor no larger than a village square. Its roofs are thirty meters below the surface, though the water above them is so crowded with suspended growth that the surface cannot be seen.

Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder around a narrow central basin. Their lower stories are stone, fitted without mortar and worn smooth at the corners; above them rise ribbed additions of dark iron and greenish glass. Pipes cross from roof to roof, then descend through the streets in bundles so thick they leave only a few passages open. Some carry warm water from the machinery beneath the square. Others are filled with cables, or with a slow-moving paste of mineral silt. Every joint is furred with fine vegetation.

The plants have made use of the whole settlement. Broad translucent leaves overlap along the walls, their stems anchored in cracks between the stones. Moss fills the lettering on old pressure gauges. Long pale fronds stream from the railings and gather beneath the pipes, where they form dense curtains that barely stir. In the central basin, a low mat of copper-colored growth covers what was once paving. It opens and closes almost imperceptibly as the water warms and cools.

There is little light. A few electric lamps remain in their cages along the main passage, each surrounded by a soft, cloudy halo. The glass of the buildings reflects them in broken strips. Farther from the lamps, the leaves are nearly black, but their thin edges show a faint green when they turn. The water itself is warm against exposed skin, and the stone, where it can be reached beneath the growth, holds a steadier warmth.

The machinery is still working. Below the basin, a pump turns with such patience that its movement is easiest to notice in the bolts: one mark on a flywheel comes around, disappears, and returns much later. The pump draws water through beds of woven roots and sends it back through the settlement. Its power comes from a bank of pressure engines housed in the oldest building, where heavy pistons descend by fractions and rise again. Nothing clatters. Now and then a soft pulse passes through the pipes, followed by the whisper of leaves rubbing against one another.

Animals are scarce. A small white fish sometimes rests beneath the pump outlet, facing the current. Near the outer wall, a shelled creature has fastened itself to a valve wheel; its fine feeding threads spread across the iron and close when a particle touches them. Empty shells lie among the roots, held in place by the woven stems.

The settlement has been underwater long enough for its edges to soften without disappearing. Steps still lead to doorways, but their treads have become shallow hollows. Iron brackets have swollen with corrosion into knotted shapes. Glass panes are intact in some rooms and absent in others, leaving the interiors open to water and plant life. Inside one workshop, rows of tools remain on a rack behind a screen of leaves. Their handles have vanished; their metal heads hang in a careful sequence, each one wrapped in a thin growth that takes its shape.

At the edge of the hollow, the seabed rises into a low enclosing bank. Thick roots have bound the loose sediment there, and the pipes end in capped outlets half buried in it. Beyond the bank, the water is darker and clearer, but little can be seen from within. The settlement keeps its own slow circulation: warm water rising through the streets, passing over glass and iron, cooling among the high fronds, and descending again toward the pump. A leaf loosens occasionally and drifts through several rooms before the roots catch it. There it settles into the crowded fabric of the place, and after a long while a new shoot grows through it.
