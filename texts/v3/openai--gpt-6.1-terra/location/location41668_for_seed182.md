The settlement occupies a hollow below the level of the surrounding ground, though it is open to the sky through a narrow slot. From above, it looks like a crack crowded with leaves and scraps of corrugated metal. Only at midday does sunlight reach the floor, and then only as a pale strip that travels slowly across a wall of pipes.

There are perhaps forty rooms in the hollow, stacked and fitted together around a single crooked lane. Their walls are brick, slag and poured concrete, with new iron braces still sharp at the corners. Little has had time to wear smooth. Ladders rise through gaps barely wide enough for a person; balconies overlap so closely that washing hung on one brushes the railing of another. Beneath them, broad leaves press through every available opening. The lane smells of hot stone, green sap and machine oil.

Water comes down from above in brief, irregular falls. After rain, gutters carry it between the roofs into cisterns no larger than bathtubs. On dry days the pipes sweat a little at dawn, and people set dishes beneath their joints. The ground is mostly dry and warm, but the masonry stays damp in its seams, where moss grows in thick, almost black cushions. Ferns root in mortar. Vines cross the lane overhead and fasten themselves to the ironwork, so that the braces have to be checked and cleared from time to time.

At the deepest end stands a small pumping works. Its flywheel turns behind a mesh screen, very slowly, drawing warm water through a bed of crushed stone. The motor has been enclosed in padded housing, and its sound is a faint, steady tremor felt more readily through a hand on the wall. A few electric lamps burn along the lane. Their amber light catches the undersides of leaves and the rims of metal buckets, leaving the upper rooms obscure.

The heat is close and persistent. Open doors give onto workshops where coils of wire, jars of fasteners and lengths of pipe fill the shelves. A person passing must turn sideways where the lane narrows. There are voices sometimes, and the scrape of a chair, but the walls absorb most sounds before they travel far. An occasional beetle moves through the moss; once in a while a pale moth settles under a lamp. There are few other animals, and the residents take care not to disturb the small creatures that find their way down.

The newest part of the settlement is a wall at the southern end, poured against an exposed face of rock. Thin green shoots have begun to emerge through the concrete itself. They appear in straight, evenly spaced rows, following the buried reinforcing bars. No one knows whether their roots found cracks along the iron or whether the bars somehow drew them there. The shoots grow too slowly to watch, and the wall remains sound, so for now they are left in place.

By evening the strip of sky has gone dark. The leaves hold the day’s warmth, and a drop falls now and then from a gutter into a waiting dish. The flywheel continues its quiet turn. In the rooms above it, lights come on one by one behind crowded windows.
