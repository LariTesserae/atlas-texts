The valley lies high enough that the cold remains in its shaded folds all year, though the ridges above it are bare of snow by late summer. From a distance it looks almost smooth: a broad green basin, with darker seams running down from the surrounding heights. Closer, the surface resolves into plants upon plants. Root mats cover the slopes, and from them rise low, branching growths with scales instead of leaves. Their crowns meet in some places and draw apart in others, leaving corridors that close again as you watch.

There is soil beneath, but little of it is exposed. Wind has worn the ridges into long, settled curves; old watercourses have rounded the valley floor. Wherever stone emerges, pale rootlets enter its cracks and spread across it. Some hold loose grains of grit in their skins, becoming rough and mineral-colored as they age. Whole banks have the appearance of weathered rock until a slow wave passes through them.

The air is cold and mostly dry. Moisture collects where the canopy overlaps: droplets gather under waxy fronds, run along their ribs and fall into hollows lined with moss. A rain may pass over one end of the valley and leave the other untouched. For days afterward, water works its way downhill through the living cover, filling small dark basins and disappearing again. Thin ice persists in the deepest pockets. The plants around them grow close and colorless, their tips silvered with frost.

Nothing here is built, though parts of the valley suggest a kind of industry. On the lower slopes stand rows of thick, jointed stalks, all angled toward the same drainage channel. Their outer layers peel away in ribbons, exposing pale tissue that hardens, splits and is shed in turn. Beneath them, flattened leaves pass water from one to another, filtering it through ranks of fine hairs. In the long light of afternoon the wet hairs shine like metal filings. The work goes on across acres, each growth following its own simple pattern, together moving a considerable quantity of water and grit.

The surface is never still for long. A patch of canopy may suddenly sag as its water drains away, opening a view into a chamber of stems beneath. Elsewhere, curled shoots spring upright as the cold lifts from them, forcing older growth aside. Mats slide on the steep ground, bunching into ridges and then separating. After rain, channels change course within an hour. A hollow that was open in the morning can be roofed by evening, while a seam farther down the valley widens into a passage.

All of this happens almost silently. The plants are soft at their joints, and the old layers underneath cushion their movements. Even a collapse makes little more than a dry brushing sound. Water ticks occasionally into a basin; ice settles with a faint click. Wind passes over the cover without finding many loose edges to shake.

Animals are scarce. A small, dark crawler may be found beneath a lifted mat, sheltering among the roots, and once in a while a pale moth crosses an open channel in daylight. Its passage is easy to follow against the green. When it lands, the surface may already be shifting under it.

From the ridge, the valley appears unchanged: the same folds, the same dark seams, the same green reaching from slope to slope. Down among the plants, every route is temporary.
