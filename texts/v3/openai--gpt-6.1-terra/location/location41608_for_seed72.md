Below the town, a pumping chamber has been opened in young, pale rock. It is no larger than a small square, though its ceiling is high enough to lose in the dimness. Brick service passages enter on three sides; iron pipes cross overhead and disappear into walls still marked by drill teeth. Much of the floor is bare. The chamber was cut wider than the machinery required, and the unused space remains hot and empty-looking.

Water seeps through the rock behind the brickwork and gathers in shallow channels along the edges. It is warm to the touch. An engine lifts it, slowly and without pause, into a pipe that goes up toward the streets. The air smells of wet limestone, oil and heated iron. Near the floor it is almost still; higher up, a faint draft travels toward an old ventilation shaft. Lamps burn behind wire cages, their light weakened by moisture on the glass.

At first the chamber seems sparsely inhabited. Then a lamp trembles with the movement of midges resting on its cage. Cockroaches cluster in the warm seams around the engine housing, and pale larvae hang beneath the surface of the collection channels. Bats occupy the cooler upper reaches near the shaft, packed into the brick joints. Now and then one drops into the open space, flies a short circuit and returns. The animals make use of every margin the machinery leaves them: wet stone, dry mortar, standing water, heat and the food carried down from above.

There is little green. A few thin shoots have found a crack beneath the shaft, and a dark skin of algae coats the channel nearest the lamp. The rock elsewhere is too newly exposed, the light too poor. Mineral deposits are beginning to gather at some of the seeps, but they have scarcely softened the tool marks.

The engine’s knock carries through the chamber, joined by the trickle of water and the fine, continuous rustling from the walls. A worker comes down to clear a strainer and check the bearings, then climbs back to the street. After the hatch closes, the water rises almost imperceptibly against the channel stones, and the pump goes on lifting it away.
