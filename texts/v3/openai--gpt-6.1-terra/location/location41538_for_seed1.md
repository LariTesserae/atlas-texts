The land lies almost level for a great distance, though it is never smooth. Old rock has been worn down to a floor of shallow basins and rounded ribs. Black water fills every hollow. Between the pools, plates of ice overlap like scales, their edges pressed together so tightly that water wells up through the seams. The low horizon is difficult to fix: a ridge seen in one direction sometimes appears, a moment later, across the water behind you.

Little light reaches the ground. It comes through a lid of cloud the color of tarnished tin and catches in the wet, making the flats glimmer without seeming to brighten them. There are no trees or reeds, no moss on the stones. A pale crust coats some of the rock, but it breaks under the touch like salt.

The flats are crowded with animals. They lie shoulder to shoulder in the shallows, dark-backed and broad as seals, with rings of hard cartilage around their mouths. Water passes through them with a heavy pumping sound. When one draws in a breath, the ice around it dips; when it exhales, warm mineral water rises in a plume and freezes into delicate needles at the surface. The animals feed on something suspended in the water—perhaps the fine metallic grains that gather in drifts beneath their bellies. The smallest follow the plumes, opening and closing transparent jaws in the dense clouds.

Others move beneath the ice. Their backs scrape along its underside, producing a continuous grinding that can be felt through the soles before it is heard. Where the ice is thin, their outlines pass in overlapping ranks: long bodies, jointed limbs, mouths that turn with a slow rotary motion. Occasionally one breaks through and takes a basking animal down. The gap closes almost at once as its neighbors shift into the freed space.

The noise carries across the whole basin. There is the hammering of water forced through narrow rock, the rasp of bodies against ice, the deep, regular thud of the larger animals pumping. At intervals a bank of them begins to pulse together, and the sound grows until the water shivers in every pool. The rhythm travels onward, taken up by animals out of sight. It resembles a vast engine starting, though there are no buildings here and nothing has been assembled. The machinery is flesh, mineral and pressure, grown into an arrangement older than the present shape of the land.

The cold is severe, but the water seldom freezes completely. Heat seeps up through the worn bedrock, and the animals keep the shallows moving. Mist forms wherever their warm discharge meets the air; it hangs low and thick, crowded with droplets that do not always fall. Some drift upward into the cloud. Others remain in place after the wind has moved the mist away, marking the former edges of the vapor in faint, suspended sheets.

Distances behave similarly. A sound may arrive before the animal that makes it comes into view, and continue after it has gone under. Crossing a narrow pool can take an hour, while a distant ridge may be reached in a few steps, its rock still cold beneath the hand when the rest of the basin draws near again. The animals seem accustomed to this. They enter water that is no longer where it was and surface among different neighbors, resuming their steady work without a pause.

Now and then the cloud lid lowers until it touches the ice. The flats disappear inside it, but the grinding and pumping continue, close on every side. When the cloud lifts, the pools have subtly changed places. The old rock remains beneath them, worn nearly flat, and the crowded animals are already settling into the new water.
