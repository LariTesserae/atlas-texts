The upland begins at the top of a staircase so broad that its steps are visible from miles away. Each tread is a platform of pale cast stone, fitted edge to edge with the next. There is no soil beneath it, as far as anyone has found: inspection hatches open onto more floors, more beams, more staircases descending through the bright interior.

Across the upland, factories stand close together without quite touching. Their long walls are enamelled white, their roofs crowded with silver ventilators and ranks of chimneys. Between them run narrow courts, elevated tracks, pipe bridges and avenues of glass. The avenues continue far enough to become a regional landscape: districts of repeating sheds, towers, pumping stations and reservoirs, laid out across a high plateau whose far edge is lost in luminous haze.

The light seems to come from the surfaces themselves. Even under the roofs, corners remain visible, though the objects in them may be difficult to identify. A bolted seam can throw a sharp shadow without any apparent source. The air is cool, and metal railings draw warmth quickly from the hand.

Rain visits infrequently. More often, water gathers as condensation inside the pipes and emerges at their joints: one drop, then another, over the course of an hour. It stains the white paving in small dark fans and collects in shallow square sumps. Some of these hold a few centimetres of water for years; others dry by afternoon. Rust is rare. The cut edges of the metal are still keen, the concrete corners clean, as though the whole plateau had been assembled recently and has not yet learned how to weather.

Plants grow only where water persists. There are thin cushions of moss along certain seams, a few wiry ferns rooted in bolt holes, and pale reeds in the larger sumps. Their roots enter narrow channels made for them in the paving. Whether the channels were intended for roots or the roots somehow requested them is a matter of local disagreement.

Animals occupy almost every level. Small grey creatures with hinged shells sleep in the cable trays; white moths settle by the hundreds on the undersides of bridges; long-legged birds pace the roofs, picking insects from the ventilators. In the sumps, translucent fish hold their places beneath the surface, turning slowly when a drop falls. Larger animals move through the empty loading bays: six-limbed grazers with felted coats, feeding on the moss that grows in the expansion joints. Their hooves are padded, and the birds seldom call. Even when a flock rises, it makes less noise than the soft hiss of a valve releasing pressure.

The factories are working, though their machinery is almost silent. Great belts travel behind the glass walls; pistons advance a fraction and rest; overhead cranes carry loads so slowly that a person can walk an entire avenue before noticing they have shifted. Once every several days a finished object appears at a loading dock: a section of stair, a wheel, a hollow white block. No one has seen it leave, but later the object is gone and something in the surrounding district is different.

A new staircase may connect two roofs that were always considered adjacent. A court that used to be narrow may now take twenty minutes to cross. The inhabitants accommodate these changes by keeping their maps in pencil, but the alterations are not entirely physical. If a bridge is built between two towers, people remember having used it in childhood, even when they watched it arrive that morning. The animals adjust more quickly. Moths are often found on a new wall before its surface has cooled.

Toward evening, the bright plateau dims only slightly. Condensation beads along the pipework. The grazers settle in the loading bays, the birds fold their legs beneath them, and the cranes continue their nearly imperceptible journeys. Somewhere below the paving, another floor is being fitted into place.
