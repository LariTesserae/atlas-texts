The basin sits high above the cloud line, though the clouds seldom let anyone see how high. It is no larger than a hamlet: a shallow bowl of dark, newly made stone, bounded on three sides by a lip that has not yet finished rising. On the fourth side, the ground falls away into white vapor. Every few minutes a warm gust comes up from below, bringing the smell of wet iron.

The stone is almost without soil. Its surfaces are smooth in some places and granular in others, as if several different methods of making rock were being tried at once. Narrow seams open slowly across the basin floor. Water beads in them, gathers, and spills into shallow depressions. The pools are never quite in the same places twice. Some evenings a rim of pale mineral lifts itself around one; by morning the rim has softened and another pool is forming nearby.

There is little plant life. A few stiff, blue-green fronds grow out of cracks, each one standing alone. Low patches of translucent moss cling to the warmest stones, where condensation runs constantly beneath them. The moss traps droplets in tiny cups and lets them go when something touches it, so an animal crossing a patch leaves a dark, wet track.

The animals are numerous enough to make the empty ground seem busy. Long-legged grazers move from pool to pool, delicately picking minute organisms from the water’s surface. Their bodies are small and almost colorless, but their feet spread wide over the soft new mineral. Rounded creatures with hinged backs shelter beneath stones; when the ground warms, they come out in crowds to drink, making a dry clattering that can be heard across the basin. Above them, dark flyers turn in the mist. They have no visible wings until they change direction, when thin membranes briefly catch the dim light.

The noise is surprising. Water ticks against the rock, the grazers call to one another in harsh metallic bursts, and the flyers produce a deep, uneven humming. From somewhere below comes a repeated booming, felt first through the soles of the feet and then heard. Each boom sends rings across the pools. The animals pause, then resume their feeding while the rings are still spreading.

Three structures stand in the basin, far apart. One resembles a narrow shelter made of black glass, open on both sides. Inside it, a person can hear the rain before it begins: a soft, accelerating patter, followed several breaths later by drops on the stone outside. Another structure is a low ring of jointed metal, half sunk in the ground. It turns a little each day, though no track or motor is visible. The third is only a suspended pane, taller than a person and thin as a strand of hair when seen edge-on. Mist passes through it. The animals do not.

These things appear to be attending to the basin. When a seam opens too quickly, the suspended pane clouds over and a faint thread of light connects it to the break. The ground settles. Occasionally the ring emits a chord so low that the water rises into little trembling cones. Afterward, a fresh shelf of stone can be found at the basin’s edge, warm and slightly yielding underfoot. Whether the structures are building the place, keeping it stable, or learning what it wants to become is difficult to say.

Light arrives mostly through the cloud, diffuse and gray-gold. At intervals the vapor parts above the rim and a much brighter sky appears for a few seconds. The animals cast sharp shadows then, revealing how quickly they are moving. As the cloud closes, their shadows vanish and the basin returns to its softer outlines.

Rain comes often but rarely lasts long. It strikes the young stone loudly, swells the seams, and drives the small animals into the shelter, where they crowd around one another without apparent fear. When it stops, heat rises from the ground in wavering sheets. The flyers descend, the grazers spread out again, and water begins its slow search for the next place to gather.
