High in the wall of an immense factory tower, behind a bolted inspection plate, there is a country no larger than a room.

Its floor is a poured ceramic plateau, lifted in three sharp terraces around a shallow basin. The edges are so clean they seem newly broken, though nothing has broken here for a very long time. Black rivets stand along the ridges like isolated boulders. In the basin lies a thin scatter of white industrial salts, dry enough to squeak under a boot. There has never been a stream to cut a channel through them, nor rain to soften the square shoulders of the hills.

Cold enters through every surface. The plateau was built directly into the tower’s upper machinery, far above the lowlands, where the outer air is thin and bitter. Banks of work lamps shine through a wire-glass ceiling, giving the chamber the plain, sufficient brightness of a workshop. Their light catches the machined facets of the terrain and the little shadows beneath its overhangs.

A few plants rise from sockets in the ceramic. They are factory-made growths: dark, jointed stalks with narrow metallic leaves, each standing well apart from the next. Their roots descend into sealed cartridges of dry chemicals. Nothing visits them. They neither bloom nor shed, and their leaves remain fixed in whatever slight turn they were given when the chamber was assembled.

The sound is enormous. Motors on the other side of the walls drive great rollers, pumps and presses, all turning at constant speed. Their combined roar fills the little landscape so completely that a person inside could not hear their own voice. Yet the noise does not surge or pulse; it holds one vast, steady note. The rivets do not rattle. The stalks do not tremble. Even the loose salt remains where it was laid.

The workers who sealed the chamber called it a test landscape. Perhaps it still is: a high, cold place made entirely by hand, held motionless inside the loudest part of the tower.
