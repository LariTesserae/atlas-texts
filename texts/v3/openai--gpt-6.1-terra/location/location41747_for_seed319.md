The city occupies a high basin of pale, weathered rock. From the rim, its roofs and works extend almost to the opposite slopes, but much of the ground between them is open: wide settling fields, abandoned yards, shallow pans of water and streets built broader than traffic has ever required. The buildings stand in groups, separated by hundreds of metres of bare stone. In clear weather you can see from one end of the basin to the other.

Water is everywhere close at hand, though there is no lake and no river large enough to name. It rises through seams in the paving, collects in the floors of disused sheds and beads on the cooler sides of pipes. The stone is porous beneath a hard crust; after rain, the basin stays wet for weeks. Thin films gather into runnels that follow old cuttings toward the settling fields. There they spread, warm and brown, among low walls furred with mineral deposits.

The air is warm even at this elevation. A haze often rests over the basin in the morning, and by afternoon it has lifted just enough to show the farther chimneys. Light falls plainly on the pale roofs and black machinery. There is little shade. Vegetation grows mostly where a wall or pipe gives it shelter: wiry grass in expansion joints, low salt-tolerant leaves along the drains, a few stunted trees in the older residential blocks. Their trunks are thick for their height, and their crowns seem to have been worn down to a common level.

The loudest thing in the city is the pumping station beneath the western ridge. Its engines drive water up from the lower seams and send it through a network of elevated iron mains. Each engine has a slow, heavy stroke, but there are so many running that their impacts overlap into a continuous roar. The sound fills the open spaces as completely as the streets. It shakes loose grit from windowsills and makes the surfaces of the shallow pools quiver.

People have built their routines around it. Workshop doors are marked with large painted symbols; notices are arranged in blocks of colour that can be understood from across a street. Indoors, conversation is possible in the thick-walled rooms of the old quarter, or beside one of the smaller engines when it is stopped for repair. Elsewhere people stand close and watch one another’s faces. Visitors often shout for the first few hours, then fall quiet when they discover how little difference it makes.

The industry is old and carefully maintained. Foundries, pump houses and mineral works occupy the lower ground, their corrugated roofs patched with sheets of different ages. Rails connect them to storage yards and to an incline climbing the basin wall. A few electric locomotives move along the rails at walking speed, pulling short trains of tanks and covered wagons. There is room for much more traffic than passes now. Workers cross the yards by established paths, stepping around puddles that have occupied the same depressions for years.

At the basin’s eastern edge, the water is allowed to evaporate in broad rectangular pans. What remains is scraped up in pale crusts and carried to the factories. Some pans have been left unused long enough for their retaining walls to slump and soften. Rain has rounded their corners; mineral scale has joined broken brickwork to the stone beneath it. From a distance the older works are difficult to distinguish from the eroded terraces on which they were built.

In a few places the water carries the engine noise ahead of the sound itself. A person walking beside a drain may hear a particular knock through the soles of their shoes, then hear it again a moment later in the air. The delay is small but dependable. Mechanics use it to locate faults in the buried mains, kneeling on the paving with one hand flat against it. Children learn to tell which pumps are running by standing barefoot at certain junctions.

Animals are rarely seen. Small birds visit the highest roofs after rain, and a few pale moths gather around the workshop lamps at night. There are fish in some of the deeper settling channels, though the water is too cloudy to see them except when they come up near the gates. The city keeps the channels open for them as well as for the flow; workers clear the screens slowly, checking what has caught there before lifting it away.

Beyond the built edge, the basin rises in long, bare steps. Runoff has worn the slopes into rounded ribs and shallow bowls, their surfaces stained rust-red, cream and grey. The same paths have crossed them for generations. From up there the city looks almost still: steam drifting from a roof, a locomotive moving between yards, a person no larger than a nail head waiting at a crossing. The roar reaches the rim without interruption. In the rock underfoot, the slower pulse of the pumps can still be felt.
