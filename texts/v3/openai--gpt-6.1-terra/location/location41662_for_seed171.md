The upland has no visible edge. It rises gradually out of the cloud and continues at that height for hundreds of miles: a cool, dim country so densely grown that its surface appears, from a distance, to be a single dark green material. Weather forms over it and has time to grow old before reaching the far side.

There is ground beneath the plants, though it is young ground. Pale mineral crust swells up through the root mass in broad, smooth backs, without the gullies and broken ledges of an older landscape. Water beads on these surfaces and gathers in shallow depressions before the plants draw most of it away. In places the crust is still soft enough to take the imprint of a frond; elsewhere it has hardened around stems that continue to thicken, slowly opening hairline seams.

The vegetation occupies nearly every available space. Thick, pale stalks rise shoulder to shoulder from a continuous floor of moss and overlapping leaves. Above them, jointed boughs weave into a canopy so deep that its upper surfaces receive a different weather from its lower ones. Rain may fall on the heights for hours before a drop works down through the layers. When it does, it arrives cold and clean, often carrying a grain of new mineral or a spore enclosed in a glistening skin.

Standing beneath the canopy would be difficult. The ground gives slowly under weight, and every apparent opening is full of fine growth: translucent threads stretched from stem to stem, curled leaf tips, damp membranes trembling in the air. Some spaces are occupied only briefly. A thick root may pull itself across a hollow over the course of a day, while a bank of flattened fronds lifts to admit a surge of water and settles again once it has passed.

The sound is overwhelming. The upper canopy catches the wind in millions of stiff, hollow blades, each one vibrating at its own pitch. Deep stems carry the vibration downward, where cavities in the mineral crust amplify it into a continuous roar. There are higher notes within it—the rapid clatter of leaves striking leaves, the hiss of rain held aloft, the sudden reports of water chambers closing—but even in a lull the whole upland hums hard enough to be felt in the teeth.

No calls come from within that noise. There are no birds above the canopy, no insects in the wet seams, no tracks pressed into the moss. Pollen travels in the water films and along the living threads. Spores are released when the hollow blades reach certain pitches, so that gusts distribute them across the upland. Fallen tissue is taken apart by mats of minute plants and folded back into the living floor.

The plants do things for which the familiar names of roots and leaves are too small. Deep in their tissues, light is gathered and stored through the long dim periods, then passed laterally for miles with almost no loss. A damaged patch can draw energy from far beyond the reach of its own canopy. Along the newest mineral seams, root tips arrange dissolved substances into ordered films only a few molecules thick; these films distinguish water from mineral, useful salts from waste, and let the young crust grow in exact, flexible layers. The process is an engineering finer than any machine, though there are no machines here and no sign that anything was built.

At intervals, broad portions of the canopy change color together, from blue-green to a subdued silver. This happens ahead of heavy weather. Leaf surfaces turn, stems alter their tension, and the roar deepens as the whole region prepares to receive rain. Later the water moves through it in slow pulses, swelling the mats and filling the hollows beneath the new ground.

By morning, where morning can be distinguished through the cloud, a low ridge may have appeared. It is smooth, densely furred, and already giving rise to a stand of hollow blades. Water runs down both sides for a little while. Then the plants close over its courses, and the ridge joins the vast, sounding upland.
