High in a mountain, above the last ordinary snowline, there is a chamber no larger than a pantry. It opens into the wall of a precipice, though from outside its entrance is almost impossible to distinguish from the seams in the rock. A person must turn sideways to enter. The drop remains visible behind them: a thin, pale wedge of distance.

The chamber is crowded with vegetation. Fronds overlap from floor to ceiling, their narrow blades as dark as tarnished copper, edged with a dull green that shows only when one looks along them. They grow out of every available crack and press so closely together that the rock is visible only in brief angular glimpses. Low, branching mats cover the floor. Small stiff rosettes fill the cups of stone. Even the ceiling bears a dense, downward-pointing growth like a field seen from underneath.

There is no water here, not in the rock or the air or the flesh of the plants. Their leaves are cool and dry to the touch, and where one has been broken in the distant past, its exposed edge is crystalline. They draw what they need from mineral vapors and the weak electrical currents passing through the mountain. Their roots lace the stone so completely that some of the finer seams seem to exist only to accommodate them.

The chamber is old. Its corners have been rounded by a slow abrasion of grit, and the exposed stone has the smooth, pitted surface of metal left in a blast of sand. The growth has settled into these contours. Nothing pushes, unfolds or falls. The leaves hold their places with such exactness that the narrow passages between them resemble parts of the chamber’s construction.

Among the fronds stand three little iron housings, each about the size of a closed hand. Their bases have been bolted into the rock. One retains a glass gauge, its needle fixed just short of the red mark; another has a length of insulated cable disappearing beneath a mat of roots. The third is open, revealing a small toothed wheel. The metal is worn but not rusted. Whatever the devices were installed to measure or regulate, they have been here long enough for the vegetation to have grown around them without quite touching them.

The place is loud. A deep mechanical note fills the chamber, accompanied by a finer, continuous rattle, as though a great factory were operating somewhere inside the mountain. The little wheel does not turn, and no vibration can be felt in the housings. The sound seems to come equally from every surface, including the leaves. It is difficult to hear one’s own breathing.

The light is poor, entering only through the high slit of the doorway. It catches on the metallic edges of the plants and on grains of pale mineral held in their branches. There are no insects among them, no burrows, no tracks in the settled grit. The air is cool and carries a faint smell of hot iron, despite the cold.

From outside, the sound is inaudible. It begins at the threshold, at full volume, and stops behind a person who steps back out. Inside, nothing ever seems to respond: not the gauge, not the fronds, not even the loose grains poised on the slope of the floor. The chamber remains densely occupied and perfectly still, with the mountain’s vast noise held inside it.
