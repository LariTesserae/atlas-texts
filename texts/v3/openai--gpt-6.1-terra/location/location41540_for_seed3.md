The city occupies a high basin where the mountains are still arriving.

Their slopes rise steeply beyond the last streets, dark and almost smooth, without the gullies that older mountains wear. Here and there a ridge has pushed through the ground like the back of an enormous buried animal. Fresh seams of stone stand open along its spine, their edges sharp, their interiors faintly warm. Surveyors place white stakes beside the seams and return each year to move them. The movement is slow enough that a person can lean against a cliff for an afternoon without feeling it happen.

There is little sky in the basin. A low ceiling of cloud catches the factory glow and holds it close to the roofs, so that even at midday the streets have the dimness of a room lit from another room. On clear nights the stars appear below the peaks as well as above them. The lower ones are reflections, people say, though there is no water in those places.

The city was built to accommodate this uncertainty. Its districts are separated by broad tracts of bare ground, as if the streets had been laid out in anticipation of buildings that never came. Rail lines cross these vacant spaces on embankments. Long workshops, power houses and blocks of workers’ flats stand in orderly clusters, joined by pipes and elevated walkways. Most foundations rest on plates that can slide. At the foot of every building there are measuring marks, and some of the older marks have risen above the doors.

A narrow railway circles the basin, carrying stone, coal and passengers between neighborhoods. Its engines sound their horns before entering the darker cuts. The horns are not especially loud, but the basin returns each one several times, with pauses long enough that conversation can resume between them. Farther off, presses strike in the factories, and ventilation fans give the city a continuous, low mechanical breath.

Water comes and goes. Fine rain can fall for an hour on the eastern wards while the western ones remain dry. At night, condensation gathers on rails, leaves and the undersides of bridges, then drops into shallow stone channels. These lead to covered cisterns in the squares. Between rains, the exposed ground is mostly dry: black mineral dust, pale gravel, slabs of new rock. A few ponds persist in sheltered hollows, dark and cold, with reeds growing thickly around their edges.

Vegetation is most luxuriant where warmth escapes from the city. Ferns crowd the mouths of drains. Moss climbs the brickwork behind the boilers, and broad-leaved vines hang from the pipe bridges in green curtains. In the open ground, plants grow farther apart: low silver shrubs, tough grasses and small trees with trunks bent away from the prevailing wind. Their roots find the fine cracks in the new stone. Sometimes, when a seam opens, a whole bed of pale shoots appears within it, though no one remembers seeing seeds there.

Animals are everywhere the people are not. Small antlered grazers browse the railway verges in herds, lifting their heads together at the passage of a train. Dark birds nest in the vacant upper floors of factories and pour out at dusk to feed on insects above the ponds. Long-bodied mammals live under the raised sidewalks; their tracks can be seen each morning in the dust between the support columns. At the reservoirs, frogs call from the reeds, and pale fish gather below the grates where warmer water enters.

The animals seem to know their way through the city better than the residents do. A grazing herd may cross an empty lot and emerge on a street that ought to be half a mile away. Workers sometimes follow such routes when they are late, though they are careful not to carry anything fragile. The distance between two places can shorten abruptly, but it does not always do so gently. Glass has been known to arrive cracked, and a bucket carried by its handle may reach the other end with its water still hanging in the air behind it.

The city keeps no official map of these passages. Instead, each ward maintains a board of recent observations: a chalked arrow, a date, a warning that a certain lane now leads somewhere else. Most changes last only a few days. A few have endured for generations and are treated as streets. Children learn them alongside the names of bridges and tram stops.

Above the northern district, a sheer wall of young rock rises into the cloud. From some windows it looks close enough to touch; from others it appears several valleys away. The factories beneath it run through the night, their chimneys releasing smoke that moves very slowly in the cool air. Sometimes the smoke reaches the wall and spreads across it, revealing ledges and recesses that were invisible a moment before. Sometimes it passes behind the wall, and can still be seen drifting on the other side.

People go on living here. They tend the cisterns, repair the sliding foundations and shake birds from the machinery before starting it each morning. In the evenings, lights come on sparsely across the basin. Between them lie long stretches of dim ground, crossed by pipes and tracks and the quiet paths of animals. Beyond the city, the unfinished ridges keep rising into the cloud, making more room for a landscape whose boundaries have not yet been decided.
