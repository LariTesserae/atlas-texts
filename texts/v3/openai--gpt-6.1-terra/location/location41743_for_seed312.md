The plateau is high enough that the clouds often travel below its rim. Beyond the rim it continues for hundreds of miles: a broad, cold country of black ridges and pale gravel basins, crossed by long folds of stone. The mountains are sharp and comparatively young. Their faces have not yet been worn smooth, and loose shards collect beneath them in long, slanting skirts. From a distance the country looks almost bare. Up close, its surfaces are crowded.

Stone houses stand shoulder to shoulder along the sheltered sides of the ridges. Their walls are built without mortar, fitted from slabs split by frost and worked with stone hammers. Roofs of turf and hide lie low against the wind. Lanes narrow between them, turn under overhanging rooms, and open unexpectedly into courts where families scrape hides or sort bone tools in the daylight. Steps, retaining walls and covered drains make a second terrain over the first. Here and there a whole slope has been terraced into habitation, each roof serving as the yard of the house above it.

The settlements are so numerous that from a high pass one can see the next several at once, their smoke rising in thin columns. There is no great central city, but the built country goes on for days: houses, animal pens, storage pits, cairns marking routes through the rock. People travel between districts with sledges and pack animals. Timber is scarce, carried up from distant valleys or salvaged and used until it can no longer be repaired. Stone, bone, horn and hide do most of the work.

Water is carefully gathered. A brief rain may pass over one ridge while the next remains dry; afterward, people go out with shallow vessels to collect what has pooled in the rock. Frost forms on the shaded walls overnight and melts slowly in the morning sun. Many roofs drain into lined cisterns, and the oldest houses are arranged around seepages that persist even in the coldest months. Far from these places, the ground is dry enough to lift as dust underfoot, though patches of hard ice remain year-round in cracks the sun cannot reach.

Vegetation keeps close to shelter. Gray cushions of moss and flowering plants occupy seams between stones; wiry grass grows around the warmer edges of the pens. In a few protected courtyards, people tend beds of small, tough roots, covering them at night with hides. Most of the animals range farther out: immense herds of shaggy grazers move slowly across the uplands, browsing what the wind uncovers. Smaller animals live densely among the walls and rubble. There are burrowing colonies beneath the terraces, nesting birds in every cliff, and pale mountain hares that venture into the lanes at dusk. Predators follow the herds, while lean dogs sleep in doorways and lift their heads as strangers pass.

It is a largely quiet place. The wind is often too high overhead to be heard in the lanes, and snow, when it falls, stays packed in hollows rather than drifting far. A stone struck in a workshop carries clearly across a court. Hooves click on a stairway; birds mutter under the eaves; a dog shakes itself and settles again. From the open heights, the sounds of a settlement disappear within a few minutes’ walk.

Change comes slowly enough for the inhabitants to notice it. A seep moves from one crack to another over a generation. A wall leans and is taken apart, its stones set back in a better order. Herd trails deepen into pale lines across the gravel. On clear days the light is bright and ordinary, revealing every distant ridge, and toward evening the long shadows of the houses climb the slopes behind them.
