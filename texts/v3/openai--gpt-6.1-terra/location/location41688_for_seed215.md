The plateau is so broad that its far edge makes its own weather. From the city’s upper terraces, clouds can be seen gathering over the interior while sunlight still falls on the outer slopes. The land rises gently for days in every direction, a high, cold expanse of new stone, pale gravel, and shallow water.

Much of the ground has the unfinished look of a place recently lifted. Long sheets of dark rock lie exposed, their surfaces smooth except where they have split into clean-edged blocks. Here and there the earth is still being raised in slow, broad folds. Survey lights mark the moving ground at night, and by morning a seam that was level may stand a hand’s breadth above the stones beside it. There has not yet been time for deep valleys to form. Water gathers instead in hollows, spreads over level places, and creeps along the fresh cracks.

It is cold enough for ice to linger in the shade through most of the year, but not cold enough to keep the surface dry. Mist condenses on the rock. Thin rains pass through without much wind. Around warm seepages, black mud stays soft beneath a skin of frost, and narrow pools hold a little steam in the morning. The water is extraordinarily clear where it rests on stone; where animals cross, it clouds to the color of milk.

Plants grow in widely spaced colonies. Cushion moss grips the sheltered sides of boulders, and wiry silver-green grasses follow the margins of the pools. On the warmer ground near the city, low shrubs spread flat against the gravel, putting out tiny dark leaves whenever the air softens. There are long stretches where nothing taller than a boot breaks the surface. Then a wet hollow appears, vivid with sedge, and beyond it the open stone resumes.

Animals move between these patches in great numbers. Small, long-legged grazers travel in loose herds, their pale coats blending with the frost until one turns its head. Dark birds gather along the waterlines, picking minute creatures from the shallows. Burrowing animals live under the moss cushions and emerge in quick rushes during the warmest part of the day. There are predators too: low, heavy-bodied hunters that follow the herds over distances large enough to be invisible from any single lookout.

For all this life, the plateau is quiet. Hooves make a soft ticking on the newer stone, but distance soon takes the sound. Birds call only occasionally. Most often there is the faint movement of water under ice, or the brushing noise of thousands of grazing mouths carried across a still basin. During a thaw, the opening cracks in the rock can be heard as a sequence of low reports, separated by minutes or hours.

The city occupies a sheltered rise near the junction of several wet basins. From afar it looks less like a cluster of towers than a second layer of geology: long gray terraces, glazed roofs, and narrow walls laid in deliberate curves. Its districts are separated by open ground where animals can pass, and the streets are raised slightly above the pools. People cross on warm walkways that shed frost as it forms. Beneath them, water moves through transparent channels to storage beds and treatment gardens.

The city was built for ground that would not hold still. Its foundations adjust as the plateau lifts, shifting whole blocks so gradually that a cup left on a table does not tremble. The pale material of the buildings repairs small fractures before they reach the surface. Thin collectors, almost indistinguishable from the stone, draw power from the cold air, the ground, and the change between them. At night the streets are lit where someone is walking; behind them the light fades, leaving windows and distant survey marks reflected in the water.

Outside the settled area, instruments are scattered sparsely across enormous distances. Some monitor the rising land. Others listen beneath the pools or count the migrations passing through a basin. The city uses what they report to open routes, close others, and move its water intakes as the surface changes. There are no fences around most of the plateau. A herd may wander through a northern district in the early morning, passing gardens and closed doors before finding its way back onto the open rock.

By afternoon the clouds from the interior have reached the city. Rain darkens the terraces and beads on the animals’ coats. Far out on the plateau, a shallow basin fills, and birds begin to arrive at its edge. The rain continues quietly into evening. Under it the city makes its small adjustments, and the new land rises almost too slowly to see.
