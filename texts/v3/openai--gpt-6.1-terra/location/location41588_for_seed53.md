High on the mountain, a shallow basin lies cupped between three ridges. It is small enough to cross in a minute, though the broken floor makes the crossing slow. Beyond its rim the slopes fall away into bright air, and lower ranges show through the haze like scratches on glass.

The basin is crowded with stone. Pale slabs lean against one another in close ranks, some upright and thin as doors, others split into layers that have slid apart without quite falling. Between them are narrow passages, pockets of crushed grit, and hollows no wider than a hand. The exposed edges are rounded by a long time in the weather, but fresh fractures run through the older surfaces, opening dark lines in the white rock.

There is no soil here, nor any green. Nothing grows in the cracks. The few dark stains on the stone are mineral deposits, left where meltwater has run and dried again.

Cold keeps most of the water hidden. It gathers as frost on the shaded sides of the slabs and as clear beads beneath their overhangs. At midday, when the sun reaches into the basin, droplets creep along the undersides of the rock and fall into small stone cups. Some cups hold water for an hour or two; others contain a skin of ice so thin that a falling drop can break it. By late afternoon the wet surfaces begin to harden again.

The light is intense. It comes down directly and also returns from every pale face, so that even the passages between the stones are bright. In places the rock has a pearly, translucent edge, and a slab casts a shadow with a faint band of color along it. As the sun moves, these bands pass over the grit and disappear. A person standing still might notice them before noticing the slow melt, or the gradual lengthening of a crack.

Near the center stands a low ring of fitted stones, perhaps once a windbreak. It encloses a space scarcely large enough to sit in. One side has collapsed, and the fallen pieces are weathered almost as smoothly as the stones still in place. Inside the ring lies a flake of dark rock with a worked edge, too sharp and regular to be an accident. It has been there long enough for a pale mineral crust to form over one face.

Farther toward the rim, three stones have been stacked in a narrow notch. The uppermost has slipped sideways but remains balanced. The stack may have marked a route across the basin, though from some angles it looks much like the other broken pillars. Beyond it there is no visible path.

The place is mostly quiet. Water ticks occasionally into the hollows. Wind makes a low note as it passes through one particular cleft, then leaves it silent when the current shifts. Sometimes there is a small, dry click from somewhere among the slabs: a grain falling, or ice loosening its hold. Larger movements are rare enough that their traces remain visible for years.

In the evening the basin cools quickly. The water left in the cups freezes from the edges inward, trapping tiny bubbles beneath its surface. Frost spreads across the worked flake and the old windbreak, covering them just as it covers the unshaped stone. Above the ridges, the sky stays bright for a while after the passages have filled with shadow.
