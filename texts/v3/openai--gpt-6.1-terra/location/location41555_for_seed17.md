The plateau lies beneath a clear, cold sea, so high above the ocean floor that its peaks come within a few hundred meters of the surface. Daylight reaches them in long white columns. Between the peaks are trenches wide enough to contain mountain ranges; their bottoms disappear into blue before they disappear into depth.

The stone is young. Black ridges rise in clean-edged tiers, split by cracks that have scarcely begun to widen. Here and there the seabed has opened into angular fans of pale mineral, as though enormous sheets of glass had been broken and left where they fell. There is no sand on the summits, no weed trailing from the ledges, no green at all. Even the gentlest current finds an edge to catch on.

The water is cold enough to numb exposed flesh almost at once. It tastes strongly of salt and metal. Above, a roof of thin ice covers much of the sea, but it is clear in places, and the pale sun passes through. The light has the ordinary brightness of an overcast afternoon, though it falls on slopes no human foot could reach without a suit.

There is a tremendous noise. It comes from the deep water moving through faults under the plateau: a continuous, layered roar, with a low note so powerful it can be felt in the chest through a diving hull. Every hard surface returns it. Narrow fissures whistle; the great basins carry a steady booming that never quite resolves into separate echoes. The noise does not rise and fall like surf. It simply occupies the sea.

Animals live throughout this expanse, though there can be miles of open water between them. Most are pale, streamlined things with dense bodies and small, protected eyes. Some drift close to the rock, feeling along its fractures with jointed limbs. Others move in loose schools far above the summits, their flanks flashing silver when they turn together. Small filter-feeders gather around cracks where mineral-rich water emerges, and the larger hunters follow them. Nothing grows on the stone, but fine organic particles descend from the waters above, enough to sustain a slow and far-reaching economy of mouths.

Now and then a machine stands on a ridge: a squat iron housing bolted into the rock, or a tower of riveted plates with its upper end lost in the bright water. Heavy cables run from some of them into fissures. The housings tremble against their fastenings, taking power from the pressure and vibration below. Their lamps remain lit. Their purposes are harder to see; no workers come, and the nearest neighboring structure may be a day’s travel away.

Around the machines the animals behave much as they do elsewhere. A cluster of white scavengers may occupy the warm underside of a motor casing for years. A long-bodied hunter may pass through their midst without disturbing them, its path almost imperceptibly altered by the iron tower.

From a summit the view is immense and nearly vacant: ridge after ridge under cold light, the occasional glint of an animal, a distant tower no taller than a pin. The roar fills it all. The water moves, the creatures feed, the machinery turns, and yet the place gives the impression of having held this same posture for a very long time.
