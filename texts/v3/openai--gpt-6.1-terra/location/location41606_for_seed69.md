The plateau is so broad that storms are born on it and spend themselves before they reach an edge. It stands high above the low country, but the air is warm. Water rises through its old stone, gathers in shallow basins, and spills from one worn shelf to another. The rivers have had a long time to work: their channels are wide and smooth, their bends established, their banks undercut into chambers where the water turns slowly before returning to the current.

There is no light. The darkness does not thin at any hour, and nothing in the landscape makes a flame or a glow. Distance is measured by sound, by the delay between a waterfall’s steady roar and the slap of its spray against a wall, or by the time it takes a scent to reach you on the wind.

The ground is crowded with growth. Thick mats of root and moss lie over the rock, springy in some places and treacherously hollow in others. Fernlike plants lift jointed stems into the damp air; their leaves are fleshy, ridged, and warm to the touch. Trees grow low and wide, bracing themselves with arches of root that create tunnels beneath them. Their bark feels like wet rope. Water collects in their forks and runs down their trunks in a hundred little streams.

Animals move through every layer. Small grazers rasp at the soft growth underfoot. Larger creatures travel the root tunnels, their shoulders brushing the wood overhead. In the pools, things with broad mouths feed on the constant fall of insects and plant matter. The air carries the rapid clicks of hunters and the answering clicks of prey, until a passing rainstorm fills every interval with the drumming of drops.

Some animals have learned to use the rain. They wait beneath the hardest downpours, where their own movement is difficult to hear, and rush out when the water shifts. Others stamp on the ground to find hollows and rivals. Their signals pass through the plateau for great distances, mingling with the deep, intermittent knocks of stone settling around underground rivers.

Those knocks are part of how the local animals navigate. They listen to the way a strike returns from a ravine or a buried chamber. When a river changes course below ground, whole herds can be heard testing the unfamiliar earth for days afterward. Travelers do the same, striking the ground with a weighted staff and waiting for its answer.

The few buildings stand on the oldest, highest shelves, anchored to bedrock beneath the vegetation. They are squat structures of fitted stone and iron, surrounded by the smells of oil, wet metal, and the hot mineral breath of machinery. Each has a heavy roof to withstand the rain and a drain large enough to carry away a stream.

Inside, water drives turbines. Shafts turn, pumps work, and dynamos send current through insulated cables laid under the roots. The machinery serves mines and workshops scattered across the plateau, far enough apart that a journey between them may take many days. At the workshops, engines press and cut metal; the finished parts are wrapped against the damp and hauled away on broad-wheeled carts. There are no lamps. Workers know the machines by touch and by the rhythms of their operation, and a changed vibration can stop a whole room at once.

Beyond each building, the plateau quickly closes in again. Roots creep over cables. Animals shelter beneath warm pipework. Water finds the edges of foundations and carries grit into every seam. Maintenance crews keep a narrow circuit clear, but farther out they mark the routes rather than trying to hold back the growth: a notched post here, a chain fixed along a steep descent, a bell set where floodwater is likely to rise.

During the largest storms, even the engines are hard to hear. Wind drives rain across the open shelves, rivers rise into their side chambers, and the ground shakes under the passage of herds moving toward higher stone. Then the storm travels on. Water continues to run for a long time afterward, and the smaller sounds return: claws on bark, wings beating close to the ground, the steady ticking of cooling metal, and, somewhere far below, a slow knock answered by another.
