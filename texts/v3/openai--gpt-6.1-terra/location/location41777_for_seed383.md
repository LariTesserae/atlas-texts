The city lies below the surface, in a hollow so broad that its far wall is only a pale interruption in the haze. Its floor is almost level. Whatever mountains once stood here have been worn down to low stone swells, rounded so thoroughly that the streets pass over them without turning.

Daylight enters through long openings in the roof, where the ground above has split and gradually fallen away. The openings are too narrow to show much sky, but their edges are coated with white mineral deposits. They scatter the light across the basin until even the shaded places are bright. At certain hours, moving bands of brilliance cross the city, sharpening every doorway and making the shallow pits in the stone look deep.

The buildings stand far apart: low enclosures of fitted limestone, circular houses roofed with slabs, ranks of empty storage chambers cut into the gentler slopes. Between them are broad streets and courts, mostly bare. The stonework is deliberate but simple. Blocks have been levered into place with wooden poles and bone wedges; the tool marks remain visible where the light falls sideways. Along the main streets, shallow channels lead toward covered cisterns. They are dry much of the time.

There are no plants. In cool hollows, moisture gathers on the undersides of the roofs and drips into stone bowls. After rain on the surface, water comes down through the ceiling in thin, scattered threads. A channel may run for an afternoon, carrying a little white grit, and then fall dry again. The cisterns smell of wet chalk and old clay.

Animals are everywhere, though it takes time to see them. Pale, many-legged things shelter in the joints between blocks. Small, blunt-snouted creatures cross the open courts in loose groups, pausing to feel the ground with their whiskers. On the higher ledges, colonies of shelled animals cling to the walls, opening slightly when the air grows damp. Their young move over the stone in such numbers that a patch of wall can seem to change color.

The city is continually coming apart and being adjusted. The floor creeps in places where the old rock rests on softer layers; a straight channel acquires a kink, a wall opens along one seam, a house settles enough that its roof must be lifted and reset. Fine debris pours from cracks in the ceiling. Sometimes an entire shelf gives way, bringing a cold rush of air and a curtain of dust across several streets.

The inhabitants build with this in mind. Walls are short and thick, and roofs rest on them without mortar, so that a slab can slide rather than break. Fallen blocks are taken up and fitted elsewhere. Each morning, people walk the channels with scrapers and baskets, clearing the silt before the next trickle arrives. They work around the animals, which return as soon as a stone is laid down.

Very little of this carries as sound. Feet pass over powder. Baskets are set down carefully. A falling shelf gives a long, muffled scrape, then the fine particles continue to settle for hours without a sound. Across the bright basin, dust rises, animals scatter, and people move between the distant buildings, while the hollow remains almost perfectly quiet.
