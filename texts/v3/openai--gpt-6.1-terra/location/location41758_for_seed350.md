The basin is full almost to its rim. Warm, dark water covers the flats, the low ridges, the mouths of several narrow valleys. Above it there is sometimes a ceiling of rain, and sometimes only more water, standing upright where the shore ought to be.

The bottom is young. Sheets of black stone lie folded against one another, their edges still keen enough to cut a drifting leaf. In places the ground has opened within the last hour: pale mineral seams shine through the cracks, and clouds of grit rise from them in slow, complicated shapes. The water is too restless to let the sediment settle for long. Whole slopes shift without warning, not worn down so much as pulled apart along fresh lines.

Plants fill every sheltered depth. Broad, translucent fronds crowd the stone; ropes of red vegetation climb from the basin floor and tangle around one another far overhead. Dense mats of green grow along the vertical faces of the water itself. Their roots enter it as if it were soil. When a current tears a section loose, the mat continues to hang there for a while, holding the shape of the wall that supported it.

There are no fish among the stems, no insects at the surface, no shells in the silt. Nothing grazes the vegetation. It grows until it breaks under its own weight, then the fragments take root wherever the water carries them. The beds are so thick in some channels that the moving water compresses them into shining green belts.

The noise is immense. It comes through the ground as much as through the water: a continuous mechanical pounding beneath the crack of shearing stone and the deep roar of currents forced through narrow openings. A loose metal panel strikes a pylon again and again, each blow arriving from several directions. Even where nothing visibly moves, the water shakes.

The machines are few and widely separated. One pumping station stands on a low outcrop, its square roof just below the surface. Its windows are black, but its turbines are running. Copper cables spill from a broken conduit and disappear into the vegetation. Farther across the basin, a line of pylons leans into the current, carrying a cable that is bright with electrical discharge. There is a second pumping station beside the first, identical except that its roof has fallen in. From some angles they occupy the same outcrop.

The water does not always preserve the order of events. A stone falls from a cliff and hits the bottom; a little later, the cliff opens and releases it. A bank of plants bends under a surge that has not reached it yet. The surge can be seen crossing the basin, a ridge of dark water forcing its way through the submerged forest, and when it arrives the plants bend a second time, more deeply. There are places where the same patch of ground appears at two different depths. Loose material gathers in those places and is milled between them.

Dim light enters from above, green near the vegetation and colorless over the bare stone. Occasionally the water flashes white along a cable or a newly opened seam. By that brief light, the basin extends farther than it first seemed: more ridges, more drowned plant beds, another isolated piece of machinery working violently in the dark. Then the flash is gone, and the pounding continues through the warm water.
