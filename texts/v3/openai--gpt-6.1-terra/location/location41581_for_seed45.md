The plateau is high enough that the darkness seems to begin at its surface. Its edge falls away into a depth too black to measure, though now and then a red seam opens far below and reveals another wall, and another, each one briefly hanging over the void.

The ground is warm, almost hot where the stone is thinnest. It has the dark sheen of cooled glass, but much of it is new enough to remain dull and soft-looking, folded into blunt ridges. There has been little time for weathering. Sharp lips stand along every crack; slabs fit against one another as if they had only just broken apart.

Across the plateau, ridges slowly rise, stop, and split. A mound swells over the course of an hour, then sinks with a long, low sound. Sometimes a narrow tongue of molten rock pushes out beneath it and travels only a few paces before its skin blackens. Elsewhere the changes come all at once: a line of stone lifts, falls, and sends a tremor through the ground that can be felt long after the sound has faded.

There is no rain here and no visible ice. Fine mineral dust drifts in the warm air, gathering in hollows until the next movement scatters it. The air smells faintly of sulfur and hot clay. It moves in irregular gusts, drawn toward new fissures or driven outward when the ground heaves.

Small pale crusts grow on a few sheltered faces of rock. They form delicate branching patterns, most no larger than a hand, and disappear whenever the surface beneath them shifts. Whether they are alive is hard to say. They seem to favor places where a little warmth seeps steadily from below. Nothing moves among them.

At intervals, rough rings of stone stand on the flatter reaches. They are low and incomplete, with a gap on one side; near several of them lie heaps of stones sorted by size. Whoever made them left no other clear trace. Some rings remain intact while the ground changes around them. Others have been pulled apart, their stones carried to different heights on the edges of a fresh break.

The sounds are widely spaced: a brittle clink, a deep settling thud, the hiss of a fissure opening somewhere out of sight. Between them the plateau is quiet enough for dust to be heard sliding down a slope. The darkness makes the open country feel empty, but the warmth underfoot and the occasional shifting of the stars behind rising ridges reveal how much of it is still moving.
