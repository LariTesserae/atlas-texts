The city occupies a low plain so broad that its far edge is only a darkening of the air. From a distance it looks densely planted, almost forested. Closer in, the green separates into long gardens and thickets, with wide paved spaces between them. Towers stand in loose groups, connected by covered walks and narrow transit lines. There is room between neighborhoods for fields, storage yards, and whole avenues that no one has yet named.

The ground is young. In several districts, black stone still breaks through the pavement in clean-edged plates, as if the streets were laid only yesterday. Elsewhere the city has poured a pale, resilient material over the rock; it meets the older surfaces in precise seams, without rubble or weathering. Steps descend into courtyards that were cut deep enough to find cooler air, but not deep enough to reach water.

Light is scarce. A permanent veil hangs over the plain, thin enough to show a blurred sun but thick enough to turn its light the color of pewter. The buildings gather what brightness reaches them. Their upper surfaces slowly change angle through the day, and at dusk a weak, greenish illumination passes down their walls into the gardens. Some towers remain dark until a person approaches; then a window-sized patch brightens ahead of them, keeping pace along the street.

Rain comes irregularly and seldom lasts. Afterward, the broad pavements hold dark puddles in shallow depressions, and the planted roofs drip for an hour or two. Most of the city’s water arrives more quietly: fins on the buildings comb moisture from the warm air, and fine threads strung between towers collect condensation. At morning the threads are silver with droplets. The water runs into sealed cisterns, or down transparent channels where roots can reach it.

The gardens are lush but carefully spaced. Trees with broad, soft leaves grow in sunken beds, their trunks wrapped in pale climbing plants. Ferns fill the shaded sides of walls, and thick mats of flowering groundcover spread wherever the soil is deep enough to hold moisture. Beyond each green enclosure there may be a hundred meters of bare plaza. The plants do not appear to want those open spaces; their runners stop at the edges of the beds and curl back toward the shade.

People move through the city, though rarely in crowds. They cross plazas on foot or travel in small vehicles that glide along the elevated lines. The vehicles are almost silent, and they do not always follow the visible track. At certain junctions they leave it, passing through a short interval in which their outlines become difficult to hold in view, then reappear farther along. A pedestrian can use some of these crossings too. From the outside, each looks like an ordinary gap between two walls; entering it brings the traveler out several streets away, with the faint sensation of having missed a step.

The crossings are marked by low blue lamps. They close during rain, when the air inside them becomes unreliable. Residents wait under the covered walks until the lamps return, listening to water tap the roofs and run through the collectors.

Animals are uncommon. Small gray lizards live among the warm stones of the garden walls, and a few long-winged birds nest high on the towers, descending at dusk to hunt insects over the cistern vents. Sometimes something larger moves beneath the groundcover, lifting a line of leaves without showing itself. The city’s caretakers leave those beds alone.

Most days the prevailing sound is a soft, intermittent ticking: collectors adjusting, branches touching glass, water falling one drop at a time into storage. From the higher walks, one can see the planted districts continuing across the plain, separated by empty streets and squares. Here and there a new building is taking shape. Its walls rise slowly from a prepared foundation, guided by machines too small to distinguish individually. By evening another pale edge catches the last available light, still sharp and unworn.
