The town fits inside a shallow basin, no larger than a few adjoining fields. Its outer wall follows the rim so closely that, from outside, there is little to see but a low ring of stone and the pale backs of roofs. Within, every strip of ground has been claimed: by houses, drains, steps, cisterns, covered workspaces, or the narrow passages between them.

Water is never far below the paving. It gathers in seams between fitted slabs and stands in the bottoms of doorways, held back from the houses by small clay sills. After rain, the passages become a connected sheet of water a finger deep. The inhabitants lift their stored grain, turn their hearthstones on edge, and wait for the slow drains to take it away.

The stone here is young-looking: dark, sharp-edged, and broken in broad clean planes. It rises through the floor of the basin in several places, where the builders have incorporated it into walls rather than try to cut it away. Thin shelves project over the lanes. Droplets form beneath them even on dry days, fed by water moving through the rock. Some shelves bear rows of small clay cups to catch the drips; others have been left bare, and shine where generations of hands have steadied themselves.

The houses are close enough that their roofs almost touch. Roof water runs from one to the next through little spouts of hollowed wood and baked clay, making its way down toward a central pool. The pool is walled, but not grand: it is irregular, crowded by steps, washing ledges, and the squared-off foundations of buildings. Its surface is usually disturbed somewhere, though seldom by more than a ring or a brief upward bulge. Water enters it through cracks in the floor as well as through the roof channels.

There are few plants. A gray moss clings to the shaded sides of walls, and a coarse, dark grass grows in the handful of places where silt has collected above flood level. People protect these patches with loose stones; the grass is useful for tying bundles and stuffing cracks. On the outer rim, where the wind reaches, a few stunted shrubs lean away from the basin, their branches nearly bare.

The air is cool and smells of wet clay, stone dust, and woodsmoke. Fires are small. Smoke slips out beneath roof edges and hangs for a while in the lanes before finding its way upward. In ordinary daylight the town is neither bright nor dark: the upper walls receive a little sun, while the passages remain under a soft, silvery shade reflected from water.

It is a quiet place for one so crowded. The common sounds are close at hand: a stone being tapped into place, water ticking into a cup, the rasp of a scraper on a hide. Occasionally a bird lands on the rim wall and calls once or twice before flying on. Small pale creatures live beneath the wet paving stones; after a heavy rain, people find them in the drains and return them to the cracks, saying that they keep the water passages open.

The level of the pool changes through the day, but not quite with the rain. Sometimes it rises under a clear sky, and sometimes it falls while water pours from every roof. The inhabitants watch a set of notches cut into one of the central steps. When the water reaches the third notch, they open the lower gates in the outer wall and let it spread over the flat land beyond. By then, the lanes are already shining, and the first householders are setting boards across their thresholds.
