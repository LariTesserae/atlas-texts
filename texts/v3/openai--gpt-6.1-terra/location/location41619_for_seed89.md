High on the mountain, a narrow opening leads down into a chamber no larger than a village square. The surrounding peaks hold snow in their shaded cuts, but here warm air rises from deep fractures in the rock. It carries a mineral smell and enough moisture to bead on the ceiling. In the afternoon, a thin blade of daylight reaches the upper wall. Below it, the chamber remains dark.

The stone has been worn into rounded ribs and shallow basins. Long ago, water passed through in greater quantities, hollowing pockets and leaving narrow shelves; now it arrives as condensation and occasional seepage. Drops gather slowly, fall, and disappear into the gravel floor. A few hollows retain a dark film of water. The bare rock between them is warm and dry to the touch.

Life occupies almost every available surface. Bats hang shoulder to shoulder in the high folds, their bodies shifting faintly as they breathe. Below them, pale beetles and mites work through accumulated droppings. Crickets crowd the cracks; moths rest in the sheltered entrance and sometimes stray farther in. Small spiders have stretched their webs across the paths between stones. There is little green growth: a dusty tuft at the mouth, a few patches of moss where water persists, and nothing much deeper inside.

The chamber is loud even when nothing large is moving. Water ticks into the basins. Crickets call from hundreds of separate crevices, their sounds merging into a rough, continuous rasp. The bats murmur overhead, and now and then a portion of the colony stirs, sending a dry clatter of claws and wings along the ceiling. At dusk many of them leave through the narrow opening, carrying the noise outside with them. They return gradually before morning, fed by insects from the slopes and valleys below.

On one ledge, a mouse turns over loose flakes of stone, looking beneath each for larvae. When it finds a beetle too large to bite through readily, it pins the insect against the rock and worries at the shell. Fragments collect in the ledge’s shallow depressions, then slowly break down among dust, droppings, and shed wings.

The place changes by small increments. A seep darkens one rib of stone for a season, then retreats; a basin dries and its animals move to the next. Moisture continues to gather on the ceiling, and the mountain continues, almost imperceptibly, to yield its grains to the floor.
