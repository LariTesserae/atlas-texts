The sea covers an old mountain country. Its highest ridges lie just beneath the surface, where waves break against them even in calm weather; its valleys fall away for hundreds of meters into blue-black water. From the surface, the region looks like a broad, restless shoal, streaked with foam. Underneath are miles of rounded summits, terraces and drowned cliffs.

The rock is very old and worn smooth. Long before the sea reached it, ice and rivers had pared the mountains down, leaving broad shoulders and deep troughs rather than sharp peaks. The water has continued the work: it rolls stones through channels, scours pale hollows in the bedrock and gathers sand in the lee of every outcrop. Here and there a harder seam stands above the surrounding floor, its flanks polished by generations of grit.

Kelp grows thickly on the shallower heights. Its long brown stems rise in bunches from the stone, then spread beneath the surface into a canopy that bends and snaps back with the surge. Red algae and encrusting growth cover the rock below. In the bright upper water, fish move through the forest in dense, quick-turning schools; farther down, anemones crowd the sheltered faces of boulders, and crabs pick among the broken shells. Seals hunt along the ridges, appearing suddenly through the stems and vanishing over a drop. The water is cool enough that their bodies look substantial, built for keeping warmth.

The currents do not hold a steady course. Tides drive them through the gaps between summits, and every swell sends another pulse over the shallows. Kelp streams flat, lifts, twists together, and tears loose. Clouds of sand pour off the ledges, briefly hiding the fish before settling again in the quieter pockets. In the deeper valleys the movement is slower, though even there loose fronds descend and rise on the returning water.

A few iron structures stand among the rocks: the remains of a survey tower on one exposed ridge, a line of pilings, a working turbine bolted into a narrow channel. The turbine turns whenever the tide runs, its cable following the seabed toward a distant shore. Mussels cover its housing; fish shelter behind it, then scatter when a seal comes through.

Sound carries throughout the water: the heavy rush of each swell crossing the ridges, stones knocking together in the channels, kelp rasping against rock. Close to the turbine there is also a low mechanical thrum. It comes and goes under the louder noise of the sea, which is continually shifting around the old, almost motionless shape of the land.
