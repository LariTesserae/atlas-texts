The highland goes on beyond the reach of sight. Its floor is pale mineral, folded into broad ridges and shallow basins, all of it worn smooth by ages of drifting grit. From one ridge the next looks close enough to cross in an afternoon; by evening it has scarcely grown larger.

The light is hard and abundant. It gathers on the white ground and returns from below, so that even the undersides of things are illuminated. Shadows have crisp edges and a faint violet cast. The air is cool enough that the bare stone never warms much, though the day is long.

Across the open flats, vegetation grows in immense, widely separated colonies. Each is lush in itself: a mound or low forest of thick, overlapping fronds, so dense that nothing of the ground can be seen through it. The nearest colony might be a dark green island several kilometres across, its margins feathered with new growth. Beyond it there may be a day’s journey of bare salt and stone before another rises into view.

The plants have no leaves as thin as the leaves of ordinary trees. Their blades are padded and waxy, jointed like fingers, and filled with slow-moving oils. At their bases, pale root-mats spread through the mineral dust, taking what they need from stone and air. Some grow in tight spirals, some in arching fans; the oldest stand higher than cliffs, their crowns spreading until their weight brings them gradually down. Fallen fronds remain where they land, bleaching and crumbling into a fine powder in which younger plants take hold.

There is no rain. Far above the plateau, cold currents draw veils of powder across the bright sky. Sometimes these descend in slow, immense sheets. The living colonies bend beneath them, trapping grains in the sticky folds of their outer fronds. Afterward, the plants unfold again over several days, shedding what they cannot use. The deposits alter the slopes a little; the slopes guide the next movement of dust. Nothing here is still for long, but almost nothing moves quickly enough to follow at a glance.

The land bears the marks of much older movement. Long escarpments have been planed back into terraces. Ridges that may once have been sharp now rise in smooth, parallel swells. In places the ground is furrowed by channels that never carried water: paths cut by dense, slow currents of powder flowing from the higher basins. Their banks are lined with mineral grains polished almost round.

Animals are scarce. Once in a while a pale, many-jointed grazer emerges from the edge of a colony and crosses the open ground, casting a small, precise shadow. It feeds on the powder left by fallen plants, then disappears into the dense growth. Something larger follows these routes at long intervals. Its tracks are deep and widely spaced, but the animal itself is seldom seen.

There are sounds, though distance leaves room around each one: the faint brushing of fronds against fronds, a dry patter as a crown sheds its burden, the soft hiss of grit moving over stone. Even the great dust veils descend almost silently. When the air clears, the colonies stand farther apart than before, each surrounded by the bright, empty breadth of the plateau.
