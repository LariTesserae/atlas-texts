The high country is warm even after sunset. A range of low ridges runs across it, their rounded backs rising and falling for miles beneath an almost continuous cover of trees. From a distance they resemble ordinary hills. Up close, their slopes are made of pellets, husks, fragments of leaf and wood, and the fine, worked earth of countless old nests.

Termites have built much of the ground here. Their galleries fill it from the ridge crests down into the hollows, where generations of collapsed chambers have settled into thick, irregular terraces. Roots bind the terraces together; rain cuts narrow channels through them, which the termites slowly repair. Old mounds soften into humps and acquire trees of their own. New walls rise against their flanks. There is no clear edge between a hill and a nest.

The canopy is dense enough that the floor stays dim through most of the day. Ferns spread from the damp sides of the ridges, and vines hang in curtains between trees whose roots grip the porous slopes. Epiphytes crowd the branches overhead. In the deepest shade there are only faint patches of grey-green light, broken by the movement of wings. The crowns above are sunlit and dry out quickly, but below them the air holds a warm, stale dampness.

Rain comes in brief, heavy falls. Water beads on leaves, runs down trunks and disappears into the worked ground. For a little while, brown streams pour through the gullies and collect in hollows lined with fallen leaves. Most of these pools shrink or seep away; a few remain under the thickest shade. Between rains the upper surfaces become friable, while the deeper chambers stay moist. At night, condensation gathers on leaves and drips for hours after the sky has cleared.

Animals occupy nearly every layer. Ants move in broad files over the roots. Beetles shelter under loose bark, and spiders stretch webs across the passages between trunks. Frogs gather at the remaining pools. Lizards hunt on the warmer mound faces, slipping into cracks when birds pass overhead. Bats roost in deep cavities and come out under the canopy at dusk. Above them, monkeys travel through the crowns, shaking down leaves, fruit and small branches onto the animals below.

The noise is immense. Cicadas sustain a high, grinding chorus from the branches; frogs call from the hollows; birds cry from one ridge to another. Even the quieter sounds accumulate: claws on bark, wings brushing leaves, the steady fall of debris. After rain, the frogs grow louder, and when one group falls silent another can still be heard farther down the slope. In places the chorus presses so hard against the ear that the small sounds close at hand are visible before they can be heard.

The shape of the country changes slowly. A trunk falls and opens a patch of light; vines and young trees begin to fill it. A gully deepens over many rains, then catches leaves and silt until roots can cross it again. The termites keep working through the ground, adding to the ridges and closing the breaks in them, while the forest grows over their older work. From one season to the next the same dark passages remain, crowded, warm and loud.
