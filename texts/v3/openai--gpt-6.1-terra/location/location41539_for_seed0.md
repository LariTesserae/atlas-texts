The land is low and broad, a plain of black, newly risen ridges extending beyond sight. Their edges are still sharp enough to catch and hold fallen leaves. Between them lie shallow basins, some no wider than a body, others spreading for miles. In the dark they are known less by their outlines than by the changes in sound: the closing-in of a hollow, the long, unbroken roar from an open flat.

It is warm. The ground gives off the day’s stored heat long after the last dimness has gone from the sky. Rain comes irregularly, sometimes in a hard rush that lasts only minutes. Much of it vanishes into cracks. What remains gathers in stone cups and under the thick litter of leaves, where animals find it by scent. Toward morning, condensation forms on the cooler ridges and runs down them in fine, brief threads.

Plants cover almost everything that has stopped moving long enough to hold soil. Broad leaves overlap in heavy tiers, their upper surfaces dark and waxy, their undersides pale as raw flesh. Creepers cross from ridge to ridge and sink roots wherever they touch down. Ferns crowd the basins; tough, jointed growths thrust up through the fresher fractures. Where the canopy opens, a faint gray light reaches them for a few hours at a time. It is enough for the slowest plants, while others draw much of their nourishment from the dense fungal mats below.

The vegetation is continually being consumed and replaced. Whole banks of leaves shudder as something feeds inside them. Broad-backed grazers push passages through the growth, followed by smaller creatures that strip their dung and comb seeds from their fur. Swarms rise from the soil in such numbers that they brush the face like thrown grit. Some have translucent wings; some have none and travel over the leaves in flowing bands. Beneath them, pale jointed hunters wait with their limbs folded flat.

The noise is immense. There is the rasp of insects, the deep repetitive calling of animals hidden in the basins, the snapping of stems and the rush of bodies following bodies. From time to time a chorus begins on one side of the plain and runs across it, each group answering before the last has finished. The sound presses against the ear until it becomes difficult to hear anything nearby. A large animal can approach almost unnoticed, its breathing lost in the general uproar.

Movement passes through the landscape in waves. A ridge gives way under the weight of wet growth, exposing a bright seam of fresh stone. Animals scatter over it while the loosened plants slide into the hollow below. Before long, the first rootlets are probing the cracks. Elsewhere a feeding swarm empties a patch of vegetation down to bare stalks; by the next rain, shoots are already pushing between them.

There are creatures here that turn stones to get at what lives underneath. One kind carries a pebble in its mouth and drops it repeatedly on the hard pods that collect beneath the trees. When a pod breaks, others rush in, shrieking, and the pebble is lost among their feet. Nothing is built to last.

In the deepest dark, small lights appear in the litter: the green points of feeding larvae, a dull glow where a fungus has spread through a fallen trunk. They illuminate almost nothing. More often the landscape is felt as warmth, damp leaves brushing against the skin, and the tremor of countless bodies moving through the ground. Beyond the nearest ridge the same life continues, basin after basin, loud enough that even its distances seem crowded.
