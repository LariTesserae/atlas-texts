The plateau begins above the cloud line and keeps rising, not into peaks but into distances. Its surface is a dark mineral skin, warm enough to feel through the soles of your feet. In places the skin has only just hardened: long black ridges stand sharp as broken glass, and the ground between them shines faintly red through narrow seams. There has been little time for weather to soften anything. Even the valleys have crisp edges, as though the land were still deciding where to fold.

Far away, banks of cloud gather at the same height as the plateau. They never quite cover it. The light is dim and copper-colored, filtered through a permanent haze of fine ash and living spores. At midday the horizon is visible as a dark band; at other hours it is difficult to tell whether a distant shape is a mountain or a thickening of the air.

Vegetation grows wherever the heat escapes slowly enough. Great mats of fleshy leaves overlap in the hollows, their upper surfaces dull and leathery, their undersides crowded with pale threads. Stiff, jointed stalks rise above them and branch into fans that turn imperceptibly toward warmth. There are forests here, though no two neighboring trees have quite the same idea of height: some spread flat over acres, while others lift smooth trunks into the haze and hang their foliage from the tops like heavy curtains. Their roots grip the fresh stone and descend into cracks that open and close with the cooling of the plateau.

Water appears briefly and in abundance, then vanishes. A pocket of cold air may settle over a ridge and leave every leaf beaded with condensation. A rainstorm can pass through a single valley in silence, filling its sharp-sided basins before the porous rock draws them down. Animals gather at these temporary pools with remarkable speed. Long-legged grazers arrive first, stepping delicately among the blades of stone; behind them come burrowing things, winged things, and small luminous scavengers that seem to have traveled inside the shadows of the larger animals. By morning the pool is often gone, and the basin is crowded with tracks that end at its edge.

The larger creatures move in loose herds through the foliage. Their hides are patterned with mineral plates, and many carry gardens of moss, tiny insects and seed-bearing vines. Some stand motionless for hours with their flanks against the warm rock. Others migrate continually, following changes in the temperature beneath the surface. Predators travel among them without much visible urgency, slender animals whose bodies flatten when they lie down; from a distance they resemble folds in the ground. Above them all, broad-winged flyers circle in the haze, gaining height on currents too faint to feel below.

The land is continually at work. Beneath the plateau, chambers fill with heated vapor and empty again. Mineral valves swell shut, release, and seal; long veins of conductive crystal carry charges between one ridge and another. At intervals the vegetation along a seam brightens in sequence for hundreds of miles, each cluster passing a blue-white pulse to the next. The leaves close as it passes, then reopen. Seed cases split. Animals turn their heads in the same direction.

There are stretches where the process becomes immense. From a high ledge you can see ranks of dark stalks bending and straightening across the plain, drawing vapor from fissures and feeding it into bladder-like growths at their bases. The bladders expand, change color, and collapse; a fine metallic dust settles over everything nearby. Later, beetle-like creatures arrive to gather the dust on their legs and carry it into the rock. Nothing here has been assembled, yet the plateau processes its own materials on a scale that makes the forests look like moving parts.

It is surprisingly quiet. Most of the motion produces only a low rustle, the soft ticking of cooling stone, or a deep pressure felt in the chest rather than heard. Herds pass with a sound like dry hands brushing leaves. Even the storms arrive with little thunder. Occasionally a sharp crack carries for a great distance when a new fissure opens, and afterward the small sounds return around it.

Distances are less dependable than they first appear. A ridge may remain visible for days of walking without coming nearer, while a stand of trees glimpsed at the edge of sight can be reached in an hour. This happens most often when the electrical pulses are passing through the ground. Animals seem to account for it: migrating herds will suddenly divide, half turning toward a feature that cannot yet be seen, the other half continuing along a route that may no longer lead where it did. The two groups sometimes meet again by evening, approaching from opposite sides of the same hill.

The plateau changes shape quickly but leaves few ruins. A new escarpment can lift during the night, carrying an intact forest on its back. A basin may flatten beneath a skin of fresh rock while its inhabitants climb onto the surrounding ridges. Occasionally a whole section of landscape becomes difficult to look at directly: the trees appear to occupy several positions at once, their trunks crossing and uncrossing without breaking. When the uncertainty passes, the forest is usually still there, though its paths lead elsewhere and unfamiliar animals are feeding beneath it.

At the highest elevations, where the air should be cold, warmth rises steadily from the ground. Here the vegetation grows lower and denser, packed into cushions between upright sheets of stone. Small animals shelter in the cushions, and larger ones come to feed, exposing bright wet interiors that close over within hours. Clouds form below the ledges and drift through valleys that may not be in the same places tomorrow.

From one of these ledges the plateau seems almost still. The dark forests extend into haze; faint lines of light travel under their crowns; distant weather gathers and disperses. Only after watching for a while does the movement become apparent: a slow turning of herds, a ridge easing upward, a patch of cloud descending to become water, and the leaves closing one after another across a distance too great to measure.
