The ground opens beneath a stand of thorn trees, where their roots have pried apart a shelf of limestone. A person can descend sideways through the gap, bracing against warm stone, and come out in a hollow no larger than a small grove. The ceiling is low at the edges and rises toward the middle. Even there, its dark ribs seem close enough to touch with a long branch.

Light enters through the gap and through a few narrow seams overhead. It reaches only partway down the walls. The rest of the hollow lies in a green-brown dimness that slowly separates into leaves, ledges, and passages just wide enough for a small animal to pass. The air is hot and nearly still. Stone holds the day’s heat long after the light above has weakened.

Water comes irregularly. After rain, it threads through the ceiling and runs down the old channels in the walls. Most of it disappears into a crack in the floor; some remains in shallow basins worn smooth over many seasons. Between rains, the basins shrink to dark thumbprints, and moisture gathers instead on roots and cool patches of stone. The floor is mostly dry, a soft grit of limestone, seed husks, and powdered leaves.

The plants keep close to the water’s uncertain paths. Ferns crowd the damp ledges, their fronds overlapping so tightly that dead ones hang for months beneath the living green. Pale vines descend from the roof, finding their way toward the basins. Broad-leaved plants grow in the brightest seam of light, while a finer, velvety growth spreads across the stone behind them. Fungal fans rise from pockets of rotting root, each one sheltering a thin dark space underneath.

Those spaces are occupied. Beetles move beneath the fans and through the leaf litter. Tiny pale crickets gather along the edges of the basins. Moths rest in folds of fern, and long-legged spiders span the narrow gaps between stems. Several small lizards live among the ledges; when one shifts, the insects nearest it become still, though farther away they continue to feed and climb. Near the entrance, bats hang in a shallow fissure, tucked so closely together that their bodies look like one piece of rough bark.

There is movement throughout the hollow, but little noise. A lizard crosses dry grit with a faint brushing sound. A drop falls after a long pause. Wings stir once in the fissure and settle again. At times the softest sound comes from above: wind passing through the thorn trees, heard through layers of root and stone.

The hollow has been here long enough for its edges to round. Old waterlines scallop the walls, and roots have thickened around fallen slabs until they seem to be holding them in place. Near the lowest basin, three stones have been set together in a rough crescent, their inner faces stained by water. No path leads to them now. Fern roots have grown over one stone, and a small lizard uses another as a lookout.

When rain finally reaches the hollow, the change begins at the ceiling. Dark patches spread across the limestone; droplets swell at the tips of roots. Then the old channels shine, the basin fills, and insects emerge from the litter to drink. The flow may last only an hour. For days afterward, animals visit the remaining water, plants lift and uncurl, and the dampness retreats slowly into the stone.
