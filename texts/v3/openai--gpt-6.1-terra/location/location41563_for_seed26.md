The basin lies below the surrounding ground, though there is no earth at its bottom. Its floor is made of interlocking iron plates, laid in long shallow folds that gather water into channels. The plates are new enough to retain their casting marks. Along the seams, pale mineral crust has begun to form, but the corners are still sharp and the bolts stand proud.

The basin is large enough that its far wall disappears into condensation. From the center, one can see several districts of it at once: a low expanse of flooded grating, a field of upright cooling fins, a bank of dark machinery, and, farther off, the faint angular outline of a crane moving along a rail. The ceiling above is also built, a span of black panels and girders suspended beyond clear sight. Water collects there and falls in intermittent showers. Some of it freezes on the way down and arrives as fine, hard grains.

It is cold throughout. Frost grows around the outlets of pipes and on the shaded sides of machinery. In the open channels, water keeps moving under a thin skin of ice, warmed just enough by the works beneath the floor. Every so often a pump starts and sends a surge through the basin. The ice breaks with a sound like a sheet of metal being folded, and the water rises over the nearest plates before draining away again.

Most of the light comes from work lamps fixed high on the walls. Their beams are yellow and narrow, and the wet air catches them long before they reach the floor. Between them there are broad areas of dimness where surfaces can be distinguished only by their reflections. Red indicator lights burn on a few distant engines; when vapor passes in front of them, whole stretches of the basin seem briefly to glow.

There are plants, though not many. A wiry sedge grows in the channels where grit has accumulated around the bolts. Its roots have to hold fast against the pump surges, and its leaves are flattened and silvered by the cold. Dark moss occupies the upper edges of a few warm pipes, leaving most of the metal bare. In one sheltered pocket beneath a staircase, a patch of tiny white growths has spread across a spill of insulation fiber. Nothing grows in soil, because no soil was brought here.

The noise is continuous but uneven. Fans roar behind the walls; a chain drags somewhere in the cooling field; water hammers down a shaft at the northern end. Over these comes the deep, regularly spaced impact of a press, transmitted through the plates underfoot. When it stops, which it does several times a day, the other sounds suddenly seem farther away. Then the press begins again, and loose washers tremble in their recesses.

Animals are difficult to find. Small blind crustaceans live in the warmer channels, grazing on films that form inside the pipes. Once in a while one is swept out onto the grating, where it folds its jointed body tightly and waits for the next surge. A few pale moths shelter in the lamp housings, emerging when the glass has warmed. Their wings are often marked with black dust.

The basin is still being assembled. At the far edge, the crane lowers another plate into place while workers in insulated coats guide it with hooked poles. A welding arc flashes blue through the mist. Beyond that light is an unfinished drop, where cables hang in loops and the pumped water vanishes into an unseen receiving chamber.

The machinery seems to control the weather as much as it controls the water. When the large exhaust fans reverse, fog pours down from the ceiling and settles over the floor; when they reverse again, it lifts in ragged columns. The plants open their narrow leaves during these wet intervals. The crustaceans leave the pipe mouths. Then the air clears, the exposed plates frost over, and everything small retreats toward a source of heat.
