At this altitude the land has spread into a vast, almost level darkness. The horizon is difficult to find: black ridges rise only a little above the plateau, and beyond them the sky is blacker still. The air is thin and bitter. No snow lies continuously here, but frost gathers in the hollows and vanishes from the exposed stone by morning, even when no sun is visible.

The plateau is mostly empty space. Broad shelves of dark rock lie miles apart, divided by troughs so deep that their bottoms can only be guessed at from the slow drift of colder air. Along the shelves grow mats of vegetation, thick and luxuriant as forest canopy, though scarcely knee-high. Their leaves overlap in tight, oily rosettes, blue-black on top and silver underneath. When the wind passes, whole acres turn silver and then dark again. Long roots descend through cracks in the stone, seeking the small, cold reservoirs left by fog.

Moisture arrives intermittently. Some nights a cloud settles over the plateau and beads the leaves with water; on others it passes overhead without touching them. The plants can wait a long time. When condensation does come, they move it inward through their fleshy stems, and a faint tremor runs across each mat as its millions of leaves tighten. By the time the fog lifts, the rock is dry again. The water has gone below, into roots and animals and chambers too narrow to see.

The animals are numerous but widely dispersed. Pale grazers, low to the ground and jointed like folding tools, travel through the vegetation in patient lines. They leave almost no trace: the clipped leaves close behind them, and their padded feet make no sound on the mats. Smaller creatures live beneath the foliage, where warmth accumulates. Sometimes a hunter crosses an open shelf, visible only because the silver undersides of the leaves turn toward it in succession. The grazers stop feeding. Long before the hunter reaches them, they have sunk out of sight.

Deep in the rock, the roots draw up mineral-rich water and pass it among themselves. The exchange generates weak currents. At certain seams the stone grows warm, and metallic salts gather in branching deposits, only to dissolve and gather again elsewhere. In the darkest hours, narrow bands of dull red move beneath the vegetation as these reactions travel across the plateau. It is a kind of industry without machinery: material lifted, separated, stored and returned, on a scale that would take many lifetimes to follow.

The ridges and troughs have kept their forms for a very long time. Their edges are worn but not soft, their faces marked by old channels that no longer carry water. Change is quicker in the thin living layer. Clouds arrive, animals feed and vanish, leaves turn, roots send their slow signals through the stone. Almost none of it can be heard. Standing on one of the open shelves, you might hear a dry leaf settle or a single drop fall into a crack, then nothing for an hour, while the dark ground continues its work around you.
