Beneath the harbor there is a street no longer than a room. It lies inside a seam in the bedrock, below the lowest foundations of the city, and every part of it is full of water.

The rock is pale and newly split. Its faces meet in clean angles around the street, with none of the rounded ledges that centuries of currents might have made. Along the floor, a scatter of sharp mineral flakes lies where it fell. The place may have opened only recently, or it may be made fresh again each time the machinery runs.

The street has four buildings, if they can be called buildings. Their fronts are fitted so closely into the stone that they have no backs. Doors, windows, drainpipes, balconies and narrow stairways occupy every available surface. A staircase climbs the side of one house and continues across its roof, where it disappears into a bright joint in the rock. Another house has a row of small windows behind which water moves in the opposite direction from the water outside.

There is no empty space between things. Cables lie against pipes; pipes are fastened to walls; thin sheets of water slide between them, carrying grains of mineral, bubbles, loosened paint and pulses of light. The doorways are crowded with glass vanes that turn without quite touching one another. Even the cracks in the stone are packed with finer structures: silver threads arranged in branching ladders, each rung vibrating at a slightly different speed.

The water is cool enough to draw heat from bare skin. It is clear close up, though the far end of the street wavers behind layers of moving current. Light comes from the seams between the paving stones. It is steady, bright and almost ordinary, giving the pale walls the color of wet limestone and making the little metal fittings shine. When a current crosses a seam, its shadow moves over the facades like a cloud passing over a neighborhood.

There are a few plants. Narrow green filaments cling to the underside of one balcony, sheltered from the strongest flow. On the roof beneath the staircase, a low mat of dark growth has found purchase in a dusting of mineral grains. It spreads slowly over the sharp edges. Nothing grazes it. Nothing swims through it. There are no animal tracks in the sediment, no shells in the corners.

The noise is immense for such a small place. Water forces itself through the glass vanes with a layered whine; pipes knock in rapid sequences; the paving gives a deep, continuous hum. Now and then a pressure door opens somewhere inside the rock, and the whole street answers with a hard metallic chord. The sound travels along the cables and returns as a finer, higher note. A listener would feel it in the teeth and ribs before distinguishing its parts.

The street appears to be an instrument built for managing the city above. Each doorway measures a different pressure. Each window admits a current whose temperature and mineral content are adjusted before it is sent onward. The balconies carry devices that fold water through distances too small to see: a stream enters a brass-colored mouth and emerges at the far end of the street colder, heavier and moving upward. Occasionally a bead of water hangs motionless beside one of these mouths while the rest rushes past. It remains there until a pipe knocks, then rejoins the flow.

At intervals the direction of the street changes. The vanes stop, reverse and gather speed; sediment lifts from the paving; the green filaments flatten against their balcony. For a few moments all four buildings shine as trapped air is drawn from their joints in threads of silver. Then the deep hum settles into a new pitch, and the suspended grains find different places to rest.

No one is present to tend it. Still, the little infrastructure keeps making adjustments. A shutter closes by the width of a hair. A seam of light brightens under a patch of fresh mineral. High on the roof, the dark mat grows over the edge of a stone flake, while beneath it the staircase conducts a current into the rock.
