Below the lowest service floor, behind a panel that is still warm from its welding, there is a room small enough to cross in six steps. Its walls are made of overlapping ceramic plates, pale as unglazed dishes. The plates do not quite meet. Between them, dark seams widen and narrow as the room settles around its machinery.

It is very cold. Moisture gathers on the bolt heads and the undersides of pipes, then travels in slow beads toward a shallow trough in the floor. The trough has been cast rather than worn, but a thin new lining is forming inside it: mineral-white powder carried out of the walls by condensation. Once or twice a day a valve opens somewhere above, and a brief, fine rain passes through a grille. Most of it freezes on contact. The rest disappears into a slot beside the trough.

The floor is a close arrangement of manufactured things: ribbed tiles, cable housings, narrow rails, capped sockets awaiting equipment. In three sockets, small tufts of engineered moss have been planted to monitor the air. Their color is difficult to distinguish in the dimness, except when a drop slides across them and their tips lift. They grow very slowly, fed by salts released from the ceramic. There are no insects among them, no tracks, no sign that anything larger has found a way in.

A low electrical hum comes and goes. Behind the plates, a mechanism advances with the restraint of a clock: a soft engagement, a pause, then a sound like a cup set down in another room. Each time it works, one plate shifts a fraction outward. Fresh sealant wells up at the edge, glossy for a few minutes before the cold dulls it. The room is being assembled from behind its own walls.

There is one light, a narrow strip set into the ceiling. It brightens briefly when water reaches the trough. In that light, faint lines can be seen crossing the tiles—outlines of fittings that have not yet arrived. Condensation collects along those lines before it collects anywhere else. By the time the mechanism brings a fitting into place, the floor has already drawn its shape in frost.
