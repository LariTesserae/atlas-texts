Below the coastal plain, a broad chamber runs for several miles beneath a low roof of clay and limestone. Its floor is nearly level and lies under warm, shallow water. From a rise near the middle, the space opens in every direction: ranks of stone pillars, pools silvered by distant lamps, and beyond them more pillars fading into haze. The ceiling hangs close enough in places to touch with a raised hand. Elsewhere it lifts into darkness, where the sound of water seems to go on climbing long after the roof has disappeared.

The ground is still being made. Fine silt settles from the water in soft, even layers, burying pebbles, shells and the occasional dropped tool. Pale mineral deposits grow around cracks in the rock. On the higher patches, the mud has only just become firm enough to bear weight; a boot sinks slowly, and the water wells up around it without any visible current carrying the print away. There are few carved channels or fallen boulders here. Most surfaces look swollen and unfinished.

Although the chamber is open on the scale of a valley, there is scarcely an empty inch within it. Water occupies every depression and beads across the stone. The shallows are crowded with small snails, worms and translucent shrimp, which stir the silt in a continuous fine haze. Crabs shelter against one another beneath flakes of mineral crust. Blind cave fish move slowly through the deeper pools, nosing among their neighbors. At the warmest vents, larvae pack the submerged rock so densely that their movement gives it a faint, living nap.

Plants are scarce. A few thin ferns grow under a shaft where daylight leaks down through a grate. Around the lamps, green films spread over damp masonry, and short strands of moss cling to the cables. The rest of the chamber is gray, brown and cream: clay, shell, wet stone and water. A smell of minerals and warm mud hangs close to the floor.

Scattered through the basin are the remains of a drainage works. Narrow walkways stand on iron legs; some end abruptly where a section has sunk into the mud. There are three low pump houses, widely separated, and a line of pipes that vanishes under the water. One pump still runs. Its motor pounds behind a corrugated metal wall, while water forced through the pipes roars at the joints. Leaks from the roof strike the flooded floor by the thousands. The low ceiling catches and returns every noise until the whole chamber is deafening. Even far from the pump, a person must shout at the shoulder of another to be heard.

Little seems to change from hour to hour. The pump beats, the water trembles, the crowded animals feed. Over years, the settling silt lifts the floor by small degrees. It gathers around the legs of the walkways and creeps up the sides of the pump houses, making new ground so slowly that its progress can be seen only against the things people left standing in it.
