High in the supporting structure of an old atmospheric engine, there is a chamber no larger than a garden shed. It has no door. The way in is through a maintenance seam that widens, very slowly, around a warm body and closes again behind it.

Inside, the floor rises in rounded shelves of black ceramic. Water runs over them in thin, continuous sheets, gathering in thumb-sized basins before slipping through pores too fine to see. The shelves were printed with sharp edges long ago. Now they are scalloped and hollowed by the passage of water, and the channels have settled into courses the machine no longer tries to correct.

The air is warm and wet enough that droplets form on the underside of each ledge. Light comes from a few dull amber seams in the walls and from the water itself, which gives off a faint gray sheen where it moves fastest. There are only a handful of plants: wiry, dark-green growths anchored in cracks, each holding a little crown of stiff leaves above the flow. Their roots reach into nutrient lines buried in the ceramic.

The chamber is crowded with animals. Pale, jointed grazers the size of grains of rice work over the wet surfaces, cropping films of bacteria. Flat-bodied hunters wait in the basins, their fringed legs spread against the current. In the air, minute flying things collect in the warmest pockets, then scatter when a droplet falls through them. All of them descend from organisms made for this place. Some still have traces of their manufacture: repeating silver bands beneath the skin, or eyes set in pairs so regular they resemble apertures in a panel.

The noise is overwhelming. Beyond the chamber walls, pressure turbines turn inside the larger structure, and their vibration enters through every surface. The shelves amplify it into a roar that fills the little room. Water rattles in the pores; the thin leaves buzz against one another. The animals feel one another’s movements through the floor and leave chemical trails in the water. A hand pressed to the ceramic can feel the hunters moving long before the eye finds them.

There is a cycle to the room, though it is hard to see at first. Warm mist enters through the ceiling and condenses on the ridges. The water feeds the films, the films feed the grazers, and the grazers draw the hunters out into the open. Every few hours the flow deepens, washing shed skins and waste into the pores. Far below, the engine sorts what it takes in and returns it as water, heat and food.

Nothing here is untouched. Even the weather arrives through a valve. Yet the chamber has been left to run for so long that its small inhabitants have altered it: widening channels, thinning films in some places and building them thick in others. In the dimness, the wet shelves shine wherever they have been recently cleared.
