The place is a shallow basin no wider than a hamlet, enclosed by a rim of stone so worn that it rises only a little above the wet ground. From inside, the rim looks like a low wall. From outside, it is almost invisible: a slight darkening in the bright grass, a curve where rainwater gathers.

The rock has been here long enough for its first shape to be beyond guessing. Its surface is soft-looking but hard underfoot, polished into broad hollows and shallow channels. Water moves through them in clear films. In places it emerges from the stone itself, not as a spring but as a continuous beading, as though the basin were slowly sweating. The air is warm, and after each brief rain a fine vapor lifts from the ground. By midmorning, sunlight reaches every part of the hollow.

There are only a few large plants. Three trees stand far apart, their pale trunks divided near the ground into smooth, arching limbs. Their crowns are dense with small leaves, but between them lie wide stretches of open stone, water, and low green growth. Ferns flourish in the channels. Moss makes thick islands on the higher shelves. A creeping plant with glossy round leaves spreads across the pools, leaving irregular windows of clear water between its mats.

At first the basin seems almost empty of animals. Then the ground begins to resolve.

The dark stitching along a fern stem is a row of tiny clinging creatures, each no larger than a grain of rice. Translucent bodies drift beneath the pool plants, hundreds to a single leaf’s shadow. The moss is full of slow, jointed things that part its fronds from within. On the exposed stone, minute animals gather around the wet hollows, their bodies flattened so closely against the rock that they resemble a tarnish. Wherever a drop falls, a pale crowd converges on it and disperses again.

There are larger animals too, though they keep their distance from one another. Long-legged grazers stand in the warm shallows, cropping the underside of floating leaves. Their necks bend in several places, allowing their heads to remain submerged while they watch the air above. Small, broad-winged hunters pass between the trees without flapping, dropping occasionally into the vegetation and rising with something held beneath them. At the edge of the basin, a creature like a smooth-backed tortoise rests in the sun while scores of smaller animals move over its shell, feeding on the film that grows there.

All this life makes surprisingly little sound. The hunters’ wings are fringed. The grazers put their feet down carefully in the soft water. Even the insects seem to communicate mostly by touch or by brief changes in color. The most persistent noise is the faint ticking of drops arriving in the rock hollows. Now and then a tree limb gives a low creak as its weight shifts in the heat.

Near the center stands a structure about the size of a garden shed. It has no visible joints and no door, only an oval opening through which a person could stoop. Its walls are thin and silvery from one angle, dark and transparent from another. A shallow layer of water lies inside, although the roof is intact. Tiny swimmers gather there in dense, turning clouds.

A second structure has fallen against the rim. It consists of six narrow arches, some broken, growing progressively smaller toward one end. The spaces beneath them are cooler than the surrounding air. Rain that enters the largest arch passes slowly through the others, hanging for a while as suspended beads before settling into a trough at the far end. The animals drink there. Whether the arches were made for them, or have simply continued their work after their makers left, is impossible to tell.

The basin changes steadily through the day. The water films retreat from the highest stone, exposing beds of tiny eggs, then return when clouds pass over and the air cools. Animals move with the water: grazers step into newly filled channels; hunters follow the grazers; the nearly invisible crowds spread over fresh wet surfaces. By late afternoon, the trees are casting broad shadows, and the swimmers in the silver structure have thinned into orderly ribbons.

Toward evening another short rain crosses the basin. It makes a close, soft sound on the leaves and brings the scent of warm stone into the air. The hollow brightens again afterward, its shallow pools reflecting the sky. For a while the animals are easiest to see: countless small bodies crossing those bright patches, each leaving a wake that closes almost at once.
