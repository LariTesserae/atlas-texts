Beneath the lowest quarter of the city is a courtyard no one can see from the streets. It occupies the space between four thick walls, scarcely wider than a room, with a floor of pale, worn saltstone. The old builders laid it in fitted blocks, though centuries of heat and grit have rounded their edges until the seams are shallow grooves.

The courtyard is mostly empty. Its center holds only hot air and a little drifting dust. Around the margins, however, green growth rises thickly from cracks in the stone: broad, dark fronds with waxy surfaces, some taller than the doorways. They are rooted in beds of porous mineral and feed on compounds drawn from the ground and the air. Their sap is a slow-moving oil. There is no water here, either in the plants or beneath them.

The city was built low in a broad basin, where the heat gathers and stays. Above the courtyard, overlapping roofs and narrow alleys admit little light. A few copper-colored bars of it move across the walls during the day, and the fronds turn slowly after them. Most of the time their upper surfaces remain almost black, while their undersides catch what dimness there is and show a dull green.

Openings in the walls lead to passages too small to stand in, old flues, and the backs of neighboring dwellings. Their stones were shaped with hand tools; thumb marks remain in the hardened clay packed between some of them. The passages connect this little enclosure to the rest of the buried town, though their farther reaches are choked with grit. No one lives here now. There are no animals, and no animal sounds.

Even so, the courtyard is loud. Heat climbs through the flues in powerful drafts, making loose stone shutters knock against their frames. Fine grains skitter along the paving and strike the walls with a dry hiss. When the wind changes, the fronds press together and part again, their stiff edges rattling like thin sheets of fired clay.

The plants are active in slower ways. New leaves unfold in the hottest hours, pushing through the old canopy at the walls. Spent leaves curl, crack, and drop into the mineral beds, where their oily tissues darken the dust. At night the stone releases its stored heat, the drafts reverse, and for a while the empty center fills with a turning column of pale grains. By morning they have settled mostly back into the seams, and the first bar of light is already reaching across the floor.
