The enclosure is about the size of a small village, though from within it seems possible to walk much farther. Its outer wall is built of hand-pressed clay bricks, still sharp at the corners. Above the wall, an even daylight falls from a pale surface too close to be called a sky.

The ground has been made as carefully as the wall. There is no soil beneath it, only layers of fired tiles laid over one another, with powdered gypsum packed into the seams. The tiles rise in low ridges and shallow basins, so that the place has the shape of a landscape without ever having been one. Here and there a ridge ends abruptly in a neat stack of unused tiles. Nothing has had time to wear smooth.

A warm, dry smell of resin fills the air. Trees grow from clay sockets in the paving: short, many-branched things with thick green ribbons for leaves. Their roots do not seek moisture. They spread through the packed seams and feed on waxy compounds worked into the gypsum by the builders. Every surface that will hold a root has been planted. Pale fronds crowd beneath the trees; little rosettes occupy the gaps between fronds; a dark green nap covers the shaded sides of the bricks. The leaves are supple and cool-looking, but snap cleanly when bent.

Animals are everywhere, though most are still. Gray insects lie lengthwise along the leaf ribs, their bodies so flat that they disappear when viewed from the side. Plump, six-legged grazers move through the undergrowth, shaving fine curls from fallen leaves with stone-colored teeth. In the crowns, small furred creatures sleep with their tails wrapped around the branches. They wake one at a time, turn toward the light, and make faint clicking sounds into their own fur.

The enclosure was assembled with simple tools. Scraper marks remain on the bricks, and the tiles bear the prints of woven mats on which they were dried. The planting sockets were shaped by hand; some still preserve a thumbprint under the glaze of hardened resin. There are no roads, only passages left between the beds, narrow enough that leaves brush both shoulders. The plants have been trained around them, but slowly and imperfectly: a frond may be tied back with fiber in one place and have grown across the path a few steps later.

Near the center stands a low platform of fitted stone, made without mortar. A scatter of discarded shells lies on it, each shell no larger than a fingernail. If one is lifted and placed on the ground, another appears on the platform in its place. This is not an illusion of counting: the new shell has its own small chips and markings, and the animal that once lived in it can sometimes be found nearby. The shells accumulate very slowly. The platform is almost bare in the morning and lightly strewn by evening.

Other things here also have more than one place. A tree may cast its shadow across a path while its trunk stands behind a wall. A grazer entering a thicket can be heard chewing from within it for several minutes before it arrives. The paths themselves are reliable only while someone is walking them. Seen from the platform, a passage may end at a brick wall; entered from the other end, it leads past three trees and returns to the platform by a different route. The animals seem accustomed to this. They pause at junctions, feeling the air with their antennae or whiskers, then proceed without haste.

The quiet is dense rather than empty. There is the dry whisper of a leaf being eaten, a brief patter on a tile, the occasional soft knock of a ripe seed case opening. No wind reaches the enclosure, but the canopy shifts over the course of a day as the plants turn toward the pale ceiling. By evening the passages have subtly narrowed. By morning, the leaves have drawn back again, leaving fine green dust on the shoulders of anyone who passed through.
