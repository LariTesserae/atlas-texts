The plain has no horizon at first. Heat presses a low ceiling of gray vapor against it, and the ground continues into that vapor without changing color: a vast, dim expanse of fitted clay plates, each one still soft enough to keep an impression. Water stands in their seams. It is warm, almost body temperature, and so shallow that the seams gleam without seeming to contain anything.

Nothing here has been quarried from an older earth. Beneath the plates are more plates, laid wet upon wet, and beneath those are beds of hand-rolled pellets, and beneath those an enormous depth of packed mud scored with the marks of wooden tools. Even the hills are made. They rise only a little above the plain, broad-backed heaps of unfired bricks, their courses irregular and their tops unfinished. At their feet lie the molds that gave them shape, half sunk in water, with no tracks leading away.

The place is young, though no one could say when it began. An edge may hold the sharpness of a fresh cut for miles. A wall may extend across the plain for an hour of walking and end in a wet thumbprint. There are no gullies, no rounded stones, no long work of wind or water. When a surface fails, it does not wear away: it softens all at once and folds, as a sheet folds when lifted from one corner.

The builders are absent, if there were builders. Sometimes the wet clay bears marks of hands much larger than a person’s, the fingers narrow and jointed in unfamiliar places. Elsewhere are the regular impressions of rope, woven reed, baskets. The reeds themselves are nowhere to be found. No roots penetrate the ground; no seeds float in the water. The materials seem to arrive already worked.

Across the plain, structures are continually coming into being with the modesty of manual labor. A ring of bricks takes shape one brick at a time. A shallow channel lengthens, its banks pinched up as though by invisible fingers. A roof of overlapping clay tiles is assembled on the ground, then slowly becomes upright beneath itself. There is no grand machinery, no bright source of power. The work proceeds by increments so small that it can be missed, yet over a single humid afternoon whole districts appear: low courts, retaining walls, stairs leading down into dry cisterns.

The districts do not remain where they were put. In the dimness, a wall bends into a circle; the circle opens and becomes a straight wall again, now farther away. A pool may spread across a courtyard without crossing the paving between them. Sometimes the same brick is visible in two places at once, recognizable by a deep diagonal scratch. If one copy is moved, the other stays. By morning both may have acquired the scratch of a third.

Rain falls often, though the cloud ceiling is so low that drops seem to detach from the air only a few feet above the ground. They make almost no sound on the wet clay. The louder noises are occasional: the hollow settling of a newly made chamber, the soft clatter of a brick turning over, a long hiss as warm water finds a passage through packed pellets. Far away, something collapses with the sound of a heavy cloth being shaken. There is no echo until much later, when it comes back from a direction where no walls stand.

Water gathers wherever the making has left a depression. Some pools are clear; others contain fine red clay that rises in curtains from the bottom without a current. Along their edges, pale films form and break apart. They may be mineral precipitates or a kind of life too simple to have a recognizable body. They leave delicate branching stains on the paving, but nothing grows upright, and nothing moves through the water under its own power.

The heat does not lessen in the dark intervals. Light seeps down through the vapor as though filtered through many layers of wet cloth, and the clay holds it for a time after the sky has dimmed. Then the plain glows faintly along its newest seams. A person walking there would see distant foundations drawing themselves out across the ground, line after line, while older rooms quietly fill with water.

At times the work accelerates. Walls rise and slump in the same breath; a field of brick molds turns itself inside out; rain hangs in rows beneath the cloud and then strikes all at once. The ground trembles without cracking. For several minutes every shallow pool shows a different arrangement of the buildings around it. When the disturbance passes, the air is still hot, the plain is still almost silent, and an immense new enclosure stands where there had been open space.

Its gate is low and carefully made. Inside, there is only damp paving, a little standing water, and room enough for another city.
