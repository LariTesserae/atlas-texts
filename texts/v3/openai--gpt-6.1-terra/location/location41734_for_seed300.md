The basin is hardly larger than a village square, and lower than the fields around it. Its rim is a close-set ring of pale rock, worn into shelves and hollows by water that has had a very long time to find its way through. Even in daylight the stone looks dim beneath its wet skin. Rain collects in the grooves, seeps through the walls, and gathers on the floor in overlapping pools.

The pools are never still. Water rises through one bed of gravel while another drains away; a sheet slides across the rock, folds around a pillar, and disappears under a shelf. Pebbles shift continually in the shallows, turning over one another without traveling far. By the time a pool has cleared, a fresh plume of silt is already spreading through it. The place changes quickly, but keeps its familiar shape: channels open and close within channels that are centuries old.

There is little soil. A few wiry plants grow in cracks above the usual waterline, their roots exposed and washed clean. Dark films of algae coat the submerged stone, except where the current strips them away. Now and then a water beetle is carried out of hiding, paddles against the flow for a moment, and vanishes beneath a flake of shale. A grey bird occasionally visits the rim, standing so still that it resembles one of the broken posts.

On the eastern side, a low brick pump house leans against the rock. Its roof has fallen in, leaving the iron machinery open to the weather. One wheel still turns whenever water finds the culvert below it, though the belt it once drove is gone. Short lengths of pipe emerge from the basin wall and end abruptly, crowded among natural spouts of water. It is difficult to tell which leaks the builders intended to collect and which appeared later.

The air is cool and moist, with the smell of wet iron. Despite all the movement, the basin is quiet. The water runs in thin sheets over smooth stone, making only a faint hiss; pebbles knock together from time to time, and the old wheel gives a soft click on each revolution. After heavy rain, bubbles rise through the deepest pool in orderly rings. The rings expand, break against the crowded edges, and are replaced almost at once.
