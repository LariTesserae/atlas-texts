The town occupies a shallow hollow scarcely wider than a few streets. From the higher road, its roofs are difficult to distinguish from the growth around them: broad leaves overlap sheets of corrugated iron, and creepers have drawn green seams across the brick walls. A fringe of old trees marks the edge of the hollow. Beyond them, the ground rises gently and dries.

It rains often, though not always heavily. Water rests in the joints of the paving and gathers beneath the raised thresholds of houses. A narrow canal runs through the center of town, its banks held by stone that has softened at the corners and darkened almost to black. Moss grows in the mortar; ferns take root wherever a little soil has collected. The canal moves slowly enough for fallen leaves to spread across its surface, but its water is always being drawn away somewhere under the buildings.

The streets are close-set and shaded. Planters crowd the steps, vines cross from balcony to balcony, and roots have lifted sections of pavement into low ridges. Some of the growth is tended and some merely accommodated. Doorways are cut clear with shears; gutters are kept open; young trees rising too close to a wall have their branches trained outward over the street. The air is warm, with the smell of wet brick, crushed foliage and engine oil.

At the lower end of the canal stands a small mill, its long brick building fitted with tall windows and a roof patched in several shades of metal. It processes the reeds and fast-growing plants harvested from the wet ground nearby. Electric motors turn rollers inside, and a pump draws canal water through the washing rooms before returning it downstream. Bundles of cut stalks lie beneath an awning, their ends dripping onto the loading platform.

The machinery gives the town much of its sound. Through open windows come the steady rattle of belts, the low knock of rollers and the hiss of water under pressure. Rain on the metal roofs can be louder still. Voices carry between the walls, raised against the mill, then fade quickly among the leaves. There are few animals to be seen: now and then a small bird moves in the canopy, or a snail crosses a damp step after rain.

The place changes slowly. Walls are repaired before they fall, channels are cleared when they begin to clog, and plants return to the same surfaces year after year. In the afternoon, ordinary daylight reaches the streets in shifting patches through the canopy. By evening the mill windows glow, and the wet leaves nearest them hold the light after the street itself has darkened.
