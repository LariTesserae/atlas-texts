A town occupies the high shoulder of a plateau, where the land is still sharp from its making. Fresh faces of pale stone rise behind it in steps and corners. Below the outer walls the ground breaks into dark shelves, each one broad enough to hold a garden or a small house before the next drop.

The houses are few, but the town is carefully laid out. Stone lanes run between them and meet at open courts. Low walls divide the slopes into terraces; narrow channels cross the lanes on flat slabs and end in cisterns sunk into the rock. The buildings are made of fitted stone, timber poles, and thick thatch pinned down with more stone. Their doors stand open. A wooden rake leans against a wall, and a basket of chipped flint rests beside a doorstep. There is room between the houses for grazing ground, gardens, and the long views that are part of living here.

The air is cool enough to sharpen each breath. In hollows between the rocks, moisture has gathered on leaves and under eaves. A thin rain hangs over one end of the town, each drop bright and separate in the daylight; the far terraces remain dry. The cisterns hold shallow, clear water. Ferns crowd their rims, and moss makes soft green seams in the masonry. In the sheltered gardens, broad leaves spread beneath tangles of berry stems. Grass grows thick around the houses, though much of the plateau is bare, pale stone.

Animals are everywhere there is cover enough for them. Small grazing creatures stand among the gardens, their dark muzzles buried in the leaves. Birds occupy the roof ridges and the jutting edges of the cliffs. A long-bodied animal with a dense grey coat lies under a bench, and another looks out from the mouth of a drain. Farther along the plateau, a herd is scattered across the open ground, with plenty of space between one animal and the next.

The people are there too: at the cistern, in the courts, on the steps outside their doors. One holds a clay bowl under the edge of the rain. Another rests both hands on the handle of a digging stick, looking toward the herd. Their clothes are woven from coarse plant fibre and fur, and their tools are of wood, stone, and a little worked metal.

Nothing in the town is passing. The rain does not fall farther; the animals do not lift their heads or take another mouthful. The people remain in their postures without weariness. Even the fine beads of condensation stay on the fern tips, too heavy-looking to believe and too steady to doubt. The place has room for every stage of a day’s work, but no hour in which one stage gives way to another.

A faint sound rests over it all: the mingled note of a bird, a voice at a doorway, and water at the lip of a stone channel. It is quiet enough that the wide spaces seem quiet also. In the clear light, a person could see the lanes descending from terrace to terrace, then the open plateau beyond, and the crisp, unweathered mountains holding the horizon.
