The place is a hollow near the summit of a drowned mountain, no wider than a small cathedral. Its rim rises into darkness on every side. The water above it is warm and almost black, but not deep enough to conceal the occasional smear of copper light, arriving from a surface that may be very far away or just beyond the next fold in the water.

The mountain is young. Black stone juts from the hollow in thin, sharp fins, their edges hardly softened. Between them lie pale slabs that have broken loose but not yet settled: they lift a finger’s width, strike the bottom, and lift again. The water rushes through the fins with such force that it seems to have edges of its own.

The noise is immense. Stone rattles against stone; currents strike the rim and return as overlapping blows. A continuous low note shakes the hollow, with brighter cracks breaking through it so often that there is almost no interval between them. The sound is not simply heard. It presses against flesh and fills the mouth. Now and then a slab splits cleanly, and for a moment the noise acquires a new, higher pitch, as if the mountain has grown another voice.

There is very little growing here. A few threads of dark weed cling in the lee of one fin, flattened so completely by the current that they resemble stains. In another crevice, translucent bulbs swell and contract, drawing a little warmth from the rock. Once in a long while, a thin animal passes through the hollow, all jointed limbs and pale undersides. It does not swim against the turbulence so much as find brief passages within it, vanishing into the stone when the water begins to turn.

Near the center is a low ring of loose rocks. Something has arranged them, though not tightly or well; each surge takes one away, and sometimes another is found in its place. Inside the ring, the bottom is covered with a fine white grit that never scatters. The noise is loudest there. The grains tremble individually, but the surface they make together remains perfectly still.

The hollow does not always contain the same amount of space. At times the far rim is plainly visible, its points crowding close behind the ring of stones. Then the current changes, and the rim recedes into a distance too great for the dim light to cross. The slabs on the bottom continue to strike at the same rate in either case. A creature taking shelter beneath one may emerge to find its crevice on the opposite side of the hollow, without having moved.

Occasionally the water goes clear enough to reveal a ridge continuing upward beyond the rim. Its fresh edges climb toward the copper light. They seem to rise for a long way before the turbulence closes over them again. When they next appear, the ridge may be descending instead, and a few white grains may be falling slowly from it, toward the ring where nothing scatters.
