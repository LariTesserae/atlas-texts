The land lies low and nearly level, a broad basin of red-brown earth worn into shallow swells. In daylight it might seem exposed, but now the moon is down and a thin veil of cloud has taken the stars. The basin is almost entirely dark. Shapes appear slowly: the rounded top of a thorn tree, the pale shoulder of a bank, the glossier black of leaves close at hand.

The heat has not left with the sun. It sits against the skin, dry at first, then damp beneath the trees where the air scarcely moves. Rain fell some days ago. Most of it has sunk away or evaporated, but a little remains in hoof hollows and in the clefts between roots. Water beads on broad leaves toward morning. The ground smells of warm dust, bruised greenery and the faint sourness of wet bark.

Vegetation fills the basin unevenly. Tangled thickets occupy the shallow channels, their branches woven so tightly that even a small animal must know a way through. Beyond them are stretches of coarse grass, open enough to cross but crowded at ground level with runners, seed heads and low, tough plants. Old trees stand wide apart, their roots exposed where seasons of runoff have carried the finer soil downhill. The channels have rounded edges; nothing here looks freshly cut. Termite mounds rise among the grass, some still occupied, others softened by years of rain and used as shelter by animals.

At first the place seems quiet because so little calls attention to itself. Then the dark begins to disclose its inhabitants. Beetles work through the litter in such numbers that a patch of leaves shifts almost continuously. Ants travel the length of fallen limbs. Moths rest underneath leaves, and spiders occupy the spaces between stems. Lizards lie flattened against warm stone. In the thickets, small mammals wait close together in burrows and under roots; farther out, a herd stands in the open, its members occasionally brushing against one another as they graze.

There are frogs around the remaining water, though only one calls, and only now and then. Wings pass overhead without a cry. A dry stem ticks against another, stops, and begins again. The nearest sounds are tiny ones: chewing, a brief scrape, the soft release of soil from beneath a foot. The abundance is less like a chorus than a multitude of separate, absorbed activities.

From somewhere beyond the basin comes a low mechanical hum, faint enough to be lost whenever the insects stir. It holds a steady pitch for a while, then changes and fades. Whatever produces it is too far away to see. Here there are no roads or buildings, only the old contours of the land and the narrow tracks worn through grass by passing animals.

The night advances slowly. Leaves release their stored warmth; insects move to new stems; a few drops gather and fall from a branch into the dust. By dawn the hollows will be busy again, and some of the animals now feeding will have disappeared into shade. The basin itself will look much the same.
