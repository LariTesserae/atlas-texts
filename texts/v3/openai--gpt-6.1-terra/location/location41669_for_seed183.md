The high basin is bright enough to make the eyes water. Its floor lies above the clouds, though the floor itself is uneven: shelves of pale stone climb toward one another, break off, and begin again at different heights. From a ridge you can see the whole basin, a region of perhaps a day’s walk, enclosed by peaks whose upper slopes have been rubbed smooth. The air is cool and thin. Sunlight comes through it without much color, striking every wet surface at once.

Water is everywhere without gathering into anything large. It lies in shallow dishes in the rock, runs through grooves no wider than a finger, and leaks from seams halfway up the cliffs. Rain passes often across the basin, brief and hard. Long after a shower has gone, drops continue to fall from the overhangs, striking the pools so closely together that their surfaces never quite clear. There are no trees, grasses, or mosses along the water. The dark margins of the pools are bare stone, stained by minerals.

The cliffs have been wearing down for a very long time. Their ledges are rounded, their gullies fluted; loose fragments collect in fans beneath them and are slowly carried inward. Yet the basin is crowded with upright forms. Thin fins of rock stand close together across the flats, some only knee-high, others taller than a person. Water threads among them, and the wind, forced through their gaps, makes a low, uneven roar. Now and then a fin cracks with a sharp report and settles against its neighbor. The sound travels from wall to wall.

A few small structures stand among the fins: rings of fitted stone, a roofless shelter, a stack of flat slabs with a hollow beneath it. Their builders used what the basin provided. The stones have been split with wedges and knocked into shape; in one shelter, a chipped hammerstone still rests in a niche. Nothing suggests when the work was done. Rainwater runs through the doorway and over the floor, but the walls remain carefully balanced.

Distance behaves differently here depending on whether a surface is wet. A ridge reflected in a pool may appear nearer than the ridge itself. If you walk toward the reflection, the pool can remain beside you for several steps, its edge keeping pace over the stone, before it is abruptly behind you. The stone rings seem to have been placed where this happens most often. From inside one, the surrounding fins hold their positions, even during rain.

There is movement throughout the basin, though no animal crosses it and no plant bends in the wind. Mist rises from sunlit pools while rain falls into others. Runnels join, divide, and disappear beneath the shelves. At intervals, shadows slide uphill against the slope of the ground, drawing together in the mouths of crevices before dispersing again. The scattered shelters cast ordinary shadows, as do the hammerstone and the loose slabs beside them.

By late afternoon the light becomes less fierce, and the basin’s water is easier to see: thousands of separate glints, connected here and there by moving threads. A shower may be falling on the far side while the near stones are already drying. The wind continues through the rock fins, mingled with dripping and the occasional clatter of a fragment finding a lower place.
