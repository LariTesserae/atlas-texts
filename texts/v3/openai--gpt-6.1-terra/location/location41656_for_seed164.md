The plateau is so high that its far edge has its own weather, though the weather never quite arrives. A bank of pale cloud hangs there, level with the ground, and is replenished as quickly as its fringes dissolve. Above it the sky is clear and thin. Below it, the land extends for hundreds of kilometres in broad shelves of black, newly exposed stone.

The stone has not had much time to soften. Ridges rise in clean, steep folds; fractures run straight for astonishing distances. In their depths, water stands close to the surface, dark under a skin of ice. Elsewhere it seeps from the rock in slow, cold beads and gathers in shallow basins. The basins do not fill or empty. Each has an outlet so slight that it is difficult to distinguish from a crack.

Vegetation gathers around these seams of water. There are dense cushions of silver-green moss, forests of broad, stiff leaves no taller than a hand, and glossy black fronds that stand upright through the ice. Some beds are several kilometres across, lush enough that the stone disappears beneath them. Between one bed and the next there may be a day’s walk over bare rock. The plants have no flowers, or none that need to be seen. Nothing flies above them; nothing moves in their depths.

The city is spread through the plateau in much the same way. Its districts stand far apart, connected by elevated pipes and transmission lines that cross the empty shelves on immense, widely spaced pylons. From one district the next can sometimes be seen as a small cluster of chimneys against the horizon. More often it is hidden by the land’s curvature, though its machinery can still be heard.

There are no streets in the ordinary sense, only service decks laid over the wet ground and broad passages between engine halls. The halls are built from dark metal and pale stone, with roofs pitched sharply against a snow that never seems to accumulate. Inside them, turbines turn. Pumps lift water from the fissures and return it farther along the network. Electrical yards hum in the open air, their insulators pearled with condensation. The cables are thick with frost on one side and bare on the other.

The noise is immense. Every district contributes a low mechanical note, and the plateau carries them all. The sound enters through the feet before it becomes audible: a pressure in the stone, then a layered roar with finer rattles suspended in it. Near the pumping stations there is also the hard, continuous rush of water through metal. It is loud enough to make the empty spaces feel occupied, although there is no one in the halls and no trace of animal life anywhere among the pipes.

The machinery does not appear to be producing goods. It keeps the water moving, the lines charged, the ice at the edges of the basins from advancing. In turn, the cold water cools the engines, and the plants hold moisture against the rock around their foundations. The system is too large to see whole, but each district repeats the same arrangement, with small adjustments for the shape of the ground.

Nothing here is motionless. Water runs, frost forms, shafts revolve. Yet the place remains almost exactly itself. A bead falls from a rock face into a basin and another swells in its place. A plume rises from a distant stack to the same height and spreads into the same thin veil. Even the sound seems fixed: a vast chord held over the young plateau, while the clear daylight rests on the wet leaves and the long, empty service decks.
