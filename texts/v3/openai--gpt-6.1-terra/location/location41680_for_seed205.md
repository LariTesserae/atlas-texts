The plateau begins above the last foothills and goes on beyond sight. Its surface rises and falls in long, shallow swells, interrupted by escarpments that have worn back into rounded bays. From a distance it looks bare: a vast ochre country under an ordinary white sun, with widely spaced columns standing on the horizon.

Up close, the ground is alive. What appears to be dust is a crust of tiny, interlocked leaves, browned on their upper surfaces and green underneath. It covers the flats, the slopes, and the exposed edges of the cliffs. When pressed, it gives slightly, then slowly rises again. Beneath it lie thick beds of pale roots, packed so densely that they take the place of soil. Old root beds have hardened into shelves; newer growth pushes through their cracks.

The columns are plants too. Some are as broad as houses, though miles may separate one from the next. Their trunks divide high overhead into thin, level branches, making crowns like open hands. Most of their leaves hang inside the hollow limbs, protected from the heat. At dawn, condensation gathers there and runs down grooves in the bark. Animals wait at the feet of the columns for the first drops.

Water is scarce enough that its arrival changes the whole neighborhood. A brief rain may cross a hundred miles of plateau without reaching the ground: it catches in the crowns and falls hours later as a slow, irregular drip. Where a branch has broken or a cliff has folded inward, water can collect for a few days. Small ferns uncurl around these pools almost immediately. Their fronds dry and fold flat again when the pool is gone, leaving a green stain that remains visible for weeks.

The heat is strongest in the open hollows, where the air shimmers above the leaf crust. Higher up, a steady wind moves through the columns. It carries the resinous smell of the canopy and the sourer smell of the root beds, warmed all day from above. At night the temperature falls enough for mist to form in the deepest folds of the land. By morning, most of it has been drawn into the plants.

Animals are everywhere, although there is room between the largest of them. Under the ground cover, pale jointed grazers travel in immense herds, their passage visible as a rippling across the flats. They eat the undersides of leaves and leave the upper surfaces intact, so that a grazed patch looks unchanged until it darkens. Long-legged hunters follow the ripples, standing still for hours before striking down through the crust. On the columns, climbing animals gather in such numbers that the bark seems to move independently of the trunk. They scrape at it, drink from its grooves, and sleep pressed together in the shade of the branches.

The noise reaches you before any of these animals can be seen. The herds make a continuous dry clicking, each small body striking its neighbors as they travel. The climbers call from column to column, and their calls are answered over distances too great for the animals themselves to be visible. Wind entering the hollow branches adds a deep, uneven roar. Near a stand of columns it is difficult to hear a voice even at close range; in the open, the sound spreads out but never quite fades.

Some evenings, whole sections of the plateau begin to pulse. A low vibration passes through the root beds, followed by another farther away. The columns respond by opening narrow slits along their trunks. Warm air rushes out, carrying clouds of fine seeds and waking the animals sheltered in the bark. No one can predict which section will begin, though the pulses often follow a rain. They may continue for an hour or through the night, traveling slowly toward the escarpments.

People have crossed the plateau for a long time. Their shelters are small and scattered: a lean-to of woven branches beneath a cliff, a low ring of stones around a hearth, a screen of cured leaves tied between two roots. The shelters are placed near columns with reliable condensation grooves, but never directly under them; falling branches are heavy, and the climbers defend the best drinking places.

Travelers carry digging sticks, stone blades, and shallow bowls plaited tightly enough to hold water for a short while. They cut strips from the living ground to bind bundles, then press the loosened edges back together. In an old campsite the cuts can still be found, raised and pale like scars. The plant cover closes over them slowly, but the bowls and hearthstones remain where they were left, islands of worked material in a surface that continues to grow.
