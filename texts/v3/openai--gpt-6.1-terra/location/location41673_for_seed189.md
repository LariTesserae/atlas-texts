Beneath the dry ground is a chamber no larger than a cupped pair of hands. Its walls, floor, and ceiling are one continuous plant: a pale green rind folded around an almost empty space.

The rind is young. Its edges are sharp where new growth has split them, and the seams have not yet softened or darkened. Fine leaves lie pressed against it like scales, each one growing from the leaf beneath. There is no soil inside the chamber. Even the small raised places on the floor are knots of living tissue, tightening and loosening in the warmth.

The air is clear and brightly lit. No opening admits the light; it appears on the inside surfaces at an ordinary afternoon angle, throwing shadows from the raised veins. As the light moves, the shadows sometimes point ahead of their leaves. A leaf may unfold to meet a shadow already waiting for it.

Most of the space between the walls is bare. Now and then a translucent thread grows straight across it, remains taut for several minutes, and is drawn back into the rind. There are no other plants, though the one that makes the chamber keeps producing different kinds of leaf: scales, threads, broad thin membranes that rise briefly from the floor and collapse. Each new form gives the place a different shape without filling it.

The noise is immense. Growth makes a dry cracking sound, like branches breaking close to the ear. The walls hum under tension, then snap in short, overlapping reports as their folds shift. Every few moments a deep note passes through the chamber, swelling until it seems too large to fit there. Afterward the nearest leaves tremble for a long time.

Moisture comes in small events. A cool patch travels across the ceiling; beads gather behind it and hang without falling. They remain while the plant opens its surface to them, then vanish into it. Sometimes a bead contains the reflection of a much larger chamber, or shows this one from a position outside its walls. When the bead is absorbed, that view disappears.

A single pale mite lives somewhere in the folds. It emerges rarely and crosses the open space on one of the temporary threads. Once, it carries a grain of stone before it, rolling the grain with its forelegs until it reaches a soft seam; there it presses the stone in, and the seam opens. The mite feeds at the opening. By the time it retreats, the plant has closed around the grain.

The chamber is still growing. Every so often its walls pull apart, leaving more air between them, but the distance across remains the length of a mite’s slow crossing. The new surface is bright and uncreased. Its first leaves begin to lift almost immediately, adding their small cracks to the sound.
