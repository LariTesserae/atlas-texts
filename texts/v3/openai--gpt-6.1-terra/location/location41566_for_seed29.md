High on the mountain, beneath a skin of black volcanic glass, a crack opens into a chamber no larger than a cupped hand. Its roof is close enough to touch its floor. Fresh faces of rock meet at hard angles; nothing has had time to round them. Along one wall, a seam of pale sulfur has formed in a thin, granular crust.

The chamber is dark except for a dull red gleam deep in the narrowest part of the crack. Heat comes steadily through the stone. The air is dry and carries the sharp smell of sulfur and hot metal. There is no pool, no damp soil, nothing growing that resembles a plant. On the sulfur seam, a film of microscopic life feeds on gases rising from below.

The rest of the space is crowded with small, flat-bodied animals. They overlap in a continuous layer, their dark shells fitted against one another and against the rock. Some graze on the film; others wait among them for a softer body to pass. Their legs move slowly, finding purchase where they can. When one pushes deeper into a crevice, the pressure travels outward through its neighbors, and the whole surface shifts by a few millimeters.

The mountain is being worked somewhere above. Machinery sends a heavy, regular pounding down through the rock, loud enough in this little chamber to make the loose sulfur grains hop. A lower vibration persists between blows, accompanied at intervals by the scrape of a drill. No shaft reaches the pocket, and no tool has entered it. The animals remain packed together through the noise, gripping tighter when the pounding is strongest.

Hours pass with little visible change. Heat rises, gas seeps through the seam, and the living film slowly renews itself. At the crack’s outer edge, a few animals ease toward a thinner place in the crowd; elsewhere, newly hatched bodies fill the spaces they leave. The stone holds its sharpness. Above it, the machinery continues until evening, then stops, leaving the chamber with the faint dry clicking of shells against shells.
