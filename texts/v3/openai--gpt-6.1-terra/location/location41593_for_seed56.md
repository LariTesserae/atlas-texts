The hollow lies just beneath the surface, under a low rise of frozen ground. Its roof is a mat of roots so closely woven that soil has gathered on top of them, making a dark ceiling a few handspans thick. Here and there a root presses through and catches a grain of ice in its fork. The hollow is no larger than a grove, though its edges are difficult to see: leaves overlap leaves in the dimness, and every apparent passage narrows into another living wall.

The cold is severe. A breath would whiten and fall as a fine crust on the nearest frond. Water remains liquid only in thin, dark seams, where the plants keep it moving through themselves. It beads on the underside of leaves and gathers in shallow cups at their bases. Occasionally a cup overflows. The water creeps down a stem, vanishes into moss, and can be heard for a long time afterward, dripping somewhere out of sight.

There is almost no exposed earth. The floor gives underfoot: a continuous pelt of pale growth, thick as turf in some places and delicate as frost in others. Beneath it, new mineral matter is gathering around the roots. The plants draw dissolved stone from the water and lay it down grain by grain, forming soft ribs and nodules that have never been weathered. They are the beginnings of rock, still porous enough to take a thumbprint. In a few centuries they may close around the hollow; for now, they smell faintly metallic when touched.

The plants provide their own faint light. It moves through them in slow bands, not quite green and not quite blue, bringing one layer after another into view: broad translucent fans, mats of tiny curled blades, rigid stalks furred with needles. The light reveals no flowers, but there are fruiting bodies, small and glassy, held close against the stems.

Animals crowd the warmer seams. Blind, many-legged grazers move beneath the floor pelt, raising ridges that settle behind them. Small wingless creatures cling to the undersides of leaves and scrape at the luminous tissue. A larger burrower sleeps curled inside a root cavity, its coat full of living spores; when it wakes, it will carry them only a short distance before sleeping again. The chill slows almost everything here. A change in the position of a leaf may take half a day.

Yet the hollow is loud. The roots draw water under tremendous tension, and each minute contraction sends a deep, resonant note through the living walls. The notes overlap with crackles from forming ice, the rasp of grazers, and the steady ticking of droplets. At intervals the whole ceiling hums. The animals pause when it happens, gripping whatever is nearest, then resume their feeding.

What gives the place its unlikely endurance is also part of its growth. Within the finest veins of the leaves are arrangements far smaller than cells, inherited and renewed as the plants spread. They gather heat from the random motion of the surrounding cold and pass it inward, keeping the water seams open. They also hold the faint light in circulation long after its original source is gone. No machine can be separated from the tissue: cut out a vein and it lives briefly, then fades like any other severed part.

The roof continues to thicken. New tips work into the soil above, feeling around stones and ice crystals, while the oldest roots below become pale and hard. Nothing appears to be happening quickly, but the hollow is being made even now, one grain and one cell at a time.
