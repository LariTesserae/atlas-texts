Beneath a ceiling of dark stone lies a city spread across the floor of an enormous excavation. The roof is too high to see clearly from most streets; its lamps show as a loose constellation, suspended on cables that sway almost imperceptibly. Their light is ordinary and pale, bright enough to cast the shadows of chimneys and gantries across the ground.

Everything that is not machinery seems at first to be plant. The streets are paved with a close-knit growth of flat, gray-green leaves, tough as conveyor belting. The walls of the long factories are pleated bark; their roofs are made from overlapping fronds that have stiffened into shallow vaults. Even the open ground between districts is a living surface, though little rises from it. For long distances there are only the leaf-pavement, the light, and the widely spaced silhouettes of towers.

The excavation is still being made. Along its far edge, cutters ease slabs from the surrounding rock and lower them onto slow carts. Behind the cutters, the exposed mineral is clean and angular, unsoftened by any weather. Spores blown from the city settle in its seams. Within days they open into thin, branching films; within a season the films have thickened enough to support the rails laid over them. The workers do not distinguish sharply between extending the city and planting it.

It is cool throughout the cavern. There is almost no water. The plants draw what they need from mineral dust and from a heavy, waxy vapor released by beds deep under the floor. Their leaves have sealed surfaces and dark pores that open only in the coldest hours. If cut, they exude a clear resin that hardens before it can drip. The air smells faintly of graphite, crushed stems, and warm electrical insulation.

The industrial districts are arranged around broad empty squares. In one, presses stamp sheets of grown fiber into housings for motors. In another, great spindles twist pale root-strands into cable. The machines run at deliberate speeds, and most of their noise is taken up by the fibrous walls: a low pulse underfoot, a muted clack when a press closes, the occasional fine hiss of a belt changing direction. Between shifts, a person can hear the leaf-pavement tightening as it grows.

Animals move across this wide, quiet city in substantial numbers, though they are seldom crowded together. Small six-legged grazers travel between the squares, rasping the mineral crust from older leaves. Long, blind climbers hang beneath the bridges and feed on the mothlike insects that gather near the lamps. In the warm housings around the generators, colonies of soft-bodied creatures tend gardens of fine white filaments; when a machine stops for maintenance, they retreat into the walls in orderly streams.

The people leave broad passages for them. A grazer can bring an entire line of carts to a halt, and the drivers wait while it crosses, inspecting the pavement as it goes. Its scraping opens the leaves to the air, which seems to help new growth take hold. The blind climbers are less welcome: they sometimes mistake moving drive-belts for prey, and each factory keeps a worker with a padded pole to lift them away.

The city is active without ever appearing hurried. Lamps brighten and dim in a long artificial day. Freight crosses the open squares. New roofs uncurl over scaffolds, and old walls are trimmed back from windows. Far out at the cutting face, the first gray-green growth spreads over the raw rock, making another piece of floor where, before long, a road will be laid.
