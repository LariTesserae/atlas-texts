Prepare for a short, self-contained visit. The plateau is not empty ground: almost every apparent patch of grit is occupied, and the one reliable-looking landmark does not behave reliably. You may be able to walk and breathe there, but you should not plan around either assumption.

## Before crossing

Establish a return point that can be operated from the plateau side, and test it with an unoccupied probe before sending a person through. If possible, keep the point continuously visible or physically connected to a base outside. Agree on a return time and a procedure for failure; do not count on a distant rescue party finding you by following directions.

Send instruments first to measure pressure, oxygen, temperature, radiation and the air’s suitability for breathing. The plateau is extremely high, and “cool” does not tell you whether an unprotected person can remain conscious there. Take independent breathing support and a pressure-capable suit unless the measurements establish that they are unnecessary. Carry water and food regardless: there is no visible water to collect, and the local organisms should not be treated as supplies.

Protect yourself against bright light, dry grit and abrasion. A sealed visor, eye protection, gloves and boots with good traction are sensible. Secure grit out of suit joints and breathing equipment. Bring a small light even though the visit is planned for daylight; there is no information about whether daylight changes.

## Moving across the plateau

Choose a short route and travel slowly. The worn shells suggest that others have passed along that path, but they do not tell you whether it is safe or where it leads. A wide, light footplate or a portable walkway may reduce the number of animals crushed underfoot, though either will disturb some of them. Accept that there may be no completely clean way to cross; minimize your route and avoid unnecessary wandering.

Do not use the notched black island as a navigation target. Its appearance from every shelter, and its remaining ahead between shelters, make it a poor measure of either direction or distance. Use several independent aids: a measured tether to the entry point if practical, recorded bearings, time, step count, and an instrumented track. None should be trusted alone. Pay attention to how the tether pays out rather than assuming its length proves the surrounding space is ordinary.

A companion should remain close enough for direct visual contact. Test radios before relying on them, and arrange hand signals: the curtailed sounds and the distant tap make it difficult to judge how well a call would carry. If your route, tether or instruments begin to disagree in a way you cannot resolve, turn back while the return point is still secure.

## At the shelters

Treat the shelters as archaeological sites, not vacant equipment caches. Photograph their contents in place; leave the blades, bowls and cords where you find them. Inhabited shells are living animals, even if lifting a bowl produces no visible response. There is no reason to assume that their stillness means insensitivity, or that removing them would be harmless.

A shelter may offer shade or a place to pause, but inspect it from outside first. Crawling inside could disturb animals at the entrance or damage a fitted structure. Bring your own groundsheet and rest equipment rather than rearranging stones or clearing shells away.

The animals’ immobility is striking, but it is not evidence that the same condition will affect a visitor—or that it cannot. Make any observations or samples remotely where possible, and keep the first visit short. Do not put bare skin against the grit, taste the salts, or handle the plants without containment and a clear reason.

## Leaving

Carry out all waste, and clean equipment at the return point so that grit, organisms and fragments are not transported elsewhere. Record what you actually observed separately from what you inferred: especially any changes in the creatures, the position of the island, the sound, and the behavior of your navigation equipment.

The most useful preparation is restraint. Bring enough life support and navigation equipment to leave promptly, then spend your limited time looking closely. The plateau’s stillness has endured through more than one human visit; it does not need to be tested by staying until something changes.
