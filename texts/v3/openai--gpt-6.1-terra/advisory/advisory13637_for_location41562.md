## Before you go

Treat the basin as an uncharacterized wilderness, not simply a dark place with strange wildlife. Its gravity, atmosphere, weather and biology are unknown. Send instruments first, if you can: check air composition and pressure, temperature, radiation, gravity, rainfall in the surrounding hills, and the speed and depth of water moving through the channels. The rain that fills them may fall well outside the basin.

Arrange a reliable return point on high ground beyond the channels, with enough room to reach it even in darkness or rising water. Visit during a verified dry interval, and leave a generous margin before any uncertain weather. Do not plan to shelter in a hollow if it rains: the hollows are the drainage system.

## What to bring

- **A self-contained protective suit**, with independent breathing supply, until the atmosphere and biological hazards have been assessed. It should resist abrasion and wetting without making it impossible to climb smooth stone.
- **Serious hearing protection.** The roar is described as overwhelming, and it travels through the ground as well as the air. A sealed helmet, hearing protection and a sound-level meter are sensible starting points; have a way to communicate without relying on being heard. If exposure remains painful or disorienting, retreat.
- **Several independent lights and navigation aids.** Carry a headlamp, a separate handheld light and backups, plus a map made by reconnaissance and a way to track your route. Do not count on natural light, or on following a channel back: they cross and rejoin.
- **Stable footwear and a walking aid**, appropriate to the suit. The rounded limestone may be slick with moisture, films or animals. Probe your footing without striking or scraping the rock unnecessarily.
- **Water, food and medical supplies brought from home.** Do not drink the mineral water or eat anything local.
- **A means of monitoring conditions and calling for retrieval**, preferably operated by a teammate or support station outside the basin. Agree on a return time and a procedure for loss of contact.

Keep equipment cleanable and contained. You do not know what local organisms might do to you, or what your own microbes and debris might do there. Plan for decontamination and quarantine on return; do not bring specimens back casually.

## In the basin

Stay on the ribs where you can do so safely, rather than descending into crowded channels. Move slowly. In your light, what first looks like a wet surface may resolve into overlapping animals, and a dark gap beside it may be deep enough to conceal something much larger. Avoid putting hands into cracks or stepping over an edge you cannot see.

The sound will make this an unusually difficult place to read. You may feel it through your feet as well as hear it, and you may not hear an animal approaching. The callers’ changing pulses could reveal movement, but they are not a dependable alarm system for a visitor who does not know their language. Nor can you assume the hunters will ignore you—or be attracted to you—because of your footsteps. Minimize unnecessary vibration and give all animals room.

Use only as much light as you need to navigate. Watch for changes in nearby behavior when it falls on them, and be prepared to withdraw rather than push through a mass of grazers or approach a heavy digging animal for a better view.

Most importantly, **leave before water reaches you**. A dry channel offers a tempting route through the ribs, but it may become a fast, crowded floodway with little warning. If rain begins or water starts moving, head by the preplanned route toward high ground; do not stop to watch the animals rising out of the troughs.

Expect the visit to be exhausting even if nothing attacks you. In the dimness, every few steps demand attention, while the noise makes conversation and orientation difficult. A short first excursion, with frequent checks on your condition and a clear turnaround time, will tell you more safely than an ambitious crossing.
