## Before you go

Treat this as a remote wetland settlement, not a deserted attraction. The town’s emptiness is striking, but it does not tell you whether its inhabitants have left permanently, are elsewhere for a season, or simply do not show themselves to strangers. Plan to visit quietly, leave everything as you found it, and retreat if your presence is unwelcome.

Arrange a reliable way home before entering. If your means of travel can be affected by water, stone, weather, or distance, test it somewhere safe and keep it protected. Leave a trusted contact your destination and a return time. Do not count on finding local help.

## What to bring

- **Sturdy, waterproof footwear** with good grip and ankle support. The stone is sharp, the paving is wet, and some paths disappear beneath clear water. Pack spare dry socks.
- **Light rain protection and breathable layers.** Warm, damp air can make heavy waterproof clothing uncomfortable; wet clothes may still become cold if the weather changes or you stop moving.
- **Drinking water and food** for longer than your intended stay. The pools look clear, but you cannot assume they are safe to drink. Bring a suitable filter and treatment as backup, not as your only supply.
- **A first-aid kit**, including supplies for cuts and blisters. Angular rock makes even a small fall consequential when you are alone.
- **Navigation tools and a light**, with backups. The repeating basins, ridges, and widely spaced houses may be difficult to distinguish once cloud or darkness closes in.
- **A waterproof bag** for your return equipment and anything else that must stay dry. A walking pole could help you test submerged paving without putting your full weight on it.

Clean your footwear and equipment before arriving and after leaving. The lack of visible animals may be normal here, or it may mean something you cannot yet understand. Either way, avoid bringing in seeds, insects, or organisms from another world, and take care not to carry anything out unintentionally.

## Moving through the town

Walk slowly. A crack filled nearly to its lip can look like a shallow seam until you step across it; a submerged path may have gaps or loose stones. Test crossings before committing your weight, and keep well back from the plateau rim, especially in haze or rain.

The roofs offer shelter, but do not assume every structure is sound. Timber platforms may be slick or weakened, and unmortared walls should not be leaned on or climbed. If you need cover, choose a stable-looking overhang without entering a building uninvited.

Leave the waterworks alone. Those little notches, basins, and channels may be essential to how the settlement manages its constant wetness. Don’t wash in them, block them, or take water from them casually.

## What it may feel like

The silence will probably be the first thing you notice: water moving beside a doorway, a breeze in the reeds, but no bird or insect sounds underneath. Give yourself time to adjust. In a place this still, an ordinary drip or shifting stone may sound startlingly close.

Visit first in daylight, with enough time to return before the light fades. Mark your route in a way that leaves no trace, pause often to look back, and resist the temptation to follow the paths indefinitely just because they continue. The town seems designed for patient movement through water and stone; you will experience it best by doing the same.
