## Before you go

Treat the plateau as a **remote, flood-prone wilderness where darkness is continuous**, not simply as a place to explore at night. A person who loses a route, breaks an ankle, or becomes separated from a party may be only a short distance from help and still be unable to find it.

Arrange your arrival at one of the workshops or another known shelter, on a high stone shelf. If possible, make contact in advance and hire a local guide for the entire journey. The inhabitants know which sounds matter, how the ground ordinarily answers, and which routes have changed since the last storm. Do not assume they navigate as you do, or that a spoken greeting or a light will mean anything familiar to them. Agree on ways to communicate and identify yourselves before arrival.

Confirm the basics before committing to a long stay: breathable air, safe temperatures, available food, and whether local water needs treatment. The warmth and abundance of water are encouraging, but neither establishes that the place is safe for a human body.

## Bring more than one way to find your way

Bring reliable lamps or headlamps, spare batteries, and waterproof backups. The description says there are no local lamps or natural sources of light; it does not establish that an imported light cannot work. Test yours at the arrival point. Even if it works, its range in rain, spray, and dense growth may be short, and it may disturb animals or people unaccustomed to it. Ask your hosts when it is appropriate to use.

Do not depend on light alone. Carry a sturdy **weighted staff**, and practice using it with your guide. It can help you feel for a drop, test a surface before transferring your weight, and follow a chain or root line. Its knock may also offer clues about a hollow, but learning the local echoes is not something to improvise above a buried river. A reassuring sound is not proof that ground will hold you.

Bring tactile route markers, cord for controlled crossings, and a way to record the route that does not require sight. A reliable mapping device could help, but do not assume satellite navigation or outside communications will work across worlds. Establish check-in times and a search plan with your hosts. Travel as a group, and decide beforehand how you will keep track of one another in heavy rain.

## Dress for being wet for days

Wear waterproof footwear with good grip and ankle support, while expecting water to get in eventually. Pack spare socks and a dry set of clothes in sealed bags. Use gloves for wet rope-like bark, chains, stone, and machinery—but keep enough dexterity to feel notches and other route marks.

Choose durable rainwear that allows heat and moisture to escape. The air is warm, but prolonged wetness can still damage skin and leave you chilled when you stop. Check your feet and any abrasions regularly. Bring a first-aid kit suited to cuts, sprains, and wound cleaning, as well as any medicines you need for longer than the planned trip.

Hearing is a navigation tool here. Protect it around machinery and the loudest waterfalls, but recognize that ear protection can also conceal approaching animals or companions. Let the guide choose when to stop and listen.

## Move as though the ground may be hollow

The soft-looking mats may conceal chambers, undercut banks, or water flowing through stone. Walk deliberately and keep clear of unsupported river edges, even where the surface feels broad and level. Established bends and smooth channels can still hide dangerous currents and submerged spaces.

Use marked routes and fixed chains rather than shortcuts beneath roots or across open shelves. A root tunnel large enough for you may also be an animal’s regular passage. If you hear movement ahead, stop and give it room rather than trying to squeeze past. Do not enter pools or undercut chambers to investigate them.

The knocking from below is useful information, but the local herds’ behavior is also a warning: when a river changes course underground, the ground above may no longer be familiar even to animals that know it well. If your guide becomes uncertain, turn back or wait on known solid ground.

## Let the weather set the schedule

Plan journeys around sheltered stopping places, and carry food, water-treatment supplies, and warmth enough to wait out a delay. A storm can erase the sounds you depend on while making rivers rise and animals move. Some predators use the downpour as cover.

At the first signs of a major storm, follow local advice toward a known high shelter. Do not camp in a root hollow, beside a river, beneath a drainage outlet, or in a low chamber that could fill. After rain passes, wait for the water to fall and the route to be checked; the landscape may remain dangerous long after the drumming stops.

## Inside the workshops

Stay with a host and keep your hands off machines, shafts, cables, and unfamiliar controls. Workers may recognize a fault by a change in rhythm before you notice anything wrong. Ask where to stand, how an emergency stop is signaled, and what to do if the room suddenly goes quiet or everyone stops work.

The buildings may be your best refuge, but they are working industrial sites, not automatically safe camps. Keep packs and clothing clear of moving parts and drains, and respect the crews maintaining the narrow circuits around them.

Most of all, arrive prepared to move slowly. On this plateau, knowing the way is not just a matter of seeing the next landmark. It is knowing when the ground sounds different, when a bell has started to move in rising water, and when a familiar stretch has become a place to retreat from.
