Prepare for this as an **under-ice expedition into deep, loud, unexplored water**, not as a cold-water dive. The summits are only a few hundred meters below the surface, but that is far beyond ordinary recreational diving, and the trenches may be much deeper. An ordinary traveler’s safest way to see the plateau would be from a pressure-rated crewed submersible, supported by a surface team and an uncrewed vehicle.

### Before you arrive

First establish where your point of arrival will be. You need a dry, sheltered staging area above the water or a vessel capable of operating among thin ice. Do not assume you can open a passage home from inside a flooded compartment or through the ice; test the return route from a safe location, and agree on an extraction schedule with people who will remain outside.

Bring a team experienced in both deep submersibles and ice operations. They should have time to survey the area before anyone descends: map the underside of the ice, locate currents and fissures, measure depth and water chemistry, and listen to the noise at several depths. The plateau’s roar is a practical hazard as well as a spectacle. It may interfere with acoustic navigation and communications, conceal approaching animals or machinery, and transmit vibration into the hull.

Choose a launch site where the ice can be monitored and a recovery opening kept usable throughout the mission. Have an independent means of locating and retrieving the submersible if its communications fail. A vehicle that reaches the surface beneath unbroken ice is not necessarily safe.

### The vehicle

Use a submersible rated, with a generous margin, for the deepest point it could plausibly reach—not merely for the planned summit visit. A few hundred meters of water already impose enormous pressure, and an inadvertent slide or loss of control near a trench could quickly take the vehicle beyond its rating. Keep well clear of drop-offs until the terrain and vehicle handling are understood.

The hull and its occupants need protection from cold and vibration. Ask the operators to assess the sound and vibration levels inside before committing to a long descent; a low note felt through the chest might be merely disorienting, or prolonged exposure could be harmful. Bring hearing protection for use where it does not interfere with essential communication, and establish simple visual or written backup signals inside the cabin.

The vehicle should carry independent power and life support reserves, redundant navigation, emergency ascent and recovery equipment, strong but controllable lights, and cameras that can function in the natural illumination. Its viewports or external cameras matter: much of the experience is the scale of the place, and a tiny screen will diminish it.

Send an ROV first if possible. Let it approach a summit, sample the water, test visibility, and investigate currents around the sharp ridges. The pale mineral fans may be brittle or razor-edged; avoid settling the vehicle onto them or running a tether across them.

### Do not plan to swim

Even if you are an accomplished diver, leave the water to purpose-built equipment. The cold can rapidly disable exposed flesh, and the depth, overhead ice, unknown currents and possible water chemistry make a suit excursion exceptionally unforgiving. The water’s metallic taste tells you little about whether it is safe; do not taste it. Treat samples and wet equipment as potentially contaminated until assessed.

If direct work outside a vehicle is truly essential, it requires a separately planned professional operation with equipment rated for the exact depth and conditions. It should not be an improvisation prompted by seeing something interesting through the glass.

### Around animals and machines

Watch the animals without pursuing, feeding, collecting or touching them. Their sparse distribution may make each encounter feel precious, but their tolerance of the iron towers does not mean they will tolerate a moving, illuminated vehicle. Approach slowly, give hunters and schools a route away, and dim or redirect lights if animals appear disturbed. Clean equipment before arrival and contain anything brought back: even a small exchange of microbes could matter in either direction.

Treat the machines as active industrial sites. The lit lamps and trembling housings suggest they are still operating, but neither their function nor their ownership is clear. Cables entering fissures may carry dangerous energy, move under load, or lead into unstable ground. Keep the submersible and any tether well away; do not touch controls, disconnect anything, or assume an apparently abandoned tower is unattended.

### What it may feel like

The descent could be strangely reassuring at first. There is daylight, and the summits appear almost clean and familiar under it. Then the scale becomes difficult to hold: a ridge that looks nearby may take a long time to cross; a dark gap between peaks may contain an entire mountain range. The thin ice above is also a ceiling, even where sunlight makes it look transparent.

The sound may be the hardest part. Because it never breaks into surf or distinct echoes, there may be no moment of quiet against which to judge it. Give yourself a short first visit, schedule breaks from listening equipment, and arrange a way to signal that you need to leave without having to explain why.

Bring a camera, but spend some time simply looking. A distant tower, a single turning school, or a scavenger tucked beneath a warm casing may be the only visible movement across an enormous view. Return before you are tired or chilled. This is a place that seems still because its processes are so large and continuous—not because anything in it has stopped.
