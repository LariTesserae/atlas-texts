## Before you go

Treat the basin as a small, active geological site, not as a mountaintop clearing. It is beautiful and busy, but its floor is still being made, and the three structures may be doing work you do not understand.

Arrange your arrival and departure **outside the basin**, on ground known to be stable. If your means of travel requires an anchor or gateway, do not place it on the young stone or within the metal ring. Set a return time and an independent recall if one is available. You may not be able to judge distance or find the edge reliably through the cloud.

Before stepping out, check whether the air is breathable and whether its pressure is safe for you. “Above the cloud line” does not establish the altitude or the atmosphere, and the warm gusts from below could carry gases you cannot detect by smell. Bring an independent air supply until the air has been tested; a filter mask alone will not supply oxygen.

A companion is preferable to a solo visit. Agree on hand signals and a turnaround point: the basin is noisy, visibility changes quickly, and the booming may make shouted conversation difficult.

## What to bring

- Waterproof layers for cold rain, with ventilation for the heat rising from the ground.
- Sturdy, insulating boots with good grip and soles that distribute weight. They should protect against both hot stone and wet mineral surfaces; no boot makes an untested shelf safe.
- A helmet and eye protection, especially in rain or when the ground is active.
- A gas monitor and a way to measure oxygen, if they work in this world. Check their readings often, particularly in low places and near gusts.
- Drinking water and food from home. Do not drink from the pools, however clear they look.
- A light, navigation line or reliable local positioning aid, and a means of communicating without relying entirely on sound.
- A camera or notebook. Plan to leave specimens where they are.

Clean your equipment before arrival and after departure. The moss, pool organisms and animals form an ecosystem whose vulnerabilities you cannot know; equally, you should avoid carrying unfamiliar material home on your boots.

## Moving through the basin

Make the first visit short. Pause near your safe arrival point and watch the floor before crossing it. Seams open slowly, but the pools move, mineral rims soften, and some stone is granular or yielding. What looks like a firm route now may not be one later.

Stay well back from the vapor-filled drop and from any fresh shelf at the edge, even if it looks broad enough to stand on. The warmth and yielding surface are reasons to observe from a distance, not invitations to test it.

The booms deserve attention, but do not treat the animals’ calm as proof that you are safe. They belong here and may tolerate conditions you cannot. If the ground movement strengthens, seams open rapidly, the air monitor alarms, or your route becomes uncertain, leave by the planned way rather than waiting to see what happens next.

The pale moss will release water when touched. Avoid stepping on it where you can; a wet track might help you see where you have been, but it also disturbs the patch and may make the stone slippery. Give the grazers and the creatures under stones room. Do not lift rocks to look beneath them or try to handle the flyers.

## The structures

Keep a respectful distance from all three. They may be machines, instruments, living constructions, or something for which those categories are inadequate. Do not touch them, place equipment on them, cross the suspended pane, or try to interrupt the thread of light when it reaches a seam.

The black-glass shelter is tempting, especially in rain. Its early patter would be remarkable to hear, but its open sides offer limited protection, and the animals use it too. Do not enter while they are crowded inside. Even when it is empty, only approach if the route is stable and you have already decided how you will leave.

If the metal ring sounds its low chord, remain clear and watch the ground rather than going to inspect the new shelf. Hearing protection may be useful, though it will not necessarily protect you from vibration transmitted through your feet.

The pane’s refusal of the animals is a boundary worth respecting, even if mist passes through it freely.

## While you are there

Expect the place to feel larger and stranger than its hamlet-sized basin suggests. Cloud hides the drop; every surface is occupied by a small movement or sound. When the vapor briefly opens, the sudden sharp shadows may show how fast the animals have been moving all along.

Stand still occasionally, on ground you have already checked. Listen to the ticking water, the metallic calls, and the boom arriving first through your soles. Watch a pool rather than trying to follow everything at once. Then leave before your safe return time, without relying on the rain shelter, the structures, or the animals to warn you of danger.
