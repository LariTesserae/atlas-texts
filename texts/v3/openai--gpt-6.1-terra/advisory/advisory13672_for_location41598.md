Prepare for a small place that may take a long time to understand. The basin is only about the size of a hamlet, but the vegetation interrupts sightlines and the motor masks quieter sounds. You may be close to your exit or a companion without being able to see or hear either.

### Before entering

- **Arrange a reliable return point.** Mark it in a way you can recognize from inside the basin, and note its direction before the banks and foliage close off your view. If your means of return must remain in place, keep it clear of roots and check it regularly; things left unattended may gradually be gripped or overgrown.
- **Tell someone your intended route and return time**, if that is possible. A companion is useful, but agree on visual signals beforehand: ordinary conversation may be difficult near the pump house.
- **Choose a dry, daylight arrival if you can.** The basin will still be damp, but good light will help you distinguish paths, roots, water and machinery. Don’t count on the motor stopping at night.

### Clothing and equipment

Wear closed, water-resistant boots with good grip, long trousers and sleeves, and lightweight work gloves. You want protection from wet vegetation and abrasion without dressing so heavily that you overheat. A broad-brimmed hat or hood will help with the steady drops from overhead; avoid loose scarves, dangling straps and anything else that could catch in plants or machinery.

Bring drinking water, a small first-aid kit, a light, and a dry bag for essentials. A whistle may be worth carrying, though it might not be audible over the motor. A camera or notebook can help you compare the positions of plants and routes over time, but don’t let looking through a screen distract you from your footing.

### Moving through the basin

Walk slowly and look down before committing your weight. The paths are described as firm, but roots cross them and stones protrude through the woven surface. Wet moss and leaves may be slippery. Your footprints may help on the way back, but don’t rely on them as your only navigation marks.

Keep clear of the settling tank, especially its broken rim. The pale plates on the surface are plants, not a floor or a dependable cover. Their openings change over hours, so a gap or edge you noticed earlier may look different later. Avoid stepping onto overgrowth where you cannot see what supports it.

The vegetation’s movements appear slow, not overtly hostile. Even so, give it room. Don’t wrap tendrils around yourself, push a hand into dense growth you cannot see through, or leave a pack on the ground for hours without checking it. If a plant has begun to grip an item, take your time freeing it rather than pulling hard enough to fall.

### Around the machinery

Treat the pump house as an operating industrial site in a wet environment. The exposed wheel, belts and places where they enter the stems are serious entanglement hazards. Stay outside their reach; do not touch the motor, belts, guards or nearby metalwork, and don’t try to clear vegetation from the moving parts. The leaves maintaining their own narrow clearance are not evidence that there is safe room for a person.

The motor is electric and the surroundings are damp, so assume there may also be an electrical hazard. If you see damaged wiring, sparking, or water running onto electrical components, withdraw. Avoid relying on the brickwork or machinery for support.

Don’t switch the motor off merely to make the place quieter. Its role in the basin is unknown, as are the consequences of stopping or restarting it. Give the belt paths a wide berth even where they disappear into foliage.

### Hygiene and duration

Bring your own water and food. Don’t drink from the pools or tank, and avoid tasting plants or handling unfamiliar growth unnecessarily. Cover cuts, wash or clean exposed skin afterward, and check your clothing and equipment before taking them onward. These are ordinary precautions for an unfamiliar, persistently wet habitat, not evidence that anything there is toxic.

For a first visit, allow enough time to get oriented, but keep the stay short enough that heat, dampness and the constant noise don’t wear down your attention. Pause somewhere stable and well away from the tank and belts. Listen and watch: the hiss of leaves folding around the machinery, the vibration of the water, and the gradual shifts in the plants may tell you more about the place than a hurried circuit would.

Leave before you are tired enough to start treating the basin as familiar. It is quiet in some ways, but it is not still.
