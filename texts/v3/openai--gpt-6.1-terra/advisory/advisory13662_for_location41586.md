Prepare to visit the hollow first by camera, and only later, if conditions allow, in person. It is small, but it is not sheltered: the same opening that lets in the light also admits a current strong enough to bend stone and keep flakes circulating. The water beyond drops into blue with no visible bottom. You should assume that losing your position at the mouth could quickly become an emergency.

### Before you go

Find out how deep the summit lies. “High on a drowned mountain” does not tell you whether it is safe for ordinary scuba, requires technical diving, or can only be reached in a submersible. Measure the water’s temperature, chemistry, visibility and current over a long enough period to capture several reversals. A brief calm may be only the pause between surges.

Record the sound as well. Its apparent loudness in water is difficult to judge from the description, but a sustained underwater resonance could be hazardous. Have someone qualified assess the acoustic measurements before putting a person inside. Earplugs are not a dependable solution for a diver: sound travels through the body, and obstructing the ears can interfere with pressure equalization.

Send a small remotely operated vehicle to the mouth first. It can map the opening, watch how abruptly the current changes, and inspect the growing ridges without placing a body or breathing hose among them. Its tether and thrusters could damage the hollow too, so begin outside, keep propulsion gentle, and be ready to withdraw if the vehicle cannot hold position.

Bring a surface team or other reliable support, independent navigation, communications suited to the environment, and a recovery plan appropriate to the depth. If you are breathing compressed gas, plan for ascent and any decompression with a qualified dive team. Do not count on making an immediate direct ascent from an unknown depth.

### If you enter

Wear thermal protection suited to the measured water temperature, and carry redundant breathing and navigation equipment appropriate to the dive. Avoid loose straps and trailing lines: the narrow mouth, unseen cracks and growing seams offer places to snag. A secure line may be useful, but only if the team has planned how to keep it from fouling the entrance or pulling across the construction.

Wait outside and watch several cycles before committing. Enter only if you can maintain control against both directions of flow, with enough space and time to turn around. Arrange a clear signal for abandoning the visit, and leave while you still have ample breathing reserve. Do not use the back of the hollow as a refuge; the description gives no reason to think the force or noise is gentler there.

The pale stone may feel invitingly smooth, but some of it has not hardened. Avoid handholds, kneeling on the floor, or bracing against the ridges. A fin stroke or a displaced piece of equipment could alter a formation that has taken a long time to begin.

Treat the little shelter and cup as someone else’s work. Do not move the stones to see whether they fall, collect fragments, or put anything in the cup. The seams above it seem to be made by the ringing, but whether the cup or arrangement has a further purpose is unknown.

### What it may feel like

Expect the light to make the room seem calmer than it is. The broad bands will slide over the floor while the water pushes and changes its mind around you. The roar may make it difficult to think or communicate. Keep your visit short enough that you can remain attentive to your position and your partner, rather than becoming absorbed in the canopy.

If you can safely pause near the entrance, watch one ridge through a full reversal, or follow a new seam until it breaks or reaches the ceiling. Then leave the cup, the trembling stones and the small silver animal where they are. The mountain is still making the room; you need not help it decide its shape.
