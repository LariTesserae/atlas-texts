## Before you go

Treat the city as an active industrial site in a hot, poorly drained basin—not as an abandoned place to explore freely. Arrange permission with the works or pumping-station staff, and ask where visitors can safely stay, which roads remain passable after rain, and what the local water and air hazards are. The people who live there will know the daily rhythm better than any map.

Set a reliable rendezvous and departure plan. A distant levee may look easy to reach across the flats, but pools, soft ground, canals and industrial infrastructure could make the route much longer than it appears. If your means of returning between worlds requires equipment or a particular site, keep it above likely flood level and accessible without crossing the basin.

## What to bring

- **Drinking water and food:** Bring enough safe water for the whole visit, plus a reserve. Do not assume that rainwater, canal water or a clear-looking pool is drinkable; filtration alone may not remove dissolved salts or industrial contaminants. Bring food and, for sustained sweating, some means of replacing salt and other electrolytes.
- **Heat protection:** Loose, light-colored clothing that covers the skin, a broad-brimmed hat, and a way to cool down. Arrange access to a reliably cooled or well-ventilated room. The dim sky does not make the heat harmless, and the paving and walls may keep radiating it after sunset.
- **Insect protection:** Repellent, long sleeves and trousers, and a head net if you expect to be near the ponds after rain. Check with locals about biting insects and any relevant disease precautions.
- **Footwear and dry supplies:** Waterproof, sturdy footwear with good grip; spare socks; and sealed bags for anything that must stay dry. Salty water and grit will be hard on zippers, electronics and metal fittings.
- **Navigation and communications:** An offline map, a light, a charged phone or radio that actually works there, and a backup power source. Do not rely on being able to see the city’s far edge through the haze.
- **Basic medical supplies:** Dressings, any personal medication, and a plan for reaching medical help. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.

## While you are there

Move in the early morning or toward evening, as the workers do, but judge the conditions rather than the clock: evening may still be dangerously hot. Rest often in a cool place and drink regularly. If you become dizzy, confused, unusually weak or stop sweating while overheated, get cool and seek urgent help; confusion or collapse in the heat is an emergency.

Stay on known firm routes. What looks like a shallow puddle may conceal a drain, a drop, electrical equipment or contaminated silt. Avoid wading, touching pond water, entering channels, or walking on pond margins without local guidance. Keep clear of pipes, pumps and evaporation ponds, and never enter a tank, culvert or other confined space.

The haze is not enough, by itself, to tell you what is in the air. Ask about current air quality and industrial releases; leave if there is a warning or you notice irritating fumes. A dust mask is not protection against unknown gases.

The older buildings deserve caution. Washed-out mortar, persistent damp and subsidence can make floors, stairs and roofs unreliable. If you want the view over the town, ask whether there is an approved, sound vantage point rather than climbing an apparently empty building.

Finally, leave the pools and their inhabitants undisturbed. The flats may seem empty at first, but the water is supporting a dense food web. Watch from a little distance, especially where birds are feeding or resting.

Expect the city to feel quieter and more inhabited at once than it looks from above: empty streets in the heat, a pump’s low hum, water dripping somewhere out of sight, and movement in nearly every pool. Give yourself time to stop and notice it—but make sure that stopping place is cool, sound and on safe ground.
