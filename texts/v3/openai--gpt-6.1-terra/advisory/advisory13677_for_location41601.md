## Before you go

Treat the plateau as a **remote, high-altitude wilderness with extreme noise and exposure**. It looks broad and almost level from its center, but the description doesn’t establish its elevation, atmospheric pressure, wind speed, temperature, or whether its water is safe for humans. Find those out with remote instruments or an uncrewed visit before committing yourself. In particular, verify that the air has enough oxygen to breathe; if it does not, ordinary altitude precautions won’t be sufficient.

If the atmosphere is breathable but the elevation is high, allow time to acclimatize rather than arriving directly on the plateau from near sea level. Go with a companion, agree on a turnaround plan, and know the signs of altitude illness. Headache with nausea, unusual fatigue, confusion, or difficulty walking is a reason to descend or evacuate; severe symptoms are an emergency.

Arrange a reliable return point and a way to summon help that does not depend on being heard. Mark the return location digitally and, if appropriate, with a temporary, unmistakable visual marker. The land extends for miles in similar pale ribs and basins, and the noise may make it difficult to orient yourself by anything except sight and instruments.

## What to bring

- **Eye and skin protection.** Wear well-fitting, high-UV sunglasses or glacier glasses with side protection, plus a brimmed hat and sunscreen. The reflected light comes from below as well as above. Carry spare eyewear: losing it could make travel painful or unsafe.
- **Hearing protection and visual communication.** Bring properly fitted earplugs or protective earmuffs rated for very loud environments. Noise-canceling headphones alone are not a substitute. Agree on simple hand signals before arrival—especially for stop, danger, come here, and return—and carry a written or electronic backup. A radio with an earpiece may help, but test it against the wind and noise rather than assuming it will work.
- **Wind and cold protection.** Wear layered clothing with a secure windproof outer shell, gloves, and sturdy footwear. Take something to cover your nose and mouth against blowing grit. Even if the air initially feels merely cool, exposure and exertion can change that quickly.
- **Enough water and food for the whole visit, plus a reserve.** The small condensation pools and damp seams are not a dependable supply. They may also be important to the animals. Bring your own water; if an emergency forces you to consider local water, assume it requires treatment and that its chemistry may be unknown.
- **Navigation and emergency gear.** Carry a map or survey, compass, satellite navigation or equivalent, a means of calling for rescue, first aid, and an emergency shelter suited to the actual wind conditions. Secure loose equipment to yourself or your pack.

Use a secure pack without dangling straps. Depending on measured conditions, poles may help on gravel, but don’t let them become something the wind can pull from your hands.

## Moving across the plateau

Keep well back from the rim, especially until you know how gusts behave there. The birds are using rising air at the edges; a force that supports them may be dangerous to a person. Stay together, and choose a conservative route through the basins rather than making directly for a spectacular overlook.

The ground will not be as flat as it first appears. Gravel fans can slip underfoot, thin fins may have loose stone around their bases, and the small pits holding water could be easy to miss in the glare. Walk deliberately and look down often. Don’t shelter beneath an overhang until you’ve assessed whether its stone is stable; a wind-sheltered hollow on open ground may be safer.

The roar is likely to be tiring in a way that is hard to anticipate. You may feel the low vibration through your feet while being unable to hear your companion a few steps away. Plan a short first visit, take breaks in a sheltered place if one can be found safely, and watch one another for fatigue. Don’t rely on an animal cry, a falling stone, or a shouted warning to alert you.

## Sharing the place

At first the plateau may seem nearly empty. Give yourself time to see the grazers among the cushions and the long-legged animals moving between hollows. Watch from a distance, and avoid approaching drinking places or bird ledges. The animals communicate through the ground as well as through sound and gesture; your footsteps and gear may be intrusive in ways you cannot easily judge.

Stay off the vegetation where possible. Those dark cushions occupy scarce pockets of soil and may be slow to recover from a bootprint. Leave the pebbles and stones as you find them, including any that a bird has knocked against a slab. Don’t feed the animals or collect specimens.

Above all, prepare to be both **exposed and disoriented**: there is little shade, little concealment, and an immense sound that can swallow nearby life. With protection, a reliable way home, and enough restraint to leave the damp hollows undisturbed, you can spend your attention on noticing how much is living in what first looks like bare stone.
