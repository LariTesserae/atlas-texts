## Before you go

Treat this as a hazardous underwater expedition, not a visit to a submerged summit. The hollow is violent, unstable, and spatially unreliable. The most important preparation is an exit that does not depend on swimming back the way you came.

If your means of crossing between worlds permits it, establish a remotely operated return point outside the hollow before sending a person. Test it repeatedly with instruments, then with a weighted dummy. It must be callable from the traveler’s position; a doorway fixed at the entry point may become unreachable when the hollow changes.

If you cannot arrange a dependable retrieval, send a probe and watch from elsewhere. There is no obvious safe place in the description where a human could wait indefinitely for help.

## Find out what the water is doing

Do not infer depth from the copper light. The surface may be close, distant, or not reachable along any ordinary upward path. Measure pressure at the intended entry point and continuously throughout the visit. Also measure temperature, water chemistry, dissolved gases, visibility, current speed, and—especially—the sound and vibration reaching the probe.

Observe through several changes in the hollow’s apparent size. Look for whether the water itself carries instruments across the space, whether objects remain in contact when the geometry changes, and whether a beacon or retrieval system still works during the transition. A probe that survives one minute has not demonstrated that a person can safely enter.

The low note and repeated cracks may injure more than hearing. Do not assume earplugs or a quiet helmet make the place safe: vibration can pass through the suit and body. The test should measure what reaches a human-sized load, including the chest and head. If exposure cannot be kept within the limits of the equipment and the traveler, cancel the landing.

## Equipment

An ordinary scuba kit is not enough. You would want a rigid or substantially protected submersible suit, rated for the measured pressure and temperature, with:

- Independent breathing gas and an emergency reserve.
- Protection against impact, abrasion, and sharp rock, including a protected helmet and visor.
- A way to regulate buoyancy and maintain position without relying entirely on swimming.
- Multiple lights, mounted so that one failure or an obstructed beam does not leave you blind.
- Instrument displays that work without sound, with redundant visual or tactile warnings.
- A retrieval control that can be operated with either hand and while disoriented.

Keep the suit’s exterior smooth and snag-resistant. A loose hose or dangling tool could catch on a fin or be drawn beneath a moving slab. Bring a cutting tool for emergencies, but do not count on being able to reach it while pinned.

A tether may help in some conditions, but here it could also pull you into a rock, snag in the fins, or become unreliable when distances change. Test its behavior remotely before using one. If it is used, it should have a guarded quick release and should not be the sole means of return.

Carry as little loose equipment as possible. Every item should be secured, and nothing should need to be dropped or planted on the bottom during the visit.

## Choose a very short visit

Enter only after watching the current long enough to identify a comparatively sheltered approach. The weed’s lee may suggest shelter, but it is not necessarily large enough or safe enough for a person. The thin animal knows passages in the turbulence that a suited human may not fit through or be able to follow.

Do not land among the pale slabs. They are already lifting and striking; one could trap a foot or split under load. Keep clear of the sharp fins and of places where a surge could drive you against them. Do not shelter beneath a slab, even if an animal does.

Stay away from the ring and the white grit. The ring may be a marker, a structure maintained by an inhabitant, or something whose purpose is not apparent. The grit’s stillness amid the turbulence is reason to observe it from a distance, not proof that it is harmless or stable. Do not touch it, sample it, rearrange the stones, or place a beacon there without a compelling reason and a much better understanding of the site.

Similarly, leave the bulbs and weed undisturbed. Avoid contact with the animal or any crevice it enters. A passing creature may be using the hollow briefly, and your presence could block the route it needs when the water turns.

## Navigation and abort rules

Do not use the visible rim as a measure of distance, and do not follow the ridge toward the light. It may appear to rise and later descend; neither appearance establishes a route out. A mapped position relative to the ring or a fin may also cease to mean what it did moments earlier.

Keep the retrieval system ready throughout. Abort immediately if:

- The return system fails a status check or cannot confirm a lock.
- The suit is struck hard, leaks, snags, or loses position control.
- Vibration, pressure, or physiological readings exceed the tested limits.
- Visibility falls below what is needed to avoid the rock.
- A spatial change produces any effect not seen during the probe tests.
- You lose confidence about where a safe clearance lies.

Make the decision to leave early. There may be no useful interval between realizing that conditions are worsening and being unable to operate the controls.

Expect communication by voice to be difficult or useless. Agree beforehand on simple visual signals and automatic telemetry. One person outside the hollow should have authority to retrieve you if your readings or movements become concerning, even if you have not requested it.

## What it may feel like

The darkness will not be still. Light may catch a fin for an instant and then show only a mass of moving water. The noise may make the suit feel as though it is under constant attack; each crack could be startling even when you know another is coming. If the hollow expands, you may lose sight of the rim without feeling that you have moved. If it contracts, rock may seem suddenly close.

Prepare for that disorientation in simulation as far as possible. Decide in advance that the changing view is not a puzzle you need to solve while you are there. Your task is to remain clear of the bottom, observe briefly, and leave.

After retrieval, examine the suit for cracks and contamination before opening it, and check the traveler for injury, including effects that were masked by the noise. Keep the instruments recording: the transition out may reveal more about the place than the few moments spent inside it.
