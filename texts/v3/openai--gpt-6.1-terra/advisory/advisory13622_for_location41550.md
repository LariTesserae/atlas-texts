Prepare for this as a high, cool settlement where the ground can drop away abruptly—and as a place whose stillness may behave in ways no ordinary traveler can predict. The town looks welcoming and inhabited, but open doors and people in the courts are not invitations you can safely interpret.

### Before crossing

Make sure your means of return works **from inside** the town’s stillness, not merely from its threshold. If possible, test the boundary first with a small expendable object and a retrieval line. Check whether the object moves, whether it can be pulled back, and whether any change persists after it returns. A watch, light, or remote sensor may also help, though none should be trusted until tested.

Have a companion remain outside with an independent means of recall. Agree on a departure time by their clock and a clear procedure if communication fails. Start with a very short visit. If there is no tested way back, don’t enter.

### What to bring

- Sturdy, grippy boots and clothing for cool, damp weather; the mossy masonry and terrace edges could be slippery.
- Drinking water and food from outside. The cisterns are shallow and clear, but their safety—and what taking water might do here—is unknown.
- A light, a simple compass or other navigation aid, and a watch. Bring devices whose operation has been checked across the boundary.
- Basic first aid and a whistle, plus a communication device tested with your outside companion.
- If the air’s composition or behavior is uncertain, carry a suitable independent air supply, again only after confirming it works inside.

Keep your load modest. Narrow lanes, steps, and shelves below the walls call for balance more than elaborate equipment.

### Moving through the town

Enter slowly, ideally on a broad, clear lane. The suspended rain is beautiful, but don’t walk straight into it: droplets that appear to hang harmlessly might resist contact, move when disturbed, or behave differently around you. The same uncertainty applies to air, vegetation, and the shallow water in the cisterns. Test carefully before approaching any of them closely.

Stay back from cliff edges and avoid climbing on walls or roofs. The pale stone is fresh-looking, and the dark shelves below offer no assurance of a safe landing. Note your route as you descend; the lanes may be clear from above but harder to distinguish on the way back.

The sound is worth listening to, but don’t assume it is an ordinary ongoing sound or that it will cover warnings you need to hear. Pause often and check your position and surroundings rather than relying on hearing alone.

### Among the people and animals

Treat everyone as a person whose ability to perceive you, respond, or consent is unknown. Don’t touch them, take their belongings, enter their homes, or interfere with their work. Give the animals room as well. They may remain motionless, but you cannot know whether disturbing one could injure it, startle it later, or change the conditions holding it still.

Leave the rake, flint, gardens, water channels, and cisterns as you found them. Even moving a stone from a roof or stepping into a garden could matter if the town ever resumes.

Above all, don’t let the absence of visible urgency persuade you to stay indefinitely. The people show no weariness, but **you might**. Check yourself against the plan you made outside, return while you still have a generous margin of strength and supplies, and let the town keep its quiet.
