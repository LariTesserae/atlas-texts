## Before you go

Prepare for a long walk through a place that may not stay arranged the way you found it. The plateau appears bright and orderly, but brightness is not the same as visibility, and a familiar route may become unfamiliar—or seem always to have been different.

If possible, visit with a companion and arrange a check-in with someone outside. Agree beforehand on a return time and on what to do if one of you becomes uncertain about a route or a shared memory. Don’t assume that a map, a photograph, or a note will be immune to the changes; use several kinds of record and treat disagreements between them as reasons to stop and reassess, not as proof that one account is correct.

Bring:

- Warm layers and gloves, especially for the cold railings.
- Sturdy shoes with good grip and protection against sharp edges.
- Drinking water and food for longer than your planned visit.
- A flashlight or headlamp, even though the interiors are bright: it may help you identify objects whose shapes are hard to read.
- A pencil, notebook, and a simple map you can revise.
- A watch and a way to call for help, if either works there.
- A small first-aid kit, including supplies for cuts.

Travel light enough to climb, but don’t count on finding a convenient exit through the factories. The stairs and courts are on a scale that can make an apparently short detour take hours.

## On the plateau

Start with a short visit near a known arrival point. Note landmarks in both directions as you walk: the view back may be quite different from the view ahead. Ask inhabitants about current routes, closed areas, and any signs they use around working machinery. Their pencilled maps are likely to be more useful than an impressive-looking permanent one.

Keep clear of belts, cranes, loading docks, valves, and pipe bridges unless you know a route is intended for pedestrians. The machinery’s slowness makes it easy to overlook, but a load that has moved almost imperceptibly all afternoon can still crush someone beneath it. Likewise, a quiet factory is not necessarily an idle one. Don’t touch a newly finished object or assume it is safe simply because it is unattended.

Watch your footing. Water collects in small fans and shallow sumps; pale paving, glass avenues, clean corners, and sharp shadows may make depth and edges difficult to judge. Inspection hatches open into an extensive interior, so leave them closed unless you have a reason and permission to enter. If you do go below, treat it as a separate expedition: the succession of floors and stairs could make an easy descent into a very long return.

The animals are accustomed to this environment, not necessarily to you. Give the grazers space, avoid disturbing sleeping creatures in cable trays, and step around the mossy seams where they feed. Don’t collect fish, plants, or animals. The persistent water in a sump may support a whole small community; it is not a reliable source of drinking water for a visitor.

## When something changes

If a new bridge appears, or a court seems much wider than you remember, pause somewhere safe. Compare notes with your companion and with local people, but don’t turn a disagreement about the past into an argument about who is trustworthy. The inhabitants live with altered memories as well as altered structures.

Make decisions based on what you can currently verify: where the walkable edges are, how long the route now takes, and whether you have enough daylight, warmth, food, and water to continue. A memory of having used a bridge before is less useful than seeing where it leads now.

Allow a generous margin for returning. Toward evening the plateau dims only slightly, so the light may not give you the usual warning that your day is ending. Set an actual turnaround time and keep it, even if the next avenue looks interesting.

Finally, leave room simply to listen and look. This is an industrial landscape, but its movements are measured in hours and days. Stand safely out of the way for a while: watch a fish turn at the fall of a drop, or mark the position of a crane and look again later. You may understand the place better by noticing how much is happening almost too slowly to see.
