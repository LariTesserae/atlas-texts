Prepare for a high, exposed mountain visit rather than a short stroll. The basin may be small, but reaching it and moving through it could be slow, and the sheltered-looking ring is no substitute for a reliable descent or proper shelter.

### Before you go

- **Verify the basics of the destination:** breathable atmosphere, altitude, gravity, weather and the route in and out. If the elevation is high enough for altitude illness, allow time to acclimatize and know when to descend. Don’t assume a familiar-looking sky means familiar conditions.
- **Choose a clear, stable weather window.** Wind, cloud or a little fresh snow could make the broken stone much harder to navigate. Plan to leave well before evening, when wet surfaces begin freezing.
- **Bring a companion if possible**, and leave someone a route and return time. Carry a reliable map and navigation tools; the three-stone stack may be an old marker, but it is neither an assured route nor easy to distinguish from the surrounding rock.
- Check whether the basin is protected or culturally significant, and obtain any required permission.

### What to carry

Wear sturdy footwear with good grip and enough support for uneven rock. Bring warm layers, a windproof shell, gloves and a hat, even if the approach is sunny. Depending on the season and approach, traction devices may be useful; don’t count on them making every glazed slab safe.

Protect yourself from the light with high-UV sunglasses, sunscreen and clothing that covers exposed skin. The pale stone reflects light into places that might otherwise feel sheltered.

Carry all the water you expect to need, along with food, a headlamp, first-aid supplies, an emergency shelter or bivy, and a communication device suited to the region. The little cups of meltwater are beautiful but too uncertain to depend on, and their water should not be assumed safe to drink.

### In the basin

Move deliberately. Test your footing on loose grit and avoid squeezing between slabs that look cracked or precariously balanced. Don’t use the standing stones as handholds without checking them, and give fresh fractures and unstable-looking pieces room. The occasional click may be only a grain or a bit of ice shifting, but it is a reminder that the stone is not entirely still.

Keep track of your entry and exit as you explore. A route that looks obvious from one side of a slab may disappear when you turn around. If you stop to watch the colored bands or the slow drip of meltwater, choose a place where you are not beneath an overhang or relying on a loose stone for support.

Treat the ring, the worked flake and the possible route marker as traces of someone else’s presence. Photograph or observe them where they lie; don’t pick up the flake, rearrange the stones or add a cairn. Leave no marks of your own.

If conditions permit, take a few quiet minutes near midday. Watch the light shift across the grit and listen for water in the stone cups. Then give yourself ample time to get down, before the basin’s wet edges harden and its passages fall into shadow.
