## Before you arrive

Treat the plateau as a remote high-altitude wilderness with an active industrial site in it—not as a ruin. The towers are still working, and their slow, quiet operation gives you little warning of what may move next.

If you can choose an arrival point, choose a broad, stable patch of exposed stone away from the towers, cable and cliff edges. Mark it visibly, record its position, and make sure you know how to return to your point of departure. Bring at least one companion if possible, and leave your route and expected return time with someone outside the location.

Check the air, pressure and weather before committing to a long walk. The plateau looks high, but its actual elevation and atmosphere are unknown. Bring a means of testing breathable air, and be prepared to leave if you cannot establish that it is safe. If the altitude is comparable to a high mountain on Earth, allow time to acclimatize rather than descending immediately into the valley.

## Dress and pack for the exposed stone

The bright surfaces and thin-looking air suggest strong glare and potentially intense ultraviolet exposure. Bring:

- High-coverage sunglasses, a broad-brimmed hat and sunscreen.
- Layers for cool mornings, cold shade and a potentially much colder night; include a windproof, waterproof shell.
- Sturdy boots with good grip and ankle protection, and gloves for sharp rock.
- A small first-aid kit, including supplies for cuts and blisters.
- Enough drinking water for the whole trip and an emergency reserve.
- Navigation tools, a light, and a compact emergency shelter.

Do not plan on the water hollows or plant cups supplying you. The water may be clear without being safe, and taking it could disturb organisms that depend on it. If an emergency forces you to use it, filter and disinfect it, while recognizing that ordinary treatment may not remove unfamiliar chemicals or toxins.

## Make a silence plan

Near the eastern cliffs, shouting may not carry even a few steps. Elsewhere, the absence of sound could conceal an approaching carrier, shifting stone or another person calling for help. Do not rely on whistles, alarms or hearing one another.

Agree on simple hand signals before entering: **stop, come here, danger, injured, return**. Keep companions in sight, especially in dense vegetation and near machinery. Carry a written-message device or notebook, and a light that can be used for signals after dark. Radios or other electronic communicators may help, but test them locally rather than assuming they work through whatever is affecting sound.

Move slowly enough to look around often. The quiet may feel peaceful, but it removes a layer of information people normally use without noticing.

## Choose a modest route

The valley is half a day across even before accounting for detours, difficult footing and the need to find a way back. For a first visit, stay on the plateau or make a short descent with a firm turnaround time. Do not set out to cross the valley merely because the terraces look gentle from above.

The gaps through the vegetation may wind unpredictably, and some follow fractures. Use a map or positional device if it works, but also note visible landmarks and mark your route with removable markers. Avoid cliffs, fresh-looking breaks, loose slabs and damp, moss-covered stone. A walking pole could help you test footing without putting your hands into unfamiliar growth.

Give the plants room. What looks like a continuous green surface is densely occupied, and roots are slowly opening the rock beneath it. Avoid stepping on cushions or forcing through mats when a stable gap is available. Do not touch the small creatures in flowers or the transparent insects, and watch where you place your hands.

## Keep clear of the machinery

The carriers and towers should be treated as moving equipment, even when they appear motionless. Give the cable, towers, anchor points and pumping house a wide berth. A carrier that has stopped in sunlight may start again without any sound. Do not stand beneath the cable, handle the pale harvest, open panels or touch indicators.

Read the raised signs from a distance if possible. They may identify hazards or restricted areas, but their meaning and language are not guaranteed to be familiar. If you encounter workers or the operators, assume the installation is in use and follow their directions if communication is possible.

## Leave before the light changes

Long shadows bring moisture to the plants and may make surfaces harder to see and less secure underfoot. Plan to be back at your departure point well before dusk, with enough margin for a blocked route or a slow companion. Carry an overnight shelter anyway; a place that changes slowly from season to season can still become hazardous quickly when you are tired, cold or unable to find the gap you came through.

The best first visit would be unhurried and small: stand somewhere stable, let your eyes adjust to the glare, observe the carriers from well outside their path, and leave the valley for another day if you cannot see a safe route into it and back.
