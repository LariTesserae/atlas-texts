Prepare for the hollow as you would for an active geothermal site, with one additional uncertainty: the ground and the things on it do not always agree with their own recent history. The afternoon light makes the place look easy to understand. Do not let that make it feel familiar.

**Before going**

- If possible, send an instrumented probe first. Check the air for oxygen, toxic gases and combustible gases, including close to the floor. A normal oxygen reading alone would not make it safe to breathe.
- Arrange a reliable way home and a check-in with someone outside. Set a short initial visit and an external deadline for retrieval. A watch may help you keep track, but do not assume it will resolve any local irregularity in time.
- Choose a landing or entry point outside the hollow if you can. Its floor is being made as you watch; you do not know which parts can bear your weight, how quickly they may change, or whether the entrance can narrow.

**What to bring**

Wear sturdy, heat-resistant boots with ankle protection, long clothing that covers the skin, and gloves. The soles must insulate against warmth from below, but you should still be able to feel uneven ground. Bring water and protect it from the heat; the plants are not a source of drinking water.

For a first visit, use a self-contained breathing supply rather than relying on a mask that filters the local air. Bring a gas monitor, a thermometer or thermal camera, a light, and a means of recording what you see. Avoid equipment that sparks until the atmosphere has been assessed. A helmet is sensible around unfinished rock and a narrow opening.

**While you are there**

Stay near your exit. Before stepping onto the pale floor, test it from a stable position and look for seams, warmth and fresh collars around the roots. Even an area that looks solid may be newly formed. Do not stand close to a plant during one of the compressions, and never place a hand or foot between its leaves. The teeth are minute, but the leaves close around mineral grains; their strength and speed are unknown.

Do not cut a leaf, touch the sap or collect fresh stone. The plants are part of the process making the ground, not merely growing on it. Disturbing one could expose you to an unknown substance or affect the floor beneath you.

Notice the rhythm underfoot. If it changes, the heat rises, gas alarms sound, or the floor begins to deform, leave promptly rather than waiting to understand the cause. Likewise, retreat if you become confused about where you entered or what has changed. Marking your route may help, but there is no reason to trust a mark—or a photograph—as a guaranteed anchor against the hollow’s stranger behavior.

Give yourself a moment to listen from a safe position. The compression, release and dry clicks may be the clearest warning you get that the place is working. Then go while the way out is still plain.
