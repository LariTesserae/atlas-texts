The first thing to prepare for is the noise. This is not a quiet hidden garden with wind in the background: the chamber itself is an instrument, and you would be inside it. The glare, the vibrating stone and the salt moving under you would make even a short visit physically demanding.

### Before entering

Find out who lives above the chamber and ask permission. The street, its wind channels and the masonry are part of a working city, not just an entrance to somewhere interesting. Local people may also know whether the seam is safe to approach and whether the garden has a name or a use.

Bring your own drinking water for the whole trip, including any time spent in the city. There is none in the chamber, and you should not assume the city has a supply available to visitors. Bring food and any other necessities too; oil cellars are not a substitute for ordinary provisioning.

Investigate the seam before committing yourself to it. Its apparent width does not tell you the dimensions of the space below, but it also does not tell you that a person can pass safely through. Test the route with a small light or camera if possible, and establish how you will get back out. Don’t pry apart the paving stones or enlarge the opening: you could damage the street, the ancient channels or the garden’s hold on the wall.

Have a companion remain outside, with an agreed return time and a way to summon help. The roar may make speech useless once you are below, so agree on a nonverbal signal beforehand. Any line or equipment passed through the seam should be arranged so it cannot snag in the plants or obstruct the entrance.

### Protection and equipment

The sound deserves caution beyond ordinary earplugs. Well-fitted hearing protection may reduce exposure, but the noise is also conducted through the chamber and felt in the body. If you can measure the sound level, do so before entering; if you cannot assess it, keep the first visit very brief. Leave immediately if you experience pain, dizziness, disorientation or ringing in your ears. Don’t count on being able to judge the danger simply by how tolerable it feels.

Wear close-fitting eye protection against the glare and airborne salt or seed powder. A suitable particulate respirator would help with dust, though it does not supply oxygen or protect against an unknown atmosphere. Check the air before entering; do not assume that because plants grow there it is safe for you to breathe. If the atmosphere cannot be verified, or the chamber requires specialist breathing equipment, leave the visit to people equipped and trained for it.

Wear sturdy clothes and footwear with good traction, and protect your head: the chamber is low, the floor is hard, and a sudden gust could upset your balance. A secured headlamp would be useful for examining the entrance and corners without relying entirely on the fierce daylight. Avoid loose straps and dangling gear.

Bring no flame. Mineral oils are present in the stone, and the wind would make an open flame unpredictable regardless.

### Inside

Plan to crouch, stay clear of the garden and keep your visit short. The hanging threads may whip nearly across the chamber, so watch a few cycles of the gusts before choosing where to settle. The salt bowl may look empty, but the powder in its far corner is the garden’s next generation. Try not to kneel in it, scatter it or carry it away on your clothes.

The little grazing creature is another reason to look rather than touch. Its relationship with the garden is unknown, as are the effects of skin oils, introduced microbes or a disturbance of the mortar. Take photographs if you like, but leave plants, seeds, salt and fragments of stone where they are.

You may find that observation comes in fragments: a fan folding edgewise, threads pulled taut, a shell-colored joint moving against the wall, then the brief slackening in which everything opens again. The footprints overhead will be hard to distinguish from the chamber’s other vibrations. If you want to see the plants follow the light, it would be better to compare photographs from separate brief visits than to remain in the roar for an afternoon.

On leaving, check yourself and your equipment for powder before traveling elsewhere, taking care not to release it into another environment. Let your outside companion know you are out, and give yourself a quiet place to recover. After that chamber, even the noise of the city above may feel unusually gentle.
