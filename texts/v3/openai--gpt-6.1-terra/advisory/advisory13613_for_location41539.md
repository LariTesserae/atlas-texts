Prepare for this as a **hot, dark, unstable wilderness with unknown biology**, not as a difficult hike. The abundance of life does not establish that the air is breathable, the water safe, or any organism harmless. Send instruments and remote cameras through first; postpone entry if you cannot establish a reliable way back.

### Before entering

- **Survey the air and ground remotely.** Check oxygen, pressure, toxic gases and airborne particles at several heights and in more than one basin. Test whether the ridges are solid, whether cracks emit gases, and how quickly rain collects in hollows. A reading at the arrival point may not represent conditions farther out.
- **Secure the return point.** Place it, if possible, on stable high ground away from dense vegetation and animal passages. Mark it with redundant visual and electronic beacons. Confirm that the return mechanism works under wet, hot conditions and that it can remain clear of encroaching plants and debris.
- **Plan a short first visit.** Use a tether or a marked route within sight of the entry point before attempting any traverse. Establish a turnaround time independent of how much progress you have made.

### What to wear and carry

Until the environment is better understood, use a **sealed protective suit with an independent air supply**, suitable cooling, and eye and hand protection. It must resist abrasion and puncture from sharp rock, vegetation and small organisms. In this warmth and humidity, heat exhaustion inside protective equipment is a serious constraint: calculate working time conservatively and carry enough reserve air and cooling for a delayed return.

Bring sturdy boots with good grip, though do not trust them to make loose ridges safe. A helmet, knee protection and a cutting tool for freeing yourself from vegetation may help. Avoid loose straps that creepers or passing animals could catch.

Carry several independent lights, including a hands-free light and backups protected from rain. The small natural glows will not illuminate a route. Use illumination sparingly where possible: you do not know what it attracts or disturbs.

For navigation and communication, take a mapped track from your entry point, durable physical markers, and instruments that do not depend on a local satellite network. Use visual or tactile signals with companions. Ordinary speech, whistles and approaching footsteps may be swallowed by the noise; electronic communications should be tested in the basins. Protect your hearing, but remember that hearing protection makes it even harder to detect nearby movement.

Bring drinking water and food from home, a first-aid and repair kit, and enough supplies to wait for extraction. Do not drink collected rain or condensation, eat plants, or touch fungal mats and animals.

### Moving through it

Travel with a team small enough to stay together but large enough to help an injured member. Keep one another visible or connected, and move slowly enough to inspect each foothold. Avoid crossing beneath loaded banks of vegetation or stopping in hollows that could receive a slide or a sudden rush of water. After rain, reassess the route rather than assuming it is unchanged.

Give moving vegetation a wide berth. A shaking bank may conceal feeding animals, and a large grazer could be close without being audible. Do not follow passages through the growth simply because they are easy to walk: they may be active animal routes. If a herd or swarm is moving toward you, retreat to a stable, open position rather than trying to force a way through it.

Leave the stone-turning and pebble-carrying creatures alone. Their behavior suggests dexterity and social interaction, but not whether they are curious, territorial, dangerous, or capable of recognizing your intentions. Observe from a distance; do not offer food or take their objects.

### Leaving

Treat the suit and all equipment as potentially contaminated. Inspect and clean them at a controlled transition point, contain the waste, and follow quarantine procedures before returning to your home environment. Likewise, clean your equipment before entry to avoid introducing foreign organisms here.

Expect the visit to be disorienting. The sound will fill your attention without reliably telling you what is close; the warmth and constant contact of leaves may make it difficult to notice a change in conditions. Set regular checks for air, cooling, location, companions and time remaining. The safest first visit is one in which you see only a little, learn what your equipment can withstand, and leave well before anything forces you to.
