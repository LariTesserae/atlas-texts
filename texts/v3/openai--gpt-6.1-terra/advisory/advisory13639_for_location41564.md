Prepare as you would for a long expedition onto a living surface in a near-vacuum, with a town that may offer shelter but cannot be assumed to offer supplies.

## Before you arrive

Find out where your arrival point sits relative to the town and arrange a meeting if possible. A traveler standing on the open plateau may be many miles from anything that looks like shelter. The bright points maintain *chambers*, but it is not clear whether their protection extends to the streets or beyond them. Do not plan on being able to breathe outside.

Bring a pressure suit with independent breathing gas, temperature control, eye protection and a way to clear charged dust from visors and joints. Check the local pressure, atmospheric composition and daylight radiation before exposing yourself, and carry enough reserve to return if your first readings are unfavorable. A dust front may make an ordinary filter, exposed cooling surface or electrical connector unreliable; choose equipment with sealed components and storm-rated protection.

Bring all the water you will need, plus a substantial reserve. There is none available locally, even as buried ice. A recycler may stretch your supply, but it cannot replace losses indefinitely. Bring food as well: the plateau’s oils, salts, gases and animals are part of an unfamiliar chemistry, not provisions.

A good first visit would be short, with a return point whose location and operation you have verified. Leave a margin for being delayed indoors by a storm.

## Moving across the plateau

The surface may feel like stone underfoot, but it is alive. Walk rather than dig, cut or drive stakes into it unless your hosts say otherwise. Use broad, non-marking supports for equipment, and avoid disturbing open pores, brittle sacs or upright growths. A scrape that closes is still a scrape; healing does not tell you whether the plant was unharmed.

Take navigation instruments that do not depend on seeing landmarks. The openness could make distances difficult to judge, and dust may erase the horizon. Mark a route digitally and carry a backup. Do not follow a cup, ridge or animal line on the assumption that it is nearby.

Keep clear of the grazers and give runners a wide berth. Sound is an uncertain warning at these distances: the sight of an animal and the arrival of its footsteps may be separated by a long interval. The transparent creatures above town may be hard to see, especially near dusk. Avoid bright lures, exposed food and collecting specimens.

## Storms and shelter

Treat the first low note as a reason to check your shelter plan, not as a spectacle to pursue. Charged dust can obscure vision, wear exposed surfaces and interfere with equipment. The bright points drift out to meet storms, but their capabilities and intentions are unknown; do not rely on them to protect you outside a maintained chamber.

Ask your hosts where a visitor should wait, whether their walls admit pressure-suited travelers, and how to enter quickly if a front arrives. Carry hearing protection: the singing walls, answering cups and booming valley may be beautiful, but they could also be painfully loud. Underground reports and bursting sacs will make the place noisy even in clear weather.

## In town

Do not force an entrance through a wall that has no door. Approach with a resident if you can, stop where they indicate and give the fibers time to loosen. Find out whether a chamber is pressurized and its air safe **before** removing your helmet. Bring a small independent atmosphere monitor rather than judging by the comfort of the residents.

Ask before touching the architecture. A building here may also be part of the organism beneath the street; cutting a fixture, attaching a cable or heating a wall could have consequences that are not obvious. Likewise, keep flames and sparks away from pores and gas sacs. Their contents are unknown.

Pack out your waste and avoid releasing water or unfamiliar chemicals onto the plant. Even water, precious to you, may not be benign to a landscape adapted to dry chemistry.

## What to be ready for

The plateau will not present itself as a creature. It will ring beneath your boots, stretch beyond sight and seem almost motionless. Then a fresh scrape will begin to close; dust will move across it; a cup on the horizon will have turned without your noticing.

Give yourself time to listen from shelter. The town is audible before it is visible, and its walls join the storm rather than merely keeping it out. Bring a recorder if your hosts permit one—but keep an eye on your suit readings and your way home. Here, the ground, the buildings and much of the shelter are all the same living thing, and you are the visitor.
