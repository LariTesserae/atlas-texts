Prepare for this as both a **confined-space entry** and a visit to a tiny, vulnerable habitat. The chamber is large enough for a person in the abstract, but the description gives you no place to put your weight without crushing something. Be prepared to look without entering.

### Before you go

- **Investigate the city and doorway first.** An empty city can still have owners, protected sites, unstable paving and hidden wells. The chamber itself may be supported, but that does not establish that the courtyard, sealed doorway or stair is safe.
- **Go with an experienced confined-space team.** Arrange communications, a way out and a rescue plan that does not depend on someone climbing in after you. Do not go alone.
- **Check the air before entry and continuously thereafter.** Cold, still air and a small, partly sealed space make oxygen deficiency or accumulated gases plausible hazards. A mask or respirator does not supply oxygen. If testing or the entry conditions are unsatisfactory, stay out.
- **Start with a remote inspection.** A small camera, introduced through an existing opening without enlarging it, may show you the chamber while sparing its floor. Even the stair is occupied: the insect colony lives beside it, and the moss begins after only three steps.

### If entry is genuinely necessary

Establish first whether there is any route and foothold that will not damage the plants, animals, web or masonry. Don’t assume the moss will support you, and don’t pull it back to find stone: those fitted cushions are habitat, not loose padding. A protective board would still compress whatever lies beneath it.

If there is no minimally damaging way in, that is your answer. If a suitable route exists, move slowly and keep your group as small as safety permits. Avoid touching the stonework or using roots and pillars for support; their apparent stability is not a guarantee that they can bear a person’s force.

Wear warm, close-fitting layers suited to a cold, low space, with a helmet if the access route allows it. Secure straps and loose equipment so they cannot sweep through the ferns or catch the web. Use clean footwear and gear to reduce the chance of introducing organisms, soil or chemicals. Bring instruments and lights that do not put out appreciable heat, and use only as much light as you need. Don’t collect plants or animals, leave food, or add water.

### What to expect

The reflected daylight makes the chamber unusually easy to see, but it depends on sunlight reaching the courtyard crack; don’t count on it at every hour or in every weather. From the stair, give your eyes time to adjust and look before moving. Much of what is alive there is small, pale, or nearly motionless.

The cold may become uncomfortable quickly, especially while you are keeping still. Your breath and body heat will also change the air in so small a room, so keep any close visit brief. The silence is not an assurance that nothing is happening: the insects are tending young, and the snails and springtails occupy surfaces that may first look empty.

The best preparation may be to arrange a careful view from the doorway, then leave the chamber as you found it—with its light, water and crowded floor undisturbed.
