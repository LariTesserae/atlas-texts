Prepare for this as a cold-water dive in an unfamiliar mountain lake, not as a walk through a flooded village. The town may look close enough to reach from the surface, but its depth, the thickness of the vegetation, and the condition of its structures are unknown.

### Before entering the water

Find out whether the lake water is safe for human skin and eyes, whether it contains infectious organisms or unusual chemistry, and whether the town has inhabitants or caretakers whose permission you need. Don’t assume that clear water is clean or breathable, or that the apparent stillness means there are no currents.

Check the shelf’s depth, the altitude, weather, entry and exit points, and whether there is boat access or a suitable shore nearby. Mountain weather can change quickly; wind may make an easy exit difficult. Arrange surface support and a way to summon help. If qualified local guides exist, go with them.

An ordinary traveler who is not already a trained diver should not try to learn in the town itself. Get appropriate training and experience first, including cold-water and altitude-diving procedures. If that is not possible, remain at the surface with a qualified companion and view the town from there, provided conditions allow.

### Equipment and dive plan

Use suitable scuba equipment, checked by someone competent, with adequate thermal protection for the actual temperature and duration. A well-fitting wetsuit may suffice in some conditions; colder water may call for a drysuit and training in its use. Fresh water changes buoyancy, so establish proper weighting before approaching the town.

Carry a dive light even in daylight: leaf canopies, walls, and suspended silt can make the lanes much darker than they appear from above. A small cutting tool accessible with either hand is sensible for entanglement, though the plan should be to avoid the vegetation rather than cut your way through it. Use a dive computer or tables appropriate to the lake’s altitude, and plan the dive conservatively. Account for the altitude at which you will surface and any higher travel afterward; don’t finish the dive and immediately drive over a higher pass or fly without following appropriate guidance.

Dive with a trained buddy, agree on hand signals and a separation procedure, and set a turnaround based on air, time, temperature, and conditions—not on reaching the central basin. Don’t rely on shouting or on locating your buddy by sound. Underwater noises can seem very close while being difficult to place.

### Moving through the town

Approach from above the open lanes and take time to establish neutral buoyancy. The springy ground is held together by plants and old silt; a careless fin stroke could uproot growth or turn clear water opaque. Move slowly, keeping fins clear of the mat, and leave room between yourself and your buddy so one disturbed patch does not blind you both.

The handrails and steps look inviting, but avoid pulling on them. Wood, cords, roots, and stone may be weaker than they appear, and they are part of both the town and its habitat. Likewise, give the roof with the grove growing through it a wide berth. Do not swim under roofs, into curtained doorways, through narrow channels, or anywhere a direct ascent could be blocked. Fine leaves and rootlets can catch equipment; old masonry can shift.

Keep a respectful distance from the fish and crayfish, and don’t disturb the broad-headed fish beneath the steps. Take photographs without moving plants or animals into a better position. Bring nothing away, and leave no line, gear, or waste behind.

### What to expect

The first impression may be brightness: the broken surface overhead, wavering bands across the lanes, fish suddenly gathering and scattering. Then the cold will make itself known. Even if the view is absorbing, pay attention to your hands, breathing, air supply, buddy, and route back. End the dive before cold or fatigue becomes a problem.

The town’s sounds may feel unexpectedly intimate—the scraping, clattering, low drumming, and leaf-crown rain arriving through the water together. Pause in an open place and listen if you can do so without touching anything. Above all, allow enough time to leave gently: rising through the green crowns toward the shifting light is part of the visit, and not something you want to do in a hurry.
