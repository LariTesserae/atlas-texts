Prepare for this as an exposed industrial site with a living habitat on top—not as a garden with a dramatic view. The beauty is accessible, but the height, machinery and noise leave little margin for improvisation.

### Before you go

Find out how the tower is entered and climbed, whether the structure is inspected, and whether there is a safe way back down. The description does not tell you that there is a lift, a stair, a door, or a reliable route to the summit. Do not assume that the engines can be stopped or that anyone understands the fan.

If possible, visit with someone who knows the tower. Agree on a turnaround time and a return plan, including how you will reach your own world if the usual route becomes unavailable. Leave that plan with someone who is not coming.

Check the weather. An open iron structure at height is a poor place to be in a thunderstorm, strong wind or rain. The shallow floor may become slippery, and there is no shelter overhead.

### What to bring

- **Well-fitted hearing protection**, preferably earplugs and compatible earmuffs. The roar is continuous and could be damaging. Keep protection on throughout the approach as well as at the summit.
- **A helmet, secure footwear with good grip, and close-fitting clothes.** Tie back hair and secure straps against wind or moving machinery.
- **Water and some food.** The air is dry and warm, and the little water collected by the plants is not yours to drink.
- **Eye protection** against grit, and a light for the tower interior and descent. Secure anything you carry so it cannot fall through the ribs.
- **Fall protection only if you know how to use it and have a verified anchorage.** Do not clip to a rib, vine, felt sleeve or other convenient-looking feature: their strength is unknown.

Bring as little loose equipment as you can. A camera is worthwhile, but use a wrist or neck strap and be ready to put it away.

### At the summit

Enter slowly and find a stable place near the center. The floor is smooth and slopes toward its lowest point; dampness and grit may make footing unpredictable. The open spaces between the ribs should be treated as unguarded edges, not windows. Stay well back, especially if wind is gusting or the noise makes you feel unsteady.

You will probably have to communicate without speech. Agree on simple hand signals before entering, including a clear signal for **leave now**. Keep companions in sight; don’t rely on shouting, and don’t split up. A radio may help if it works there, but have a visual plan as well.

Give yourself a few minutes to adjust to the strange mismatch between sight and sound. A leaf may tear loose without your hearing it. You may feel the floor and ribs vibrating, and the uninterrupted roar may become tiring even through hearing protection. If you feel dizzy, develop a headache, notice a strange smell or have trouble breathing, leave promptly rather than trying to endure it.

Watch the animals without handling them. The felt sleeves shelter moths and beetles; leaves may have sleeping lizards on their backs. At dusk, stand quietly away from the openings and let the bats pass through. Don’t use flash or bright lights on them, and don’t pick flowers, disturb the sleeves, or pour water into them. Their small, carefully balanced supply belongs to the place.

The vines’ response to the engines takes days. On a short visit you are unlikely to see a bud open because a note swells; you may only notice which stage the flowers have reached. If you hope to observe the cycle, plan repeated visits rather than assuming you can safely remain at the summit for days.

Allow enough time and energy for the descent. Once you are somewhere quiet and safe, check in with your companion and the person expecting your return. The silence afterward may feel almost as remarkable as the tower.
