## Before you go

Treat the plateau as remote, wet wilderness with an unknown atmosphere and an unknown biosphere—not as a mossy field you can simply walk onto.

First, confirm that your return route works from the plateau itself. Mark and protect it, and arrange a check-in schedule with someone on the other side. Test communications over distance: the terrain offers few landmarks, and mist or the living canopy may interfere with equipment. If possible, survey from above or send a remote probe before committing people.

Measure air pressure, oxygen and other gases at the arrival point. The place is high enough that travelers notice thinner breathing, even if the air feels humid and still. If the atmosphere is otherwise safe but the oxygen pressure is low, allow time to acclimatize and bring supplemental oxygen. Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue or poor judgment are reasons to stop and descend or leave; severe breathlessness at rest or confusion is an emergency. Do not count on acclimatization to make an unsuitable atmosphere safe.

Assume the native spores, droplets and plant surfaces are biologically unfamiliar. Ordinary drinking-water treatment does not guarantee safety against unknown life or chemistry. Until the environment has been assessed, use a properly tested protective suit with independent breathing air, and bring all food and water from a known-safe source. Have a means to remove and contain contaminated outer gear before returning.

## What to bring

Choose equipment for **continuous damp**, not merely for rain:

- Waterproof, breathable outer protection if the atmosphere permits it; otherwise, a suitable sealed suit. Bring dry insulating layers in waterproof bags.
- Supportive boots with secure footing, plus spare socks and a way to dry or replace them. Wet feet will become a problem long before the landscape looks hostile.
- A walking pole or probe for testing ground ahead, and a rope for group travel or assisting someone out of a depression.
- Reliable navigation with a mapped route back to the arrival point, redundant power, and physical markers that can be retrieved without damaging the vegetation.
- A freestanding shelter with a waterproof floor, sleeping insulation, and a way to keep gear off the ground. Do not plan on finding dry wood or a naturally dry campsite.
- Emergency supplies, a first-aid kit, oxygen monitoring where appropriate, and enough carried food and water for delays. A storm may develop slowly, but mist and darkness can still make travel impractical.

Keep electronics sealed and accessible without exposing everything else. Expect condensation on lenses, instruments and the inside of any shelter.

## Moving across the plateau

The greatest danger may be ground that looks solid because plants are growing on it. Some mats span deep water; some ridges contain old trunks; other surfaces lift under a foot and settle again. A grazer’s weight is not proof that a person can cross safely.

Move slowly, probe before transferring your weight, and avoid dark hollows, unusually springy ground and areas where the vegetation changes abruptly. Travel with a companion, leaving enough space that both of you do not break through the same patch. If you use a rope, practice its use beforehand: on yielding ground, an untrained rescue attempt can put both travelers in danger.

Do not rely on the old path. It is a useful sign that something once traveled there, but it disappears, and the land has had ample time to grow over whatever lies beneath. Likewise, a view from a slight rise may show you an immense distance without giving you a useful destination. Record your route as you go rather than trusting the broad folds of vegetation to look familiar on the return.

## Living things and shelter

Give the animals room, especially where you cannot see the whole animal. A distant moving back and a widening gap in the leaves are enough reason to stop and wait. Avoid the translucent animals at dusk, the leaf-underside creatures, and anything that seems to gather around your lights or breath. There is no basis for assuming they are dangerous, but neither is there a basis for assuming they are harmless.

Leave the chambers alone. Even one that looks empty may be occupied behind the narrowed entrance, or may be part of the life cycle of the animals that built or use it. Make camp in your own shelter rather than cutting an entrance or borrowing a roof.

Keep lights low when you can do so safely, take out everything you bring, and avoid collecting specimens without a containment plan. The living surface is both habitat and ground; a campsite or repeated footpath may leave a longer trace than it first appears.

## How the visit will feel

Expect the plateau to be harder to read than it is to cross quickly. Distance opens around you, but the horizon remains indistinct. The ground moves a little after each step. Water arrives one drop at a time from several layers of leaves, and the dimness beneath the crowns may make afternoon feel like evening.

Plan for short days and generous margins. Pause often to listen, check your position and inspect your gear. The calls may come from animals you never see, and the weather may spend many hours gathering before it reaches you. In a place where so much changes slowly, the safest preparation is to resist the urge to hurry merely because nothing appears to be happening.
