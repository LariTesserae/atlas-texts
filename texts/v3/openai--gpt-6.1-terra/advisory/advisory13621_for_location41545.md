Prepare for a slow, exposed crossing. The ground may look almost level, but a route that appears dry from a distance can break into shallow pools and soft mud. Distances may be difficult to judge across the pale, hazy basin, and there are few tall landmarks.

### Before you arrive

- **Confirm that the air, sunlight, temperature, and local organisms are compatible with humans.** A familiar-looking wetland is not necessarily a safe one. Arrange a reliable way back and leave someone your intended route and return time.
- **Learn what the surf implies locally.** The sea sounds close, but the description does not establish whether the basin is tidal or how quickly it floods. Check tides, rain forecasts, and any seasonal changes before setting out; have a route to higher ground.
- **Choose a clear landing or entry point on firm, elevated ground.** From there, survey the basin before walking into it. A modest rise may be the only obvious refuge if water spreads across the plain.

### What to bring

Wear lightweight clothing that covers your skin, a broad-brimmed hat, and sunglasses suited to glare off water and pale rock. Bring sunscreen and insect repellent, though you should not assume either is effective against unfamiliar local life.

For your feet, use sturdy footwear with good grip that can get wet without becoming unmanageable. Polished rock may be slippery, and mud can conceal deeper depressions. Gaiters or long trousers can help protect your legs; carry dry socks and footwear for afterward. A walking pole is useful for testing uncertain ground, but do not rely on it to make deep or sticky mud safe.

Carry more drinking water than the short vegetation suggests you will need. The pools may be salty, contaminated, or biologically unfamiliar, and the slow seepage is not proof of drinkability. Bring food, a first-aid kit, a navigation device and backup compass, a charged light, and a means of communication that works across your route. A small dry bag will keep essential equipment usable after a slip or rain.

### While you are there

Move deliberately, following the higher stone and gravel where possible. Test mud before putting your weight on it, and avoid committing yourself to a broad crossing whose far edge you cannot see clearly. Mark your route on a map or device rather than relying on similar-looking rock swells to guide you home. If the water is rising, the weather turns, or your return route becomes uncertain, leave early.

There may be little shade, and warmth held near the ground can be more tiring than the air initially suggests. Rest on firm ground, drink regularly, and watch for overheating. The brightness, dampness, frog calls, bird noise, and distant surf may make the place feel both peaceful and strangely enveloping; allow enough time that you do not have to hurry back over wet stone.

Give the birds and pool margins space. Watch with binoculars rather than approaching nests or flocks, and avoid stepping on green mats, sedges, snails, and muddy shallows when a durable route is available. Do not collect animals or plants, and clean your footwear and equipment before entering and after leaving so you do not carry organisms between worlds.

Finally, pause and listen. Much of the interest here is in small movements—the strike of a wading bird, water gathering beneath a shelf of stone, a pool edge discovered only when it catches the light. This is a place better visited at walking pace than crossed as quickly as possible.
