## Before you go

Treat the settlement as an exposed high-mountain community built inside an active watercourse. The cold and altitude are serious, but the most distinctive hazards are the glare and the fact that a safe route can change while you are standing on it.

Arrange a local contact before arrival if you can. Ask where visitors should enter, where they can stand without obstructing work, and whether there is a sheltered place to stay. Bring a way to communicate that does not depend on shouting: a translation aid, a notebook with waterproof pages, and patience enough to watch before acting.

Plan your departure as carefully as your arrival. A route across the hollow in the morning may be flooded by evening, and the slopes beyond it disappear into cloud. Tell someone outside the settlement when you expect to return, and carry a reliable way to call for help or leave the world if your means of travel permits it. Do not assume a rescue party can reach you quickly.

## Clothing and equipment

- **Protect your eyes from all directions.** Bring properly UV-rated glacier glasses with side shields, plus a spare pair. Ordinary sunglasses may leave enough light entering from below and the sides to cause snow blindness. Ask about local slotted shades if yours fail, but don’t arrive relying on being given a pair.
- **Dress for cold and wet together.** Use insulating layers, a windproof outer layer, waterproof gloves, and spare dry socks sealed in a bag. A change of dry base layers is worth more than extra decorative clothing.
- **Wear waterproof boots with dependable traction.** Test them on wet ice before you come. Carry removable ice grips, but ask before using sharp points on fitted stonework or around woven gates.
- **Bring a staff or trekking poles.** They can help you balance and probe a step, but probing does not establish that a stone will remain fixed. Keep the tips away from gates, walls and animals.
- **Carry drinking water and food.** The clear water in the lanes is salty; a normal hiking filter or boiling will not remove the salt. Unless you have reliable desalination equipment, bring your own water or arrange a safe supply locally. Pack enough food for delays without expecting a small settlement to feed an unannounced guest.
- **Bring skin and lip protection.** Reflected light can burn exposed skin even in bitter cold. A face covering helps with both sun and wind.
- **Pack out waste.** Have sealable bags or containers for rubbish and human waste, and ask about local sanitation arrangements. Do not wash, urinate, or dispose of anything in the channels.

If arriving from a lower elevation, allow time to acclimatize. Know the signs of altitude illness, and be prepared to descend if symptoms worsen. The settlement’s small size does not make it a safe place to wait out serious illness.

## Entering the hollow

Stop at the edge and watch. Notice which lanes people use, where they set their feet, and what they do when water changes direction. If a resident is willing to guide you, follow their route exactly—even if another line of stones looks easier.

Keep your pack compact and close to your body. The lanes are narrow, the bridges low, and someone may need to carry a gate or move a stone past you at short notice. Give way early. Avoid standing in a channel, on a bridge, or in a doorway while deciding where to go.

Some stepping-stones are moving. Even a stone that was sound on the outward journey may have shifted by the return. Step deliberately, keep your weight balanced, and do not jump from stone to stone. If you cannot see a safe next step, stop somewhere stable and ask for guidance rather than trying to improvise.

The ice’s soft sigh is worth noticing, but it is not a dependable warning system. Thin ice over moving brine may break without a dramatic sound. Falling in could soak your insulation quickly and leave you struggling for footing among moving stones.

## Living among the water

Do not rearrange stones, lift a gate, clear a drain, or remove anything that looks like debris unless a resident specifically asks you to. The old wall inside a newer one may be doing work you cannot see. So may a loose-looking stone. A well-meant attempt to improve a path could redirect water into a house.

If the flow reverses, move out of the workers’ way and follow local instructions. The description gives no reason to think the reversal is an emergency for residents, but it does mean your assumptions about current, footing and drainage may suddenly be wrong. Watch for loose ice and animals moving with the water; do not try to cross a lane simply because it was shallow a moment ago.

Treat dripping from beneath a roof or a newly flooding lane as a reason to alert your host, not as a problem to investigate alone. Avoid climbing onto roofs: they are weighted, crowded, and may not be designed for a visitor’s footsteps.

## Animals, plants and neighbors

Give the shelled grazers room. Do not collect them, block their cracks, or scrape the polished rock to see what is underneath. Clean your boots and equipment before entering the settlement to reduce the chance of bringing in unfamiliar organisms, and clean them again before leaving.

The pale scavengers are accustomed to finding scraps around people, but that does not make them tame. Do not feed, corner, or try to touch them. Keep food and waste sealed. Likewise, leave the birds and their nesting gaps undisturbed. If residents are paying attention to the birds or scavengers, pause and look before continuing; their attention may mean something locally important that you do not yet understand.

Do not step on the grass by the warm seep or move its protective stones. Its small size makes it easy to overlook, but its roots help hold the bank together.

Inside a dwelling, ask where wet boots and clothing should go. Water may be welcome in the lanes but not on a sleeping place or against a wall. Keep your voice moderate: in a place this quiet, you can make yourself conspicuous without meaning to. The quiet also makes listening useful. You may hear a gate being moved, a tool at work around a corner, or someone trying to get your attention.

Bring a modest, practical gift if your contact confirms it is appropriate; do not assume scarce materials or food are automatically welcome. Above all, make yourself easy to work around. The families here spend much of the day maintaining a delicate arrangement. The best visitor is one who notices that work, leaves it undisturbed, and is ready to move when asked.
