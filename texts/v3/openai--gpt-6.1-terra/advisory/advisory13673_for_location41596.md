## Before you go

Treat the abandoned room and the settlement as two separate sites, both fragile. The room may have an unsafe floor, unstable fittings or contaminated dust. Inspect it from the doorway first, and arrange a safe route out. Bring a light, a mask for dust, and a small mat or board to kneel on **away from the crack**; do not place weight over the chamber.

If possible, visit first at ordinary size. A small, cool light held obliquely and a magnifier will show you more than a finger in the opening. Even a breath, a loose thread or a speck of skin could be substantial down there. Keep the crack clear, and don’t pour in water or use smoke to trace the passages.

Listen, too. What sounds like distant work may be entirely water and stone, or it may conceal activity you cannot distinguish. The worked objects suggest that someone has been there; they do not tell you whether the place is deserted.

## If you can become small enough to enter

Do not shrink until you know how you will return. Leave a trusted companion outside with a means of restoring you, a signal plan and an agreed deadline. Test communication through the floor beforehand. The stone and the water noise may make speech useless, and a line thin enough for you to carry could still snag a roof or block a passage. Plan a retrieval method that does not require dragging equipment through the settlement.

Enter with as little gear as possible, but bring:

- A light you can dim or switch off.
- Secure footwear with clean, non-shedding soles.
- A way to keep warm and dry, without loose fabric.
- An independent air supply if you cannot establish that the chamber’s air is safe at your scale.
- A reserve of drinking water and food, rather than taking anything from the basin.

The water is the chief hazard. At that size, a bead between paving stones may be an obstacle rather than a splash; a falling drop could knock you down or trap you against the basin. The drain’s roar suggests a confined flow with little room to escape. Stay well back from the basin and the paving seams, and never enter the covered drain. The streets and roofs may also be slick even where they look dry.

Expect the sound to be disorienting. Protect your hearing if you can do so without losing all awareness of your surroundings. Pause after entering and let yourself learn the rhythm of the drops before moving.

## Among the houses

Move slowly and look for an unoccupied place to stand. The masonry is fitted without mortar; do not lean against a wall, lift a slate, or step across a roof. Avoid the pale animals, including the ones hidden in seams. They may be harmless, but you do not know what they eat, how they defend themselves, or what you might introduce to them.

Leave the flakes, pebbles and drilled shard where they are. They may be tools, offerings, markers or something you would not recognize. Photograph or draw them without touching them, provided your light does not disturb anyone.

If inhabitants appear, stop and give them room. Keep your hands visible, lower your light and wait for them to approach or withdraw. Do not assume the buildings are abandoned merely because you saw nobody from above. Be prepared to leave without making contact.

Finally, don’t rely on the chamber behaving according to ordinary expectations. The unchanging wet margins and the droplet that hangs while watched are observations, not an explanation. You may be looking at a stable flow, an unusual local effect, or something stranger. Keep your own time, check in with your companion, and leave at the planned signal even if nothing inside seems to have changed.
