Treat this as a brief visit to a fragile, active structure, not as an ordinary cave. Much of what looks like floor, wall, or ceiling is alive, and even the new “stone” is soft. The greatest challenge may be finding a way in without damaging the hollow or becoming trapped.

**Before entering, scout remotely.** Locate an existing entrance and check its dimensions all the way through; apparent passages inside may end in dense foliage. Don’t cut through the root ceiling or excavate a route. If there is no opening large enough to use without forcing the plants apart, admire it remotely and leave.

Assume the air is unsafe until tested for oxygen and contaminants. The cold is severe enough that exposed skin, equipment, and batteries may fail quickly. Bring:

- An insulated, sealed cold-weather suit with independent breathing air, rated for temperatures colder than those measured on arrival.
- A way to monitor temperature, air supply, and your remaining safe exit time; keep a generous reserve for retreat.
- A low-output, adjustable lamp with a backup. The plants’ light is beautiful but too faint and slow-moving to navigate by reliably.
- Communication with someone outside, plus a recovery plan that does not depend on attaching ropes or anchors to the living roof.
- Clean, decontaminated equipment and a way to contain anything that comes out on your suit. Spores and unfamiliar microorganisms could travel in either direction.

Avoid carrying heat into the hollow unnecessarily, but **do not compromise your own insulation** to protect it. An unsealed visitor’s breath would frost the leaves; body heat, loose fibers, and tracked-in soil could all alter this small environment. Don’t drink the water in the leaf cups or touch the fruiting bodies.

Inside, move slowly and keep your party small. There may be no truly harmless place to put a foot: the pale floor supports burrowing animals and delicate new growth. Before entering, consider whether a clean, lightweight platform could distribute your weight along a short route without crushing the pelt; if even that would cause unacceptable damage, stay out. Don’t press the porous mineral ribs, however tempting the thumbprint might be.

Expect darkness that gradually resolves into layers of fans, blades, and needle-furred stalks as the light travels through them. The sound may be startlingly large for such a small space: deep notes in the walls, ice cracking, droplets ticking. The ceiling’s hum appears to make the animals grip and pause. Treat it as a cue to stop moving, observe, and be ready to withdraw—not as proof that the roof is about to fall, but not as something to ignore either.

Set a short visit time in advance. Watch the position of your entrance, since overlapping leaves can make the space difficult to read. If you disturb the burrower or uncover a warm seam crowded with animals, back away rather than trying to get a closer look.

On leaving, inspect and clean your suit in containment before returning to another environment. The most faithful souvenir is a recording: the sound of a roof humming and the slow passage of light through a place still making itself, grain by grain.
