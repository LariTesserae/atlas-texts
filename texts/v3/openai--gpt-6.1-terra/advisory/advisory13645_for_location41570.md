## Before you go

Treat the city as an expedition site, not a settlement. Its streets and chambers resemble architecture, but nothing in the description suggests shelter, supplies, inhabitants, or routes designed for human bodies. The chief danger may be that the place looks legible: a doorway looks like a doorway, a neighboring district looks close, and a street seems to connect the intersections at either end.

**Plan a short reconnaissance first, with a dependable way home.** Do not enter until your means of returning has been tested from the intended arrival point and can be operated without finding a particular distant landmark. If your travel method requires a fixed portal or device, keep it in sight during the first visit and establish whether the ground beneath it remains stable. Leave a support team outside with a scheduled return time and clear instructions for what to do if you miss it.

Send instruments ahead if possible. At minimum, check:

- Air pressure and composition, including oxygen, carbon dioxide, and contaminants.
- Temperature, radiation, and the concentration and character of the airborne powder.
- Gravity and footing at the arrival point.
- Whether radio, other communications, and your return equipment work locally.
- Whether a camera or marked probe can be observed continuously while crossing a doorway and then brought back.

An uneventful test at one threshold will not prove that another is safe. The animals’ tracks suggest that connections between openings can behave in ways the visible streets do not explain; they do not tell you whether a human could follow them, or return.

## What to bring

Bring everything needed for the visit and for an unplanned wait. There are no visible pools, and the dark film and wall animals should not be treated as food or water.

- **Breathing protection appropriate to the measurements.** Until the air is verified, use an independent air supply rather than assuming a dust filter is enough. Even breathable air may contain fine mineral particles; protect your eyes and keep dust out of equipment.
- **Water, food, and insulation.** The sky opening provides light but no apparent warmth. Measure conditions rather than judging temperature by sight.
- **Reliable lighting**, with spare power. The ambient light may be sufficient to see by, but shadows and the apparent position of the opening are not trustworthy for orientation.
- **Sturdy boots, gloves, and a helmet.** The streets are ribbed mineral and the forms have thin, worn edges. Choose footwear that lets you feel changes in the surface without exposing you to sharp grit.
- **Navigation and recording equipment:** a continuously recording camera, a simple written log, local markers that can be retrieved, and instruments for checking movement or deformation if available. Do not count on a compass, satellite navigation, or a map made elsewhere.
- **A first-aid kit and enough reserves for delay**, including reserve breathing supply if used.

Travel with at least one companion if you can. Stay close enough to communicate directly, but avoid linking yourselves in a way that could pull one person after another across an edge or unexpectedly changing passage.

## Choosing where to walk

Start in a broad, apparently stable area near your return point. Spend time watching before moving farther. Look for dust rising from terrace edges, powder suspended in horizontal bands, fresh cracks, walls leaning toward one another, and floors that seem to be lifting. The city’s movement is mostly slow and quiet; waiting for a loud warning may not help.

Keep away from the escarpment and the outer chambers open to it. The drop cannot be judged by ordinary visual cues, and districts that look nearly reachable may not be connected at all. Do not attempt to cross between neighboring heights merely because they appear close.

Avoid passages whose walls are narrowing or touching, and do not shelter beneath a newly opening arch or beside a wall that has slumped into a dust apron. A large chamber might offer room to stand, but its hollow walls are not necessarily structurally safe. If you need to pause, choose an open position with more than one plainly visible way back toward your return point.

When dust suddenly sinks or rises, or a terrace begins to move, stop advancing. Protect your breathing and eyes, get clear of edges and constricting walls if you can do so without rushing, and reassess the route. The animals pressing themselves flat during a slip are a useful sign that something is happening, but not a reliable safety alarm.

## Finding your way back

Use the route you actually walked as your primary reference, not the skyline. The same terraces can appear in more than one direction, and the sky opening changes apparent position. Record turns, thresholds, distinctive wall folds, and the appearance of intersections as you pass them. Look back frequently: a route may be much harder to recognize in reverse.

Place removable markers only where they will not obstruct the return route or damage the site. Check that each marker remains where you put it and is visible from the next one. Even then, treat markers as evidence, not guarantees: a street can lengthen, a passage can close, and a doorway may not lead where its surroundings suggest.

For an initial visit, set a modest limit: return while you still have abundant air, power, and time, and before the route becomes complicated. If an intersection changes, a marker is missing, communication fails, or the return point no longer behaves as expected, abandon further exploration.

Do not follow an animal through a threshold to see where it comes out. Watch from a distance and record what you can. Its ability to navigate the walls may depend on its size or biology, and its track ending cleanly at a wall does not establish a safe passage for you.

## While you are there

Expect emptiness on a scale that is difficult to prepare for. The spaces are enormous, yet the light gives little sense of time or weather. There may be no familiar sound except your own movement and equipment. Give yourself a task and a turnaround time before entering; both are easier to honor than a vague intention to “look a little farther.”

Leave the animals and their grazing film undisturbed. Avoid scraping samples from the walls on a first visit, and clean dust from your gear before leaving according to your expedition’s containment procedures. The film supports the only visible life there, and neither its biology nor the effects of carrying it elsewhere are known.

The best first visit may be brief: arrive, verify that you can leave, observe one district through an interval of stillness and change, and return with measurements. The city will offer many apparent invitations to continue. None is as important as keeping the way back.
