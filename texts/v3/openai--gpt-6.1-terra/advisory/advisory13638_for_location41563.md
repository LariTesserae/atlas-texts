Treat the basin as an active industrial site, not an abandoned landscape. The cold and fog are serious, but the greatest dangers may be the things hidden by them: moving machinery, live electrical equipment, sudden water surges and an unfinished drop.

### Before entering

Get permission and, if possible, arrange to be accompanied by someone who operates or maintains the works. Ask where visitors may walk, how the pump cycles are signaled, whether the fans and press can start without warning, and where the unfinished edge begins. Identify more than one exit and a warm, dry place to retreat to.

Do not go alone. Agree on a turnaround time and a way to summon help that will work over the machinery noise. A radio with a noise-compatible headset may be useful; confirm reception inside rather than assuming it. Bring independent lights and spare power, since the fixed lamps leave large gaps in visibility.

Have qualified personnel assess the atmosphere before entry, especially in low pockets, shafts and enclosed areas. Fog is not itself proof of bad air, but it can conceal a release, and the basin’s ventilation behaves unpredictably. A respirator is not a substitute for adequate oxygen. If the route involves a confined space, use the site’s confined-space procedures and rescue arrangements rather than improvising.

### What to wear and carry

Dress for cold *and* wet: insulating layers under waterproof outerwear, with spare gloves and dry clothing waiting outside. Choose clothing that fits close enough not to catch on bolts or machinery. Wear a hard hat, eye protection, hearing protection and sturdy, slip-resistant safety footwear appropriate to the site. Ice may defeat ordinary traction, so follow local advice about footwear rather than assuming it will grip.

Carry a small, secured pack with water, a light, a whistle or agreed signaling device, and basic first-aid supplies. Keep straps and loose items tucked away. Depending on the actual depth and current, personnel may require flotation equipment and a water-rescue plan; don’t assume the channels are shallow everywhere.

### Moving through the basin

Stay on designated routes and well back from the crane, suspended plates, welding and unfinished edge. Do not look directly at the welding arc. Watch for falling water and ice as well as loads overhead. The sharp plate corners, raised bolts and grating are easy to miss when you are watching the fog.

Avoid walking through channels or flooded grating unless the operators have explicitly made the route safe. The water may be moving faster than it appears, conceal gaps, or be near energized equipment. When a pump starts, retreat to a known safe position rather than trying to cross ahead of the surge. A pause in the press or a clearing of the fog does not mean the machinery has stopped for good.

Mark your route with your guide’s help and keep your group close enough to communicate. The press’s impacts may be felt through your feet; when it stops, the apparent hush could make the place seem larger and emptier. Neither feeling is a reliable guide to distance.

### While you are there

Give the small life room. Don’t pull up the sedge or scrape films from pipes, and leave stranded crustaceans to the next surge rather than reaching into moving water. The white growths beneath the staircase are unidentified; avoid touching or disturbing them, particularly where insulation fibers are loose.

Plan a short first visit. Standing still to take in the lamps, silver leaves and red glow is tempting, but wet cold accumulates quietly, and the view can disappear when the fans reverse. Leave before anyone is chilled, confused about the route, or low on light or communication power. Outside, warm up gradually, change out of wet clothes and check for slips, cuts and cold injury.
