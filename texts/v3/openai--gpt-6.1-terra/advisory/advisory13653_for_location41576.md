This is a place to visit slowly, on a dry day, with the expectation that you may decide not to enter at all. The hollow sounds intimate and sheltered, but its entrance is a constricted opening in fractured rock, and its water and air may change with conditions you cannot see from inside.

### Before you go

- **Check the weather beyond the hollow itself.** Rain falling elsewhere in the surrounding limestone may still find its way underground. Choose a dry period with no rain forecast, and leave immediately if water begins appearing in the ceiling channels.
- **Go with an experienced caver or local guide.** Check whether access is permitted and whether the bats are protected or especially sensitive at that time of year. Tell someone outside your party where you are going and when you expect to return.
- **Assess the entrance without relying on the roots or fallen slabs.** Roots growing around a rock do not necessarily make it secure. If the gap is unstable, too tight to move through comfortably, or would make retreat difficult, stay out.
- **Consider the bats before entering.** They are close to the only described entrance, so disturbance may be unavoidable. If they are active or you cannot pass quietly without approaching them closely, choose another time or leave the hollow to them.

### What to bring

Wear a helmet with a headlamp, sturdy shoes with good grip, and light, durable clothing that covers your skin against thorns and rough stone. Bring at least two independent backup lights, with spare batteries. Carry water from outside; the shallow basins are for the hollow’s inhabitants and should not be assumed safe to drink.

Pack lightly enough that you can turn around and climb out without becoming wedged. A small first-aid kit and a way to communicate outside are useful, though a phone may not work underground. If the entrance requires a serious scramble or technical descent, get competent local guidance and suitable equipment rather than improvising.

The air is described as hot and still. Rest and drink before entering, and do not count on the hollow as a cool refuge. If anyone develops a headache, dizziness, unusual breathlessness, or nausea, leave promptly. Do not use a flame to test the air.

### Inside the hollow

Give your eyes time to adjust, and move your light gently rather than sweeping it repeatedly across the bats. Look before placing each hand or foot: the ledges, fungal fans, leaf litter, and narrow gaps are occupied, even where nothing first appears to be moving. Avoid brushing through the ferns or stepping into a basin.

Keep your group small and your stay brief. Don’t touch or handle the bats or other animals, and avoid disturbing droppings or dusty deposits. If a bat touches or bites someone, wash the area and seek medical advice promptly.

The three stones near the lowest basin may be an old human arrangement, or they may have a significance that is no longer visible. Leave them exactly as they are, along with the plants growing over them. Take photographs without rearranging anything, collect no specimens, and carry everything you brought back out.

Pause once you are settled and listen. The hollow’s life may become apparent only after you stop moving: a lizard on the grit, an insect at the water’s edge, wind filtered through the roots above. Keep track of the entrance while you do. If the light fades, the air feels wrong, the rock shifts, or water starts to run, the visit is over.
