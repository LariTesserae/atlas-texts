Prepare for a working city, not an expedition into wilderness—though you may find wilderness between one street and the next. The most important things to bring are patience, good boots, and a willingness to accept that a familiar route may no longer be familiar tomorrow.

### Before you arrive

If you can choose your point of arrival, choose an inhabited ward near a railway stop, preferably in daylight. Arrange a meeting with a local contact who knows that particular ward; a resident of the opposite side of the basin may be no more reliable a guide to its current passages than you are. Ask how visitors are expected to register, where to find the observation board, and which routes to avoid.

Make sure your means of leaving is dependable under uncertain spatial conditions. If it relies on fixed coordinates, sight lines, a signal, or a device left at your arrival point, find out whether it will still work if the distance between you and that point changes. Do not assume that walking back along your outward route will return you to the same place.

Pack for cool damp, mineral dust, and low light:

- Waterproof layers and sturdy, slip-resistant boots, especially for wet rails and stone.
- A dependable lamp and spare batteries; reflective clothing will help railway and factory workers see you.
- Eye protection and a well-fitting particulate respirator for dusty ground or industrial areas. Leave if you encounter fumes or air you cannot identify.
- Drinking water, basic food, medication, and a small first-aid kit.
- A paper notebook for directions and observations. Protect electronics and other fragile possessions in padded, water-resistant cases.

If you do not know whether local water is safe for you, bring enough for your initial stay and ask residents about treated sources. Covered cisterns are part of the city’s infrastructure, not necessarily public drinking fountains.

### Moving through the city

Check the ward’s observation board every day, and again before making an important journey. Note the dates on warnings: a route described last week may already have changed. Ask a local to explain the chalk marks rather than guessing at them. Children here learn passages as part of the street network; as a visitor, you have not had that education.

Use established streets, walkways, and the railway when possible. Keep away from tracks and dark cuts except at designated crossings, and give trains plenty of room. A horn returning from the basin may not tell you where the engine is.

Treat any unexpected shortcut as a hazard, not a convenience. A bucket’s water can lag behind it, and glass can crack in transit; those accounts give you no reason to assume that a human body would pass through unharmed. Do not follow animals across vacant ground to see where they emerge, and do not test a suspicious passage with your hand or a piece of equipment. If a route suddenly seems wrong, stop somewhere safe and ask for local help rather than pressing on or running back through it.

Travel with someone who knows the area when you can. Agree on a meeting place in an occupied building rather than an empty lot, and tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return. Carry a means of communication, but do not rely on it as your only way to get help.

### Around buildings, rock, and water

The sliding foundations and measuring marks are working safety features. Do not step over barriers, interfere with stakes, or enter a building because it looks abandoned. Give workshops, pipes, ventilation outlets, and raised walkways the same respect you would give an operating industrial site anywhere. Ask before photographing workers or entering factory grounds.

Keep your distance from fresh seams and cliff faces. Their movement may be too slow to feel, but sharp rock, unstable ground, and unfamiliar heat are immediate enough hazards. The northern wall is especially difficult to judge by appearance: a view from a window is not a sound basis for deciding how far away it is, and smoke passing behind it is not an invitation to investigate.

Rain can be local, so carry your waterproof layer even if your own street is dry. Watch for condensation dripping from bridges and pipes, and for slick rails after dark. Avoid the ponds’ edges and reservoir grates unless a local guide says an area is accessible.

Give the animals room. The grazers, birds, and mammals know the city on terms you do not; they may also be startled, protective, or carrying unfamiliar organisms. Watch from a distance, do not feed them, and check your boots and clothing before returning home.

### Settling in

Choose lodging in an occupied building whose residents can explain its exits and any current local warnings. A window looking toward the northern wall might be memorable, but do not choose a room solely for the view. Ask about ventilation, drinking water, and whether the building has a procedure for foundation repairs or an unexpected change in access.

The city will be dimmer and louder than it first sounds on paper. Bring earplugs if you sleep lightly, and leave time to rest: the fans’ continuous breath, the presses, and the delayed repetitions of railway horns may take getting used to. After dark, the gaps between occupied clusters can be long and poorly lit, so plan evening travel before the lights begin to come on.

Finally, let the residents set the pace. They tend cisterns, clear birds from machines, and update boards because this is home, not because every day is an emergency. Read the warnings, pay attention to ordinary routines, and leave room in your plans for a street to be unavailable—or for a local to tell you that the sensible way there today is not the way you came yesterday.
