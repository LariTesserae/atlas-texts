## Before you arrive

Plan for a hot, wet upland rather than a cool mountaintop. The air is breathable, but its heat and humidity may make exertion surprisingly difficult. Spend your first day near your arrival point, walking slowly and drinking regularly. Even in thick air, the actual altitude, pressure and oxygen concentration are unknown; bring a reliable atmosphere monitor and a way to leave promptly if you develop a headache, confusion, unusual breathlessness or other symptoms.

Wear light, long-sleeved clothing that dries reasonably well, sturdy boots with good grip, and a brimmed hat. Bring insect protection even if none of the visible animals resemble familiar biting insects. Waterproof your supplies rather than yourself: you may want a light rain layer, but a heavy shell could become unbearable in the heat. Gloves and trekking poles would be useful on wet stone.

Carry more safe drinking water than the short distances might seem to require. There is water everywhere, but its clarity tells you little about its chemistry or microorganisms. Bring a purifier appropriate for biological contamination, a separate way to assess or remove chemical contaminants, and reserve water in case neither is sufficient. Do not drink from the bowl in the shelter simply because it has been provided.

## Moving through the high country

The plateau invites long walks, but the green shelves conceal steep drops, deep seams and waterlogged ground. A waterfall that disappears into moss may emerge beside a drop; one that passes through the cloud floor gives you no view of where a fall would end. Stay well back from rims, especially where spray keeps the rock slick. Do not climb the cliffs or trust the fleshy wall plants as handholds.

The open valleys and repeated ridges may look straightforward until you try to retrace a route. Carry an independent navigation system and a physical record of your return path; do not rely on a clear view of the sun, the cloud floor, or shadows that may not point where expected. Mark your route unobtrusively and remove any markers on the way back. If you have a multiverse return device, keep it on your person, protect it from moisture and test it before wandering far.

Set modest distances. The dense air may carry sound beautifully, but it will not necessarily carry a call for help to anyone able to answer it. Travel with a companion if possible, tell someone where and when to expect you, and have a firm turnaround time.

## Among the animals and plants

Pause often and let your eyes adjust to the grass. The copper-backed grazers are easy to miss until you are close to them; give them room and avoid separating animals from one another. Watch the broad-winged animals themselves rather than using their shadows to judge their position. A shadow moving against its owner is reason enough not to trust ordinary visual cues here.

The low clicking near pools may be harmless, but its source is concealed. Approach quietly, remain on firm ground, and back away if the chorus changes abruptly in response to you or if you notice movement around your feet. Avoid touching or sampling the plants, however intriguing the cliff gardens are. Their responses to warmth, and any effects they might have on human skin, are unknown.

Keep food sealed and leave no scraps. On returning, clean your boots and equipment and follow whatever decontamination procedure is available for travel between worlds. The aim is to protect both this place and the one you go home to.

## The shelter and the poles

The shelter may be a refuge, a dwelling, an instrument, or something whose purpose you would not recognize. Approach it respectfully. Look and listen before entering; do not assume that an open doorway means it is unoccupied or offered for your use. If you do shelter there, avoid touching the walls or disturbing the bowl, and be ready to leave if the structure changes or an inhabitant arrives. For a planned overnight visit, bring your own shelter and choose a site away from watercourses, cliff bases and animal paths.

Give the three poles and the distant ridges a wide berth, particularly toward evening. Their apparent inability to support themselves, and the arcs between them and the ridges, suggest something operating beyond familiar construction. The animals’ calm is reassuring about the *frequency* of the event, not necessarily its safety for a visitor. Watch from a distance with a clear route back. If the clouds begin brightening or arcs appear, do not move closer to investigate; avoid exposed ridgelines and be prepared to retreat.

## What it may feel like

The most difficult adjustment may be to the place’s pace. At first the water and animal calls will seem continuous; after a while you may begin to hear individual falls, separate calls and the slow rise of the clicking chorus. The scale of the ferns and the hidden grazers may make ordinary distances hard to judge. Beneath the warm air, the cloud floor will look almost solid, though it offers no footing at all.

Give yourself time to sit somewhere safe, drink water and watch. This is a landscape where much is happening slowly and much is hidden in plain sight. You need not understand the poles, enter the shelter or reach the farthest valley to have visited it well.
