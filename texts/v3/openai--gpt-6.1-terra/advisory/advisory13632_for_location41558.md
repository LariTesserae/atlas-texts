Prepare for a brief, quiet visit, not a crossing. The basin is small, but altitude, heat, unstable stone, and water behaving in unfamiliar ways make distance a poor measure of effort. Give yourself time to observe from the edge and permission to leave without reaching the structure.

### Before you arrive

Choose an arrival point on stable ground near the rim, if you can verify one. Do not assume the black ridges are solid or that the clouds below conceal a safe descent. Arrange a return route that does not depend on finding a path out through the landscape, and test whatever means of return you use before stepping away from it.

Treat the elevation as serious even though the light is bright and the plants are flourishing. Bring a portable oxygen supply, a way to monitor your condition, and enough time to acclimatize if your method of travel permits it. If you develop a severe headache, confusion, unusual breathlessness, or poor coordination, leave rather than trying to rest it off in the basin.

A companion who can help you retreat would be valuable. Keep each other in sight, agree on a turnaround time, and make sure someone outside the location knows when to expect you. Do not count on ordinary radio signals working across ridges—or across worlds.

### What to wear and carry

Wear sturdy, closed boots with good grip and protection against sharp rock. Young volcanic stone can cut, shift, or give way; wet glassy patches may be especially slippery. Long trousers and gloves will help if you stumble, though gloves are not an invitation to handle the plants or the structure.

Bring:

- Drinking water and food from home. Do not drink from the pools or eat anything growing there.
- Layers for cold air as well as heat, plus rain protection.
- A walking pole to test footing without putting your hand down.
- A light and a watch; neither the passing clouds nor the wetness of the stone seems like a reliable clock.
- A gas monitor suitable for volcanic environments, if available. Hot ground can release dangerous gases without an obvious warning.
- A camera or notebook for observations. Avoid collecting samples unless you have a compelling reason and understand the consequences.

Keep your hands free enough to balance. Move slowly, and do not step into a pool simply because it looks shallow: the water may be very hot, and the rock beneath it may be thin or uneven.

### In the basin

Pause before entering. Listen, watch the pools, and look for steaming patches, cracks, loose surfaces, and places where the filaments are moving. The apparent stillness of the water does not tell you its depth, temperature, or direction of flow. Water that reappears higher on a wall may also be moving somewhere inside the rock under your feet.

Give the plants and animals space. A folding leaf might be responding to you, to water, or to something else entirely; there is no need to find out by touching it. Do not approach the drinking creature closely, and take care not to trample the fern bed or the margins of the seep. Much of the basin is bare, but the wet bare rock may be precisely where water or filaments are traveling.

The structure deserves the same restraint. Its surface does not show a person standing in front of the ridge’s reflection, so a reflection may not be useful for judging where you are relative to it. Approach only if the footing is clearly safe. Do not pull a filament, block one with a heavy object, scrape its tracks, or try to force an entrance into a structure that has no door. It may be tending the basin, or it may be part of a process neither you nor the plants can afford to interrupt.

If its surface clouds while you are there, stop and watch from where you are rather than hurrying closer. The plants’ response suggests that the change matters, but not whether it is dangerous. Leave if the ground heats noticeably, gas alarms sound, water begins moving unpredictably around your route, or you feel unwell.

### What the visit may feel like

You may hear more than you expect: a drop on a leaf across the basin, a tick in the ferns, your own breathing made conspicuous by the thin air. The daylight will look reassuringly ordinary, while the rain arrives from below and a pool fills without rippling.

Find a safe place to stand and let the place happen around you. Watch long enough to see a filament change course or a leaf begin to fold. Then go back the way you came, before fatigue or curiosity makes the few minutes across the basin feel much longer.
