The courtyard may be only a few steps across, but reaching it could be the harder and more dangerous part of the visit. Prepare for a hot, confined, dusty place with an unknown atmosphere and no local water—not as a courtyard where you can simply pause in the shade.

### Before you go

- **Find and inspect a route in.** The openings described include passages too low to stand in and reaches choked with grit. Don’t assume any of them is passable by a person or that the way out will remain clear. If possible, send a camera or small remotely operated probe first, and check the stability of the flues, shutters and surrounding masonry.
- **Arrange a team and a rescue plan.** Have people outside the confined area who know your route, expected return time and emergency procedure. Test communications through the walls. A retrieval line may help on a suitable route, but it can snag in narrow passages; don’t rely on it without assessing the path.
- **Measure the environment before entry.** The presence of plants does not establish that the air is breathable. Check oxygen, potentially hazardous gases, temperature and dust conditions, including in the passages. Continue monitoring while inside. If the atmosphere is unknown or unsafe, use appropriate supplied-air equipment and a trained confined-space team; an ordinary dust mask does not supply oxygen.
- **Bring all the water you need, plus reserve.** There is none to collect from the plants or ground. Bring food and any medication as well, and plan a short visit with generous margins for delays.
- **Plan for heat, including the heat of your equipment.** Measure conditions rather than guessing from the dim light. Protective clothing and breathing equipment can make heat strain worse, so set conservative exposure limits and have a cooler place outside where you can recover. Night is not necessarily safe: the stone continues to release stored heat.

### What to wear and carry

Wear sturdy, close-fitting clothing that protects against grit and abrasion without catching on fronds or masonry. Bring eye protection, gloves, knee protection for crawling, and footwear with reliable grip. A helmet is sensible around low stonework and knocking shutters.

Carry a dependable light and a backup, even if you expect daylight: only narrow bars reach the courtyard. Protect lights, cameras and other equipment from fine dust. Hearing protection may be useful, but choose it so you can still hear alarms or communicate with your team.

Avoid flames and unnecessary sparks. The oily plant tissues and dust may present a fire hazard, though their properties are unknown. Don’t handle, taste or cut the plants, and don’t count on them as a source of drinkable liquid.

### While you are there

Move slowly and keep your route back in view or otherwise reliably marked. The center may look empty, but the floor has shallow seams full of grit, and drafts can shift grains underfoot. Give loose shutters and the crowded margins a wide berth. The fronds are tall and stiff; pushing through them could damage the growth and obscure your footing.

The place will probably feel more enclosed than its open center suggests. The light is scant, the walls are thick, and the noise never quite resolves into something familiar: stone knocking in its frames, grains hissing across the floor, leaves rattling together. Don’t use the absence of animal sounds as a cue that anything has gone quiet or safe.

If you have a choice, avoid the period when the reversing night drafts lift grains into a column at the center. Visit when measurements show the most manageable conditions, not simply at the coolest-looking hour. Leave the mineral beds and old clay joints undisturbed, take everything you brought back out, and turn around early if the heat, air, visibility or route is worse than expected.
