The chamber is small enough that the main danger may be the transition into it: a sideways step from an exposed mountainside into darkness and a noise that starts at full volume. Prepare for the approach and retreat at least as carefully as for the chamber itself.

### Before climbing

Travel with experienced mountaineers who can assess the route, weather, altitude and anchors. Acclimatize gradually, and plan a turnaround time that leaves ample daylight and energy for the descent. The entrance is difficult to distinguish from the rock, so establish its location from a safe vantage rather than searching along the precipice edge.

Bring ordinary high-mountain protection: layered clothing, gloves, eye protection, food, drinking water and a reliable way to summon help. The plants contain no usable water; do not count on the chamber for supplies.

Use a rope system secured to sound rock **outside** the chamber. Have a partner remain in a safe position, and agree on simple rope-tug signals before approaching. The sound may make speech impossible, while the narrow doorway may make it difficult to see one another.

### At the threshold

Put on hearing protection before entering. Use earplugs and earmuffs together if they fit under your helmet, and carry a sound-level meter if possible. The noise’s source and physical properties are unknown: ordinary hearing protection may reduce the risk, but should not be taken as proof that a prolonged stay is safe.

Wear a helmet and bring a hands-free lamp with a backup. Keep both lamps dim enough to avoid dazzling yourself against the metallic leaf edges. A light from behind may also help you find the narrow exit.

If equipment is available, sample the air from the doorway before putting your head inside, checking at least oxygen and common hazardous gases. The absence of water and the smell of hot iron do not establish whether the air is safe. A dust mask can reduce inhalation of particles, but it cannot supply oxygen or protect against every vapor.

Enter slowly, keeping your tether clear of the rock and vegetation. Expect the sudden sound and close, dark surroundings to be disorienting. If you feel dizzy, confused, short of breath, or unable to locate the exit, step back out immediately. Agree in advance that anyone can end the visit without needing to explain why.

### Inside

One person at a time is probably enough. The floor mats, hanging growth and cramped space leave little room to move without crushing something or becoming entangled. Place your feet deliberately, and avoid using the plants, cables or iron housings for support.

Treat the chamber as both a fragile habitat and a site of unknown machinery. Do not pull leaves, collect mineral grains, touch the gauge or turn the exposed wheel. The gauge’s proximity to its red mark may mean something—or nothing comprehensible—but there is no need to test it. The lack of visible movement is not evidence that the devices are inert.

Keep the visit brief. Look, listen through your protection, and photograph without rearranging anything. Do not assume that a recording or meter will capture the sound in the same way you experience it, or that its apparent stillness means there are no effects on you.

When leaving, turn carefully toward the pale wedge of the doorway and check your footing and tether before stepping back onto the exposed approach. Once safely away from the edge, check one another for hearing changes, headache, dizziness and symptoms of altitude illness.

The best preparation is to make retreat easy. This is a place to observe without forcing it to reveal what it is.
