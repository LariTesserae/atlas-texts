Treat the plateau as a remote high-altitude desert with two additional hazards: **the ground moves, and the dust does things you cannot yet explain**. The ruins may offer shelter, but none should be part of your survival plan until its behavior has been tested.

## Before you arrive

Make a remote survey first, if your means of travel permits it. Measure air pressure, oxygen, temperature over a full day, radiation, and dust composition. “Cool and very thin” might mean difficult breathing; it might mean a pressure at which an ordinary human cannot survive without a pressure suit. Do not count on acclimatization to solve an unknown atmosphere.

Observe at least one complete cycle of the moving seams and several storms. In particular, find out whether the surface movement is confined to visible cracks or whether broad plates tilt, rise, or separate without warning. Watch your proposed arrival site long enough to see what passes through it.

Arrive with a companion or support team, and arrange a reliable extraction method that does not depend on finding a particular stone marker after the ground has shifted. Keep the return point in sight or continuously tracked if possible. If your gateway requires equipment on the surface, anchor it only after the ground has been assessed; have a second means of leaving if one is available.

## What to bring

- **A pressure-capable environmental suit**, with breathable gas, thermal control, eye protection, and a substantial reserve. Use the level of protection indicated by measurements, not by the fact that the sky looks blue.
- **Dust-tight filters, seals, and covers.** The metallic particles may abrade mechanisms, bridge electrical contacts, or affect instruments. Carry spare filters and protected, redundant equipment.
- **Water, food, and oxygen for an extended delay.** There is no visible standing water, and distances are deceptive. Plan in days, not by how near something looks.
- **A storm shelter** designed for abrasive dust and the measured wind conditions. Test that it can be erected and occupied without exposing you to the air. Avoid pitching it across a seam or in a channel where dust is accumulating.
- **Navigation and tracking equipment with independent backups:** surveyed coordinates if they work, inertial navigation, physical bearings, and a record of your route. Protect electronics, and do not assume a compass or radio will work normally around metallic dust.
- **A probe or pole, inspection camera, and sealed sample containers.** These let you investigate cracks and structures without putting your body into them.
- **A first-aid and extraction kit**, including a way to repair suit damage and move an incapacitated companion.

Secure loose gear. A small dropped object could vanish into moving dust, and opening a pack repeatedly will bring grit into everything you own.

## Moving across the plateau

The most important adjustment is to stop trusting your eyes. Clear light and distant visibility make the plain look manageable, but a ruin that seems close may be a day away. Measure progress against your instruments and your available supplies. Set a firm turnaround time before leaving shelter, and obey it even if the destination still looks tantalizingly near.

Walk slowly enough to inspect the ground ahead. Seams described as too straight to be natural deserve particular attention, but the absence of a visible crack is not proof that a plate is stable. Do not step into a gap, camp beside one, or assume it will remain the same width while you cross. If you must traverse an area of active movement, keep your party spaced so one shift cannot trap everyone at once.

Stone stacks may be useful as local markers, but they are not a dependable map. Photograph and record landmarks along with your track; a stack may remain intact while the terrain between it and the next marker changes.

Watch the dust as well as the horizon. Opposing bands may reveal local flows that are not obvious from the apparent wind. High shadows can arrive before their source is visible, and a distant wall can change direction quickly. The copper-colored growths curling inward might provide another warning, but they are not an instrument. If visibility begins to fail or the storm’s movement becomes uncertain, retreat to verified shelter early rather than trying to outrun it.

## Shelter and ruins

A dome is worth investigating, but approach it as an unknown machine, not a friendly refuge. Its denser air and clean floor patch suggest an active effect whose limits and purpose are unknown. Sample the air before considering removal of any respiratory protection; pressure alone does not establish that it is breathable. Check whether the entrance remains open, whether dust or air moves unexpectedly across the threshold, and whether your instruments behave normally inside. Keep your companion and extraction equipment outside until you understand more.

Likewise, do not walk casually between the leaning slabs just because they have remained standing. The slowing grains show that something unusual is happening there. Observe from a distance, then use a remote probe if there is a reason to investigate.

The circles avoided by moving seams may eventually prove to be stable ground, but that is a hypothesis, not a guarantee. Do not choose one as your sole camp or gateway site without watching it through a meaningful period of surface activity.

Leave the growths and animals undisturbed. They are sparse, adapted to conditions you are not, and of no established use as food or water. Avoid carrying local material back into your habitat without containment and decontamination.

## What it will feel like

Expect isolation more than spectacle. Much of the danger may be nearly silent: a storm occupying half the horizon, dust streaming in contradictory directions around your boots, a seam opening while the broad landscape seems unchanged. Even the apparent calm may be misleading.

Find a stable, sheltered place to stop and look, but keep your suit on, your route home known, and your supplies within reach. At evening, when the plain turns gray and grains begin tapping against the stones, the next storm may already be closer than it appears.
