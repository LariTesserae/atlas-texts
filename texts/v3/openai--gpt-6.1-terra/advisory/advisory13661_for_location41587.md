## Before you arrive

Treat this as a visit to a working flood-control system that happens to contain a vast city. The main danger is not that everything is wet; it is that a familiar-looking street can become a watercourse within minutes.

Before committing to an arrival point, verify the basics that the description cannot establish: whether the air is safe to breathe, the local organisms and water pose infectious risks, and local food is suitable for a human. If those checks are unavailable, arrange a short, supported reconnaissance rather than stepping straight into a crowded district. Confirm that your means of returning home works from inside the basin, and keep it accessible above flood level.

Choose an arrival point in a well-served **upper district**, near reliable shelter and a staffed transit stop. Avoid the lowest streets, freight yards, active construction and canal edges until you understand local warnings. A local guide would be especially valuable for the first few days—not just for language, but for reading the city’s changing traffic, gates and safe routes.

## What to bring

- **Lightweight waterproof layers** with ventilation. The rain is warm and brief; a heavy coat may leave you soaked in sweat instead. Expect spray and falling drips even after the sun returns.
- **Secure, waterproof footwear with excellent grip**, suitable for slick stone and stairs. Avoid open sandals. Ankle-deep water is not necessarily shallow enough to make safe footing visible.
- **Hearing protection** that reduces the noise without completely isolating you. Bring a spare pair, and ask which alarms you must still be able to hear.
- **A dry bag or waterproof pouch** for identification, money, medicine and your return equipment. Secure it to your body so a crowd or current cannot easily take it.
- **A compact light** and a way to communicate silently. A small display or written card may help, though you should learn local light signals rather than inventing ones that could be mistaken for warnings.
- **Drinking water and food for your first day**, plus any medication you need. Do not assume canal water, rainwater or water from a transparent conduit is potable.
- **Basic wound-care supplies.** In a place where surfaces remain damp and street water carries waste and grit, clean even small cuts promptly and keep them covered.

Pack light enough to climb stairs quickly. A large wheeled case will be awkward on wet, uneven paving and may become a hazard during a surge.

## Learn the water before exploring

On arrival, ask your host or guide to show you the flood signals, the meaning of warning horns, the nearest refuge stairs, and how to tell whether a gate release is imminent. A horn sounding almost constantly is not useful unless you know which pattern concerns your street. Find out whether warnings are also given by lights or displays, especially if you are wearing ear protection.

Make a habit of noticing **the nearest way up**. Before entering a low street or enclosed passage, look for stairs or a marked refuge and make sure the route is not blocked. Watch the locals, but do not rely solely on following a crowd: they may know shortcuts or have access to places you do not.

If a flood warning sounds, move promptly to the designated higher level. Do not wait to photograph the water, retrieve a dropped object or judge the current for yourself. Brown water can conceal steps, grates, debris and sudden changes in depth. Stay well back from canal edges and spillways, and never enter a flowing street simply because local vehicles or animals can withstand it.

Once the surge has passed, allow for another hazard: the pavement may be coated in fresh grit or slime, and water may continue falling from above for hours.

## Moving through the city

Distances here can defeat intuition. A city that can be crossed for days is not a place to navigate by the apparent skyline alone; mist, stacked walkways and repeated structures can make a nearby destination difficult to reach. Obtain a current local route map and check whether it accounts for closures, flood gates and construction. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return.

Use established transit where possible. Keep clear of loading areas, autonomous cranes, moving structural frames and the paths of vehicles shifting to higher tracks. A bridge that visibly adjusts under a crowd may be behaving normally, but that is not an invitation to inspect its joints or stand in a marked maintenance zone.

In crowded passages, move with purpose and keep belongings close. Expect conversation to be difficult. Arrange meeting points in advance, with an alternative in case a gate closes the route between you and your companions.

## Food, water and shelter

Choose accommodation on a higher level with an identified flood refuge, dependable drinking water and a way to receive local alerts. Ask about noise before booking: the city may remain loud through the night, and a room beside a spillway or machinery shaft could make sleep difficult. Earplugs may help, but make sure you can still receive an emergency alert.

Eat where local guidance confirms the food is safe for you. The absence of fields and trees in the visible city does not tell you what people eat or where it comes from, and unfamiliar ingredients should not be assumed edible. Likewise, do not touch the pale crusts on the walls or collect water from them; they are described as mineral deposits, not a resource for visitors.

## Animals and local life

Give canal swimmers and the animals feeding in street water space. Do not feed them, handle them or assume that creatures accustomed to traffic are safe to step on. Keep your hands away from nesting sites, exhaust galleries and bands of shells on retaining walls. The animals are part of the city’s working environment, but they may bite, carry irritants or occupy places where people should not linger.

Above all, take your cues from the people who maintain and inhabit the place. Shopkeepers raising their goods, pedestrians moving toward stairs and crews clearing sensors are not performing for visitors; they are keeping pace with conditions you are only beginning to understand. Give them room, ask before photographing people or workplaces, and follow instructions promptly.

The most comfortable visit will probably be a series of short excursions from a dry, well-located base. Go out prepared for a sudden climb, come back to dry your clothes and rest your ears, and let yourself get used to the rhythm: hard rain, the sound of gates, a street briefly given over to water, and the crowd returning when it is safe.
