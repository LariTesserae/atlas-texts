## Before arrival

Arrange to be met at a tower, rather than arriving somewhere in the open basin. The city may look empty, but it is inhabited, and the distances between buildings are large enough to turn a short-looking walk into an all-day exposure. Ask your hosts which entrances are meant for visitors, how they prefer to communicate, and whether there are periods when the ground or machinery becomes more active.

Confirm that the atmosphere is breathable and that its pressure and temperature are tolerable for a human. The description tells you little about either. Bring an independent air supply until those facts are established, and do not assume that the dampness, vegetation, or visible animals make the air safe for you.

Plan a short first visit with a reliable return point. Tell someone outside the basin where you expect to be and when you should return. Ordinary location instruments may not describe the bridges or the deeper spaces accurately, so agree with your hosts on a way to summon help that works in the city.

## Protect yourself from the sound

Treat the noise as your most immediate hazard. A roar loud enough to shake your chest may be capable of injuring your hearing, and hearing protection does not necessarily protect the rest of your body from strong vibration.

Bring well-fitted earplugs and industrial earmuffs that can be worn together. Have a spare set of each. Do not rely on active noise cancellation alone. If you can, measure the noise and vibration at your arrival point before committing to time outdoors; be prepared to leave if the exposure is beyond what your equipment can manage. Even inside a tower, cups tremble, so an interior is not automatically a quiet refuge.

Agree on visual signals with your companions before going out: *stop, danger, I need help, return,* and *I cannot continue* should all be unmistakable. Carry a light or high-contrast marker for attracting attention, and a writing surface that can be used in dust or poor light. Touch may be a local means of conversation, but ask how and when it is appropriate rather than reaching for someone unexpectedly.

Expect the sound to be tiring as well as loud. You may have difficulty sleeping, thinking, or noticing that something is wrong. Schedule short excursions and genuine recovery time; do not make your first crossing of the basin at the end of a long day.

## Traveling between towers

Go with a local guide if possible, and travel with a companion. Establish the actual distance and route before leaving, including sheltered places along the way. A tower visible through haze is not necessarily close, and a bridge may connect places in ways your eyes or instruments do not predict.

Wear sturdy boots with cut-resistant soles and ankle support, long trousers, gloves, and eye protection. The young mineral ridges are sharp enough to cut leather, so inspect footwear and exposed equipment during stops. A walking pole may help you test your footing, but do not insert it into cracks or seams. Carry a substantial first-aid kit for cuts, including supplies to clean and dress a wound; exposed bands of blue and copper are not evidence that the stone is safe to handle or that water in its cracks is safe to drink.

Bring more water than the planned journey should require, along with food, sun protection, and shelter suitable for both heat and the damp after nightfall. The basin’s water sources are small and dependable for its own ecology, not necessarily abundant or safe for you. Do not drink collected drops or tower-basin water without your hosts’ guidance and appropriate testing or treatment. Avoid gathering from plants or seams unless you have permission.

Move deliberately around the great canopies. Their ribs may extend across an acre despite a narrow trunk, and the spaces beneath them shelter animals. Do not lean equipment against them, tie shelter to them, or assume a branchlike rib can bear your weight. These plants gather scarce water and contribute to the landscape’s acoustics; damage to one may matter well beyond the patch you can see.

## Animals and quiet places

Give grazing herds room. A single translucent animal may be only the nearest member of a widely scattered group, so scan the horizon before crossing its path. Do not approach, feed, or attempt to touch any animal, however delicate or unafraid it appears.

Be especially cautious around pockets of relative quiet behind ridges. The relief from the roar may be tempting, but predators are said to wait where the sound is interrupted. Your hearing may not warn you of movement, and removing protection there could leave you exposed again as soon as you step out. Ask your guide which sheltered routes are safe; do not choose a quiet hollow as a rest stop simply because it feels comfortable.

As the stone cools, small creatures emerge in streams and gliders feed above them. Watch where you put your feet and avoid blocking their routes through the vegetation. A dusk journey may bring welcome relief from heat while making both terrain and animal movement harder to see.

## Towers, seams, and bridges

Treat the towers as occupied workplaces and homes, not convenient landmarks to enter at will. Windows that seldom brighten do not mean a building is empty. Wait to be invited, and ask where a visitor can safely rest, obtain water, and remove or check hearing protection.

Stay clear of seams in tower foundations. A space opening deeper than the foundation should allow is not something to investigate, photograph at close range, or test with a dropped object. The machinery below the basin is immense, partly inaccessible, and apparently integral to the city. If a seam opens near you, move back and follow your hosts’ directions.

Use a bridge only with permission and clear instructions. Its apparent length may tell you very little about the journey. Before entering, establish where it leads, whether you can return by the same route, and what to do if you become separated from your guide. Keep your companions in sight and do not stop to explore side openings.

The city’s inhabitants gather vibration, heat, and water. What looks like a railing, a bare foundation, or an unused basin may be part of that work. Ask before touching, collecting, or placing equipment.

## If rain comes

The brief rain would be worth seeing from a safe place: one ridge darkening while the next stays dry, leaves tipping toward the curtain, gliders dropping beneath the ribs. Arrange to watch it from a tower or another approved shelter rather than rushing out across the stone.

Afterward, surfaces may be slippery even where the surrounding ground has already dried. Do not shelter beneath a canopy without checking that it is safe and permitted; it may be full of clinging animals and continuing drips. Leave the filled basins and leaf water to their accustomed users unless invited to take some.

Finally, plan a decontamination and health check when you leave. Dust will travel on your boots, and neither you nor the basin’s life should be assumed immune to the other’s microbes. Clean your gear as directed by your hosts, document any cuts or unusual symptoms, and allow yourself quiet time afterward. The silence outside the city may feel startling after its continuous roar.
