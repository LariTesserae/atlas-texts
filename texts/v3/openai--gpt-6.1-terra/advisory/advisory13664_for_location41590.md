## Before you go

Treat this as an expedition into cold, pathless marshland with additional hazards that are not understood. The familiar dangers—wet clothing, concealed holes, exhaustion and losing your bearings—are enough to make it serious. The strange behavior of water, sound and reflections makes it harder to judge when those dangers are close.

Do not make your first visit alone. Arrange for a companion and, if possible, someone outside the region who can monitor your return point. Agree on a maximum visit time, a turnaround time and a rescue plan before entry. Because events and signals may not follow ordinary timing, these are precautions rather than guarantees: establish what to do if your watches, observations or communications disagree.

If your means of traveling between worlds can be placed deliberately, put the return point on the highest, most stable ground available, preferably near the worn-down hills rather than in a basin. Confirm that it remains usable after rain. Do not assume you can find it again by following water downhill.

Before committing to a long visit, check the air and weather with suitable instruments. The description gives no reason to assume the atmosphere is unbreathable, but it does not establish that it is safe either. Likewise, find out what you can about local organisms and chemistry; the moss’s ability to concentrate metals is a good reason not to drink untreated water.

## What to bring

Dress for prolonged cold rain rather than for a single shower:

- Waterproof outer layers, insulating layers that retain warmth when damp, gloves and a warm hat. Carry a complete dry change in a sealed bag.
- Waterproof boots with good grip and secure ankle support. Gaiters may help, but do not count on any boot to keep you dry if you step into a hidden hollow.
- A walking pole for testing ground ahead, plus a headlamp and spare power. Light will help under cloud and in groves, even during the day.
- A reliable way to mark and retrace a short route, such as conspicuous removable markers, alongside a map, compass and positioning device if they work there. Check whether each instrument remains consistent rather than trusting one alone.
- Drinking water brought from outside, high-energy food, first-aid supplies and an emergency shelter. A conventional filter may remove sediment and organisms but not necessarily dissolved metals or unfamiliar contaminants.
- A means of communication and a simple written record of observations. Keep watches and other instruments independent where possible, so a single failure does not leave you without a reference.

Carry a light rescue line if your party knows how to use one, but be cautious about tying yourselves together while moving: interlaced shrubs and unseen openings could snag it or drag a second person into trouble.

## Moving through the low country

Start with a short reconnaissance from the higher edge. The pale sheets in the basins may look solid, but the description explicitly warns that they can be water covered by vegetation. Moss may conceal water or peat; ribbed stone may be slippery; hollows can be deeper than their surroundings suggest. Test every questionable step and favor exposed, stable high ground without assuming that rock is dry or secure.

Move slowly enough to notice what the ground is doing. The sounds will be distracting: roaring water below, wind in reeds, stones knocking and rain seemingly arriving from every direction. Do not rely on hearing alone to locate a hazard. A sound may precede its source, and even an ordinary underground channel could be hard to place.

Keep the party together and within sight. Mist can erase the horizon, and the pale trunks and repeated ridges may offer few distinctive landmarks. Mark your route frequently enough to retrace it in poor visibility, but avoid damaging vegetation or disturbing the mineral-threaded moss. Photographing landmarks from both directions may help, though the unusual reflections mean images should be treated as another observation, not proof of a route.

Avoid crossing pools, entering narrow channels or pushing deep into the groves on an initial visit. A pool that reflects another part of the marsh is not a map, and there is no evidence that it is a safe passage. Similarly, the fishlike shapes beneath stone and water flowing briefly uphill show that connections occur; they do not tell you what would happen to a person who entered one.

## When conditions change

The effects are strongest after rain, and rain is frequent. You may not get a reliably “quiet” period. If a shower approaches, move toward known firm ground early rather than waiting for the strongest effects and trying to retreat through them.

Treat an apparent change of season, a reflection that does not match its surroundings, water flowing against the slope or a sound arriving before its cause as a reason to **stop and reassess**, not as an invitation to investigate at close range. Note the time, location and conditions if you can do so safely. If your route, return point or companion appears inconsistent, withdraw along the last route you can verify together. Do not split up to compare what each person sees.

The plants and mineral network appear to register disturbances across great distances, sometimes before their apparent causes. Whether this is awareness, an unfamiliar physical process or something else is unknown. There is no need to presume hostility or intelligence, but there is reason to tread lightly: avoid collecting specimens, cutting vegetation or disturbing water flow unless the visit specifically requires it and you have a plan for doing so safely.

Leave while you still have energy, daylight and a margin for confusion. Expect to spend much of the visit damp, cold and unable to see far, with constant noise making the landscape feel less predictable than it may look. The most valuable first trip would be a brief one: confirm that you can enter, stand safely, observe, retrace your steps and return before trying to understand the marsh from within it.
