## Before you go

Treat the city as an expedition into an unlit, unstable industrial environment, not as a place where you can simply arrive and look around. **Do not enter until you have measured the atmosphere, temperature, pressure, gravity and radiation**, and established a way home that does not depend on finding your arrival point again in the dark.

Send a probe first if you can. Have it check the air at several heights: warm mineral water may release gases that collect in enclosed walkways or low spaces. Map a small landing area and test whether the floor will support you. A surface that looks like stone may be a living animal, a thin crust of ice, or a flexible part of the building.

Plan a short first visit. The city is occupied, but nothing in the description guarantees that its occupants are human, that they breathe air, or that its spaces were made for you.

## What to bring

- **A self-contained breathing system and protective suit**, at least until the environment has been independently verified. Choose insulation and waterproofing for prolonged exposure to cold spray, with enough mobility to climb and recover from a fall.
- **A flotation aid and a recovery tether**, configured so they will not snag readily on cables or animals. The water is cold, crowded with life, and moving through submerged structures; swimming may be impossible even if the surface looks calm.
- **Several independent lights**, including a hands-free one and a low-output setting. Carry enough power to leave if a light fails. Avoid assuming that the gray flickers of the feeding veils provide useful illumination.
- **Serious hearing protection.** The noise is described as tremendous and may become dangerous inside resonant walkways. Use communications equipment tested while wearing it; ordinary speech or shouted warnings may be useless.
- **Navigation and return equipment** that does not rely on sound, a clear view, or a stable magnetic field. Mark your route with removable, non-damaging markers, and carry an independent means of locating your exit.
- A hard helmet, cut-resistant gloves, high-traction footwear, spare power, first aid and a means of calling or triggering extraction. Factory-sharp rock and ice can defeat ordinary outdoor clothing quickly.

Go with a trained partner and an external support team if possible. Agree on hand or touch signals before entering, including a signal to leave immediately. Do not tether partners together without considering the risk that one fall or snag could pull both into the water; a controlled safety line to a verified anchor may be better.

## Moving through the city

Stay on a surveyed route above the water. Test each walkway before committing your weight, and avoid lower passages that may have buckled as new stone rises. The ground’s motion is slow, but the damage it causes can already be present: a suspended room or passage may be under strain in ways you cannot see.

Do not clip safety lines to an unidentified cable or fastening point. Some of those immense cables hold the city against forces that are not apparent at the surface. Use anchors assessed by the survey team, and leave pipes, apertures and structural connections alone.

The enclosed walkways may be the most tempting routes, but also some of the loudest. Expect vibration through your boots and hands. Pause periodically to check one another for fatigue, disorientation and equipment damage; a steady roar makes it hard to notice a new danger by sound.

Look carefully before touching any surface. Animals cover the piers and cluster beneath walkways, and their feeding veils may be nearly invisible. Give them generous clearance rather than trying to push through them. Their slow movement does not make them inert, harmless or easy to extract yourself from. Do not take specimens, feed them, or put anything into the water.

Avoid the open channels on a first visit. The immense backs drifting there may be difficult to distinguish from stable platforms, and the current pressing through the lower works could draw a person or small craft into submerged streets. If a boat is eventually used, survey the route and current first and provide a recovery system that does not require someone to jump in after you.

## Contact and conduct

Assume the city may have inhabitants capable of noticing your arrival, even if you see no one. The buildings’ service apertures, heated passages and structural systems suggest functions you do not understand. Do not interpret a lack of visible people as permission to enter sealed rooms or operate anything.

If you encounter an occupant, keep your distance, make your movements deliberate and use whatever means of communication your team has prepared. Light and sound may have meanings or effects here that you do not know; avoid flashing powerful lamps or broadcasting noise as a first greeting.

## When to leave

Turn back immediately if your breathing supply, suit seal, light, communications or return navigation becomes unreliable; if a route shifts or floods; or if either traveler becomes disoriented. Set a conservative turnaround time before entry and keep to it, regardless of how much farther there is to see.

The strongest impression may be how little of the city you can visually grasp. You may feel a trembling floor, cold spray and a wall of sound while your lamp reveals only a few meters of black structure and overlapping bodies. Resist the urge to go farther just to make sense of it. On a first visit, reaching the edge of that experience and returning safely is an accomplishment.
