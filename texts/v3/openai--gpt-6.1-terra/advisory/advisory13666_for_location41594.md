Prepare to visit it as a deep, cold ocean floor, not as a place where a human can dive. The description doesn’t establish the depth or the water’s chemistry, so have those measured before choosing a vehicle. The weak light far overhead is not a reliable guide to how much pressure you’ll encounter.

### Before you go

- **Use a pressure-rated submersible or enclosed habitat.** It should be rated for the measured depth with a substantial safety margin, and able to hold a breathable atmosphere and comfortable temperature without relying on the surrounding water. An ordinary diving suit is not a substitute.
- **Survey remotely first.** Send down a probe to measure pressure, temperature, currents, visibility and water chemistry. Check for gases or minerals that could damage equipment. Map a small landing area as well as a route back out.
- **Plan an independent return.** Carry reserve power, oxygen and carbon-dioxide scrubbing capacity, redundant navigation and a way to recover the vehicle. Don’t assume there will be a surface ship, a rescue service or even a reachable shore in this world.
- **Keep the water and living material outside.** The microbes may be harmless to you, but neither direction of contamination is understood. Use sealed equipment, clean it before arrival, and contain anything brought back. Avoid sampling unless it is necessary and permitted.

### On the plain

Expect the lamps to reveal only a small piece of it at a time: dark fronds, grit, a seam in the pavement that might disappear beneath the growth a few metres away. Beyond the lights there may be nothing visible at all. Bring redundant, dimmable lighting and cameras; use only as much light as you need, especially around the sparse green growth.

Move slowly and stay clear of the bottom. Thrusters can raise silt that takes a long time to settle, obscuring the vehicle and smothering growth. A ridge that looks solid may be a slumped wall, and a flat-looking bed may conceal loose blocks or a shallow channel. If you must settle, choose a surveyed site and use broad supports rather than cutting or anchoring into the masonry.

Mark your route electronically and keep an independent inertial or acoustic navigation system. The old walls might seem like natural guides, but they vanish under deposits, branch, and reappear elsewhere. Don’t depend on following one home. Monitor the current continuously: it may be weak when you arrive and strengthen gradually while you are occupied.

Treat the blocks as both archaeology and habitat. The tool marks are part of the place, as are the films and mats growing over them. Don’t pull away vegetation for a better view or shift a loose stone merely to see what lies underneath. A camera on a gentle manipulator is a better first choice than a hand.

### What it may feel like

Inside the vehicle you will hear your own machinery more than the plain: fans, pumps, perhaps the occasional transmitted vibration. Outside, the described sounds—the rubbing fronds, a block settling—may be too faint to hear without instruments.

Allow time simply to watch. The place offers few quick events. A current may only begin to reveal itself in the slow lift of a blade; a wall may become recognizable only after you have followed its buried line for some distance. What looks at first like an empty, natural seabed is also an immense piece of hand-laid work, still supporting life and still, grain by grain, coming apart.
