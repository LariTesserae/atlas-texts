## Before you go

Treat this as a remote, cold expedition into a place where the map can change under your feet. The plateau is not overtly hostile, but its dangers accumulate quietly: damp clothing, an underestimated distance, a shelter that is not where you expected it, a doorway that no longer opens onto the room you left.

**Establish that an ordinary human can breathe there before committing to the journey.** The clouds below the rim suggest considerable elevation, but they do not establish the atmospheric pressure or oxygen level. Use a remote probe or protected reconnaissance to check pressure, oxygen, temperature, and any airborne contaminants. Even if the air is breathable, plan for gradual acclimatization if altitude is significant. Carry a pulse oximeter and know the signs of altitude illness; worsening headache, confusion, breathlessness at rest, or loss of coordination are reasons to descend or leave.

Confirm that your means of returning works *from the plateau itself*. Do not assume that a portal, beacon, or fixed coordinate will remain reachable merely because it was there when you arrived. If possible, arrange extraction that can locate you independently of the local roads and buildings, and test it before traveling far.

Go with at least one companion. Leave an itinerary and a firm check-in schedule with someone outside. Agree in advance on when a missed check-in triggers retrieval rather than an extended wait.

## Clothing, shelter, and supplies

Dress for prolonged exposure just above and below freezing, with wind and intermittent moisture:

- Wear a moisture-wicking base layer, insulating layers, and a windproof, waterproof shell. Avoid relying on cotton.
- Bring waterproof boots with good grip on smooth, wet stone, plus spare socks kept in a dry bag. Gaiters will help in frost, shallow water, and mossy ground.
- Carry warm gloves, a hat, and a face covering. Pack a complete dry insulating layer for emergencies.
- Bring a freestanding shelter and a sleeping system rated well below the temperatures forecast or measured. Do not count on finding an intact roadside shelter when you need one.

Pack all food for the planned visit plus a substantial reserve. The vegetation may be lush, but nothing described establishes that it is edible or safe. There are no animals to hunt, and the great distances between settlements make resupply uncertain.

Bring a stove and fuel sufficient for the whole trip. The hearths are useful as signs of shelter, not promises of usable firewood. Cutting the low forests for fuel would be both uncertain and damaging.

## Moving through the city

Plan short stages, with generous reserves of daylight, warmth, food, and water. A causeway is a useful surface to follow, but it is not a reliable promise that its destination remains at the expected distance—or remains on the route at all.

Start by exploring close to a secure base. Record routes in several ways: photographs, sketches, written descriptions of junctions, compass bearings, and the weather at the time. A compass and a conventional map may still be useful locally, but neither can resolve a road that has acquired more pavement between two markers. Electronic positioning may also fail or give a deceptively precise answer; test it rather than trusting it.

Markers and a continuous line can help you maintain contact and document what has changed, but do not assume they guarantee a way back. The description explicitly includes markers returning with extra stretches between them. Avoid separating from your companion, especially in low cloud or inside buildings.

The polished bowls may help you judge conditions along a road presently reachable. Look at them, photograph them, and compare their reflections with what you subsequently encounter. But a dark or white reflection tells you something about weather, not necessarily the destination, distance, safety, or persistence of the route. Cracked bowls may be misleading or inactive.

If the weather lowers while you are traveling, consider stopping in a safe, known place rather than trying to reach a settlement seen earlier. Allow enough margin that waiting out a front is an option.

## Houses and shelters

Approach abandoned structures cautiously. Check roofs, lintels, and walls from outside before entering; a long-standing stone building can still have an unstable section. Watch for slick floors, covered cisterns, and openings hidden by growth.

The interiors require an additional precaution. **Do not enter an unfamiliar room unless you can afford not to come straight back through the same doorway.** A passage may lead somewhere different the next morning, and a building can contain more space than its exterior suggests. If you investigate, go together, keep the expedition short, carry your full emergency kit, and do not leave essential supplies on the other side of a threshold.

For overnight shelter, a sound roadside hut may offer valuable protection from wind, but keep your tent ready. Be careful with stoves in enclosed spaces: ensure ventilation and avoid carbon monoxide exposure. Do not assume an old hearth draws safely.

## Water and living things

Water is present, but it is scattered and often hidden in cracks or held beneath vegetation. Carry enough to bridge long dry stretches. Covered jars, cisterns, condensation screens, and shallow basins are promising sources, but their age and contents are unknown. Inspect before collecting, and use a treatment system appropriate for microbes; boiling or ordinary filters will not necessarily remove dissolved chemical contaminants. If you cannot assess the source, use water you brought or a tested supply.

The moss beds and low forests are living systems, not convenient bedding or guaranteed safe ground. A thick moss surface may conceal water or an uneven hollow, and swollen growth can close over paths. Walk around beds where practical, probe uncertain footing with a trekking pole, and pitch your shelter on durable bare ground rather than on vegetation.

Spores drift in the air. Their effect on humans is unknown, so bring well-fitting particulate respirators and eye protection, particularly for handling dense growth, entering dusty buildings, or traveling in windy conditions. Avoid touching your face after contact with plants, and clean gear before returning through your portal. Do not eat, burn, or bring specimens home without a containment plan.

## What it may feel like

The plateau is likely to be harder to read than it first appears. The ground rises so gradually that there may be no dramatic moment of arrival; then a glance back reveals weather beneath you. Settlements look like outcrops. Sounds travel strangely through the quiet. A grove that seems close may be separated from you by an unexpectedly long reach of stone.

Give yourself time to stop and observe without making progress the measure of the day. Watch a condensation screen begin to wet, or a moss bed swell after rain. Notice where the builders placed shelters and how their gutters still feed the plants. Those details may tell you more about living here than a hurried attempt to reach the next district.

Leave the bowls, screens, tools, and stonework as you found them. They are both traces of the inhabitants and parts of a system that still works. Carry out your waste, including food scraps. And when evening light makes the houses difficult to distinguish from the rock, be glad you chose your sleeping place while you could still see it clearly.
