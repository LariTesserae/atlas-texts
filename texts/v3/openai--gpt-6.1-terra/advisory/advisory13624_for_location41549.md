## Before you go

Treat the town as a deep-water settlement until you know otherwise. The pale light overhead tells you neither how deep it is nor whether there is a reachable surface. Arrange a reliable return route and a safe base outside the settlement before you arrive; do not count on finding breathable air, familiar food, or a place to shelter locally.

Bring a pressure-rated submersible or a suit designed for the measured conditions, with independent breathing supply, thermal control, communications, lights, and a substantial reserve of power. An ordinary diving kit is not a safe default. Have someone remain at the base to monitor your return, and plan for any pressure transitions required by your equipment and physiology.

Survey the water first. Temperature may change sharply near the warm seams; mineral-rich seepage could be hot or chemically hazardous even where the surrounding water is cold. Check for suspended particles, currents, and whether your equipment’s intakes and filters can cope with them. Take everything you bring back out again, including waste: this is a young and delicate environment, not an empty patch of sea.

The air bubbles around some houses may be tempting. Do not enter one to breathe unless its pressure and atmosphere have been independently checked and its occupants have invited you in. A pocket of trapped gas is not necessarily breathable, and disturbing it could damage someone’s home.

## Moving through town

Approach slowly and from a direction the inhabitants can see. Use soft, directed lighting rather than flooding the terraces with a beam. Let your hosts indicate where to land, hover, or anchor; the ground that looks substantial may be fresh crust over a dark pocket of water.

Follow the marked routes. Keep your fins, equipment, and any trailing lines close to your body, and avoid letting thrusters or prop wash stir the silt. A brief burst of disturbed sediment could obscure a path for you and settle over fields that other people depend on. The ropes are useful guides, but they are not necessarily strong enough to take your weight; ask before holding on.

Give the seams, basins, and newly formed shelves a wide berth. A surface that has cooled enough for plants may still be fragile, and a path may have been moved since your last visit. Do not lean on walls, roof slabs, fences, or mineral pillars. The roofs are weighted for a reason, and the stonework may have taken seasons to fit.

If the water clouds, stop rather than trying to push through. Mark your position without attaching anything to the settlement, stay with your guide if you have one, and be prepared to wait or withdraw along your verified route. Bring navigation equipment that does not rely on clear visibility, but do not assume its signals will behave as expected in unfamiliar water.

When a grazer comes through, follow the residents’ example and give it space. Don’t approach it for a better look or try to drive it away from a field.

## Visiting people

Arrange contact through someone who can communicate with the residents, if possible. Their bodies, senses, language, and customs are not specified, so don’t assume that human gestures, spoken words, or gifts will be understood. Let them set the pace. The quiet, deliberate work of the town leaves room for you to wait rather than interrupt.

Ask before touching tools, crops, buildings, or the pale strands grown in flooded chambers. Avoid bringing unfamiliar organisms or leaving objects behind. If a gift is appropriate, agree on it in advance rather than offering food or materials whose effects you do not know.

Plan a short first visit. Even through a suit, the place may feel strange: light above without a visible sky, dark water below, and faint knocks carrying from chambers you cannot see. Spend some time simply watching how your hosts move—where they place a hand, which crust they test, when they pause for a grazer or a drifting veil of silt. They know a town that is still becoming ground.
