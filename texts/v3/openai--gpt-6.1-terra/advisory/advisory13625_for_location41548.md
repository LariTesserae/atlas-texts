## Before you go

Treat the plateau as a remote high-altitude wilderness, not a warm-weather walk. The sunlit rock may feel almost hot while the air is too thin for comfortable exertion, and the wind can turn a short crossing into a dangerous one.

If your means of travel allows it, survey the arrival point first. Measure oxygen and atmospheric pressure as well as temperature; an apparently clear, breathable sky tells you little about either. Check that the return route works from the plateau and that wind or moving debris cannot obstruct it. Ideally, place it in a sheltered location well back from a cliff, but not at the bottom of a hollow that could flood.

Bring another person if possible, and leave someone outside the destination with your route and a firm return deadline. Do not count on finding the builders of the shelters, usable water in their bowls, or an easy way down.

## What to bring

- **Water for the whole visit and a reserve**, plus a reliable way to disinfect additional water. A filter helps with sediment and microorganisms, but does not necessarily remove dissolved chemicals. The brown runoff and old bowls are not automatically safe to drink.
- **Eye protection, a dust mask or respirator, and hearing protection.** Grit may be driven sideways, and the sustained roar in a hollow could make both communication and awareness difficult.
- **Layered clothing:** sun protection, a windproof outer layer, and insulation for shade, wet weather and a potentially cold night. Warm afternoons do not establish the nighttime temperature.
- **Sturdy footwear and gloves** for abrasive stone and sharp edges. Keep straps, loose fabric and equipment secured so they cannot catch the wind or blow away.
- **Navigation and communication tools:** an offline map if one exists, compass, satellite-capable communication if compatible, and a prearranged check-in schedule. Do not rely on shouting.
- **A shelter rated for strong wind**, with anchors appropriate to rock, as well as emergency food, first aid and a light. Practice pitching it in difficult conditions before relying on it here.

If pressure is low, acclimatize where feasible and know the signs of altitude illness: worsening headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, confusion or breathlessness at rest. Descend or leave rather than trying to push through serious symptoms. Supplemental oxygen is not a substitute for an untested pressure environment.

## On the plateau

Start early. Morning offers the best opportunity to cross exposed pavement before the afternoon wind gathers strength. Move between sheltered seams and ridges, but remember that a cut in the cliffs or a gap between ridges may funnel the air into a stronger gust. Stay well back from cliff edges: they are weathered, and the wind is uneven enough to upset your balance without warning.

The basins will be tempting because they offer shelter and vegetation. They also make it easy to lose sight of a route across the surrounding ridges. Mark your position on a map or device, agree on hand signals, and stay close enough to your companion to see them through dust. In the roar, even a person a short distance away may not hear a warning.

Be cautious around the old slab shelters. Look from outside before entering: the stones may have shifted, and animals—or their builders—may use them. Do not dismantle one for material or take its tools. If you must shelter in one, choose a sound-looking structure outside a drainage channel, keep your exit clear, and be ready to move if rain starts. Your own tested shelter is a better primary plan.

A brief cloudburst can send water down seams that were dry minutes earlier. Avoid camping in the lowest part of a hollow, beneath loose cliffs, or beside an obvious runoff channel. If rain catches you in a basin, move out of the drainage path before water begins to collect. Watch your footing afterward: smooth stone and mud can both become treacherous.

The green bands may offer relief from the wind, but move through them gently. Avoid disturbing fern roots, nests or animals, and do not eat unfamiliar plants. A broad-winged glider overhead is a reason to watch quietly, not evidence that the open ground is safe.

## Toward evening

Plan to be back at your exit or securely sheltered before the wind shifts. The pulsing note may be air resonating through cavities or stone, but its cause is not established. Listen without pursuing it into an unfamiliar hollow or toward a cliff. If you feel vibration, notice loose stones moving, or find that the sound affects your balance or hearing, withdraw to a safer position.

The most useful preparation here is restraint: carry enough to be independent, give the wind the right of way, and leave before curiosity turns a manageable visit into an unplanned night on the plateau.
