Prepare as you would for a short walk into an unfamiliar desert at night, with the added precaution that your way home may be harder to find than the hollow itself. Nothing in the description suggests an immediate supernatural danger, but darkness, dryness, and disorientation are enough to warrant care.

**Make the return your first concern.** Before entering, establish exactly where and how you will leave. If you arrive by a portal or other unusual means, confirm that it will remain accessible and that you know how to activate it. Carry a light that can mark your return point without damaging the ground, and don’t wander so far that you lose it. A map, compass, or navigation device may help, but don’t assume any one of them will work in an unfamiliar world.

**Bring your own water.** The dry air will make itself felt quickly, and there is no visible water to rely on. Carry enough for the visit and a delay, rather than treating this as a place where you can replenish your supply. A little lip balm may make the air more comfortable.

Wear sturdy, closed footwear with good grip: the ground is nearly level, but grit can slide underfoot and fractured stone can be sharp. Long trousers and light layers will protect you from scrapes and sun if you stay until morning. Bring a warmer layer too; the stones are warm now, but desert temperatures can change substantially.

Carry a dependable flashlight or headlamp, with a backup and spare power. Use enough light to place your feet and find the low banks. You can dim or switch it off once you are standing securely, to let your eyes adjust and see the starlight on the stones. Don’t trust the apparent black spaces between rocks to be clear or level.

Check the wider weather and terrain before descending. A shallow hollow may collect sudden runoff even when rain falls some distance away. If storms are possible, stay out of it. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return, and bring a basic first-aid kit and a means of communication that you have reason to believe will work.

Once there, move slowly. Step around the dark patches of ground crust where you can; they may be living communities that take a long time to recover from a footprint. Give the shrubs room, and avoid turning stones or reaching into crevices. The absence of tracks and rustling doesn’t prove that nothing lives there.

You needn’t do much once you’ve found a safe place to stand or sit. Let the light rest for a while and listen: the small click of a stem or the slip of a grain may be all the sound you get. Keep your water and light within reach, remember where the way back is, and leave the hollow much as you found it.
