## Before you go

**Plan to observe remotely first.** The chamber is no larger than a cupped hand, and most of that volume is occupied by columns and ridges. An ordinary human cannot enter it intact at ordinary size. Even if your means of travel can shrink you, you may find no seam wide enough to accommodate your body without pressing against a living sprig or a wall.

Send in a miniature, contactless probe if one is available. Map the seams in three dimensions and watch for longer than seems necessary: the growth and shedding are described as extremely slow, so a structure that appears motionless may nevertheless be actively maintained. Determine whether there is an open route in and out, not merely a cavity large enough to appear habitable on a map.

Do not excavate a doorway through the crust. The white material is not a lining over solid rock; it is the whole structure, made by the growths themselves. Removing a little of it could alter the chamber’s support, its vapor supply, or the surfaces against which neighboring columns rest.

## Protect yourself—and the chamber

Treat the mineral vapor as unbreathable until it has been analyzed. Its dryness tells you nothing about whether it is toxic, irritating, corrosive, or even able to supply oxygen. Use a sealed suit with independent air and temperature control. Avoid equipment that vents moisture, sheds fibers, or leaves residues: the sprigs need no water, and an ordinary human’s breath, skin oils, and microbes could be a major intrusion at their scale.

Check that any size-changing method preserves the functioning of your suit, instruments, and air supply. Do not assume that a device which makes you small also makes the environment safe: pressure, heat transfer, adhesion, and the behavior of sound may all be different from what you expect.

The note deserves particular caution. Its loudness is described, but its frequency, means of transmission, and effect on a human body are unknown. Measure it from outside before exposing yourself. Bring hearing protection and vibration monitoring, while recognizing that earplugs alone may not protect against energy conducted through the suit or body. If the note proves hazardous, observe through a probe rather than entering.

## If entry is possible

Choose a route that allows you to remain clear of the surfaces. **There may be nowhere to stand.** A tiny hovering vehicle or a suspended instrument might be less damaging than boots, though even airflow, exhaust, static charge, or vibration could affect this close-packed place. Test any propulsion at a distance first.

Keep away from the glassy tips. They are the only sharp features described, and they belong to the organisms. Do not break off a sample or try to move a sprig aside. Its precisely maintained shape may be part of how it survives.

Use your own navigational markers, preferably virtual ones. The pale columns and multiplied light may make the seams difficult to distinguish, and there are few familiar landmarks. Keep a continuous route trace back to your entrance and leave well before your air or power reserves become limiting. If the only way forward requires widening a seam, turn back.

Expect the light to be cold and nearly shadowless. It may make the chamber feel open at first, until you try to move and discover how little empty space there is. The unbroken note could become difficult to ignore precisely because it never changes. Arrange a way to mute its transmission to your habitat or communications feed if possible, and let someone outside monitor your condition.

## What the visit is like

From a safe position, you would see white surfaces close on every side, their edges rounded where they meet. In the narrow gaps, the glassy sprigs rise with faint yellow at their tips. Nothing visibly feeds or builds, yet all the architecture around you has been laid down by them.

The most respectful visit may be a brief one: look, listen if it is safe, record the chamber without adding much to it, and withdraw. Its apparent stillness is not evidence that nothing is happening. It is a place whose living processes work on a timescale too slow for a visitor to see.
