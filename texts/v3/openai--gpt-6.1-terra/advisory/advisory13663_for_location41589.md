Prepare for a city that is inhabited and carefully maintained, but spread over distances that may feel much larger on foot than they look from a high walkway. The gardens offer shade and shelter; the plazas between them may leave you exposed for a long time.

### Before you arrive

- **Arrange a reliable way home.** Record where you entered, how to activate your return route, and what to do if it is unavailable. Do not assume a local crossing leads back to your point of arrival.
- **Learn the local conventions if you can.** In particular, ask how pedestrians identify usable crossings, whether they require permission or payment, and what the blue lamps indicate beyond closure in rain.
- **Bring supplies for a delay:** drinking water, food, any necessary medicines, and a way to call for help if you know it will work there. Water is visibly collected throughout the city, but cistern water should not be assumed accessible or safe for you to drink.
- **Wear comfortable, sturdy shoes** and light clothing suitable for warm, humid air. Add a compact rain layer. The rain is brief, but it can interrupt travel.
- **Carry a light**, preferably one with adjustable brightness, and a charged backup. The city has illumination, but it is weak in places and may depend on your approach. A map or navigation aid that works without a network would also be useful.

If possible, have a local contact meet you. The streets are expansive, some avenues are unnamed, and the crossings make ordinary distance an unreliable guide.

### Moving through the city

Start with a short walk under the covered routes and learn how the place feels before attempting a long crossing of the plain. Note distinctive towers, garden layouts, and fixed landmarks. A route you can describe as “three streets from the covered walk” may be more useful than one based on an unnamed avenue.

Treat the pedestrian crossings with care. From outside, one may look like a simple gap, yet take you several streets away. Watch how residents use one, confirm its destination if possible, and check the weather before entering. **Do not enter during rain or while it is closed.** Allow time to wait under cover if the weather changes, and expect the missed-step sensation on emerging.

The vehicles are quiet and may leave their visible tracks. Look around before stepping onto or near a transit route rather than relying on sound.

### Living with the place

Stay on paths and out of planted beds unless invited in. The gardens are lush, but their spacing appears deliberate; the open paving is not an invitation to take a shortcut through the greenery. Do not disturb the beds that caretakers leave alone, especially where something larger is moving beneath the leaves. Watch the lizards and birds from a distance.

Likewise, leave the condensation threads, channels, cisterns, and building surfaces alone. They are part of the city’s working infrastructure, even when they look delicate or decorative. Keep clear of foundations where new buildings are forming: the machines doing the work may be too small for you to see.

Give yourself time to adjust to the light. The blurred sun and pewter sky may make it difficult to judge the hour, while the greenish evening glow and moving patches of light could make familiar surroundings feel subtly unfamiliar. Carry a watch, and arrange a meeting place that does not depend on a particular wall staying lit.

Finally, plan to be still for a while. Under a covered walk after rain, you can wait as the residents do, listening to the collectors and watching for the blue lamps to return. In a city this quiet and widely spaced, patience is part of the equipment.
