## Preparing for the plateau

Prepare for a place that is cool, wet and difficult to read at a distance, but not necessarily hostile. The city’s apparent sameness may be more disorienting than its size: courts and terraces repeat over miles, and a familiar route can disappear into cloud or rain. Much of what makes the city habitable is also easy for a visitor to mistake for unused space.

Before departure, learn what you can about the plateau’s **actual elevation, season and local customs**. “High enough for clouds” does not by itself tell you whether altitude sickness is a concern. If the elevation is substantial, ascend gradually where possible, allow time to acclimatize, and know the symptoms of altitude illness. A worsening headache with nausea, unusual exhaustion or breathlessness at rest is a reason to seek help and descend, not push onward.

### What to bring

Pack for prolonged damp rather than a single downpour:

- Waterproof outer layers, warm insulating clothes that retain some warmth when damp, and a dry change sealed in a bag.
- Comfortable, water-resistant footwear with good grip on wet stone, plus spare socks. Avoid relying on completely smooth soles.
- A compact shelter or bivy and an insulating sleeping mat, in case you cannot reach lodging. Ask where you may use them; an empty court may be part of the drainage system.
- A headlamp and spare batteries for covered passages or an unexpectedly late return. Don’t assume there will be artificial lighting.
- Drinking water, a reliable means of treating more, food for delays, basic medical supplies and any essential medication.
- A compass, a waterproof notebook and a way to make a local map. If you carry electronic navigation or communication devices, treat them as useful only after you have established that they work here.
- Whatever provisions your means of multiverse travel requires for a return, kept dry and separate from everyday supplies.

Travel light enough to negotiate narrow passages and wet steps without brushing against walls or needing to set your pack in a channel.

### Finding your way

Arrange a local guide or host if you can. Someone who knows the district can distinguish an ordinary court from one deliberately kept clear for floodwater, and a usable passage from one best avoided after rain.

On arrival, note your entry point carefully and establish a return route before exploring. Sketch turns, changes in level, distinctive walls and major channels; count junctions where useful. Do not depend solely on a view from a ridge, since the roofs and watercourses may look very different once you are among them or the cloud comes down.

Give yourself generous time. A route that is dry in the morning may have water moving through it later, including water emerging from foundations after the rain has stopped. If visibility becomes poor, stay in a safe, known place rather than wandering through unfamiliar courts.

### Moving through the city

Walk slowly on stone, especially at channel edges and where moss grows. Shallow water can conceal uneven paving or a sudden step. Be cautious around submerged openings, covered drains and passages beneath old rooms; don’t enter them just because they appear passable. After heavy rain, avoid low enclosed spaces and follow local advice about which routes have cleared.

**Leave slabs and channels as you find them.** A stone that looks loose may hold part of a wall or control a watercourse. Likewise, don’t clear silt, dam a trickle or divert water without being asked and shown how. The inhabitants manage flows across whole districts, and a small alteration can send water somewhere it was not meant to go.

Clean, empty courts with watermarks deserve particular care. They may be intended to receive overflow. Don’t camp in one or leave belongings there, even under a clear sky.

### Staying well and being a good guest

The water is abundant, but its safety for drinking is unknown. Ask where residents obtain drinking water and treat it unless you have a reliable reason not to. Keep food protected from damp, change wet layers promptly, and check your feet for blisters and skin softened by prolonged moisture. Even a cool, calm day can become uncomfortable when you stop moving.

Bring a modest gift or trade goods if appropriate, but learn local expectations first. Ask before entering courtyards, photographing people or buildings, or taking shelter in an apparently abandoned room. In a city built and maintained communally, boundaries and obligations may not be obvious to a stranger.

Keep some distance from animals and do not disturb birds or drains to get a better look. More generally, take the inhabitants’ quiet routines as a guide: watch where people walk, where they leave space for water, and what they do after rain.

The best preparation is to arrive with time to observe. Spend your first morning near your host or guide, learning how the courts connect and how the water moves. Once you can recognize those patterns at walking pace, the vast, repeating city will be easier to visit without getting lost—or inadvertently interfering with the work that keeps it dry enough to live in.
