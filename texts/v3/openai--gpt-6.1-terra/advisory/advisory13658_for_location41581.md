## Before you go

Treat the plateau as an active geological site, not a piece of solid ground with occasional eruptions. The surface is making and breaking itself. A place that seems quiet for an hour may swell, split, or drop without giving you a useful route away. The black edge is not merely a cliff to avoid: the interior can open into fissures too.

**Do not arrive on foot without first surveying it remotely, and do not make the plateau your base.** If your means of travel allows it, keep a vehicle or extraction point somewhere demonstrably stable and visit the surface only briefly. If you cannot find stable ground or guarantee rapid extraction, observe from a distance.

### Find out what the air and ground are doing

Send instruments before people. A remote probe should measure oxygen, pressure, temperature and air movement, as well as sulfur compounds, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide and other potentially hazardous gases. The faint sulfur smell is a warning, not a measure of safety; some dangerous gases are difficult to smell, and concentrations can collect in hollows or change suddenly near a fissure.

Also check gravity, radiation and whether the darkness corresponds to a long night, an absent sun, or something else. “High” describes the plateau’s position above the void; it does not establish that the air is thin in the way it might be on a terrestrial mountain.

Use thermal imaging and repeated overhead scans to map hot ground, fresh lava, cracks and changes in elevation. Watch candidate landing areas for longer than the apparent quiet intervals. The mound that takes an hour to swell is useful evidence, but the sudden lifting and falling of a stone line is the more important warning: **slow movement does not rule out abrupt failure**. A drone or hovering probe is preferable to relying on a sensor placed on the surface, since the ground beneath a sensor may move with it.

Keep well away from the edge and from visible fissures. There is no reason to assume that the crust near either will support a person, a vehicle, or an anchor.

### Equipment and party

Bring a small team with geological expertise, a dedicated observer, and someone responsible for the extraction system. Nobody should wander out of sight or sound of the others. Agree in advance on a short visit, a route, and conditions that end it immediately: new cracking, rapid ground deformation, a tremor, a gas alarm, or any uncertainty about extraction. Retreat at the first indication, rather than waiting to identify the cause.

Until the atmosphere is thoroughly characterized, use a **sealed suit with an independent air supply**, not merely a dust mask or filter. It needs suitable thermal protection, puncture-resistant gloves and boots, and soles that can tolerate hot, sharp stone. Even then, ordinary protective gear is not a license to approach molten rock or stand on a thin crust over a hot void. Check the suit and air supply before departure, and carry enough reserve for a delayed return.

Bring:

- Independent lights, including a wide-area lamp that reveals cracks and changes in level rather than only a narrow beam ahead.
- A helmet light and a backup light on a separate power source; darkness should not become an emergency because one battery fails.
- Thermal imaging, gas alarms, communications and a way to track one another’s positions.
- Eye protection and a plan for dust entering equipment during a gust.
- First-aid supplies for burns, cuts and inhalation exposure, with medical support available after extraction.

A rope may help the party stay together, but don’t assume it can arrest a fall: a rock anchor is only as reliable as the slab holding it. A hovering platform or an extraction system that does not depend on local stone would be more reassuring, provided it has been tested in the air and conditions here.

### Moving on the plateau

Move slowly enough to inspect each step, but do not linger to admire something from an exposed position. The glassy surface and sharp lips can cut through equipment; the dull, soft-looking folds may be recently formed and still hot underneath. Test the temperature and apparent thickness of a route remotely where possible. Neither a black skin nor a surface that has stopped moving proves that it is cool or strong.

Avoid hollows, where dust and gases may collect, and keep clear of places where air is being drawn into or expelled from cracks. Don’t follow the sound of a hiss toward its source. Keep a generous distance from any swelling mound, fresh break or tongue of lava, including after it has blackened.

The quiet may be misleading. You may hear a brittle clink or dust sliding before you can see what caused it, but the description offers no guarantee that a major break will announce itself. Have the observer watch the ground and horizon rather than becoming absorbed in the feature being studied. Rising ridges shifting the stars behind them could help reveal movement, but instrument readings and a clear retreat rule are better than relying on eyesight alone.

### The crusts and the rings

The pale branching crusts might be mineral deposits, living things, or something unfamiliar. Photograph them in place with a scale and record their temperature and surroundings. Avoid touching, breathing in or sampling them on an initial visit. If a later expedition needs samples, prepare containment and decontamination procedures first—for the travelers’ safety and to avoid introducing terrestrial organisms.

Give the stone rings the same restraint. They suggest deliberate construction, but not who built them, when, or why. Some survive amid movement; others have been torn apart. Photograph and map them without rearranging stones or using the rings as shelters, markers or anchors. Their apparent persistence does not establish that the ground beneath them is safe. Watch from a distance for any sign that their makers, or anyone else, still use the site.

Plan decontamination and an equipment inspection on return. Dust may be abrasive, chemically irritating, or biologically significant; sharp glass may have damaged seals without an obvious failure during the visit.

The experience is likely to be stranger than a conventional volcanic landscape: darkness overhead and at the brink, warmth rising through your boots, long silences interrupted by evidence that the land has changed shape. Prepare to leave with your questions unanswered. On this plateau, a successful first visit may be little more than a few careful measurements and a safe return.
