Prepare for the country as you would for a remote cave with an unfamiliar atmosphere, not as a garden you can walk into. Its light and apparent abundance may make it feel hospitable, but neither tells you whether the air is breathable, the ground will bear you, or the water is safe.

**Keep a reliable way home.** Arrange a return point you can find without depending on landmarks inside the valley. If possible, leave a teammate and communications equipment on the other side, and send a sensor or remotely operated probe through first. Set a conservative turnaround time before entering. The unchanging light gives you no indication of passing hours, and the landscape’s stillness is not proof that time behaves normally—or abnormally.

**Check the air before removing respiratory protection.** Measure oxygen, carbon dioxide, temperature and humidity; use whatever screening is available for toxic gases and airborne particles. A supplied-air system is preferable for an initial visit, because an ordinary filter cannot make oxygen or remove every unknown hazard. Carry more air than the planned trip requires, and leave promptly if your instruments or equipment fail.

Wear lightweight protective clothing, gloves, sturdy boots and eye protection. The heat is steady, and close-fitting equipment may make it harder to shed body heat, so keep the first excursion short and monitor yourself and your companions for overheating. Bring drinking water and food from home. The clear beads and cups may be beautiful, but their cleanliness to the eye tells you nothing about their chemistry or biology.

**Move gently and leave a trail you can follow.** The floor looks soft and newly formed; test it before committing your weight, especially near bulges, root banks and mineral nodules. Don’t assume a trunk or arch will support you. The crowded avenues can conceal holes and force you into tight passages, so avoid squeezing into places where you cannot turn around with your equipment on. A line back to the entrance, laid without cutting or crushing growth, may be more dependable than a map of similar-looking arches.

The roofed chambers are tempting places to rest, but they are not necessarily shelters. Check their footing, air and entrances before entering, and don’t stay where a shift in the foliage could close your route out. There is no visible darkness in which to sleep comfortably; bring an eye mask if an overnight stay is ever judged safe, though a first visit should not be one.

Treat the whole place as living and easily disturbed. Don’t collect the pale animals, drain the cups, cut a passage through foliage or use flame. Photograph and record rather than take samples unless there is a compelling reason and a containment plan. Clean equipment before arrival, and bag clothing and gear on return to avoid carrying organisms in either direction.

Finally, make a few simple observations at intervals: photograph the same water bead, note the position of a curled leaf, listen for the hum, and compare your watch with a clock outside if communication permits. The valley may be changing too slowly or subtly to see. Give it time without assuming you understand it—and leave while you still know the way back.
