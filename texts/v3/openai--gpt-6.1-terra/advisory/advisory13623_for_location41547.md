Prepare for this as an exposed, wet highland **with unreliable distances**, not as a meadow. The apparent flatness offers little shelter, and the ground beneath your feet is alive, waterlogged, and sometimes moving. Your first visit should be short, in daylight, with a safe return point close enough that you can reach it without depending on the horizon looking the same on the way back.

### Before you go

Find out, if you can, whether the air is breathable and how high the plateau is. “Above the clouds” does not by itself tell you the altitude. Bring an atmospheric monitor and, if altitude is significant, allow time to acclimatize. Do not assume the clear air means mild weather: wind, wet clothing, sleet, and exhaustion can bring on hypothermia quickly.

Arrange for someone outside the basin to know when you are due back. If your method of travel permits it, place your exit on stable ground beyond the living mat, and verify that it works before walking away. Leave yourself a generous margin: distance, visibility, and travel time may all change.

Go with at least one companion. Agree beforehand that either of you can call off the visit for any reason, especially worsening weather, confusion about the route, or a change in the ground beneath you.

### Clothing and equipment

Wear warm layers that retain insulation when damp, a waterproof shell that seals well against driven rain, and a hood you can turn your head inside. Protect your eyes from sleet and windblown fragments. Waterproof gloves matter both for warmth and for handling equipment.

Choose waterproof boots with good ankle support and soles that grip wet vegetation. Avoid overly heavy boots that would be difficult to pull free. Carry dry socks and a spare insulating layer in a sealed bag. A walking pole can help you test the surface and keep your balance, but do not trust a firm probe result to mean that the ground will remain firm.

Bring:

- A map or sketch, compass, and positioning device, while treating all readings as potentially unreliable during the rhythmic episodes.
- A watch and a simple written log of direction, elapsed time, weather, and landmarks.
- A whistle, lights, and a communication device with a check-in schedule.
- A compact emergency shelter rated for strong wind, plus warmth supplies and first aid.
- Water and food of your own. Do not drink basin water or eat the vegetation or animals without knowing they are safe.
- A light rescue line and a cutting tool, packed where you can reach them. Do not trail a line continuously through the mat or reeds where it could snag.

### Moving through the basin

Keep to a modest pace and watch each step. The surface ranges from springy to hard, but either may conceal shallow water, loose layers, burrows, or an opening that has begun to knit shut. The mist rising from your footprints is an interesting sign of disturbance, not a reliable marker: wind and cloud may erase it almost immediately.

Avoid torn edges, green scrolls peeling back from the mat, and areas where the surface ripples with hidden movement. Give reed stands considerable room, particularly when their chambers are beating or the wind is rising. An uprooted stand can move across the basin while still active.

Stay close enough to your companion to maintain contact in cloud, but do not crowd onto the same suspect patch of ground. If someone sinks or becomes caught, avoid rushing in after them. Work from firmer ground, use a pole or line if possible, and free them promptly and gently before the living edges close further. Treat even a brief immersion as a serious cold exposure.

The animals appear accustomed to the landscape, but not necessarily to you. Give grazers and birds a wide berth; never approach a burrow opening or place yourself between an animal and its escape route. The trembling roots may be living tissue: leave dropped vegetation alone and minimize damage to the mat. Clean your boots and equipment before leaving so that you do not carry material between worlds.

### When the basin starts “running”

The mechanical throb is a warning to become conservative, not a sign that help or machinery is nearby. If the reed stands begin taking up the rhythm together, stop and take stock. Do not chase a landmark that seems to recede, navigate by an animal’s apparent size, or use a reflection to judge distance. The description gives no reason to assume that ordinary surveying, GPS, or even a measured rope will behave predictably during an episode.

If you still know your route and conditions permit, begin returning early, using several references together: your log, direction of travel, nearby physical features, and communication with your companion. If those references conflict, do not press farther into the basin to resolve the puzzle. Find the safest nearby ground you can, stay together, and wait for the rhythm or visibility to improve if that is safer than moving.

### When to leave

Leave well before evening and at the first sign of a serious storm. A basin that was merely wet can flood as wind drives water across it; a reed stand that was a landmark can be carried elsewhere. Do not plan to camp on the mat unless you have no alternative. Shelter stakes may not hold, the ground can flood or heal around objects, and the low ridges offer uncertain protection.

The most unsettling part may be how ordinary the animals make it look. They keep grazing while distances change and ice forms on their backs. Let that be a reason to watch them with wonder, not a reason to follow their example. You are a visitor without their knowledge of the place.
