Prepare for a small, working settlement where **staying dry and keeping your footing will take more attention than covering distance**. Everything is close, but a few steps off a walkway may put you into saturated ground or dense growth, and beyond the basin the mountainside drops into cloud.

### Before you arrive

If you can choose your arrival point, aim for an established path or the communal yard, not the sedge or an apparently grassy slope. Arrange a return point you can recognize in rain and cloud. Bring a way to call for help that does not depend on seeing someone: vegetation and the noise of water could make both sight and voice unreliable over a surprisingly short distance.

Find out the elevation if possible. If the basin is high enough for altitude illness to be a concern, allow time to acclimatize, and don’t treat a headache, nausea or unusual breathlessness as merely the effects of a wet climb. Have a plan to descend if symptoms worsen.

### What to wear and carry

- **Waterproof layers and a warm insulating layer.** Expect cool rain, wet vegetation and few opportunities to dry clothing quickly. Avoid relying on cotton for warmth.
- **Boots with good grip and ankle support**, plus dry socks in a sealed bag. Moss, wet timber and smooth stream stones can all be slippery. Bring a walking stick or trekking pole for balance and for testing uncertain ground.
- **A waterproof pack liner or dry bags**, rather than trusting a pack cover alone. Protect your sleeping gear, spare clothes, documents and any equipment needed for your return.
- **Midge protection:** long sleeves, repellent suitable for your skin, and perhaps a head net. A small first-aid kit should include blister care and supplies for cleaning cuts.
- **A headlamp**, even for a short visit. Cloud and the thick vegetation may make it dim earlier than expected.
- **Safe drinking water and a treatment method.** The spring is central to the settlement, but clear mountain water is not necessarily safe to drink, especially around people and animals. Ask before drawing from it.

Keep one complete set of clothes dry for sleeping or an emergency. If you stay several days, tend to your feet: change wet socks when you can, dry your skin thoroughly, and deal with rubbing before it becomes a wound.

### Moving through the basin

Follow the raised paths and watch how residents use them. Step carefully on short timbers and stones; a plank may be slick, loose or partly submerged. Don’t jump down onto a patch that merely looks firm, and don’t use a fence for support without checking—it may be living willow, a barrier for animals, or both.

After heavy rain, ask which routes are safe. The description suggests frequent small slumps and blocked drains, not necessarily a constant major slide, but the steep slopes deserve respect. If a bank is newly cracked, moving, or shedding material, give it room and tell someone. Avoid exploring below the basin or above it in cloud without a local guide.

The paths and drains are part of the settlement’s daily work. Don’t leave packs across them, dam a trickle for convenience, or pull stacked poles and reeds out from under an eave. What looks like spare material may be needed for the next repair.

### Among the residents and animals

Arrive visibly and announce yourself from the path. Ask where visitors may stand, sleep, wash and relieve themselves, and whether there is a customary way to approach the spring or hearth. Voices carry poorly over running water, so get a person’s attention before speaking rather than shouting from a distance.

Keep gates as you find them, give the goats room, and don’t feed or handle the ducks and chickens unless invited. Shake out bedding and boots before use; in such a densely inhabited place, small creatures are likely to shelter in any convenient dry space. A breathable insect net for sleeping could make the visit much more comfortable.

Because you are crossing worlds, be especially careful about **biological contamination in both directions**. Clean footwear and equipment before arrival and before departure; don’t bring live plants, seeds, soil, insects or animals unless their transfer is known to be safe and permitted. Avoid tasting unfamiliar plants or untreated food, and follow any local health or quarantine rules. Carry needed medicines in their original containers and don’t assume local remedies will be compatible with them.

Finally, leave time simply to settle into the rhythm of the place. Pick a secure spot near the yard, keep your rain layer close, and listen: people calling over water, ducks in the sedge, birds moving through the alders. The basin is busy and welcoming-looking, but it is also continuously being maintained. A considerate visitor notices that work and asks before adding to it.
