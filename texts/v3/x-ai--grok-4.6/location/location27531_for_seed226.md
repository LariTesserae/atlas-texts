The col is still deciding whether it is a pass or a basin. You feel that first in the knees: the ground is not finished tilting. A high saddle of pale stone, barely a generation old as stone goes, has been heaved up between two older ridges and left wet. Water has not yet learned the long habits of carving. It simply appears—beading on raw faces, welling from seams that have not closed, condensing on every underside as if the air itself were being wrung.

What stands here is a town the size of a large cloister, or a small city that forgot to spread. Walls, courts, stepped streets, a ring of cistern-houses, three unfinished towers, a market floor that is mostly empty flags. All of it fitted to the new rock rather than imposed on it. The masonry is primitive and exact: split blocks, wooden cramps, lime that never quite dries, gutters adzed from single trunks and pegged under every eave. No iron to speak of. The few metal things you see are soft copper bells and a handful of worn bronze chisels laid out as if their owners had only stepped away.

It is lush the way a wound is lush. Ferns have already taken the joints. Mosses drink the weep of the walls and hang in slow green beards. Where a court holds still water for half a day, horsetails come up in ranks. There are shrubs with leaves like wet leather, and one kind of tree—never tall—that roots in any ledge and drops aerial roots into the next channel down, as if stitching the town to itself. Flowers are few and pale. Everything green is intent on the water, not on display.

The emptiness is structural. Streets are wider than they need to be. Halls are built to cathedral scale and then left with a single bench, a single hearth, a single hook in a wall. You can walk a long time between a person and another person. The builders seem to have raised a city for a population that has not arrived, or that already left, or that is arriving so slowly the stone will be old before the rooms are full. The density of bodies is almost a void; the density of stone and leaf is not.

Sound is the thing that occupies the vacant volume. Because the rock is young it rings. Because the town is all hard faces, hanging wood, and water, every drop is doubled. There is a constant loudness that is not quite roar and not quite rain: a braided noise of seeps, of gutters finding their pitch, of a spring under the market floor speaking through a grate, of wind (there is always a little wind at this elevation) dragging across wet moss and empty doorways. When someone somewhere uses a mallet, the report lives a long time. When no one does, the water is enough. You raise your voice without meaning to. Birds, on the rare occasions they cross, sound like thrown stones.

Light is ordinary mountain light, which here means it arrives clean and then immediately complicates itself. Cloud sits on the col more days than not, but the cloud is thin and moving; sun comes through in cold sheets. Surfaces shine. The new stone is almost white where it has not yet taken stain, so the courts glare softly even under grey. Interiors are dim only by contrast, and they smell of lime, leaf-rot, and the metallic sweetness of unweathered mineral.

Temperature is a constant cool that lives in the hands. Breath shows. Water never quite becomes ice and never quite becomes comfortable. The lushness is a cool lushness: cloud-forest logic at an altitude that has only just been invented. You dress as if for a long spring that will not commit.

The geology is embryonic in the strict sense. Bedding planes are still arguing. In one cutting at the west edge you can see the rock changing its mind—a lens of something darker shouldering up, not yet a stratum, just an intention. People here quarry what they live on, slowly. A day’s work might free six blocks and then stop because a seam began to weep too hard, or because the face rang wrong. Scaffolding of lashed poles leans against half-born walls and is itself already mossed. The town and the mountain are under construction in the same tense. No one seems hurried. The dynamic of the place is patience: mineral patience, vegetal patience, the patience of a settlement that builds as if centuries were a local material.

Fauna is a rumor with occasional proof. A pale salamander in a cistern. Something hoofed that comes at dusk to the outermost spring and leaves prints already filling with water. Insects concentrated around the few still pools, then gone. The loudness is not theirs.

What is unusual is quiet and structural rather than spectacular. The water does not always run downhill. In two or three places a runnel climbs a handspan along a hairline crack, as if the stone were still exhaling and the film of water were riding the breath. No one comments on this. Gutters have been designed to catch it either way. Also: certain courtyards hold echoes longer than their size should allow, as if the emptiness were a second architecture. Stand in the center of the main court after a gust and you will hear the gust again, smaller, from a direction that is not the wind’s.

Rules, insofar as a place has them:

The wet is not a season; it is the condition of the stone. To live here is to accept that everything you own will be damp at the edges, that fire is for tea and for lime, not for drying the world.

Work is hand work and it is slow. A wall goes up the way a ridge goes up. You do not finish so much as pause in a useful shape.

Empty space is not waste. The wide courts, the hollow towers, the long uncovered stairs are how the sound is organized, how the plants are given room, how the new land is given time to settle without being crowded by use.

Do not drink from the first spring you see. The youngest water tastes of the inside of the mountain and will cramp you. The cistern-houses mark which basins have stood a day. That is a civic fact, almost the only posted one.

At night the town does not go dark so much as it goes inward. A few fat lamps. The water-noise does not lessen; it only loses its sparkle and becomes a single thick presence. You sleep to it the way you might sleep to a nearby sea, except the sea is above you and inside the walls and under the floor, and it is still being born.

If you stay long enough you notice the slowest motion of all: the col continuing to rise, a rate too small for a day and just large enough for a life. Door sills that were flush last year take a thin wedge now. A drain that ran true now hesitates. The builders already know this. They leave gaps. They use wood where stone would crack. They plant the stitching-trees on purpose. The city is not fighting the mountain’s unfinishedness. It is the mountain’s unfinishedness, given rooms, given gutters, given a name that the wind and the water keep pronouncing too loudly for anyone to hear it the same way twice.
