The hollow sits no higher than a man could walk in a day from any surrounding flat, a shallow, almost circular basin perhaps two hundred paces across and never more than a few body-lengths deep at its lowest point. Its slopes are so gradual, so long-settled, that the eye accepts them as ordinary ground until it notices there is no horizon line, only a gentle rise of earth on every side that cuts the sky into a low, even lid. The light that reaches the floor is always the same: a flat, uncolored gray, as if the sun itself has been thinned and spread until it no longer casts a shadow worth naming. Nothing here is bright. Nothing here is dark. The air holds a mild, unmoving warmth that never quite becomes heat and never quite cools.

The soil is a fine, pale clay that has been pressed and re-pressed for so long it has the texture of old leather. It stays faintly damp even when no rain has fallen. Moisture lives in it the way memory lives in stone: not as standing water but as a constant, slight give underfoot, a coolness that rises through the soles if one stands still. In three or four shallow pans, water collects in thin, perfectly still sheets no deeper than a finger. These pans never grow, never shrink, never ripple. They simply are, their surfaces filmed with the same gray light. Condensation beads on the undersides of the few larger stones and drops at long, regular intervals that have no beginning and no end. The sound, when it comes, is a single, soft tap that the silence immediately reclaims.

Vegetation is little more than punctuation. Thin, gray-green stems rise here and there, widely spaced, never forming a thicket or even a clump large enough to cast a true shade. Their leaves are narrow and tough, adapted to wait. A few low, woody shrubs cling to the inner slopes where the clay is slightly richer; they look as if they have always been this size. Lichen, the color of dried bone, maps the north-facing faces of half-buried rocks in slow, unchanging continents. Nothing flowers. Nothing dies in any way that would leave a mark. The plants simply persist at the same height, the same density, season after season that do not arrive.

There are no animals. No insects stitch the air. No birds cross the low circle of sky. The quiet is therefore complete except for those infrequent taps of water and, once in a great while, the almost inaudible sigh of clay settling a fraction further into itself. Even that sound feels ancient, already accounted for.

Scattered across the basin floor are the only signs that anything was ever made. A low, broken course of fitted stones, no higher than the knee, describes a partial rectangle near the center; the blocks are so rounded by weather and so nearly the same color as the clay that they read first as natural outcrop and only later, if one looks long, as work. A few smaller stones nearby still show the ghost of a right angle. Elsewhere a single upright slab leans at the same tired angle it has held for longer than anyone could remember, its face pitted into a map of nothing. These remnants do not dominate. They are simply there, as settled as the slopes, as unchanging as the light. Whatever hands placed them have been gone so long that the basin has forgotten the difference between built and unbuilt.

The whole place exists in a state of finished weathering. Edges have been sanded by centuries of the same rare rains, the same dew, the same infinitesimal shifts of clay. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is growing. The hollow has reached the shape it will keep. Time here is not stopped so much as completed; the equilibrium is so thorough that even the idea of change feels like a rumor from some other, more restless country. One can walk the entire circumference in a few minutes and find only the same damp clay, the same sparse stems, the same dim warmth, the same patient stones. Then one can walk it again. The place does not object. It does not notice. It simply continues being exactly itself.
