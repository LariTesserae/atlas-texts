The Lowworks sit in a shallow dish of land that never quite drains. Not a valley—too small, too flat-bottomed—more a settlement-sized dent, as if a thumb had pressed the world here and left the print. Cool air pools in it. The light that reaches the flagstones is ordinary daylight thinned by a standing haze, the kind that makes iron look darker than it is and brick the color of old tea. Moisture does not fall so much as appear: a film on every horizontal, beads along rivets, a permanent damp in the mortar lines. Puddles occupy the same shallow wounds in the paving they have occupied for longer than anyone bothers to measure. They neither spread nor dry. The water in them is neither clean nor foul; it is simply the Lowworks’ water, faintly metallic, faintly animal.

There are no plants. Not a weed in a crack, not moss on the north faces, not even the greasy algae you would expect on wet industrial stone. The chemistry is wrong for them, or the compact was made without them, or they were edited out so long ago the ground no longer remembers how to host a root. What grows is simpler and less willing to be called growth: a mineral smear in sheltered corners, a pale bloom of rust-eating film on the undersides of tanks, a yeast-stink in the condensation traps. The world here is brick, slag, wet iron, packed clinker, and animal.

The built thing is a town that never thickened into a city and never thinned into ruin. A ring of low worksheds, a pump house with a cracked but enduring campanile of brick, one long mill whose clerestory windows are opaque with old grease and breath, a cooling tower like a truncated kiln, a short terrace of workers’ houses whose doors hang open on dark, dry rooms. Between these, space. Not picturesque emptiness—industrial emptiness, yards of setts and iron lids, a canal cut that holds a finger of motionless water, a loading apron big enough for wagons that do not come. You can stand in the middle of the main yard and feel the buildings as separate presences, each with its own weather of drip and echo. Paths exist because feet and hooves and whatever else uses this place have worn them into a final shape. The wear does not continue. The grooves are finished.

The machinery is on. It has always been on. Dynamos in the mill basement hold a single note; belt-lines slap with a rhythm that has forgotten how to hurry or lag; somewhere behind the pump house a triple-expansion engine talks to itself in a language of well-oiled concussions. Nothing is being made that leaves. Or everything that is made is consumed on site, instantly, by the other population. Electrified lamps burn in the mill even by day, a normal yellow, their filaments long ago settled into the exact brightness this circuit will always provide. Chimneys breathe a thin, cool exhaust that smells of wet coal and old grease. The smoke does not stain further. The brick is as stained as it will ever be.

Then the animals.

They are the substance of the place. They are what the empty yards are for. At first the eye tries to parse them as one species and fails, then succeeds in the worse way: they are one species, but the species does not hold a single outline. The common body is low, slick, the color of condensation on zinc—hairless or so finely furred the wet makes it a pelt of water. Some are the size of cats, many the size of calves, a few heavy as ponies, all the same animal at different stops of a scale that does not imply age. They have too many joints in the neck. Their mouths open farther than the skull should allow. They do not hunt in any way that changes the pattern of the yard. They occupy.

They occupy the rooftops in a living coping. They pack the open doorways of the terrace until the dark rooms behind are a throat of bodies. They stand in the canal cut up to their chests, drinking or not drinking, motionless except for a shiver that passes through the whole rank as one shiver. They cover the loading apron so completely that the flagstones are a rumor, and yet there are still gaps—always the same gaps—wide enough to walk if you walk the route the gaps permit. The density is overwhelming and also exact. You could count them, if counting were a thing this place rewarded, and tomorrow the count would be the same, and the positions would be the same to within the width of a flank.

They are loud. Not in bursts. In a standing weather of sound. A wet clicking from the small ones, a bronchial roar from the large, a thousand throats finding the same two or three notes the engines already hold, so that machine and animal have long since become a single chord with a flesh part and a metal part. When a relief-valve speaks, a ridge of backs answers. When the dynamo’s pitch lists by a fraction it never actually lists, the clicking leans with it, imaginary correction, eternal agreement. The noise has no climax and no rest. It is the temperature of the air made audible. After a time it stops being noise and becomes the medium you move through, like the damp.

They eat the film on the iron. They eat condensation. They eat one another with a courtesy so complete it leaves no absence: as a flank opens and is entered, as a throat works, the surrounding bodies close by the width of what was taken, and somewhere else—always out of the direct line of sight, as if the yard were too polite to show the ledger—an equivalent mass is already standing where a gap had been prepared for it. No births are seen. No deaths remain. The food web is a circle drawn in grease pencil and then engraved. The cool keeps them from fever. The damp keeps their hides from cracking. The factories keep the note they sing to.

There are people, or there are the gestures of people. A figure in oilskins crosses the yard on the permitted path, twice a day by the light, and does something with a valve that does not need doing. The animals part around the figure the way water parts around a post that has always been there. Another figure sits in the pump-house window and does not look out, or looks out and has been looking out since the window was last cleaned, which was never. Their faces are not secretive; they are simply finished. If they were to speak, they would have to speak under the chord, and nothing in the Lowworks suggests speech is still a technology in use.

The strangeness is not an event. It is the grain of the place. Shadows fall correctly and remain correct. A drop gathers on a lintel, falls, is replaced by a drop of the same size. An animal on the mill ridge lifts its too-long neck, tastes the cool, lowers the neck to the identical angle. You begin to suspect that what you took for life is a maintenance of shape. Or that life here is only the name for a balance so old it has worn through the bottom of time and come out the other side as a kind of mineral. The fauna are overwhelming the way a cliff is overwhelming: not because they advance, but because there is so much of them, and the amount does not know how to be less.

Walk the boundary and you will find it without markers. The last sett. The last smear of rust-film. A thinning of the chord into ordinary wind, which here is only a rumor from above the dish. Beyond, the land is merely low and damp and empty of this particular settlement of engines and throats. Turn back and the Lowworks are as they were, as they are, as they remain: a small industrial weather, cool, wet, loudly inhabited, finished. The puddles hold the sky. The sky, in the puddles, does not move.
