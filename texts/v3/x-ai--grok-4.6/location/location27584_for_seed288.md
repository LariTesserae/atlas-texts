The air here has no memory of moisture. It is cool in the way of stone that has not seen the sun in a time too long to measure, a dry coolness that settles into the lungs and stays, tasting of powdered calcite and the faint metallic tang of salts that have nowhere left to go. There is no drip, no seep, no hidden spring. Whatever water once moved through this place finished its work ages ago and departed, leaving the chemistry of the world to proceed without it.

You stand in a basin that was once, perhaps, a sea or a river-cut valley, now roofed by its own collapsed and recemented ceiling. The space opens far beyond any single glance. Streets—or the memory of streets—run out in shallow, braided depressions, their edges softened into the surrounding flats until it is hard to say where pavement ends and the primordial dust begins. The dust itself is the final argument of erosion: rock, mortar, bone, and leaf reduced to the same pale flour, lying in drifts so ancient they have their own stratification. Nothing sharp remains. Corners have been argued down to curves. Towers, if they were ever towers, have slumped into broad, rounded mounds whose sides are scored with the last, almost decorative traces of what might have been windows or stair-wells. The layout is still urban. You can walk a central avenue the width of a dry river and feel the radial logic of side-passages, courtyards, and the low remains of what must have been a market or a gathering floor. It is a city that has finished becoming geology and now simply is.

Light arrives as a dim, sourceless dusk, the color of old brass left too long in a drawer. It does not come from openings to any surface you can name; it seems to sweat from the rock itself, a weak phosphorescence in the mineral veins, enough to see by and no more. Shadows are long suggestions rather than facts. In this light the vegetation declares itself slowly. It is not jungle and it is not desert scrub. It is something that has learned to be lush on almost nothing. Thick-leaved rosettes crowd the leeward sides of the ruined mounds, their surfaces matte and waxy, storing whatever trace of vapor the air still grudgingly holds. Between them rise stands of silver-grey shrubs whose branches interlace into low, architectural canopies, each plant spaced as if by old habit, never crowding. In the deeper hollows a carpet of minute, drought-hardened succulents and crustose lichens forms a living mosaic, grey-green and surprisingly dense, drinking from the stone’s last internal damp. The greenery does not hide the city; it annotates it, growing in the same patient geometries the masons once used.

The animals are everywhere and they are loud. That is the first and last fact of the place. Herds of pale, long-limbed browsers move across the open flats in loose constellations, their hooves whispering in the dust, but their voices do not whisper. They call in overlapping, resonant bellows that the cavern walls take up and return until the sound becomes a physical pressure, a continuous roar that has no beginning and no end. Smaller things—clicking, chitinous, many-legged—occupy every crevice and add their own layered stridulation, a high, mechanical chorus that never pauses. Winged shapes, leathery and nearly colorless, lift from the higher remnants in sudden flocks whose wingbeats and cries fold into the greater noise until you cannot separate one source from another. The fauna is abundant yet the density remains sparse: great empty intervals of dust and stone lie between the moving groups, so that each cluster of life feels like an island in a dry archipelago. They do not hurry. A herd may take half a day to cross a plaza. A roosting flock rearranges itself with the deliberation of weather. Everything here has already decided its pace and will not be hurried.

The city does not change. Or rather, it changes at the speed of the dust that settles another grain, the lichen that advances another millimeter, the slow slump of a wall that has been slumping since before any living memory. There are no seasons that you can feel, only longer and shorter intervals of the same dim cool. The sound itself is the one constant dynamism, a deafening stasis, the acoustic equivalent of the erosion that has already done all it can do. Whatever built these streets did so without metal, without machines, without anything that would now be recognized as a tool. The stones were stacked, perhaps by hands, perhaps by something that used only its own body and time, and then time continued without them. The current inhabitants live among the results as if the architecture had always been a natural landform, using the hollows for shelter, the high places for lookout, the open dust for grazing. They have no need to remember builders. The place has settled into its final, almost-flat form and intends to remain there.

Walk far enough and the character of the districts shifts by degrees. One quarter is little more than a field of low, eroded plinths, each crowned with the same hardy rosettes. Another retains the ghost of a grid, its “blocks” now shallow basins where the succulent carpet grows thickest and the clicking things are loudest. A third is dominated by a single massive, slumped massif that might once have been a citadel or a temple; animals stream around its base in slow rivers of pale hide, their calls bouncing off its flanks until the whole structure seems to vibrate. There is no center that matters more than any other. The scale is simply the scale of a city that has outlasted the idea of cities.

Above, the ceiling is a distant, irregular sky of the same worn stone, stained here and there with the same faint glow. It does not drip. It does not threaten. It has already fallen, long ago, and been incorporated. The world down here is finished with catastrophe. It has arrived at the quiet, roaring end of its processes and found that end surprisingly full of life—life that asks for almost no water, almost no light, almost no change, and in return fills every cubic measure of air with sound. You can stand in one of the great open intervals, the dust motionless around your feet, the cool dryness on your skin, and feel the place continue exactly as it has continued, patient and deafening, for longer than the surface has words for.
