The first thing that is not the light is the noise.

It arrives as a single fact, the way gravity does. There is no attack, no swell, no weather-front of sound moving toward you. You are simply inside it, as you are inside the thinness of the air. It has the character of a river heard from inside the river, except that there is almost no water here. It is wind and stone in a conversation so old it has forgotten how to stop. The pitch does not wander. The volume does not dip. If you shout, the shout is eaten at once and does not come back even as an echo. After a while the body stops offering shouts.

The place is a high saddle between two older masses of mountain that no longer have the look of ambition. They are not peaks in the hungry sense. They have been peaks. What remains is a worn, pale upland, a country of backs rather than summits, lifted so far that the sky around it has gone a hard, inked blue and the sun is a white coin you cannot look at. Elevation here is not a drama of cliffs and hanging ice so much as a fact of distance from everything else. The valleys that belong to ordinary weather are a long way down and cannot be seen. Clouds, when they occur at all, occur below. They look like someone else’s problem.

Light is the second fact, and it is nearly total. The air is too thin to soften it. Every surface that can throw glare does so: the bleached plates of schist, the polished insides of the wind-scoops, the mica that still winks in the older beds, the rare standing puddle no larger than a palm. Shadows exist, but they are cut as if with a blade and contain a cold that feels personal. There is no gold in this light and no mercy. It is the light of a high, dry noon that does not appear to be going anywhere. Time-of-day is a rumor from lower country.

The saddle itself is broad enough to be a region. You could walk half a day and still be on it, still in the same unmodulated roar, still under the same vertical sun. It is not a single terrace. It is a system of shallow troughs and residual knolls, of long gravel fans and sudden drop-offs into slot-gullies that lead nowhere useful. The stone is the color of old bone and kiln-ash, banded in places with darker, greener seams that the wind has etched proud. Erosion here is not an event. It is the settled condition of the rock. Everything that was going to fall has fallen. Everything that was going to be fluted has been fluted. The wind has been at this work long enough to have finished it, and now it only maintains. The forms are complete. They look as if they have always been this shape, which is nearly true.

In the lee of the larger knolls the rock is riddled with pipes and organ-slots, some no wider than a finger, some you could crawl into if the thought of doing so in this noise did not feel like a kind of drowning. The wind is threaded through all of them at once. That is the body of the sound: not a gale in the open, exactly, though the open is not still, but a vast instrument that cannot be turned off. The note is not musical. It is too large for music. It is a pressure in the sinuses and a grit between the teeth. After an hour you stop hearing it as hearing and start receiving it as weather in the bones.

Water exists as a kind of stubborn exception. It does not run in any quantity that would explain anything. There are dark stains on the north faces of stones where breath condenses at night, if night can be said to happen, and those stains never quite dry. There are shallow tarns in the lowest scoops of the troughs, each one a cold lens no deeper than a boot, filmed with grit, trembling continuously in the wind so that they never hold a proper reflection. A single thread of melt, if it is melt, crawls the length of the longest trough and disappears into a swallow-hole as if embarrassed. The air itself is faintly damp in the way of a room where someone once boiled water. You feel it more in the lungs than on the skin. Lichens take what they can. Nothing else is invited.

Vegetation is a rumor confirmed only if you kneel. Crusts of map-lichen in rust and sulfur and a gray that matches the stone exactly. A few cushion-plants, hard as coral, nested in the lee of particular rocks as if assigned there. In one trough, a wiry grass no taller than a knuckle, evenly spaced, as though something had planted it and then lost interest. There are no shrubs. There is nothing that casts a shadow of its own. The green, such as it is, is a mineral green. It does not suggest growth so much as persistence.

And yet the place is crowded with animals.

This is the disagreement the saddle has with itself. The eye, taking in acre after acre of pale, unoccupied stone, reports emptiness. The spaces between things are the main thing. A standing figure, a boulder, a tarn: each sits in a waste of glare. Density is a joke. You could fire an arrow any direction and be nearly certain of hitting nothing that cares.

The ear reports otherwise, once you have subtracted the wind, if subtraction is possible. And the closer looking reports otherwise. The flutes are inhabited. Pale birds the color of the schist occupy the larger pipes in numbers that do not make sense until you have watched one wall for a long time and understood that the speckles are not mineral. They do not so much fly as get thrown by the wind from slot to slot, and their voices are not separate from the instrument; they are a brightness inside it, a secondary scream that has been going for so long it has the same unchanging quality as the stone-note. On the open fans, herds of something low and long-necked — not quite goat, not quite antelope, coats the exact dirty white of weathered feldspar — pick their way from lichen-patch to lichen-patch without ever bunching. They do not startle as a group. They startle as a landscape, a sudden rearrangement of pale shapes that was already almost invisible. Between the stones, in every crack that holds a thread of damp, there are insects. Not a cloud. Not a plague. An even dust of them, always, a glittering that might be mica until it moves. They stridulate at a pitch that lives just under the wind, a second deafness. Small mammals, all ear and tendon, occupy the boulder-fields so thoroughly that the fields read, after a while, as a single organism that occasionally shows an eye.

Nothing here is rare. Nothing here is concentrated. Life has been poured into the place the way grit has been poured into it, evenly, without drama, and then the volume of the world has been turned up around it until the life becomes another texture of emptiness. You can walk for an hour and see only stone and then realize you have been watched the entire time by a thousand things that match the stone. The abundance is a kind of camouflage that failed upward into totality.

There are no buildings. There is almost no evidence that building has ever been considered. In one place, on the rim of a trough that commands nothing in particular, three stones have been set on one another with a care that is not the wind’s. The stack is old enough to have taken the same polish as the bedrock. It does not mark a grave or a border that can still be read. It is the kind of gesture a person makes when they have primitive tools and a need to put a hand on the world, and then they move on, and the world does not notice. Elsewhere, in a sheltered slot, a scatter of flakes — stone on stone, the simple arithmetic of a cutting edge — and a darkened smear that might have been a fire the size of a cup. That is the entire archaeology. The saddle has no use for habitations. Nothing here is still enough, in the human sense, to be lived in, and everything here is still enough, in the other sense, that living-in would be a kind of vandalism the place would simply ignore.

The temperature is the temperature of high shade, even in the sun. Cool. Not the theatrical cold of snow-lines and dying fingers. A coat-cool. A morning-in-autumn cool that does not change when you wait. Sweat, if you make any, is a mistake the wind corrects immediately. The damp in the air never quite becomes rain. Rain, one suspects, is something that happens to other elevations, a rumor like evening.

What is unusual is not any single object. The birds are only slightly the wrong shape. The light is only slightly too vertical. The sound is only slightly more total than wind-and-stone should be able to manage. The unusual thing is the refusal. This country does not develop. It does not have weather in the narrative sense. It does not have a morning that is different from its afternoon. The wind does not drop at dusk because dusk is not admitted. The herds do not migrate because there is nowhere in the relevant world that is different from here. The tarns do not freeze or flood. The three stones remain three. Equilibrium was reached a long time ago, longer than the fluting, and the saddle has been keeping it the way a held note is kept — not by effort, but by the absence of any reason to stop. If there are seasons, they occur as rumors in the lichen. If there is night, it is a thin idea that cannot get purchase on all this brightness.

You can walk the troughs until your legs understand the scale — not a peak, not a room, a small nation of pale rock and roar — and nothing will have changed except your own water and the fact of your having been added, briefly, to a place that does not add. The animals will have been around you the whole time, screaming and glittering and matching the ground. The wind will have been the same wind. When you leave, if you leave, the first thing you will notice in the lower country is not the thickness of the air. It is the scandal of quiet, and the way shadows have edges that move.
