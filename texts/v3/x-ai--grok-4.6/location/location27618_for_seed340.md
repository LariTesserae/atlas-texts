The first thing is not the dark. There is no dark to speak of. Light here is the color of old paper held to a window, a dry evenness that does not come from a sun you can point to. It wells from the membrane overhead — a living ceiling stretched taut across a basin you cannot see the far rim of — and from the plants themselves, which keep a low internal glow in their veins, as if they had swallowed lamps and forgotten them. The light does not flicker. It does not cast a clean shadow. Everything is equally disclosed and slightly wrong.

You are below the world. That much is certain. The membrane is not sky; it is skin. Press a palm to a pillar and you feel, faintly, the weight of whatever country lies above — salt, perhaps, or baked clay, or a desert so finished it no longer bothers with dunes. No water has come down in living memory. There are no seeps, no condensation, no morning films on stone. The air rasps. It tastes of pollen that was never wet, of resin dust, of the fine silica the plants exhale instead of breath.

And yet the basin is a forest. Not a forest as a surface-dweller would mean it. The plants are the ground. They are the walls. They are the unfinished hills. What would be geology elsewhere is here a slow riot of cellulose, lignin, silica-lace, and something darker, almost mineral, that the older trunks secrete and then grow back through, as if trying to become stone and failing, and trying again. Nothing here drinks. The chemistry is closed and private. Each stem keeps its solvents inside — oils, terpenes, a thick pale sap that never beads on a cut but only gums and then hardens into a scab you could strike a spark from. They take their substance from the raw rock that is still arriving.

That is the other first thing: the place is not done. The floor buckles. New ribs of matter shoulder up through the weave, pale and unbarked, already putting out leaves that look like knives left too long in a fire. Elsewhere a whole stand will slump, not from rot (there is no rot; there is no moisture for rot) but from some internal decision, a collapse of purpose, and the fauna will be on it before the dust settles.

The fauna.

They are not visitors. They are the other half of the volume. You cannot find a cubic arm’s-length that does not contain a body, a wing, a jointed limb, a throat. They live in the plants the way grit lives in a wound. They are the sound.

It is not possible to speak of the sound as if it were weather. It is the weather. It is the architecture. It does not pause. A continuous, structured roar — stridulation stacked on stridulation, the rasp of chitin on silica-leaf, the pistol-crack of seed-cases that do not open so much as detonate, the low tectonic groan of a trunk deciding to become a cliff, the higher scream of things with membranes for lungs, or for ears, or for both. There are frequencies you feel in your teeth. There are frequencies that make the dry air seem to curdle. After a time you stop trying to sort them. The basin has a single voice and it is using all of it.

Move and you move through bodies. Not a stampede exactly — stampede implies a direction, a fear, an end. This is occupancy. Beetles the size of fists with shells like fired clay; moths that never drank nectar because there is none, whose mouths are files; things like birds if birds were assembled from dry husks and given too many joints; pale runners that go on four limbs and then six and then, when the ground heaves, simply roll, becoming wheels of fur and tooth. They do not hunt in any way you can comfortably watch. They graze the plants and they graze one another and they graze the new rock as it arrives, and the distinction is not one they honor. When a fresh ridge tears up through the litter, it is already covered — larvae in the cracks, flyers stitching the air above it, something long and many-legged tasting the unweathered mineral with a tongue like a strip of bark.

The heat is constant and dry. Not a killing heat. A kiln left overnight, a greenhouse with the glass painted out. Sweat would be a kindness and does not occur; whatever lives here has made other arrangements. The plants radiate. Stand too long in a thicket of the taller ones — they are not trees so much as load-bearing ideas, fluted, hollow, ringing when struck — and the warmth coming off them is like standing near a sleeping animal the size of a house.

Scale asserts itself slowly because there is so much in the way. Then a gap opens, or a collapse opens one, and you see. The basin goes on. Ridges of living matter, some still pale and embryonic, some dark and plated with their own shed bark like shingles. Valleys that are only the spaces between growths. In the middle distance a formation like a butte, except it is breathing, except its strata are rings of old leaf. Farther, haze: not moisture-haze, spore-haze, the air thickened with the reproductive dust of everything that has ever tried to be here. The far wall of the cavern, if there is one, is a rumor the light does not confirm. You could walk a day and still be in the same grammar of place.

Scattered through this, as if left by a people who were never sure they were people, are the marks.

A ring of upright stones in a clearing that will not stay a clearing; the stones themselves furred already with a dry moss that is not moss. A platform woven of the local stems, lashed with fiber so tough it sings when the wind (there is wind: the membrane overhead flexes, the heat rises, the whole basin breathes in lurches) finds it. Tools: scrapers of a dense black glass that might be plant and might be the memory of a volcanic event the cavern had before it was a cavern. A figure, once, carved into a living pillar, the face already half-eaten by new growth, the mouth open in a shape that might be singing. No metals to speak of. Nothing that required a forge. Whoever comes here, or came, works with what can be broken, split, knotted, and endured.

They cannot stay long in any one shape of the land. The land will not permit it. A shelter built against a trunk will, within a cycle you could count on your hands, be inside the trunk, or under it, or hanging from a limb that did not exist at dusk. The turbulence is not weather riding over a stable world. The world is the turbulence. Hills are born and spent. Whole districts of the forest go over together, a dry avalanche of timber and bodies, and then the exposed raw is greened — no: browned, silvered, rusted — in a frenzy of germination that does not wait for rain because it has never heard of rain. Seeds here do not swell. They ignite. A crack, a twist, a pale spear, and then leaves like hands opening in a hurry.

The rules, as they can be guessed:

Nothing is wet, and nothing is dead for long, and those two facts are the same fact. What falls is packed into the substrate by the fauna and by the plants’ own restless roots, which do not seek water but seek purchase, and heat, and the chemical rumor of unclaimed mineral. The membrane above is not a roof so much as a parent. It sheds a continuous fine grit — not rain: a dry sifting, like flour, like ash — that the uppermost canopy catches and metabolizes. Some say the membrane is itself a plant, the first plant, and the basin is only its interior weather. Some say the opposite: that the forest grew up and knitted a sky because the true sky was unendurable, or gone.

Light holds steady. There is no day to wait for and no night to fear, only this parchment illumination in which everything is visible and nothing is restful. Eyes here are not built for dark. Many of the fauna have too many of them, or surfaces that are all eye, or no eyes and a hearing so fine the deafening roar is, for them, a map, a language, a food.

You can, if you stand in one of the rarer still pockets — the lee of a butte that has not yet decided to walk, the hollow of a trunk wider than a house — hear underneath the roar a second sound, lower, almost kind. The sound of things becoming. Fiber stacking. Crystal stitching itself into a vein. A thousand thousand jaws at work on the idea of a landscape. It is not a peaceful sound. It is only quieter than the rest, the way a furnace is quieter than an explosion.

Keep walking and the place does not repeat so much as reiterate. New colors in the leaves: a sudden stand of white, bladeless, like paper lanterns that learned to root. A field of low growth that is all flower and no stem, the flowers hard as nuts, clacking together in the wind until the clacking becomes another layer of the noise, a percussion section the basin did not know it needed. A chasm where the floor failed, not deep, not wet, its walls already bristling with the next forest, hanging sideways, hanging upside-down, obeying a gravity that is only a suggestion.

Somewhere, always, something is being made by hand. A new lashing on a platform. A flake struck from the black glass. A mark cut into bark that will be gone by the time the cutter returns, if the cutter returns, if returning is a concept this topography will continue to support. The built world here is a rumor the living world is too busy to finish hearing.

There is no horizon that is not made of bodies. There is no silence in which to test whether you still have a self. The basin does not care. It is warm, and dry, and deafening, and it is not finished, and it will not be finished, and that is the whole of the law.
