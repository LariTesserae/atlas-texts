The depression is newer than the idea of rain.

You come to it as a lip of brick—unweathered, knife-edged, the color of a loaf taken too soon from the oven—and then you step down, because the whole country sits lower than the waste around it, a shallow tray pressed into a plateau that has no name and almost no weather. The drop is only the height of a tall person. After that you are inside a made thing, and there is no further outside.

Every surface has been touched. The floor is a mosaic of fired sherds set on edge, a field of small vertical blades that click faintly under a heel and then forget they were asked to make a sound. Between the sherds, no soil: only a packed dust of ochre and crushed shell and the palest ash, tamped with the same palms that built the walls. Those walls are adobe and kiln-brick and stacked river-stone that never knew a river, only the memory of one, carried here as a shape. The corners are still sharp. Thumbprints remain in the plaster like a second, quieter script. Limewash holds the day’s heat and gives it back at night as a slow animal breath. Nothing has slumped. Nothing has been rounded by wind or pilgrimage. The place has the posture of something finished last month and then told, firmly, to stay.

It is not large. From the north wall you can see the south if the hanging screens are drawn. A hamlet would cover it; a cathedral nave would recognize its proportions and then be confused by the lack of an altar. What it has instead is density. Walkways the width of two shoulders. Niches cut into niches. Terraces no deeper than a coffin, stacked until the sky is a ragged rectangle. Reed screens, bead-curtains of unripe seedpods, lattices of thornwood lashed with gut. You cannot raise an arm without brushing something that was placed there on purpose.

The lushness is dry.

It occupies the terraces the way a rumor occupies a room—fully, without permission, without drinking. There are plants that still remember chlorophyll: dwarf olives trained along wires of twisted sinew, their leaves the color of tarnished coin, stomata sealed as if in prayer. There are banks of air-plants and ghost-tillandsia, pale and powdery, drinking the warmth off the brick. There are shrubs of saltbush and old-man saltwort packed so close their silver backs make a single pelt. But most of what reads as garden is not, strictly, botanical. Vines of braided hair and dyed bast have put out leaves of scraped parchment and thin-beaten native copper, and the leaves have begun, inconveniently, to photosynthesize heat. Felt trees—yes—felt, needled and shaped over armatures of bone, have taken root in the mortar and now drop a lint that smells faintly of sheep and incense. Papery inflorescences the color of old blood open along the colonnades; they do not wilt because wilting is a water-habit and water is a story told about other places. The whole mass is olive, rust, bone-white, the green of oxidized copper, the green of dried tea. It is lush the way a storeroom of tobacco is lush, the way a coral head is lush, the way a packed choir is lush. You wade.

No basins hold liquid. There are basins—dozens—carved from single blocks, lined with bitumen, stoppered with clay seals stamped by a thumb. They hold sand, or light, or a dark grit that might once have been grapes. One jar, no larger than a child, sits in a recess behind a screen of thorns and is never opened. People (there are people, or there were) speak of it the way other cultures speak of wells. Moisture exists only as a private fact inside living tissues. The air itself is a kiln’s afterthought: warm, faintly mineral, tasting of lime and crushed coriander and the dust of insects.

The insects are everywhere. That is the first fauna you notice, because they are the ones who have not learned to be entirely silent. Beetles with shells like drops of cooled tar click once, then wait a long time, then click again. Moths the size of open hands roost in the felt trees with their wings folded into the nap; when they move, they move like a thought you almost had. Lizards occupy every unmortared seam—sand-colored, brick-colored, one species the exact pale of limewash, so that a wall will blink. There are birds that do not fly so much as step downward through the layered air, their plumage felted, their feet zygodactyl, their throats sequestered. They do not sing. Song, here, is considered a kind of leaking. Small dogs with cropped ears sleep in the shade of the terraces and dream with their eyes open. Something like a monkey, or a lemur, or a well-made puppet of a lemur, travels the thorn-lattices on hands that have too many knuckles. At dusk—dusk is a real thing; the light is ordinary, a clean warm fall from the unlidded rectangle of sky—the air thickens with bodies. You feel them more than you hear them. A pressure of wings. A dry snow of scale. The quiet is not empty. It is occupied, the way a held breath is occupied.

The builders worked with what a person can hold. Adzes. Bone needles. Fire, and the patience fire teaches. No iron that you can see, or only the rumor of a cold-hammered knife kept for ceremonies that have not yet been invented. Joinery is lashing, pegging, the interlocking of cut stone. Bricks still show the straw that bound them. Someone combed patterns into wet plaster with a twig and then stopped, as if the pattern had finished itself. The craft is total and close-handed. There is no natural substrate left to argue with the work. If there was bedrock, it has been tiled over. If there was a spring, it has been forgotten so completely that even the forgetting has been plastered. The enclosure is the world. Beyond the lip, the plateau is a rumor of ochre and heat-shimmer, and even that may be a painting on the outer face of the wall, badly maintained.

Because the place is young in the way a scab is young, the weirdness has not had time to weather into folklore. It is still happening, slowly, in the joins.

Distance is the first thing to slip. A walkway that should take twelve steps takes nine, or twenty, depending on whether you are carrying something that casts a shadow. Shadows themselves are not reliable. At noon they pool underfoot like spilled oil and refuse to point; later they fork, so that a single felt-tree darkens two terraces at once, and the lizards in both stripes of shade believe themselves unique. Rooms appear behind screens that were flush to the wall yesterday. The rooms are already furnished in the local manner—niches, a sleeping ledge, a basin of light—and the plaster is still damp, still printed, as if the masons had only just ducked out. No one claims the work. The work claims itself.

Objects forget their categories. A ladder left against a terrace will, over a week, decide it is a kind of vine, and put out a rung that is also a leaf. A leaf, conversely, may harden into a tool and be used, once, to scrape a pot, after which it is ashamed and crumbles. The mute birds sometimes hatch from pots instead of eggs. The pots do not seem to mind. There is a goat—there is always a goat—that walks through the north wall in the late afternoon and emerges from a mural of a goat on the south wall, slightly more faded, chewing. The mural is updated by no one. The update takes a season.

Time here is a resin. Things happen, but they happen at the speed of staining. A crack begins in a vault and then thinks better of it for a year. Flowers (the parchment ones, the copper ones, the few true ones) open as if unfolding a map of a country that no longer exists. The reality-tears, where they show, show as a kind of patience: a place where the air is thinner, or thicker, or briefly tastes of a river no one present has standing to remember. If you put your hand into such a place you feel the grain of the world running the wrong way, like wood planed against itself. You take the hand back. The hand is the same hand. Usually.

At the center, more or less—centers migrate—there is a court no wider than a room. The sherds of the floor there have been set in a spiral that does not quite close. In the unclosed gap, nothing has been built. It is the only unmade spot, a mouthful of packed dust, and even that has been tamped. People leave offerings that are not wet: a twist of hair, a beetle-shell, a word written on a leaf that will not decay because decay is a water-habit. The gap does not accept them so much as fail to refuse them. Around it the density increases, as if the garden were leaning in to hear. The quiet is deepest there. You can hear the lime thinking about the next hundred years of not-cracking. You can hear, if you are unlucky or very still, the sound of a place that was entirely constructed realizing that construction is a kind of wound in the given world, and that the wound is widening, not quickly, not with any drama, just the way a new wall settles—by a finger-width, by a degree, by the small betrayal of a right angle that is no longer sure it was ever right.

Night does not cool it much. The bricks give back what they were given. The constructed sun—no: the real sun, over a fully invented ground—leaves a last band of ordinary gold on the western coping, and then the rectangle of sky goes the color of a bruise that has decided not to hurt. The moths open. The lizards become the walls. Somewhere a single clay bell exists that has never been struck. The plants, which do not drink, continue their dry and implausible thriving. The goat is a mural again, or a goat again, or both, chewing the idea of a vine. And the whole small country, sharp-edged, hand-made, warm as a sleeping spine, holds its breath in the way of things that have only just begun to exist and have already started, very slowly, to come apart at the rules.
