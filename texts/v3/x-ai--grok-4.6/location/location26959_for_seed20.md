The basin is no larger than a nave and already too much water for that.

It sits where a ridge should have been, a young high place still showing its breaks: pale, unweathered facets of stone that look cut rather than worn, edges that would slice cloth, a few black blades of basalt standing like unfinished ribs. The uplift is recent enough that the rock still smells of hot mineral and wet dust when the water briefly recedes. Elevation is felt in the thinness of everything that is not water—the air, when you find a pocket of it, is already exhausted—and in the way the surrounding world drops away on every side into a dim, unreadable slope.

Water occupies nearly all of it. Not a lake with a surface. The water is the volume. It stands, it hangs, it falls in every direction at once. Warm, almost body-warm, with the faint sweetness of rain that has never touched soil. You move through it as through a room that has decided to be an ocean. Visibility is a few body-lengths of green-grey twilight; the light arrives already used, filtered through hundreds of meters of falling and rising liquid that has nowhere else to go. Sometimes a brighter seam opens—sun or something like sun trying the upper membrane—and the whole basin flares the color of bruised jade before the membrane closes again.

There is almost no vegetation. What exists clings. Hair-thin mats of something that might be moss or might be mineral, the color of old bronze, rasping on the newest stone. A single pale stalk, jointed like a knee, grows from a crack and is immediately bent double by current; it has no leaves. That is all. The water does not feed much. It scours.

Sound is the architecture. It is continuous and total, a roar with no source and no decay, the noise of a waterfall that has forgotten which way is down and so falls everywhere. You feel it in the sinuses and the teeth. Speech is a rumor you tell yourself. Occasionally a different note punches through—stone shearing, or a pocket of air collapsing—and then the roar swallows the difference and continues. The deafness is not absence; it is saturation. After a while the body stops trying to locate the sound and simply vibrates with it.

The place will not hold still. Currents reverse without warning. A wall of water that was vertical becomes a floor, then a ceiling of falling weight. The sharp young rock is being both made and unmade: new faces shear off and spin away as dark shards that dissolve before they can become sediment; other faces bloom, slick and faceted, as if the mountain were still deciding its own outline. Erosion here is not patient. It is argument. The basin itself pulses—narrower, then wider—like a throat. You can walk the entire circuit in minutes if the water permits a circuit; often it does not. Boundaries exist only as a change in the quality of the roar, a slight lessening that means you have found the lip, the place where the hanging ocean thins toward ordinary rain and the slope begins its long drop.

Scattered through this are marks that are not geology. Three low shapes of fitted stone, older than the uplift or perhaps only differently timed, sit half-swallowed. They have no doors, only openings the water uses as it pleases. One holds a single standing slab incised with lines that do not stay lines; they drift when you look away, as if the cut itself were still liquid. Another is only a ring of blocks, each block humming at a pitch just under the roar, a frequency you feel in the wrist. Nothing mechanical. No metal that remembers being made. Whatever built them used weight and water and the same broken rules the basin now obeys. They are not shelters. They are more like notes left in a language the storm is still writing.

Life is rare and brief. Once, something pale and flattened—more fin than body—shears through the green and is gone, leaving a ribbon of colder water. Once, a cluster of translucent bells pulses against a stone face and then unknits into ordinary bubbles. Nothing stays. The ecology is the turbulence itself: things appear, fail to cohere, become current.

The rules are local and unreliable. Down is a suggestion. Breathing happens, somehow, in the thick water as easily as in the thin air; the distinction between them is one of the things the place has not finished deciding. Time thickens and thins with the currents: a moment of stillness can last long enough to watch a new rib of stone grow a handspan, then the next heartbeat flings you against a wall that was not there. Memory of the outside world arrives waterlogged, colors leached, sequence optional. The weirdness is not decoration. It is the weather. Reality here is a young stone still cooling, still cracking, still being shouted into shape by a sound that has no origin and no interest in stopping.

If you find the lip and the roar eases a fraction, you can look out—or down, or through—and see only more dim water wearing the shape of distance. The basin does not end so much as it forgets to continue. Behind you the fitted stones continue their slow argument with the flood. The single pale stalk bends. The light stays used. The water, warm and deafening, keeps arriving from every direction at once, as if the sky itself had been inverted and wrung out over this one small, newly-made, already-drowning height.
