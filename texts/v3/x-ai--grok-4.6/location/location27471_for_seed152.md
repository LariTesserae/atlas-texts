The hollow is younger than the last long rain. It sits in the low country like a lid that someone has only just lifted: a steep-sided bowl perhaps eighty paces across, its rim flush with the surrounding scrub so that you can walk within ten steps of it and never know. The walls are still sharp. Fresh faces of pale stone catch what little light filters down, unrounded, unsmoothed, glittering where crystals have not yet been dulled. Nothing here has learned the long grammar of weather. The rock is still deciding how to be rock.

Warmth wells out of it. Not the thin heat of open sun but a held, breathing warmth, as if the ground itself has not finished cooling from whatever motion opened the bowl. In the dimness—always a little dimmer than the sky above, always slightly amber or grey-green—the air feels close and damp without being wet. Moisture lives on surfaces. It beads on the undersides of the first leaves. It films the stone at dawn and again at dusk. It gathers in the hairline cracks that still creep, almost silently, through the new walls. When a brief rain comes, it does not so much fall as arrive: a short, muffled percussion on the rim, then a hundred thin silver threads finding their way down faces that have no channels yet. The water disappears into the floor as quickly as it comes, leaving only dark maps that fade within an hour. In the lowest center a shallow lens of it sometimes persists for a day, clouded with stone-dust, reflecting nothing.

Plants have already begun their work, but they have not yet closed ranks. The vegetation is lush in the way a single well-fed thing is lush: each specimen extravagant, almost indecent against the raw mineral. Broad, waxy leaves the size of platters rise on stalks from seams of grit that have washed in from above. Ferns uncoil in isolated fountains, their fronds still wet from the last condensation. Moss has taken the north-facing ledges in thick, saturated cushions that squish under a careful foot and then spring back. Between these lives there is a great deal of emptiness—pale stone, angular rubble, the clean geometry of a place that has not yet been softened. The green things stand apart from one another like first arrivals at a gathering who do not yet know the names of the others. Vines have started, thin and ambitious, feeling along the walls, but they have not braided. Nothing here is old enough to cast true shade; the dimness is the hollow’s own, a quality of depth and vapor rather than canopy.

Around the inner rim, where the slope gentles for a few paces before dropping again, a settlement has begun. It is not a city in any sense that would survive a map, yet it is organized with the stubborn clarity of people who have decided this new wetness is worth claiming. Dwellings of packed earth and woven cane stand in a loose ring, their walls still the color of the stone they were scraped from, their roofs low and thatched with the same broad leaves that grow below. Paths have been packed by feet rather than cut; they shine slightly with use. A central platform of stacked, unmortared blocks—the blocks themselves still showing the bright scars of being broken free—serves as both drying ground and gathering place. There are no tools more complex than a digging stick or a fire-hardened point. Everything that stands has been made with hands, weight, and the patience of fiber. Smoke from the morning fires is thin and pale and does not travel far; it hangs in the warm air and then dissolves. The arrangement feels urban in miniature: a logic of proximity, of shared water-catchments scraped into the rock, of alleys that are only the spaces between one family’s wall and the next. It is a town the size of a secret.

Life has arrived faster than soil. The fauna is abundant in the way pioneers are abundant—everywhere, and still slightly astonished. Small frogs the color of wet stone occupy every persistent drip. Insects stitch the dim air in brief, glittering threads and then vanish. Lizards hold the warmest faces of rock, throats pulsing. Birds come and go from the rim in near silence, their wings making a sound like someone turning a page. At night the hollow fills with a low, continuous presence of things moving: the dry tick of beetles, the occasional liquid note of a frog, the soft collision of a body against cane. Nothing here is loud. The stone swallows echoes. Voices from the ring of dwellings stay close to the speaker, as if the air itself prefers not to carry them.

The place is never still. New cracks appear overnight, hair-thin, then wider, sighing out a little more warmth. A plant that was not there yesterday occupies a fresh seam of grit. Water finds a slightly different path after each rain and leaves a slightly different stain. The people shift a wall when the ground under it settles. A pair of ground-doves claims the thatch of one roof and is tolerated. The hollow is doing several things at once: cooling, wetting, greening, being claimed. It has the restlessness of something that has not yet chosen its final shape, and the quiet of a room that does not yet know it is a room.

Stand at the bottom beside the vanishing puddle and look up. The rim is a ragged circle of ordinary sky, surprisingly close. The walls are still too new to have stories. The leaves are still too few to hide you completely. You can hear your own breath, and the faint, continual sound of water choosing its first habits, and somewhere above, a single bird crossing the opening without calling. This is as large as the world gets, for now. Everything else is still outside, and older, and drier, and does not yet know the hollow is here.
