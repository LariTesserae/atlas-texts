The Calcine Shelf does not begin. It has always been mid-sentence.

You come up onto it the way you come up into a fever: not by crossing a border so much as by noticing that the last reliable thing—horizon, weight, the contract between a foot and a surface—has been quietly rewritten. The land is high. Not mountain-high, not heroic. A table, an old one, lifted long ago and then left to think about it. Whatever lifted it finished the job so thoroughly that the edges have forgotten they were edges. They slump into heat-haze the way old masonry slumps into ivy, except there is no ivy, and the haze is not always where the air is hottest.

There is almost no water. There has not been water in any useful sense for a duration that has itself become a kind of geology. What moisture exists is a rumor inside minerals: hydroxyl ghosts in latticework, a sour film that condenses at night only in the creases of things that are not quite rock, then burns off as a metallic breath before any shadow has lengthened. The ground does not thirst. Thirst is a relationship. This place concluded that relationship and filed it.

What you walk on—if walk is the word, if you are even the sort of presence that walks—is a crust of sintered dust, salt-glass, and slag that learned, over uncountable summers, how to hold a shape. It is pale in some stretches, the color of bone left in a kiln, and elsewhere the color of old pennies, of liver, of the inside of a spent cartridge. Wind has had time. Wind here is not weather so much as a continuous industrial process that no one built: a river of superheated air dragging grit in long, patient strokes. The grit has polished the high places into lenses and scoured the low places into channels that look designed and are not. Nothing here was designed. That is one of the rules, and like most of the rules it is only mostly true.

The air is loud. Not with events. With continuance. A standing roar occupies the middle of hearing the way a factory occupies a valley, except the factory is the valley’s idea of itself. You hear grinders that do not exist, belts that have no pulleys, a deep tessellated hiss as of scale being stripped from something the size of a district. The sound does not peak. It has already peaked, sometime in a previous century, and is now the climate of the ear. When it seems to change it is only because you have moved relative to a fold in the heat, or because one of the fauna has decided to be nearer than it was.

The fauna.

They are everywhere and they are almost nowhere. Density is a cruel joke on this shelf. You can stand in a place that feels as empty as the inside of a bell and still be outnumbered. They do not fill volume the way cattle fill a field. They occupy the available meanings of a volume. Some are visible in the ordinary way: long, low processions of carapace the color of cooled pours, each segment a vented plate, each plate weeping a thread of vapor that is not steam. They move the way continents move if continents were allowed to get hungry. Between one landmark and the next a line of them will have advanced a body’s length. You will not see the step. You will only notice that the shadow-lattices have transferred, that a ridge you were using as a compass is now a hip.

Others refuse to settle into a single optical agreement. There are flocks—if flocks is kind—that exist primarily as interference. They pass through the bright air as ripples in the relationship between color and distance. Where they concentrate, the shelf’s far features stutter: a distant butte becomes two buttes, then a furnace door, then a memory of a butte you have not yet reached. Their sound is a chord of shearing metal sustained past any honest metallurgy. You feel it in the teeth you do not have.

Still others are the heat given loyalties. They roam as slow storms of incandescence, taller than they are wide, with something like shoulders only because the mind, under duress, will invent shoulders rather than admit a vertical furnace is considering you. They do not eat plants. There are no plants. There has never been the green argument here. What they metabolize is gradient: the difference between the baked face of a slab and the slightly less baked interior; the difference between one layer of cooked air and the next; the difference, increasingly, between a place and the idea of a place. They leave behind vitrified tracks, glass ropes sunk into the crust, which the wind then spends decades frosting into dullness. The tracks are not paths. Paths imply destination. These are the fossilized opinions of appetite.

Because the world is old in its present tense—established, weathered, in the long balance of its own bad habits—the fauna have had time to become landscape features that occasionally remember they are not. A ridge will turn out to have a pulse. A mesa will slough a flank and the flank will walk a little way and lie down and become a lesser mesa. Nothing hurries. The temperature is a punishment that has forgotten it is punishment and become policy: bright, dry, a heat that does not flicker so much as insist. Light is ordinary in the way a wound can be ordinary. A sun, or something performing the duties of a sun, stands in a sky the color of overfired enamel and does its work without poetry. Shadows are sharp at noon and then, as the day leans, they grow complicated, because the fauna interfere with straight lines the way industry interferes with silence.

There are no buildings. There are things that look like the afterimages of buildings, the way a bright window leaves a pane on the eyelid. Scaffolding made of cooled dripstone. Gantries that are only the negative space between three slow-moving bodies that have not noticed they are composing a machine. If you stare too long at a particular alignment of vents and ridges you will be certain you are seeing a plant—smokestacks, a sawtooth roof, a conveyor’s long persuasion—and then the certainty will slide, and you will be looking at geology again, or at a herd, or at a place where reality thinned and briefly remembered factories.

That thinning is the shelf’s other climate. Weirdness here is not spice. It is load-bearing. Distances disagree. You can walk toward a dark seam for the better part of a day and find it is a crack you could step over, or a canyon whose far wall is a different hour. Sound arrives from directions that do not exist on a compass, industrial and intimate: riveting, the collapse of a hopper, a whistle that is not a warning so much as a thesis. Sometimes the roar parts and you hear, very clearly, the tick of cooling metal, though nothing near you is cooling. Sometimes your own outline arrives a few seconds late and stands beside you, patiently, like a worker waiting for a shift change that was canceled in a language no one kept.

The ecology, such as it is, is a closed circuit of heat, abrasion, and attention. Smaller fauna—if the word smaller still applies when one of them can be a cloud of cutting glitter that takes an afternoon to pass—graze the glass ropes, the salt-scurf, the shed plates of the larger walkers. They make a sound like rain on a foundry roof, which is a cruel sound in a place that has never known rain. They are eaten in turn by things that are mostly mouth and kiln, things that open and remain open, drawing the glitter in as a luminous slurry, exhaling a dust that will, in a few decades, be another kind of ground. Nothing here is in a hurry to complete a sentence. Predation is a weather front. Reproduction, when it happens, looks like a fault propagating: a split, a doubling, two heats where there was one, both already ancient.

The rules are local and not always transferable.

Matter prefers to be exhausted. What can be calcined will be. What can be worn will be worn until wearing becomes a form of rest.

Identity is a renewable resource and a pollutant. The shelf will lend you a self if you arrive without one, then bill you in distortions.

Nothing is built, but building happens: alignments, smeltings, the slow assembly of one body from the rejected parts of others. Industry without intention, or intention so old it has become a mineral.

Water is a historical inaccuracy. If you bring any, it will become a story the air tells against you.

The empty places are not safe. They are simply the gaps in a crowding that does not use space the way crowds do. You can be alone and completely surrounded. You can be in a throng and hear only the wind’s long file-work on a single stone.

At the far end of whatever counts as a region—beyond the glass rivers, beyond the herds that are also horizons—the shelf does not end so much as lose interest in being one kind of place. The roar changes key. The light remains ordinary and becomes, by remaining ordinary while everything else declines to, the strangest thing left. Something like evening may occur, a reddening that is less sunset than the fauna deciding, collectively and without consultation, to be a different temperature. The slow walkers settle into their own shadows and become indistinguishable from the land that made them, or that they made, the distinction having eroded along with everything else.

You do not camp. Camping is an optimism. You find a hollow the wind has finished with, a bowl of dull glass, and you wait in it while the night—if this is night—fills with the tick and groan of a world cooling by fractions, never enough, and with the nearer sounds of things that do not sleep so much as pause between acts of being overwhelming. In the dark the industrial noise goes on. It has nowhere else to be. It is the sound of a process that outlasted its purpose and then outlasted the need for purpose, and is now simply the way this high, dry, crowded emptiness continues to mean itself.

Morning is a white lid being set back on. The first walkers detach from the ridgelines. A flock of interferences crosses the sun and for a little while there are too many suns, all of them ordinary, all of them working. The shelf, which has not begun and will not conclude, resumes the long work of being exactly this, and nothing else, and also, when the heat hits the right seam in the air, several other places that do not have names because naming is a kind of water, and there is no water here.
