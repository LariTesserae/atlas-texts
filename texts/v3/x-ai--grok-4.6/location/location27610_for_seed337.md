The plain gives almost no warning. Grass thins, the ground hardens into a pale crust, and then the world simply stops having a surface. What opens is not a canyon cut by age but a wound still being enlarged: a sunken basin perhaps a league across, its walls stepped and faceted, its floor a confusion of platforms, ramps, and half-born streets. You stand at the rim in ordinary daylight and look down into a town that has not finished deciding it is a town.

The stone is new. That is the first thing the place insists on. Faces of rock still hold the bloom of the last cut—no lichen, no rounding, no memory of weather. Blocks sit where they were pried free, some still attached by a last uncut keel of stone, as if the geology itself were only now being persuaded to become architecture. There has been no erosion here worth the name. Wind has not had time. Rain has only stained. The basin is embryonic: a city and a quarry and a geology occurring in the same gesture.

You descend by switchbacks that are also streets. They are packed. Not with plants—there is almost nothing green except a dark seam of moss where seepage finds a ledge, and a few wiry shrubs that someone has transplanted into baskets of spoil and then forgotten. The packing is human and mineral. Scaffold of lashed poles occupies every terrace. Ropes, rollers of green wood, hide slings, copper-edged chisels, mauls of river cobble bound in rawhide. People move in files so close that shoulders brush; they give way without speech because speech would require room that does not exist. Dwellings are already cut into the walls—rectangles of shade, some with hides for doors, some still open mouths of unfinished chambers. Between the dwellings: stacks of dressed stone, stacks of undressed stone, stacks of chips, water skins, ash from last night’s small fires. There is no interval. Every horizontal surface has been claimed by a task or a body or the residue of both.

It is cool. The walls hold the day’s heat poorly and give it back as a faint mineral chill. Breath shows in the mornings. Fingers stiffen on the tools. The temperature is the temperature of deep shade and wet rock, even when the disk of sky overhead is bright. Light itself is ordinary—sun, cloud-shadow, the slow crawl of noon down one face and up the other—but it arrives as if into a well. Mornings the eastern terraces glare white and the western ones stay blue. Late day reverses it. Night, when it comes, is a lid.

Water is present the way a rumor is present. It does not run in any committed channel yet. It sweats from the fresh cuts. It beads on the undersides of overhangs. After rain—which is occasional, never generous—it finds the lowest ledges and stands there in sheets no deeper than a palm, cloudy with stone-flour, until someone scoops it into jars or cuts a groove to lead it toward the cisterns they are still arguing about, in gestures, at the basin’s center. There is a spring, or the idea of a spring, at the lowest point: a place where the floor weeps more honestly, where they have piled a ring of unshaped blocks and called it a well. The air tastes of wet lime. Condensation writes faint maps on the inner walls of the sleeping-rooms and then erases them by midmorning.

Sound is small. That surprises you, given how many people there are. The basin swallows. Chisel-strikes reach you as clicks, not as ringing. Sledges hiss on wooden rollers. Someone coughs; it does not carry. A rope tautens and complains once. Children—there are children, squeezed into the same density as everyone else—play in the chip-heaps with a hush that seems learned from the stone. No dogs. Almost no birds. Once in a long while a pale lizard appears on a sun-warmed block and is gone. The fauna of the place is rumor plus that lizard. Whatever lived on the plain does not come down. Whatever might live in deep rock has not yet been invited by time.

And yet the place is not still. It is being made at every hour that light allows. Teams open new faces. Other teams dress yesterday’s faces. A third kind of team hauls, and a fourth kind cuts sockets for beams that have not been felled yet, and a fifth kind is already living in rooms whose ceilings still show the marks of the first wedges. Processes overlap without a center. Weather arrives as a brief darkening and a cold smell and then a sequin of water in every tool-cut; then it leaves and the work resumes as if rain were only another kind of chisel. At dusk the fires are many and very small, because there is no spare wood and no spare space for a blaze. Smoke climbs the walls in thin threads and frays against the rim. People eat what can be carried down: grain, dried fish from a river that is not this place, a little oil. They sleep in shifts so that a room can hold more bodies than it has floor.

The rules are practical and have not yet hardened into law. You do not take a finished block that still bears another crew’s mark. You do not foul the weeping-place. You give way on the ramps to anyone whose load is heavier than yours. You do not sing, or if you do you sing inside your teeth; the basin has taught them that a raised voice is a waste and a kind of rudeness toward the work. Newcomers are put to the chip-baskets until their hands understand the grain of this particular stone. The stone itself is the only calendar: how many faces opened, how far the lowest street has dropped, whether the well-ring has been raised another course against the seep.

From the rim, at certain hours, the whole of it can be seen at once—an open mid-sized country of labor, town-shaped, valley-shaped, not yet a monument and already more than a pit. Terraces recede in pale steps. Ramps stitch them. The packed figures read as a single texture, like seed in a cut pomegranate, except that the seeds are moving. You can watch a block begin the day as cliff and end it as a corner. You can watch a family carry their hides from a ledge that will be demolished tomorrow into a chamber that did not exist last month. The city is the act of becoming a city, performed in cool damp stone, under ordinary light, in a quiet so consistent it feels like a material.

Nothing here is ancient. Nothing is finished. The geology is younger than some of the children. If you put your palm on a wall you feel not the patience of mountains but the slightly greasy cool of rock that has only just learned air. Tomorrow they will cut again. The basin will deepen by a hand’s breadth, or widen, or both. The moss will try the new seams and mostly fail. The spring will be persuaded, or not. Night will set its lid. And the place will go on being born in the only way it knows: crowded, downward, almost silent, one unweathered face at a time.
