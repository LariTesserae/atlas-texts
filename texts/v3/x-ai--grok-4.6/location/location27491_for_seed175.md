The place is a stone bowl no larger than a cathedral floor, set into the shoulder of a high ridge. You do not come upon it gradually. The ridgeline is dry wind and pale grass and then the ground simply fails, and you are looking down into a hanging amphitheater whose walls weep.

The rock is old in the settled way: not jagged, not freshly broken. It has been rained on and breathed on until every edge is a lip. Dark, fine-grained, warm to the touch even in shade. Heat comes up through it as a patient fact, as if the mountain were a body that has not cooled. The water does not fall from any stream you can name. It condenses on the inner face of the bowl and beads and runs. It issues from seams. It stands in shallow, perfectly still saucers worn into the ledges. The air is thick with it—warm, mineral, a little sweet, like rain on heated stone that has never quite finished falling.

Nothing here is crowded. That is the first strangeness, once the warmth and the wet have been accepted. The vegetation is lush the way a single sentence can be lush. There are perhaps thirty plants in the whole basin, each given a terrace or a crack or a pedestal of its own, and each one is extravagant. Broad leaves the color of deep tea, veined in paler green, large enough to roof a crouch. Stems as thick as a wrist, ringed like old bamboo, always shining. Some hang in curtains from the upper lip and never quite reach the floor; their tips drip without pause, and the drips have cut bright grooves in the rock beneath, grooves so smooth they look poured. Others stand alone on the floor of the bowl in their own dark pools, roots visible through the water like pale hands. They do not compete. The spaces between them are wide and deliberate, floors of wet stone and shallow water and nothing else. You can walk a long time from one to the next. The emptiness is part of the planting.

There are no animals. Not a midge. Not a worm in the mud of the saucers. The silence is therefore almost complete, and what sound there is belongs only to water: a thread-sound, a ticking on leaves, a single note when a drop hits a pool from a height. No wind reaches the floor. The air moves the way warm air moves in a closed room—slowly, upward, along the weeping walls, and out over the rim as a faint breath you feel only if you stand at the edge.

Light is ordinary and that, here, is slightly wrong. Midday comes in clean and lays the plants’ shadows exactly where shadows ought to go. Evening golds the wet rock. Nothing in the light itself misbehaves. And yet because nothing else moves—no bird-flicker, no leaf turned by an insect—the light seems to stay longer than it should, as if the bowl were a vessel that held brightness the way it holds water. Shadows pool in the saucers and do not quite match the leaves that cast them; they are a half-breath late, or a half-breath early. You notice this only if you watch.

The plants themselves are not quite plants as other places keep them. Cut a leaf (no one living has, but the cut would behave this way) and the sap is clear and warm and smells of iron and rain. The larger stems bear no flowers. Instead, at long intervals measured in years, a pale plate of tissue lifts from a node, thinner than paper, and remains for a season, trembling in no wind, and then dissolves back into the wet. Pollen, if that is what it is, does not leave the bowl. It settles on the nearest water and makes a brief iridescence and is gone. The roots, where they show, have the look of something that remembers being arranged. Not built—there is no masonry, no cut block, no path that was ever a path—but once, a very long time ago, someone with a hand-tool and a great deal of patience may have encouraged a channel, or seated a young stem in a chosen crack, or rubbed a stone smooth so that water would sheet rather than fall. Those gestures have been weather for so many centuries that they read now as geology. A single flake of worked flint lies under silt in one of the upper saucers, edges softened to the same lip the rock has. It might be a tool. It might be a coincidence the stone offered. Nothing else human remains.

Change here is a tide with a period of decades. The water-level in the floor-pools rises a finger’s width after a season of heavier mist and falls again. A leaf lasts years. When one finally yellows it does not drop so much as slump, and the plant absorbs it, and the groove beneath receives one more season of drip. The amphitheater is in balance with itself. It has been this shape long enough that the shape feels like a decision the mountain already finished making.

At night the stone holds its heat. The weeping does not stop. If you stayed (and the place does not forbid staying; it simply offers no company and no food that an animal body would accept) you would hear only the same thin water, and see the wet leaves take starlight the way they take noon—exactly, and a little too calmly. Toward dawn a skin of vapor lifts from the pools and climbs the walls and goes out over the rim into the colder air of the ridge, and for a few minutes the bowl breathes as a single lung.

That is all. A small high room of warm stone and sparse, dripping green. No names on it. No paths in or out except the ones water has already chosen. The rest of the mountain continues, drier and windier, as if this wet pocket were a thought the ridge has not yet decided to finish.
