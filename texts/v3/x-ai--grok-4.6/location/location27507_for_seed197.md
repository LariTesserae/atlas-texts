The place is a bowl no wider than a long shout, if shouting were still possible here. You come down into it without quite noticing the rim — the land only eases, warm and low, and then the light goes slightly green and you understand you have been enclosed. What encloses you is not stone. There has never been stone. The walls are trunk and the trunk is wall, and the distinction has been weathered out of usefulness over a time no one kept.

It is a city in the way a hive is a city and in the way a throat is a room. From the rim you could count the standing forms on two hands and have fingers left: great columnar lives, fused at the hip, hollowed by their own centuries into halls. Between them the ground is a packed, breathing pavement of root-plate and old leaf-leather, the color of tea left in a cup. The structures stand far apart. That is the first wrongness you can name. A settlement of this organization should crowd. This one has left itself mostly air. Each bole is a neighborhood. The distances between them are plazas that feel, once you are in them, larger than the bowl that holds them. You can stand in the center of the main emptiness and the facing doors — if doors is the word for those vertical slits of thinner cambium, weeping a clear sap that never quite drips — recede as you approach, or arrive too soon. The arithmetic of walking does not survive the first crossing.

Everything is plant. Not overgrown: *of*. The substrate underfoot pulses a half-degree warmer than the air. Where a street would have a gutter there is a vein. Where a cornice would throw shade there is a gallery of living buttress, winged and fluted by a growth so old it has forgotten it is growth and thinks of itself as posture. The roofs are single leaves the size of sails, held out on petioles thick as thighs, and they are not still. They clock the light. They drink what little weather comes.

Water here is a rumor that keeps its appointments. It does not run so much as visit. Mornings, the undersides of the sail-leaves sweat a fine condensation that gathers, travels the ribs, and falls in irregular strings into cistern-hollows the size of basins, the size of rooms, never the size of rivers. Sometimes a real rain crosses the bowl — a brief, warm, indecisive rain — and the whole architecture becomes a machine for not wasting it. Gutters that are vines tighten. Surfaces that were matte go dark and slick. For an hour the air smells of tannin and something sweet, like fruit that has decided not to ripen. Then it is damp again, only damp, the perennial condition: a film on bark, a coolness in the deep grain, pockets of standing wet in knotholes that have become tarns, complete with their own minute weathers. A thread of actual flow exists. It appears from a split in the easternmost bole, crosses three plazas in a bed of black, polished root, and is gone into a mouth of moss before you can decide if it is a stream or a leak. People — the word is already failing — step over it as over a threshold.

The temperature is the temperature of a closed hand. Warm, not hot. The air has weight but not cruelty. It has been breathing in and out of this same wood for so long that it has taken on a taste, faintly pepper, faintly rot in the honest sense, the rot of a forest that has reached an understanding with itself. Nothing here is young except the fauna, and even the fauna feel rehearsed.

Because the spaces are so empty, the life is all the more a violence of number. The air between the boles is a weather of bodies. Birds in sheets. Not flocks — sheets, turning as one fabric, their undersides flashing the same tired silver. They roost in the leaf-roofs and the roofs bow, and the bowing is a sound, and the sound is already too much, and that is before anything else has started. Insects are not incidents; they are a medium, a grit in the light. Along the bark-streets go the walkers: things with too many joints and a patience that looks like civic duty, carrying nothing, going everywhere. In the cistern-hollows, pale fish that have never known a current mouth at the meniscus. Higher, in the galleries, something like cats if cats were moss and hunger, draped where the light is best, watching the plazas with the boredom of landlords. There are grazers the color of wet paper who crop the living pavement; the pavement grows back before their hind legs have passed. There are hunters who do not hide, because hiding is a waste in a place that is already so loud. Predation here is not a secret. It is a schedule. You can stand in a plaza and watch a chase complete itself three times in an hour, same route, different animals, as if the empty air preferred a particular shape of panic and kept requesting it.

The sound is the true architecture.

It has no off. It has no underneath. To call it deafening is to pretend there is still a self that could be deafened, a private room in the head where sentences might finish. There is not. The bowl is a resonator and everything that lives here knows it and cannot stop. Growth itself is audible: a continuous baritone crack and stretch, cellulose yielding, the boles making the noise of ships and of old houses and of ice, all day, all night, a sound like a city being slowly bent. Over that, the birds. Over that, the insects in their several keys, a grit that becomes a scream if you try to find its edges. Water, when it moves, is percussion in the hollows — drop, drop, a pause that is never long enough, then a run of drops like fingers on a wet drum. The walkers’ feet on the root-pavement are a hush so multiplied it becomes a roar of silk. And then the other sounds, the ones that do not belong to any throat now present: a market that is not held here; a rain that is not this rain; a voice counting in a language the wood remembers and the air has not agreed to forget. Echoes arrive early. Sometimes a cry goes out from the western bole and is answered from the eastern before it has been made, and both animals look startled, and neither stops.

This is how you understand the place is not only old but unwell in a way that has become its health. Space does not add. Time does not queue. You may walk a circuit of the five great boles and find a sixth that was always there and will not be there when you turn. A plaza you crossed at noon is, at the same noon, a vertical shaft lined with leaves that show you the sky from underneath, as if the bowl had been inverted while you blinked. The light remains ordinary through all of this — a decent, usable daylight, greened, stippled, never miraculous — which makes the errors worse. The sun is doing nothing wrong. The world is.

There are rules, if rule is a kindness you can still extend.

Nothing is inert. If you lean on a wall it will, after a while, lean back, not in affection, in turgor. A cut bleeds and then seals with a tissue that has opinions; later the scar will be a window or a mouth or a stair, depending on what the surrounding growth decided it needed. There are no tools. There have never been tools. What looks like craft is appetite given a century to finish a thought. The “streets” migrate. Slowly, the way a sandbar migrates. A path that served the morning grazers is, by late heat, a ridge, and the traffic has found another depression and begun, by the simple pressure of many pads, to declare it a road. Districts are not mapped; they are fed. The northern bole is a place of ripening — its inner chambers hang with fruit that is also furniture, heavy, dripping, attended by everything with a mouth. The southern is a place of hush that is not quiet (nothing is quiet) but of a different grain of noise, lower, wetter, where the hunters wait because the acoustics make a stepping mouse sound like a falling branch and they have learned to love the lie. The central emptiness is common. It belongs to whoever is crossing it, which is always everyone.

The city has no government and a fierce civic order. Niches are addresses. A particular hollow, a particular height of gallery, a particular cistern: these are held, contested, inherited in the only currency that works, which is occupancy and the willingness to be loud enough to keep it. At dusk — dusk comes as a dimming of the green, a warming of the already-warm, a change in which leaves are loudest — the whole bowl performs something that is not a ceremony and not not one. The sail-roofs tilt. The condensation starts its work. The bird-sheets come in. The sound climbs a stair it has climbed every evening of its life and stands on a landing that should not be able to hold it. You feel it in your teeth and in the hinges of you. For a few minutes the reality-errors thicken: you may see the same animal die in two places; you may hear your own footfalls approaching from ahead; the riverine thread may run uphill, briefly, as if remembering a different down. Then the landing is vacated, the sound becomes merely impossible instead of terminal, and night begins, which is the same place with the light taken away and the insects promoted.

You could leave. The rim is always near. The bowl is small. That is the last wrongness, the one that waits until you have tried: smallness here is a kind of courtesy the place extends to the idea of elsewhere. The climb out takes the time it takes to forget a name. At the top the air is only air, the warmth only weather, and behind you the roar continues, sealed, as if a lid had been set on a cup that is larger inside than any mouth that could drink it.

What remains, if you look back, is a dark green punctuation in the low land. A grove. A hamlet of trees. A quiet thing, from this distance, in ordinary light, damp at the roots, full of birds you can no longer hear.
