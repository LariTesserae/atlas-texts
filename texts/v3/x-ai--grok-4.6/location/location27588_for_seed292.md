The last high places stand like worn knuckles of an old hand, all that remains after the world finished grinding itself down. They rise from a single, planet-wrapping ocean that has had geologic ages to find its level and then forget there was ever anything else. The rock itself is the color of wet iron and old bone, rounded by rains that fell before there were names for rain. No sharp ridges left, no hanging valleys, no cirques: only long, slumped shoulders and broad, rain-polished domes that the sea has been chewing at for so long the chew-marks have become the only architecture.

From any of these summits the view is the same: water, then more water, then the next dark hump of land thirty or forty kilometers off, half-dissolved in spray. The ocean is not a surface so much as a moving wall. Swells arrive already the size of hills, their faces streaked white, and they do not break so much as collapse, folding over themselves with a sound like freight trains meeting in a tunnel. Between the big waves run smaller, angrier ones that interfere and climb and slap the air into a continuous white roar. Rain does not fall here; it is simply the air deciding to be water for a while. Visibility is a few hundred meters of driving gray, then nothing. The light is ordinary daylight strained through ten kilometers of cloud and spray, the color of old pewter, bright enough to see by, never bright enough to cast a true shadow.

The remaining land is almost an afterthought. What little soil still clings is black, acidic, permanently saturated. It supports a low, ferocious greenery that has no interest in height: mats of moss thicker than a man’s thigh, liverworts that glow a sullen emerald, ferns whose fronds are narrow as knives so the wind can pass through them. Every vertical surface—cliff, boulder, the last standing wall of some forgotten concrete shed—is furred with this stuff. It drinks the rain and the salt equally and asks for nothing else. Below the tide line the same impulse continues without pause. Kelp forests begin in the intertidal and simply keep going down, their holdfasts locked into the ancient basalt, their blades thirty, forty meters long, so densely packed that a diver (or the things that live here) must shoulder through them like a man walking through wet drapery. Between the kelp, red and brown algae carpet every rock in overlapping sheets. The water itself is thick with phytoplankton that turn the upper ten meters a cold, milky jade.

Life is loud and close. Gulls and petrels work the spray-line in screaming thousands, their cries shredded by wind. Below, the kelp holds entire cities of fish that move as single muscles: silver, then dark, then silver again. Seals porpoise through the mid-water, hunting. Something larger—whale, or the local equivalent—sounds in the deep channels between the drowned ridges, a single long note that arrives through the rock as much as through the air. Crabs the size of dinner plates click and scuttle in the intertidal, armored against the constant grinding of cobbles. Everything eats, everything is eaten, everything is wet. There is no quiet interval.

Human marks are few and already half-digested. On the higher shoulders sit the skeletons of processing plants: corrugated sheds whose roofs have been peeled back like tin cans, conveyor belts frozen mid-sag, a single smokestack still standing because it was guyed with cables now thick as a man’s wrist with rust and mussel. A few tidal generators still turn in the passes, their turbines groaning with every surge, lights flickering on some forgotten control board. Wooden pilings stick out of the lower slopes like broken teeth; the villages they once served are only rectangles of darker weed. Now and then a buoy or a section of pipeline surfaces in the trough of a wave, spins, and is gone again. No one maintains them. No one needs to. The machines that still run do so out of leftover momentum and the sheer volume of moving water.

The weather does not change so much as reconfigure. A storm is not an event; it is the default. Fronts grind into one another for days, the barometer wandering without pattern. Hail mixes with rain. Waterspouts walk the middle distance like pale, swaying towers. The air is so charged with salt and electricity that hair stands and metal tastes bright on the tongue. Yet none of it feels unnatural. This is simply how an old, finished world sounds when the last of its heat and the last of its water are still negotiating their long, noisy truce. The mountains will be lower next century, the ocean a few centimeters higher, the kelp a little thicker. Nothing here is in a hurry except the water itself, which has never learned how to be still.
