The first thing is not water as you know it from a shore. It is water as volume, as a room with no walls yet, a cold that arrives through the suit or the skin all at once and then becomes the only temperature there is. Light comes down in long, unhurried columns, not the green-black of a living ocean but a paler, cleaner blue, as if the sun had been filtered through glass and poured. You can see a long way. That is the second thing: how far you can see, and how little there is to see.

You hang. Above you the surface is a moving lid of silver, sometimes dented by rain, sometimes torn by a wind you will never feel. Below you the floor is not yet a floor. It is a suggestion, a change in the blue, and then, after a long descent that does not feel like descent so much as a change of mind, it resolves into something poured.

It was poured.

The bottom of this place is a continent of formwork that was never stripped. Hexagonal caissons the size of city blocks meet along joints as straight as ruled lines. Where the pours met they left seams of darker grout, and along those seams the first thin films of life have taken—nothing you would call a meadow, only a faint olive haze, a roughness the hand would notice. Bolt-heads stand in ranks. Expansion joints run for kilometers, rubber and steel still black, still smelling, if you could smell down here, of the yard they came from. Nothing has been rounded. The edges could cut. This sea is younger than its weather.

There is weather. The basin is large enough. From a pump-house spine half a day’s swim away the operators will tell you the fetch is measured in hundreds of kilometers, that a front can walk the surface from one retaining wall to the other in a night, that the artificial tide they run for circulation can stand against a real gale and lose. Down here the storm arrives as a change in the light first: the columns smear, the silver lid goes pewter, and then the whole volume begins to lean. Fine silt that has not yet learned where to settle lifts in sheets and hangs. The cables hum a half-tone higher. You hear the storm before the current turns—an industrial baritone, turbines taking load, the great intakes on the western works opening another throat.

Those works are rare. That is the rule of density here. You may travel until your light dies and meet nothing that was not water and the idea of a floor. Then a shape. A condenser island: a squat ziggurat of intake bells and heat-exchange plates, still faintly warm on the lee face, a private weather of its own, a shimmer you can swim through. Fish have found the shimmer. They hang in it the way dust hangs in a loft, not many species, not yet—silver things with the blunt heads of things bred for tanks, and a darker, older immigrant, something like a cod that came in through the locks when the last gates opened and the basin took its fill. They do not school so much as occupy. There is room. There is always room.

Further on, a run of towers that were never meant to be underwater and were left anyway when the plan changed and the water rose to the design mark in a single season. Their windows are open. Their stairwells are chimneys of colder water. Eels use them. The eels were not stocked; they arrived the way eels arrive, which is to say no one can quite say. On the landings, in the silt that gathers against the risers, there are already the small industrious lives: amphipods, pale crabs the width of a thumbnail, the first mussels in lines as straight as the welds they have chosen. Sparse as lichen on a new wall. The towers go up and up and do not reach air. Their roofs are still a long blue way from the silver. Birds do not come here. There is no above for them that is not a metal weather-deck days away by boat.

Sound is a structure. It has beams and bays. Closest, always, the small noises of a filled thing settling: a plate ticking as it equalizes, a groan along a kilometer of pipe as the daily thermal cycle turns, the grit of something walking on concrete—a crab, a maintenance crawler on its scheduled rail, the two indistinguishable until you see the lights. Middle distance, the plants. They do not sleep. Circulation is the law; still water would stratify, would bloom, would spoil the cooling contract this ocean was poured to keep. So the pumps speak without pause, a stacked chord of fifty-cycle hum and the deeper wander of impellers in throats wide enough to swallow a hull. On the hour a sluice changes its mind somewhere over the curve of the world and the note shifts. Far sound is weather and traffic: the long empty note of a surface hull, the white tear of a storm, once in a while the single coded blast of a lock accepting a barge from the old rivers that now end in valves.

The water itself is cool enough to keep a body honest. It came from those rivers and from the sky and from a hundred cut aquifers, mixed in the settling lakes, treated just enough to stop the concrete from rotting in its first decade, not enough to make it dead. You taste mineral. You taste the lime still leaving the new pours, a faint alkali that the fish tolerate and the first weeds exploit. In the photic band, where the light is still a version of day, there are racks—purposeful, lonely agriculture. Kelp on cables, in plots as rectangular as fields, each plot a dark acre with a service buoy lost in the glitter far above. Between plots, nothing. The farmers come down in bells. Their lights are the only warm color. When they leave, the kelp moves in the artificial tide like hair in a sleeping mouth.

No stone was left that was not dressed. If there was a valley here, it was lined. If there was a mountain, it was cut down to a footing and bolted over. The retaining walls, when at last you reach one—and you can travel a week and only suspect you are nearer—are not cliffs. They are revetments, stepped, each step a road for the machines that built them, each riser stamped with a contractor’s mark still sharp. Algae blackens the sunny faces in streaks. The shaded faces are bare as a basin in a showroom. At the foot, rubble that is not rubble: rejected segments, unused gates, a crane that was cheaper to flood than to lift, already wearing a beard of the same thin green. Divers have written their names in the silt on its boom. The names are still there. Nothing has had time to bury them.

Night, when it comes, is not the night of the deep. The works keep their own day. Navigation chains along the main channels, yellow and mean. The condenser islands wear coronas. From below you can see the surface traffic as constellations being towed. In the true dark between the marked roads the fauna go about a business that is already older than the basin. Something large, not many of it, moves along the joints where the warm effluent ghosts. You hear it more than you see it: a passage of displaced water, a sound like a tarpaulin drawn over a hull. Normal life, in the sense that life is what happens when you leave a door open. No one put a name on it in the stocking lists.

The rules are industrial and therefore simple. Level is maintained. Temperature is a specification. The locks open on a schedule published in three languages and a code of lights. You do not touch a live cable; the cables are live. You do not enter an intake; the intakes do not care what you are. The basin was finished, then filled, and the filling was a year of brown water and drowned survey towers and a noise that people on the old rim said they felt in their teeth. Now the water is clear because it is new and cold and hungry, and the hunger is being answered slowly, in the seams, on the racks, in the open windows of the towers that were left. The place is not wild. It is not tame. It is running.

On certain mornings, when the surface is glass and the light goes down as if it had been invited, you can hang over a pour-joint and see your own shape, small, and the ranks of bolt-heads going on until they become a texture, and the texture becoming a horizon that is only more of the same work, and you understand the size not as a number but as a kind of patience: someone decided to make an ocean the way you make a dam, and then they kept going until the dam had weather, and fish, and a sound that never stops, and edges that have not yet learned to be old.
