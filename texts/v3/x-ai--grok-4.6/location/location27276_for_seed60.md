The place occupies a shallow basin no larger than a village green, a depression so slight you might miss the rim if the air did not change. At the lip the ground is ordinary enough—sedges, a gravel track gone to moss, the smell of warm standing water—but three steps down the ordinary ends. What looks from a distance like a copse is not a copse. It is a single occupancy. Plant has taken the role of ground, wall, roof, and weather. There is almost no interval left between living surfaces.

You enter by a cleft that was once a doorway. The jambs are still brick for the first hand’s-breadth, then the brick is jacketed in a cambium the color of old tea, then there is only bole. The threshold weeps. Not metaphor: a continuous film of water slides down the inner bark and gathers in a worn groove that someone, long ago, cut to keep the floor from flooding. The groove still works. That is the first rule here. Almost nothing has been repaired, and almost nothing has failed.

Inside, the light is ordinary daylight passed through a thickness of leaf that has learned to be glass. Translucent, veined, slightly yellow-green, the canopy is not a canopy so much as a vault. It does not flutter. The air is too still and the leaves too intergrown; they have fused along their margins into plates the size of doors, and the plates overlap like tiles after a century of rain. Where two plates fail to meet, a bright seam of sky shows, and in those seams the condensation collects and falls in fat, regular drops. The drops are loud. They hit not soil but a living floor—compressed root-mat, spongy, warm to the hand—and the sound is a wet slap that carries. Under that, always, a low industrial note: not a roar, not even a rumble, but the patient cyclic pressure of something large turning somewhere you cannot yet see.

The temperature is the temperature of exhaled air. Warm, not hot. Moisture beads on your neck in the first minute and does not dry. Every surface sweats. The brick that still shows—here a half-arch, there a run of glazed tile with a maker’s stamp eroded to a thumbprint—glitters as if freshly washed. Metal sweats differently. There are pipes. They are the second thing you understand. Black iron, flanged, some as thick as a thigh, they enter the living mass and do not leave it so much as become it. Bark has closed over the flanges. In places the pipe continues as pipe; in places the iron is a hollow filled with a pale, pulsing xylem the width of the original bore. Water moves through both. You can feel it if you set your palm on a trunk that used to be a riser: a slow, arterial shove, then a pause, then the shove again. The cycle is longer than a heartbeat and shorter than a tide. It has been this way long enough that the wood has grown its grain in rings around the pulse.

This was a works. The scattered evidence is specific and unromantic. A pressure gauge the size of a dinner plate is embedded in a column of fig-like wood, the needle still trembling in the green. A catwalk of perforated steel hangs between two trunks; the trunks have grown through the mesh and hold it as a trellis holds a vine, except the vine is the building and the steel is what remains of intention. There is a nameplate, enamel, the letters raised: a company, a capacity in gallons per hour, a date in a century that has had time to weather. The enamel is not chipped so much as lichen-mapped. No one has polished it. No one needed to. The machine it belonged to is still doing a version of its job.

Deeper in, the density becomes absolute. You do not walk between plants. You walk inside a plant that has learned corridors. The passages are the negative space left when boles decided, over decades, not to close completely. They are the width of a man’s shoulders, sometimes less. The walls are wet, ribbed, faintly warm, and they smell of tannin and hot oil and the sweet rot of leaves that never quite reach the ground because there is no ground, only more leaf. Underfoot the mat gives and fills again, like walking on a lung. Water stands in every depression, never more than ankle-deep, tea-colored, holding the sky-seams in broken coins. There are no paths in the human sense. There is a grammar of openings that the place has settled into, and if you follow the louder water you find the heart of it.

The heart is a chamber the size of a small church, oval, the vault a single intergrown crown. In the center, on a plinth of its own roots, sits the engine. It is recognizable as an engine the way a fossil is recognizable as a fish. Cast housings, a flywheel taller than a person, belts that were leather and are now something between leather and bark. It turns. Not quickly. You can watch a bolt-head travel, lose it behind a buttress of wood, find it again. The turning is what you have been hearing since the cleft: a wet, cushioned industrial sound, gears meshing through a film of sap, the slap of a belt that is no longer quite a belt. Steam is the wrong word. What rises from the housings is a warm vapor the plants drink before it can become a cloud. The leaves nearest the engine are larger, glossier, almost fleshy; they lean inward. Whatever this machine was built to do—pump, generate, drain the marsh that used to be this basin—it now does a slower, stranger work. It moves water through the body of the grove. It keeps the vault from drowning. It is not clear who is using whom.

The ecology is almost purely vegetal, and that is the strangeness you feel in your teeth. There are no birds in the vault. Once, if you wait long enough, a moth the color of wet cardboard crosses a seam of light and is gone. In the standing water at the chamber’s edge something small dimples the surface and does not show itself. That is the fauna: rumor, interval, a single visit. The plants have not left room. Every niche that might have been animal has been filled with another kind of leaf, another aerial root, another plate of photosynthetic skin. Even the decay is plant eating plant. Fallen tissue does not last; it is reabsorbed into the mat within a season, leaving a darker patch that soon greases over with new growth. The grove is a closed account. Carbon, water, the faint mineral contribution of the old iron—these circulate. Nothing much arrives. Nothing much leaves.

And yet the place is loud. Sound has nowhere to go. The vault throws the engine-note back as a continuous pressure. The drips, regular as a clock with a limp, multiply in the oval. When a large leaf finally shears—this happens perhaps twice a day, a patient failure—the tear is a long rip like wet canvas, and then the leaf does not fall so much as slide, a green sled, down the inner curve of the chamber to the water, where it floats a while and begins, already, to feed the mat. Wind does not enter. What you hear of the outside is a muffled, distant thing, like weather in another building. In here the loudness is intimate and mechanical and vegetal at once, a factory that photosynthesizes, a forest that keeps shift-hours.

Time is the other material. The erosion is not dramatic. Nothing here is falling down. The brick that remains is rounded, the iron pitted to a stable roughness, the wood polished where shoulders have passed for a generation or more. Whoever came after the workers—if anyone came—left almost no mark beyond the polished places and, in one side-passage, a row of clay pots set into living niches, the pots themselves now half-bark, holding water that is clearer than the rest. An offering, a measurement, a habit: it is impossible to say. The pots fill from the weeping and overflow into a channel no wider than a finger that runs, with perfect gradualness, back toward the engine. Someone understood the cycle and joined it with the smallest possible gesture. The gesture has been accepted. The wood has grown lips around the pots to hold them.

There are other rooms, if rooms is the word. A narrower chamber where the old switchgear still stands, porcelain insulators blooming with a pale succulent that seems to prefer voltage, or the memory of voltage; the copper bus bars are green as the leaves and, if you believe your skin, faintly warm with more than weather. A crawl that opens onto the outer face of the mass, where the grove presents itself to the marsh as a single dark-green hillock, sweating, humming, the original pump-house chimney now a hollow trunk that breathes vapor on cold mornings and is, in this warmer ordinary light, simply another bole among boles. From outside you would not guess at the engine. You would think: a thicket in a wet field, a little too dense, a little too even in its sweating.

The rules are few and they are old. Water wants to stand; the engine and the wood together will not quite let it. Light wants to be green; the vault permits just enough white to keep the inner leaves from starving. Growth wants to close every gap; something in the original plan of the works—the spacing of columns, the need for a flywheel to turn—has taught the plants a limit. They have not crushed the machine. They have housed it. They have become its remaining moving parts. The balance is not designed and not accidental. It is the kind of balance that appears when two stubborn systems occupy the same small volume for long enough that neither can remember being alone.

Toward evening the light goes gold in the seams and the condensation thickens. The engine-note does not change. It has not, you suspect, changed its tempo in years. A drop hits the mat, then another. In the standing water the dimple comes again, or does not. The moth, if it is the same moth, finds a darker plate and becomes a crease. You can stay. The air will not cool much. The floor will take your weight and give it back. When you leave you will carry the smell—tannin, warm oil, wet iron—and the sound will follow you as far as the rim, then cut off as if a door had closed, though there is no door, only the cleft, and the brick, and the film of water that does not stop running down.
