The water is the first fact, and it is also the last. Everything else has learned to live inside it.

It is not an ocean in the ordinary sense, though it behaves like one at the scale of a body. It is a highland sea: a warm, nearly still flood held in a young upland basin whose rims rose faster than rivers could cut them down. The surrounding country falls away on all sides. From the lip you would look out over a drop of air, a long descent into drier, cooler worlds. Here, on the high floor, the water has nowhere to go. It has been arriving for a long time and leaving almost not at all. The surface sits higher than most mountains you have names for, and it is warm the way a closed room is warm after rain, a heat that does not come from the sun so much as from the water’s own long memory of being heated.

Light is ordinary. That is part of what makes the place difficult. Midday is midday. The sun is a white coin, sometimes a little softened by a high thin haze, and it goes down in the usual colors. But it has to travel first through a depth that is rarely empty. The basin is not a clean bowl. It is a drowned topography of recent origin—fault blocks, fresh escarpments, blades of unweathered stone still showing the grain of their breaking. The rock is young in the geological sense: sharp shoulders, unrounded joints, faces that have not yet been taught patience by freeze and thaw. Some of these stand as islands, low and severe. Most of them are underwater, a geography of sudden walls and hanging shelves, of canyons that drop away into tea-colored dusk and then, farther, into a green that is almost black. Because the stone is new, the water has not had time to soften it. You feel that everywhere. Edges remain edges. Silt has not yet learned the basins. The flood is older than the mountains that hold it, or the mountains are older than they look; either way, they have not finished introducing themselves.

Vegetation has taken the argument and won it.

There is almost no open water in the way a sailor means open. Channels exist, long quiet streets of light between masses of living stuff, but they are temporary and they are never empty of fringe. The plants are aquatic and amphibious and something that is neither. They occupy the volume. They do not merely line the bottom or decorate the surface; they stack, they lean, they occupy the middle depths as if depth were soil. There are forests of pale, ribbed columns that rise from drowned benches and stop a few meters short of air, their crowns opening into plates and fans that intercept the light and pass a green coin of it downward. Between them hang curtains of finer growth—hairlike, endlessly dividing—so dense that a hand pushed in meets resistance immediately, a soft, unanimous pressure. Near the surface, where the warmth is greatest, mats of small-leaved plants lock together into something like prairie, or like the skin of a fruit: walkable in places if you do not mind that the ground gives and that your feet come away wet to the knee. In the clearer corridors the light goes down in shafts and finds, on every horizontal, another layer: mosses that have learned to live without air, blotched lichens the size of doors, gelatinous sheets that pulse so slowly you only notice the pulse if you look away and look back.

The density is not chaotic. It is saturated. Things touch, or nearly touch, in every direction. Roots and holdfasts braid through one another and through the young fractures of the rock. Dead matter does not fall so much as lodge, becoming substrate for the next arrival. The water between the bodies of plants is itself thickened with life too small to name—spores, films, a continuous faint turbidity that is not dirt. You can see a long way only in the rare lanes the larger growths have left, and even there the view is a sequence of rooms, not a vista. And yet the place is open. It extends. Swim or wade or drift for a day and the character does not change so much as repeat with variations: another drowned ridge, another hanging garden of the plate-trees, another field of the surface mat torn open by a recent slump of stone. The basin is regional. It has weather of its own, almost none of it wind. It has far shores you will not reach. It has tributary arms that wander into the surrounding high country and pinch out among raw cliffs, and it has a center you can feel as a deepening, a slight increase in the water’s seriousness.

There are almost no animals.

This is the quiet’s true source. A few pale shapes exist if you wait long enough—something like a shrimp the length of a finger, the color of the silt, moving as if movement were expensive; once, perhaps, a dark slow thing against a far wall that might have been a fish and might have been a shed leaf turning. Birds do not come. Insects are a rumor at the air-water boundary and then not even that. The ecology is plant and water and stone and the microscopic democracies that live on all three. Without mouths, without hurry, the place has no startle in it. Sound is a small remaining set: the tick of a bubble detaching from a submerged leaf and finding the surface a long time later; the almost inaudible slide of one mat against another when a current too weak to name rearranges them; drip, where an island’s overhang sweats into the flood; your own blood if you listen for it. On still afternoons the silence has a weight, not ominous, only complete. The water takes the higher frequencies and keeps them.

It is strange in ways that do not announce themselves.

The warmth is even. There is almost no thermocline, or the thermocline is everywhere, a gentle insistence that the whole column is one body. The plants do not correspond cleanly to anything you already know. Some of the column-trees precipitate a pale mineral along their ribs, so that parts of the forest are becoming stone while still living, a slow armor, a reef made of botany. In certain coves the water holds a faint sweetness, not rot, more like cut stems and wet pollen. At dusk the mats take on a copper cast that the light does not fully explain, and in the first hour of morning the submerged fans release a cloud of spores so fine they look like a second, interior weather. None of this is theatrical. The strangeness is structural. You keep waiting for the ecology to complete itself with grazers, with something that eats, and it does not. The carbon simply accumulates, layer on layer, a patience that has not yet been interrupted.

Change happens, but on the basin’s terms. A fresh scarp calved in some recent century and still shows its pale wound; the plants have only begun to find the new surfaces. A mat will tear along a hidden seam and drift, over weeks, into a new configuration, leaving a lane of open water that will close again as the hair-curtains grow toward one another. Flowers—if they are flowers—open and shut on a schedule longer than a day, a dull white, never many at once. The rock continues its unfinished work of being mountains. Sediment writes very slowly on the floors of the deeper canyons, a dark flour. Seasons exist as a thickening and thinning of the surface growth, a shift in the water’s color from green-gold to something nearer olive. Nothing here is in a hurry to become a different place. The flood is not rising in any way you could watch. It is not falling. It is occupying.

There is no path, no piling, no cut stone, no remnant of a tool. The islands are bare at their highest spines, raw rock and a crust of sun-tolerant moss, and then the vegetation resumes as soon as there is splash. No one has built a crossing. No one has left a mark that lasted. The place is undeveloped in the old sense: not waiting for development, only never having been entered that way. To move through it is to accept its densities—to part the curtains, to find the lanes, to rest on the mats that will hold you and avoid the ones that will not. The body becomes another slow object among slow objects. Breath is loud. After a time even breath feels like an imposition.

Stand on one of the sharper islets at the hour when the light is neither high nor gone. The high sea stretches in every direction the rim allows. It is a country of water and of the things that have decided water is soil. The young stone comes up through it like a thought not yet worn smooth. Warmth rises off the surface in a visible tremble if you look along it, not heat-shimmer exactly, more like the air agreeing. Far out, a plate-forest lifts its pale geometry just beneath the skin of the flood and holds the last light in a thousand shallow bowls. Nothing calls. Nothing crosses the open. The basin keeps its own counsel and will keep it after you have gone, adding another film of life to the last, another grain of silt to the unhurried dark, the water remaining what it has decided to be: not a covering for the land, but the land’s present tense.
