The basin is no larger than a village green and older than any village. Whatever mountain or dome once stood here has been worn down past memory of height; what remains is a shallow dish of stone so long rounded that it no longer has a rim, only a soft darkening of the ground where the water prefers to stand. You can see the whole of it from any point inside. The light is high and white-gold and does not cast useful shadow. It lies on the water like a second skin.

The water is not deep. It is everywhere. It films the stone, beads on every stem, hangs in the warm air as a fine continual condensing that never quite becomes rain and never quite stops. When you walk, the ground gives a little and then weeps into your print and then forgets you. There are no true banks—only places where the film thickens into sheets, and sheets into quiet pools no deeper than a forearm, and then thins again. The stone beneath is the color of old teeth and has no corners left.

Vegetation occurs as isolated events. A clump of something like rushes, but thicker, succulent, the color of wet jade, stands ten paces from the next clump, and between them there is only bright water and the flat worn rock. The plants are lush where they are—leaves so full they look ready to split, flowers that do not so much bloom as overfill—but they do not join. They keep the distances of furniture in a large unused room. Their roots, when you see them at the edges of a pool, are pale and numerous and seem to drink more than they need.

The fauna is what the place is for.

There are not many of them. That is the first correction the eye has to make. You count, over an hour, perhaps seven distinct bodies large enough to have weight. They are overwhelming anyway. Each one occupies attention the way a cathedral occupies a square. They are not the same species, or they are one species that has forgotten how to stay one shape. The nearest is a long, low animal the length of a fishing boat, lying half in a sheet of water, half on stone, the color of the stone itself until it breathes and a slow wave of warmer gold moves under the skin. It has no visible eyes. Its mouth, when it opens to take in the film of water and the things living in the film, is a clean dark oval that makes no sound at all. Another, farther off, is upright and thinner and stands among the jade rushes without touching them, as if the plants and the animal had long ago agreed on clearances. A third is only visible as a continual slight rearrangement of the brightest water, a presence that refuses outline.

They do not call. Nothing here calls. The air is so quiet that you can hear the water deciding to become mist and the mist deciding to become water again—a faint incessant ticking at the edge of hearing, like cooling metal, except warm. The animals communicate, if they communicate, through the water table. When the long one shifts, every pool in the basin answers with a single delayed pulse, and the upright one turns its featureless head a few degrees, and something under the stone—something that might be more of them, or the roots, or the last working part of whatever was built here—answers later still. The whole dish is a single slow instrument.

What was built is scattered the way the plants are scattered: a few objects that have not agreed to become geology. Near the center, a ring of material that is not stone stands a handspan above the water. It does not wet. Insects (there are insects; they are small, metallic, and they do not fly so much as hang and then relocate) refuse to land on it. The ring is the temperature of the air exactly, always, and if you put your ear near it you hear less than silence—an active subtraction of sound. Another remnant is half-sunk at the western fade of the basin: a short upright slab with no tool marks and no erosion, through which the bright light passes as if the slab were a thickness of the light itself. Behind it the world is slightly delayed. A third thing might be a structure or might be an animal that has not moved in a very long time; moss that is not moss beads on it and never dries.

The engineering, whatever it was, has outlasted the need to look like engineering. It keeps the light even. It keeps the sound down. It keeps the water from ever quite leaving and never quite flooding. It may be what flattened the last of the old height, or it may simply have waited until the height was gone and then made itself at home in the leftover dish. The physics of it are local and slightly wrong. Distances inside the basin do not add quite the way they should; walking from the ring to the slab takes longer than walking back. The animals do not appear to notice. They were either made for this, or they grew into it the way the rushes grew into the wet.

Processes continue. The mist cycle tightens toward what would be afternoon if the light ever changed angle, and the water on the stone draws itself into tighter beads, and the long animal opens its oval mouth more often. Small lives—larval, translucent, more numerous than the large ones by tens of thousands—rise to the surface film and are taken, or are not taken and sink again to do whatever they do in the warm dark just under the rock. The upright one sheds a skin that does not look like a skin. It floats, iridescent, and then the water takes it apart into nutrients so fine the rushes lean toward them. Predator and prey are the same conversation held at different speeds. Nothing here is in a hurry except the light, which is always arriving too completely.

At the vague edge of the basin the world becomes ordinary again by degrees: the water thins to damp, the stone remembers it once had texture, the silence develops holes through which a distant wind can be inferred. Inside, the rules hold. The place has settled into a final form that is still moving. It is not dying. It is what is left when dying has already been finished with and something quieter and brighter and more thoroughly inhabited has been allowed to continue. The animals will still be here when you look back from the edge. They will not have changed position much. The water will have rewritten your footprints and then rewritten the rewriting. The ring will still be taking sound out of the air. The light will still be too much, and warm, and wet on the skin like a hand that does not quite touch.
