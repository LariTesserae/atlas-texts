The highland does not end. It has been lifted so long and worn so evenly that “mountain” is the wrong word; what remains is a single continuous upland the size of a weather system, its own slow climate crouched upon the world’s shoulder. Every ridge is a faded replica of the last. Every shallow dish between them holds water because the rock will not let the water leave.

The stone weeps. It has been weeping since the last ice withdrew and never learned another habit. Moisture beads on every exposed face, gathers in the hairline joints, then issues in patient threads that never quite become streams. The threads run across lichen the colour of wet slate, disappear into cushions of sphagnum, reappear lower down as if the hill itself were a saturated sponge being gently squeezed by its own weight. After rain—and rain is only a thickening of what is already happening—the weeping becomes a glaze. Surfaces shine without glare. The air is permanently at the point of condensing; walk fifty paces and your hair and the insides of your sleeves are beaded.

Nothing is dry and almost nothing is empty. Vegetation occupies every attitude the land offers. Trees stand so close their canopies interleave; their trunks are not bark so much as vertical bogs, layered with liverwort, filmy fern, and the slow architecture of moss that has been growing in the same cool twilight for centuries. Between the trunks the understorey is a single packed organism: sword ferns, giant woodwardias, pale umbellifers whose stems are half water, and a thousand lesser green things that have no names because no one has ever needed to distinguish them. Epiphytes load every branch until the branches themselves sag and drip. There is no gap a hand can pass through without touching living tissue. Even the air is occupied—drifting veils of cobweb and seed-down, clouds of midges that do not bite so much as cling, the occasional larger moth whose wings are permanently darkened by moisture.

The ground is not soil. It is a saturated, springing mat of root, peat, and collapsed moss several metres deep. Step and the water comes up around your boot in a dark circle that takes a long time to fade. Depressions are marshes; slopes are marshes that happen to tilt. In the flatter bowls the water stands openly, tea-coloured, filmed with pollen and the shed skins of insects. These pools are connected by channels no wider than a stride, moving so slowly the surface tension is visible. The whole upland is a single hydrological body, water table at or above the surface, circulating with the unhurried pulse of something vast and old.

Light arrives as it should: a pale, even daylight filtered through high cloud and the perpetual low mist. Colours remain true—every green distinct, the rust of dying fronds, the sudden white of a bog orchid. There is no drama of shaft or shadow, only a soft, workable illumination that makes wet surfaces gleam and the undersides of leaves glow a quieter green. Night, when it comes, is simply the same world with the light turned down; the dripping continues, the fauna continues.

The fauna is everywhere and almost silent. Amphibians occupy every film of water: thumb-sized frogs the colour of wet bark, larger ones that hang motionless from moss like misplaced fruit, salamanders that move only when the drip-line shifts. Insects are a constant fine rain of bodies, their sound absorbed before it forms. Birds exist as sudden, low movements in the mid-storey—short flights, wet feathers, no song. Larger animals pass like weather. You become aware of them first as a change in the dripping, a momentary interruption, then the dark shape of something deer-sized easing between trunks, coat permanently darkened, hooves sinking and rising with small, swallowed sounds. Predators are the same: they wait in the density rather than chase. Everything here has learned that motion is expensive and noise is wasted.

The quiet is structural. Waterlogged biomass eats vibration. Mist eats the rest. What remains is individual: a single drop striking a leaf, the faint suck of a hoof leaving peat, the almost inaudible click of an insect adjusting its wings. Sometimes, if you stand still long enough, a deeper note becomes perceptible—not quite sound, more a pressure. It is the movement of the water itself through the buried network of joints and solution channels, a slow hydraulic engine the size of the highland. The rock is riddled with these passages; they fill and empty on cycles longer than weather, lifting the surface seeps, dropping them, lifting them again. The land breathes water. That is as close as the place comes to industry: a vast, patient, unowned machinery of circulation, producing nothing but more of itself, scaled beyond any purpose except continuance.

Erosion finished its loud work ages ago. What remains is adjustment. A saturated bank slumps once in a generation and is immediately clothed again. A tree leans until its mossy crown touches the next, then stays. The forms are settled. The highland does not change so much as continue, cool and packed and dripping, large enough to make its own drizzle, its own stillness, its own green dusk at noon. You could walk for a month and the only difference would be which particular curtain of moss you parted, which particular dark pool reflected the same pale sky. The place has no centre and no edge that matters. It is simply the condition of being high, wet, and finished.
