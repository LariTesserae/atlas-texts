The high country has not finished arriving. It is still being lifted—plate against plate, a slow industrial shove you cannot hear except as a pressure in the molars, a distant working that never quite becomes thunder. What stands here is not yet mountain in the finished sense. It is a raw, unfinished table of rock pushed into the thin air, its edges still sharp, its faces still wet from the inside.

Water is everywhere and almost nowhere. It does not run in rivers because the rivers have not been given time to invent themselves. Instead it weeps. The stone sweats. Thin sheets of melt and rain and condensed cloud slide across unweathered faces and vanish into joints that have only just opened. In the hollows—broad, shallow, undrained—water stands in dark, cold skins no deeper than a calf. These are not lakes. They are delays. Marsh-edge without a marsh: algae-black shallows, a skin of ice that forms and unforms in the same hour, a faint mineral smell like wet iron and old snow. The air itself is a condensing thing. Breath hangs. Cloud does not pass so much as catch and shred and re-form on the rising land, so that the light is always the color of a bruise just beginning to fade. Dim, even at what must be midday. No shadows with edges.

Vegetation has barely begun the argument. Lichen in iron-orange and dull sage clings to the newest fractures. In the wetter benches, a low, wiry grass—more wire than grass—holds in scattered tufts, each tuft a small independent republic with meters of bare gravel between. Moss, black-green, in the seeps. That is nearly all. The rest is stone and water and the spaces between.

And yet the place is not empty of life. It is empty of *stuff*, of clutter, of the mid-distance furniture other landscapes provide. Between one presence and the next there is a great deal of cold air. The fauna use that air. They arrive in quantities that would crowd a valley; here they only punctuate. Herds of something thick-coated and low-slung—call them goats if you need a word, though the horns are wrong and the gait is more like a machine finding purchase—move in loose constellations across the benches, always at a distance that makes them look like a single slow organism coming apart. Birds in numbers that do not match the forage: dark, compact, riding the updrafts that peel off the still-warm rock. They do not sing. They click. In the standing water, life is denser and more secret—clouds of something larval that dimple the surface, pale crustaceans the size of a thumbnail that turn over the silt in industrial patience. You hear them only if you kneel. Mostly you do not kneel. The cold comes up through the knees too fast.

There are no buildings. There has never been a building. What feels industrial is the land’s own work. Steam vents in ranks along a fault that has not decided whether it is a ridge or a wound. They do not roar; they breathe, a low, regular exhalation that could be a foundry heard through wool. The water that issues from them is hotter than the air has any right to allow, and it lays down pale terraces of mineral the way a factory lays down slag—thin, brittle, growing by the day. You can watch the edge of a terrace lengthen if you are willing to be still long enough for the cold to become a problem. Somewhere under the plate, heat is still being spent. The mountain is not a ruin of heat. It is a plant that has not yet been switched off.

Sound stays close to the ground. Drip. The small collapse of a skin of ice. Wind that has not gathered enough fetch to howl, only to hiss in the wire-grass. Once in a long while the deep working—a single, felt note, more pressure than noise—as another increment of rock is persuaded upward. The animals do not startle. They have been here long enough to treat uplift as weather.

The scale is a valley that has not been cut, a region that is all rim and no interior. You can see a long way and it does not help. Features repeat at intervals that feel designed and are not: another wet bench, another rank of vents, another loose herd far enough off that you cannot be sure they have faces. The emptiness is not desert emptiness. It is the emptiness of a floor that has been cleared for work that has only just begun. Everything here is mid-process. The drainage is embryonic. The peaks have not been given their names by weather. The light has not decided whether it is dawn or a storm that will not quite arrive.

Stand still and the place continues without you. Cloud tears and knits. A vent finds a new throat and the mineral terrace begins again a meter to the left. The herd reassembles around a seep you had not noticed. Ice forms on the lee of a stone and is gone. Nothing is finished. Nothing is old. The cold is the only thing that feels permanent, and even that is only the altitude’s opinion, waiting to be revised when the land finishes rising, or when it stops.
