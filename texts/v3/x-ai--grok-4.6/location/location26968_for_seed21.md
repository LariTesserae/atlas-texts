The lip is the first honest thing. You come up through a notch in pale, new rock that still holds the grain of its breaking, and the world drops a little, not far, only enough to make a bowl. The bowl is perhaps three hundred paces across and shallower than it looks. Its rim is a broken circlet of basalt and glass, edges unblunted, as if the mountain had been punched up last season and no weather worth the name has had time to argue with it. Heat comes off the stone in sheets. It is not the dry heat of desert plateaus. It is wet heat, close, a heat that has already decided what it will do with your clothes and your breath.

Inside the bowl the air is thicker than it has any right to be at this elevation. It condenses on the inner faces of the rim and runs. The rock weeps. Not metaphor: the basalt is beaded, always, and the beads fatten and find one another and become threads, and the threads become a dozen small falls that never quite commit to falling. They sheet, they hesitate, they reattach. Where they pool, the water is almost too hot to stand in and perfectly clear except at the edges, where a pale mineral crust is growing so fast you can watch the lace thicken if you are willing to be still.

There is no single source. The water is in the stone and in the air and in the machines.

Three of them. They stand at irregular points on the floor, not centered, not aligned with anything a surveyor would respect. Each is a pillar, taller than a house, narrower than a tower, of a smoked, translucent stuff veined with a dull copper light that does not travel the way light should. The veins brighten and dim on a cycle that is not quite regular. The pillars have no doors, no seams you can trust, no obvious work. They sweat. Condensate runs their flanks in sheets and collects in shallow rings at their bases, rings that are not quite circular, as if the ground itself were being persuaded into a shape it only half-remembers. The air immediately around each pillar is cooler by a degree or two and much wetter. You can see the humidity as a faint standing haze, a private weather.

Whatever they are doing is not on any schematic that belongs to this century. They appear to be folding moisture out of a larger atmospheric body than the bowl itself contains, or else they are borrowing from a layer of sky that is not the one you walked under to get here. The physics does not close. Heat should rise; here, some of it slides sideways along the floor in slow, visible pulses, like something being herded. Water should cut downslope; in two places a rill climbs a low ridge of sinter for a few body-lengths before remembering gravity and falling back. No one has written the rule. The pillars do not explain themselves.

Between the machines the ground is mostly open. Young stone, black and dove-gray, plated with new silica, cracked in polygons that still have sharp corners. The cracks hold a little water and a little green. Vegetation is lush where it is allowed to be and absent where it is not. Around the hotter pools: mats of a low, succulent moss the color of oxidized copper, fat-leaved, always wet, giving off a faint sweet mineral smell when the heat finds them. In the lee of the rim, where a thin fan of cooler air sometimes spills over, there are ferns with black stems and fronds so finely divided they look like smoke held in a shape. They grow in clumps, not a carpet. You can walk between them without brushing. A few shrubs, no taller than a person, hold clusters of white, waxy flowers that open only when a pillar’s copper veins brighten. The flowers do not face the sun. They face the nearest machine.

The rest is space. Heat-shimmer. The particular emptiness of a place that is finished enough to be itself and not yet old enough to have filled in.

Light is ordinary daylight, if daylight can be ordinary when it arrives through a thin, standing steam. Shadows are short and soft at the edges. Midday is brutal. Late afternoon the rim throws a hard blue shade across half the bowl and the water’s surface goes from white to the color of weak tea, and you can see, then, that some of the pools are deeper than they look: shafts, not basins, going down into a clarity that does not end where it should. Something pale moves in one of them, once, and does not come again. Fauna is rare. What lives here does not advertise.

There is a sound like held breath. The falls do not crash. They hiss, they tick, they make the small continuous noise of a kettle that will never quite boil. Stone ticks as it cools by a fraction and heats again. Once in a long while a new fracture opens in the rim with a single dry report, and a fresh sheet of water finds the break and begins the work of shining it. Wind does not enter properly. The bowl keeps its own air. When a gust does come over the lip it dies in the steam and leaves only a momentary colder stripe across the skin.

At the north side, half-swallowed by a lobe of new sinter, there is a low wall of fitted stone that is not the same age as the rest. Older, or else made of a patience the mountain has not yet learned. Three courses high, no mortar, a doorway without a door. Inside, only shade and a floor of packed ash and a single object: a shallow dish of the same smoked material as the pillars, dry, its inner surface filmed with a salt that tastes, if you are foolish, of iron and rain. Someone stayed. Someone left the dish. The wall is already being climbed by the copper moss.

The processes do not stop. The pillars cycle. The rills rewrite their own beds by a finger’s width between morning and dusk. Mineral lace advances across the black stone like a slow frost that has chosen the wrong temperature. The ferns lift and settle. Once, toward evening, a creature comes down the inner face of the rim: long, jointed, the color of wet basalt, too many limbs that do not all seem to touch at once. It drinks from a pool, or seems to, lowering a head that is mostly mouthparts and heat-sense, and then it goes back up the rock without using the obvious holds. It does not look at you. There may be others. The bowl does not feel hunted. It feels occupied by a timetable you are not on.

Rules, insofar as they can be felt and not spoken:

The heat is a condition, not a weather. It does not pass. You can leave it by climbing the notch; beyond the lip the highland air is merely warm and the wind is ordinary and the stone is older. Cross back and the wet heat takes you at the throat.

Do not put a hand on a pillar when the veins are bright. People have. The skin comes away cleanly, as if it had been persuaded it was finished with you.

Water taken from the climbing rills will not stay in an ordinary cup. It finds the rim and goes over. Water from the weeping walls is only water, almost too hot, tasting of rock and a faint sweetness. It will keep.

The deep pools show, if you look long enough, a sky that is not this one: a darker blue, two small moons, a high pale smear like a ring seen edge-on. The reflection does not ripple when the real sky’s clouds move. It has its own weather. No one has gone down far enough to say whether the other sky is a trick of the material or a place.

Night in the bowl is not dark so much as dim. The pillars hold a residual copper. The water holds the last of the day longer than it should. Sound thins further. You can hear your own blood, and the mineral growth, which is a sound like sugar burning at a great distance. The flowers close. The moss tightens. Something in the deep shafts knocks, once, softly, as if testing a door from the other side.

The mountain is still arriving. Tomorrow there may be a new crack, a new fall, a fourth pillar or the ruin of one. The bowl is small enough to know in an afternoon and strange enough that an afternoon is not a measurement it accepts. It is a room the high country made, or was made into, and it is busy with its own becoming, and it does not require you. It will weep and shine and hum whether you stay on the rim or climb down among the ferns and the rings of impossible water and stand, sweating, in the quiet, while the air condenses on the new stone and the copper light goes on doing whatever it is that current physics has not yet agreed to describe.
