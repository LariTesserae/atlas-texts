The heat does not come from above. That is the first thing the body understands, long before the eyes decide what they are looking at. It arrives from the ground and from the air at once, a single temperature with no gradient to walk toward or away from, as if the basin has only ever known one climate and intends to keep it. The light is the color of tea left too long in a cup: brown-gold, thin, already used. It does not fall so much as it soaks. There is a sky, somewhere, but the sky is a rumor conducted through steam.

What you stand on is not finished being ground. It has the give of bread taken from the oven a minute early—crust, then something still plastic underneath. In places it has begun to remember stone: pale ridges of something like mudstone, warm to the touch, that weep continuously along every joint. The water does not run so much as it appears, beading out of the half-made rock as if the rock were perspiring. You can press a thumb into a ledge and leave a print that will still be there, identical, if you come back a year from now. Nothing here is in a hurry to harden, and nothing is allowed to crumble. The land is locked in the hour of its own becoming.

Water sits near the surface everywhere, a second skin. There are no proper rivers. There are sheets, films, lenses of warm water held in shallow saucers of the unfinished clay, tea-dark, motionless except where something living shoulders through them. After what would be rain in another country—though rain here is less an event than a continuous negotiation between air and leaf—the whole basin shines as if oiled. The air itself is wet enough to drink if you walk with your mouth open. Condensation finds every cooler surface and fails, because there are no cooler surfaces. Drips form on fronds and on the undersides of stones and on the flanks of animals and they hang there, considering, and then they fall back into the same heat they came from.

Vegetation has taken the incompleteness of the ground as an invitation. It is not jungle in the ordinary sense; jungle implies a history of striving, of canopy wars already won. This is denser and less decided. Horsetails and tree-ferns and things that have not yet chosen whether they are shrub or reed occupy every register from the mud up to twice a person’s height, and then a second, slower architecture begins—broad leaves the size of doors, stacked so that walking is less travel than negotiation, a continuous turning of shoulders. Roots do not so much anchor as float in the wet sediment, a woven mattress. Where a trunk has found a slightly firmer welt of ground it stands as if surprised to be vertical. Nothing is old. Nothing is a seedling. Everything is the same mid-succession forever, a green that has reached its argument and stopped.

The basin is larger than a meadow and smaller than a country. You can walk a long morning and the character of the place will not change, only rearrange: a slightly deeper tarn of standing water, a hummock where the embryonic stone has shrugged a little higher and the ferns grow coarser, a corridor where steam vents from a crack and the plants lean away as if from a rumor. Features repeat at the scale of a town. There is always another sheet of water just past the next thicket, always another weeping ridge. The horizon is a suggestion made of vapor and stacked leaves. Open, in the way a crowded room is open if you can still see the far wall.

And the room is crowded.

Life here is not abundant so much as it is the medium. The air between the fronds is occupied. Things crawl the undersides of every leaf in such numbers that the leaves themselves seem to have a second, moving venation. The warm water holds so many bodies that the surface never quite stills—not ripples from wind (there is almost no wind) but the continuous adjustment of frogs, of something like newts the length of a forearm, of insects that skate and then are taken from below. The ground mattress of roots is a highway. You cannot put a hand down without touching something that was already there. Wings make a sound like rain that has learned to aim. The chorus is not birdsong exactly; it is a pressure, a mid-frequency roar of throats and stridulation and the wet slap of bodies that have always been arriving at the same places. Loud, but not chaotic. Loud the way a factory is loud when every machine has found its pitch and will not vary it.

The animals do not migrate. They do not thin in one season and thicken in another. There are no seasons. The same densities hold. A particular crossing of two fern-trunks will have, if you watch long enough to be sure, the same occupancy it had when you first noticed it: the same dark amphibian in the crotch, the same thread of ants (if they are ants) using the same wet highway, the same heavy, slow thing like a crocodilian that is not quite a crocodile half-submerged in the same tea-colored pane, only its eyes and the ridged suggestion of a back. When something is eaten—and things are eaten constantly; the air smells of chlorophyll and warm copper—the vacancy lasts less time than it takes to name. Replacement is not recovery. Replacement is identity. The basin does not remember losses.

Dimness is part of the treaty. Whatever sun exists beyond the steam has been reduced to a directionless glow, enough to photosynthesize by, not enough to cast a shadow with an edge. Colors run together: olive into rust into the pale gray of the sweating stone. Eyes here are large. Many of the overwhelming fauna do not bother with color; they are the color of wet bark, of mud, of the insides of leaves. You become aware of them by displacement, by the way a mass of green is slightly too thick, by heat that is a fraction warmer than the already intolerable air. At what would be night in another climate the dimness simply deepens by a shade, brown-gold to brown, and the sound does not drop. The frogs do not need darkness as a cue. They have never been given a different hour.

There are no paths that were made. There are only the places where so many bodies have passed that the root-mattress is smoother, the water a little more stirred, and even those traces do not persist as design. Nothing has been stacked. Nothing has been cut and left. The occasional fallen trunk—and they do fall, in the sense that a trunk will be horizontal where one was vertical, without the interval of falling ever quite being available to witness—is already a garden, already a bridge, already inhabited to saturation. The world has no tools and no need of them. Digestion and growth and the slow chemistry of warm wet stone are the only industries.

What is unusual is not any single creature. It is the fidelity. Stand still long enough and you begin to suspect that the basin is not living in time so much as it is displaying a single moment with infinite internal motion. The steam always hangs at the same height. The weeps on the half-stone always find the same channels. A particular call—high, then two notes lower, then a rattle like seeds in a gourd—arrives from the same quarter at what your body insists must be intervals, except the intervals do not accumulate into a day. You could leave and return and the place would not have the courtesy to have changed in your absence. The embryonic ground would still be embryonic. The heat would still have no source you could point to. The water would still be deciding whether to be air.

Walk out toward what you hope is an edge and the features continue their town-sized variations: a broader sheet of water where something very large maintains a presence so constant it has become a landmark, a slightly brighter glade where the canopy thins and the dimness lifts to the color of weak honey and the insects are a weather, a depression where the unfinished geology has slumped and the vegetation grows as if poured. You will not find a river leaving. You will not find a slope that commits to being a hill. Elevation here is a rumor of a few meters, enough for water to consider moving and then forget.

The rules are simple and they do not develop. Heat is total. Moisture is total. Life occupies every volume it can reach and then occupies the idea of volume. The ground will not finish. The light will not strengthen. Sound will not pause. If there is a geology still being born, its birth has been paused at the crowning, and the pause has lasted long enough to grow a world on the infant’s skin—lush, loud, dim, crowded past reason, and so perfectly balanced that balance has become a kind of stillness, a roar that does not travel, a place that is always almost a landscape and will not, for any witness, become one.
