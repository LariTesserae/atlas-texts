The pocket is not a cave. Caves are excused from authorship. This place has never been excused.

You reach it by a seam most people treat as a stain: a dark vertical wetness in the outer weather-skin of the high works, where the plateau-city’s rind thickens into its oldest pumping architecture. From the terrace the seam is nothing. A weeping. A fault in the patina. Step into it and the city drops away as if it had only ever been a lid.

Inside, the volume is smaller than a chapel and busier than a gut. Eight or nine meters across at the widest, the floor a shallow dish, the ceiling a series of overlapping throats. Every surface is made. There is no bedrock, no native stone, no soil that was not extruded, printed, grown as a membrane and then left to its centuries. The material has the color of old tooth and wet bronze. It takes fingerprints and then slowly swallows them. Where it has been wet longest it has gone the dull milky green of neglected copper, not as corrosion exactly, more as a decided finish: the structure completing a sentence it started when it was poured.

Water is the sentence.

It does not fall so much as arrive from every direction the walls remember. Capillary pipes no wider than hair open and close in slow peristalsis. Sheets of condensate gather on the undersides of the throats, fatten, and peel away in panes that hold their shape a half-second too long before they tear. That half-second is the only openly unusual thing here: standing waves in the chamber keep thin water standing in midair like poorly remembered glass. You can put a hand through them. They reseal with a sound like a cheek against a pillow, except nothing in this room is quiet enough for the comparison to survive.

The sound is the room’s true architecture.

A designed overflow, once, a pressure-relief for the high works’ weather engines: warm vapor forced down from the altitude intakes, wrung, rung, dumped. The makers tuned the throats. They have not been retuned. What was a controlled organ stop is now a continuous, physical blow. Water hitting water, water hitting worn ceramic, air slammed through necks that have narrowed with mineral bloom. It is not a roar you hear so much as a roar you become a cavity for. Conversation is a pantomime. Bone conduction carries more meaning than air. After a minute the noise stops being information and becomes climate: a warm, wet, total pressure on the skull, the way deep water is a pressure, except you can still breathe and the breathing itself is loud.

Dimness helps. There is no fixture. What light there is comes as a dilute green-gray seep through the same pores that weep, and as a slower, living phosphor from the things that live here. The phosphor is not decorative. It is waste. It collects in the standing sheets and makes them look briefly like windows onto a thinner, kinder place, then the sheets tear and the kindness goes with them.

Vegetation is an afterthought the room has almost rejected. A few rubbery, pale stalks occupy the highest driplines, each no taller than a forearm, leafless, more like exposed tendon than plant. They drink the mineral film and put out a sour, warm smell when bruised. Between them, a blackish velvet of something that might once have been moss and is now only a conductive lichen, patchy, unwilling. That is the entire flora. The rest of the density is animal, water, and the layered junk of a machine that has been digesting itself into habitat for longer than anyone local can name.

The animals use the noise the way fish use current.

Filter-moths, if moths had carapace and no interest in flame, hang in the standing sheets by the hundred, wings locked into the vibration, combs on their legs sieving protein out of the mist. When a sheet tears they drop a handspan, catch the next one, and resume. Their bodies tick. The ticking is inaudible until you press your skull to the wall, and then it is a second deafness underneath the first, a rain of small hard clocks.

In the dish of the floor, where the water finally agrees to be a pool a hand deep and blood-warm, things like glass eels stitch themselves through the shallows. They are not glass. They are the same printed family as the walls, gone soft and mobile, a sanctioned leftover of whatever self-repair swarm once kept the throats patent. They eat moth-waste and each other and the lichen’s slower edges. Their passing leaves temporary grooves in the silt—except the silt is not silt, it is powdered wall, a flour of worn metamaterial that sparkles once when a phosphor-sheet breaks above it and then goes dull again.

Along every ledge, in every pock the centuries of tuned water have chewed into the right-angles, click-crabs farm the green bloom. They are abundant to the point of tactility: you cannot set a palm down without finding a shell already there, warm, indifferent. They drum the substrate with a mouthpart evolved or designed for this exact resonance. The drumming does not carry as sound. It carries as a texture in the larger noise, a roughness, the way a scarf is still a scarf in a gale.

The temperature is the temperature of a mouth. High outside, thin, the plateau wind can cut; in here the weather engines’ waste heat has nowhere honest to go, so it loiters, sodden, a climate the size of a room. Breath condenses on the inner surface of your teeth. Clothes give up. The air is a second wet garment.

Nothing here is new and nothing is ruined. That is the established part, the long settled form. Channels that began as milled gutters are now river-stone curves. Boltheads, if they were ever boltheads, are nodules under glaze. The original grid of the print-layers shows only when a crab has cleared a patch, a faint hatching like very old paper. The place is in balance the way an old lock is in balance: water in, water out, life in the difference, the difference loud enough to be a kind of silence if you stay long enough to let your hearing quit.

The rules are local and unwritten and anyone who works the high rinds knows them without liking them.

Do not block a throat. The standing sheets will fail and the room will forget how to be a room for the length of a night, and the eels will come up the walls.

Do not take the crabs in quantity. They keep the bloom from sealing the pores. Seal the pores and the seam on the terrace stops weeping and someone far below misses a scheduled condensate and a district goes dry for an afternoon, which is how pocket-ecologies get noticed, which is how they get cleaned, which is how they end.

The moths are not edible in any kind way.

If the noise drops, leave. A drop means a valve upstream has thought a thought the makers did not finish programming. The next sound may be the whole pocket clearing its many throats at once.

Stay, and the place continues its one long activity: wringing a high, thin sky into a hidden, deafening wetness, feeding what can bear to live inside a machine’s oldest mouth, wearing itself smoother, never quite finishing, never quite natural, warm as blood and dim as a closed eye, a whole weather folded into a stain you could cover with two outstretched arms.
