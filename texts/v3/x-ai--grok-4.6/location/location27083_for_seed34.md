The first thing is not a skyline. It is the way the air holds water at this height: a cool film on the tongue, a constant almost-rain that never quite decides to fall. Breath leaves you already silver. Stone underfoot is never fully dry. You are high enough that the horizon sits strangely near and strangely far at once, a pale rim of weather rather than of land, and everything between here and that rim is built.

Not built recently. Built the way a reef is built, or a river terrace—added, abandoned, occupied again, worn into its own logic until construction and geology stopped arguing. Walls meet walls at angles that once meant streets. Those streets are now shallow courses. Water uses them. It has used them so long that the gutters are the architecture, the architecture the drainage. You walk in a city that has become a watershed, or a watershed that learned the grammar of courtyards.

The stone is dark where it is wet, which is almost everywhere: a dense, fine-grained stuff the color of old iron and old tea. Where a cornice still throws a little shelter, the face of a block dries to a paler gray and shows the original dressing marks, long strokes of some vanished chisel. Most surfaces have forgotten that. They are rounded at the arrises, stained in vertical maps of rust and lime, pocked where freeze and seepage have done their slow unmaking and then stopped, as if the weather and the masonry reached an agreement centuries ago. Nothing here is collapsing. Nothing here is new. The city is in the middle of its own long afternoon.

It goes on. That is the fact that keeps arriving. District after district, the same close grain of courts and lanes and stepped platforms, climbing and descending the slow rolls of a highland the size of a climate. From any tower—there are thousands, most of them only a few stories, a few of them loftier, all of them truncated or roofless or wearing a last course of open sky—you see not an edge but a continuation. Roofs like wet slate. Lightwells like throats. Avenues that run for a day’s walk and then dogleg for reasons no one living remembers, if anyone lives. The scale is continental. Weather has neighborhoods. A rain that is only mist in the upper terraces becomes a true falling in the lower ones, three hundred feet down and ten miles west, where the air is fractionally warmer and the stone weeps in earnest, whole façades lacquered and running.

Vegetation is an afterthought the water permits. Moss in the seams, black-green, saturated, so soft it seems like a second pointing between the blocks. Algae in the perpetual seep-lines, olive and rust. In a few courts that catch a little more light, a shrub has rooted in a crack the width of a finger and grown into a lean, wind-combed thing no taller than a person, leaves small and waxed against the cool. That is almost the whole flora. The city does not have parks. It has damp. What would have been soil was long since washed into the channels and laid down again as a thin gray silt in the bottoms of cisterns.

The cisterns are everywhere, because water is the city’s remaining occupation. Roof-cut basins overflow in threads. Intramural tanks, once capped, now open, hold rain the color of weak tea and the temperature of a cellar. Below the streets—always below, because this place thinks in layers—there are vaults that sound when you pass over them, a dull fullness, stone drums half full. The water is near the surface even when you cannot see it. Press a palm to a wall and it comes away cold and clean. In the deepest cuttings, where avenues were carved down through older fabric, the rock itself sweats. Mineral crusts grow there like pale lichen, ring after ring, the city’s second masonry, deposited from the inside out.

Sound is a luxury the height and the wet both refuse. Rain, when it commits, is a hiss so even it becomes a kind of silence. Drip has a rank and a schedule: the same eave, the same interval, for years. Wind moves through empty windows and does not howl; it is too tired, or the apertures are too many, and the air only trades places with itself. Once in a long while something larger happens—a sheet of water detaching from a high cornice, a distant collapse of overflow from one terrace to the next—and the report travels the lanes like a single word and is gone. You hear your own coat. You hear the tiny tick of grit under your heel. That is most of the acoustic world.

Life other than moss is scarce and shy of the open. A bird the color of the wet stone drops from a lintel, is a brief dark mark against the normal daylight, and is gone into a drain-mouth. In one standing pool, under a skin of pollen that cannot have come from near here, something small dimples the surface and then thinks better of it. Tracks in the silt of a sunken forum might be a fox’s, or the memory of a fox. The city could feed more than this. It does not. Whatever equilibrium it found included emptiness as a load-bearing part.

Daylight is ordinary and therefore slightly shocking: a true sun, when the cloud thins, throwing real shadows of merlons and broken balustrades, the shadows moving as they should, hour by hour. Most days the light is white and even, the sky a low milk. Evening comes as a blueing of the puddles first. Night is not dark so much as specific. Every wet surface keeps a little of the last light and gives it back in the wrong direction. You can walk by the gleam of the stone itself.

The activity is hydrologic, and it never clocks out. Fronts drag themselves across the plateau and wring out according to the city’s own relief—here a sudden curtain in a district of taller stacks, there a hours-long drizzle trapped in a basin of older, lower building. Water climbs the walls by capillary and descends them by gravity and the two motions pass through each other without comment. A plaza that was dry at dawn is a sheet by midafternoon, ankle-deep, wind-textured, reflecting a second city that is only cornice and sky. By dusk the sheet has found the old outlets, the carved throats at the downhill lip, and gone, leaving a dark watermark and a few stranded leaves of that one permitted shrub. Tomorrow the watermark will be a little higher or a little lower. The city is not flooding. The city is breathing water, in and out, at the pace of weather.

Under that breath the structure holds. Foundations have settled into their final seats. Arches that were going to fail have failed already, long ago, and the rubble has been arranged by rain into new, lower thresholds. What remains is load-path and habit. You can feel, walking, that the place is not a ruin in the romantic sense. It is a finished form that includes its damage, the way a mountain includes its scree. Established. In-balance. The erosion is no longer an event; it is a climate.

And still it is dense. That is the other fact that does not get smaller. Wall to wall, court inside court, stair folded against stair, the built thing occupies almost all the volume it was given. You are rarely more than an arm’s reach from masonry. Alleys pinch until the sky is a wet ribbon. Rooms open into rooms that were never meant to be passed through this way, their doors gone, their uses erased, only the fact of enclosure left. Stacked construction: a later platform poured or laid across an earlier roof, then another, then a channel cut through all three. The city is thick with itself. At night, if you stand in a deep well of walls and look up, the stacked silhouettes make a geology of rectangles, and the mist moves through them like a slow thought.

No tool left behind. No metal that was not already oxide. No glass. Whatever hands fitted these blocks did their work and ceased, and the work remained as stone remains. There is no mechanism here, no hinge that still turns, no pipe that was not a carved runnel. The urban is mineral and old and sufficient. Infrastructure is gravity, and slope, and the memory of intention in the way a street still aims at a gate that is now only two worn jambs and a view of more city.

Far out—always farther, because the scale refuses a last district—the same thing continues under a different weather. A colder shelf where the seep has become ice in the deep shade of north walls, black and clear, re-melting each afternoon into the same stains. A broader, flatter quarter where the buildings are lower and the water stands in genuine lagoons between them, still, reflecting, the city as its own marsh-edge, every threshold a reedless shore. A high spine of construction along a ridge, towers in a line like a broken aqueduct that forgot the water and became the water’s source, cloud tearing on them, their western faces permanently dark with run-off.

You could walk a month and change climates without leaving the masonry. That is what the numbers asked for, and what the place became: a highland of organized stone, wet to the grain, cool, quiet, nearly empty of flesh, dense as a packed chest, vast enough to make its own rain, and busy only in the way a coast is busy—water arriving, water leaving, stone keeping the score in mineral and stain. The light is just light. The rules are weather and weight. Nothing here is trying to be strange. It is only very large, and very finished, and unable, at last, to get dry.
