The low country has no name because nothing has ever walked it long enough to need one. It is a single living lowland the size of a climate, a freshly risen continent of tissue that finished arriving and then refused to become anything else.

You would call it a marsh if a marsh could stand up and hold its own weather. Water is everywhere and almost nowhere: not rivers, not lakes, but a continuous near-surface wetness, a film and a seep and a held breath. The air itself is a cooled condensate, a warm dark dew that never quite falls and never quite lifts. Every surface weeps. Every hollow holds a still, black lens. The water tastes faintly of iron and sugar and something solvent, as if the ground were always in the middle of a reaction it will never finish.

There is no soil. The substrate is plant. Root, bole, rhizome, blade, bladder, buttress—every grammar of growth stacked and intergrown until the distinction between organism and landform becomes a courtesy. Trunks rise not as trees but as columns of living pipe, their interiors chambered, their walls thick with cambium that looks poured rather than grown. Between them the space is filled, not crowded: hanging curtains of vascular tissue, shelves of photosynthetic (or rather chemosynthetic) plate, palisades of young, unblunted leaf like black glass. The leaf-edges are still sharp. Nothing has had time, or permission, to round them. This is a young country locked at the moment of its first hardness.

It is dark the way a closed mouth is dark. No sun reaches here; perhaps none was ever offered. What little radiance exists is chemical and residual—faint foxfire in the seams, a slow phosphor in the dew, a dull heat-glow in the thicker trunks where exothermic synthesis never quite stops. You see by that, or you do not see. Distances collapse. A gallery that feels intimate opens, after an hour of walking that is not walking so much as threading, into a nave the size of a weather system, then closes again into a throat. The scale is continental and also cellular. Weather happens inside the body of the place: standing fogs that occupy whole provinces of canopy, pressure differentials between one vascular district and another, a warmth that is even, tropical, unvarying, like the interior of a working lung that has decided never to exhale.

Nothing animal lives here. The silence is not the silence of absence so much as the silence of a process that has no moving parts left to hear. Sap does not tick. There is no drip with a rhythm you could count; the water is already where it means to be, beaded, filmed, pooled, perfectly allocated. Once in a great while—if “while” means anything—a single bead detaches and travels a stem with a sound like a soft consonant spoken in another room. That is the loudest thing the country knows.

And yet it is industrial in the way a liver is industrial, or a blast furnace that grew instead of being riveted. The architecture is production. Chambers the size of valleys hold enzyme baths the color of old brass and midnight. Conduits thick as river-meanders carry feedstock—minerals rasped from the young stone beneath, dissolved, fractionated, recast as lignin, wax, alkaloid, structural sugar. Heat exchangers of living wood dump warmth into the condensing air so evenly that no gradient survives. There are cooling towers that are fruiting bodies, stacks that are hollowed inflorescences never meant to open, settling tanks that are simply the geometry of certain trunks. No one built any of it. No tool-mark, no joint, no artifact. The factories are the plants; the plants are the land; the land is a single, finished argument about chemistry. It manufactures itself and then manufactures the conditions of its own stillness. Equilibrium is not a rest. It is the product.

Because nothing changes, the youth of the place is permanent. The ridges of bark still show the stretch-marks of first emergence, pale and taut. The angular buttresses have not been softened by any season. Spores hang in the dark air in the exact constellations they assumed when the last motion ceased, a suspended weather of reproductive dust that will never land, never germinate, never need to. Time here is a held note. The vastness has weather of its own, but the weather is a portrait of weather, a fog that occupies and does not pass, a warmth that does not migrate, a wetness that does not flood or drought. You could sleep against a weeping wall for a century and wake to the same bead in the same place on your cheek, the same unstruck silence, the same low industrial interior of a world that grew a foundry and then, having no further instruction, simply continued to be one.

Far in, past districts of plate-leaf and districts of pipe-wood and districts that are only pressure and dark water, the living mass thickens toward something like a heart that is not a heart: a density of reaction so complete that the plant-as-ground becomes plant-as-atmosphere, a warm black broth of suspended chloroplasts that never saw light and do not require it. There the country thinks, if thinking is the slow balancing of a million chemical accounts. There the silence has weight. There the strange becomes ordinary: a planet-scale organism that is also a factory that is also a marsh that is also a night that will not end, recently born and already eternal, wet to the root and dry of history, producing nothing but the conditions of its own unfinished, perfect work.
