The land has forgotten how to rise. Whatever mountains once stood here were ground away so long ago that even their dust has been sorted, resorted, and laid down again as this endless, almost-level skin of silt and peat. You walk for hours and the horizon does not change: a pale, warm brightness sitting on a line of darker wetness, the two colors meeting with no drama. The elevation is so slight that the whole region behaves like a single shallow dish left out in the rain.

Water is not an event here. It is the ground’s ordinary condition. Every footprint fills before you lift your foot. The air itself is thick with it—warm, condensing, beading on every surface that is even slightly cooler than the afternoon. Rocks, where they still exist, weep steadily; they are the last, low knobs of something much older, their surfaces slick with a film that never dries. Between them the earth is a mosaic of standing shallows, black-mirror puddles, and slow, braided sheets that slide almost invisibly toward some distant, unseen collecting place. After rain the entire plain becomes one continuous, ankle-deep lake whose surface is broken only by the occasional clump of vegetation and the wakes of things that live in it.

Those clumps are few. Vegetation has been reduced to what can endure perpetual saturation and almost no mineral novelty. Isolated stands of reed, each no taller than a person, grow in loose, circular rafts that look as if they could drift if the water ever decided to move faster. Here and there a single, stunted tree—its bark silvered and peeling, its roots splayed like the fingers of something trying to stay above the mud—stands alone for a kilometer in every direction. Between them the ground is mostly bare wet silt, stained dark by tannins, or covered in a thin, slippery skin of algae that iridesces when the light hits it just so. Nothing here is in a hurry to grow. The plants have the look of things that have already decided their final, modest shape and will keep it until the sun itself changes.

The light is ordinary, which in this place means it arrives already filtered through vapor. Midday is a high, white glare that flattens every shadow; late afternoon turns the water into sheets of dull gold and the distant reed-rafts into black paper cutouts. There is no true night-darkness, only a slower dimming while the air continues to sweat.

Sound does not wait for permission. The frogs begin before the light has fully decided what color it wants to be and they do not stop. Their voices—low, wet, overlapping, innumerable—fill the entire volume of the air so completely that silence would feel like a wound. Insects add a second, higher layer: a continuous, metallic shimmer that seems to come from the water’s surface itself. Water drips from the weeping rocks with a patient, arrhythmic percussion. When a larger animal moves—a slow-wading bird, a heavy-bodied amphibian sliding between pools—the sound is a wet tearing, as if the landscape itself were being gently unzipped. Wind is rare and when it comes it only rearranges the existing noises, never replacing them.

Life is everywhere and nowhere dense. The abundance is the abundance of small, repeated existences rather than of crowding. Every pool holds tadpoles or their later, legged versions. Clouds of midges rise and fall like slow breath. Wading birds stand motionless for lengths of time that would be absurd anywhere else, then strike with a suddenness that feels like the only fast thing this place still remembers. In the deeper channels—scarcely deeper—things with smooth, dark backs break the surface and submerge again without hurry. Nothing here chases. Nothing here flees far. The distances between one living presence and the next are generous; the spaces themselves feel inhabited by the possibility of more life rather than by its absence.

Time has settled into its oldest habit. Tides, if they still reach this far inland, take half a day to lift the water a hand’s breadth and another half to let it fall. Seasons are a slight thickening or thinning of the humidity, a barely perceptible shift in which frogs sing loudest. The mud cracks in one century and heals in the next. Even the algae seem to advance and recede according to calendars no one is keeping. The landscape has already become the thing it was always going to be; it is now only maintaining itself, exhaling water, inhaling water, waiting with the patience of something that has already outlasted every mountain that once tried to stand on it.

There are no paths because nothing needs them. There are no stones large enough to sit on that have not already been worn into the same wet, rounded language as everything else. The only artifacts are the ones the water itself leaves: a perfect ring of bubbles around a reed stem, a single feather floating without destination, the momentary geometry of overlapping ripples that last just long enough to be seen and then become the next version of the same surface. This is the world after the world has finished making itself. It is still here, still warm, still loud with the voices of things that found a way to stay.
