The basin is new. You can tell by the way the green still has edges.

Whatever floor this place once had—lake-clay, ash, the packed memory of a river that forgot its job—has been eaten and replaced. There is no bare ground left to argue the point. What you walk on is living: a compressed weave of root-felt and collapsed leaf, warm as skin, giving a little, never quite the same path twice. The valley itself is low and broad, the kind of country that should hold a town and a few surrounding fields and then give up into hills. It does hold a town. The town is the fields. The fields are the walls. Everything that would have been architecture, paving, roof, cistern, monument, and ruin is plant, and the plant is not finished deciding which.

It is warm here in a way that does not need the sun to prove it. Heat comes up through the root-mat and hangs in the layered air between canopies. Moisture is not weather so much as a habit. Nothing is wet for long. Nothing is dry. Condensation beads on every cuticle and runs in thin, indecisive sheets; it gathers in the hollows of petioles, in the cups of flowers large enough to stand in, in the elbow-joints of stems that have grown into flying buttresses overnight. Occasional rain arrives as a rumor first—the light going a shade more gold, the air thickening—and then as a brief, vertical argument that never quite reaches the lowest levels. The upper city drinks it. Later, at some hour that is not clocked, the city sweats the rain back down as a warm, sweet drip that smells of crushed chlorophyll and something like iron.

There are no animals. That fact takes a while to become true in the mind, because the motion is constant and the sound is a physical pressure. What moves is vegetable. Tendrils search and retract. Entire façades lean, correct, lean the other way. A colonnade of bamboo-equivalents detonates upward in a single hour, internodes cracking open with reports like wet timber in a fire, and the street that ran beneath them is now a nave. An hour after that the nave is a thicket. An hour after that it is a plaza of low, glassy succulents that pack so densely their skins squeak against one another in the heat. The city is urban in the way a fever is urban: organized, multi-storied, infrastructural, and unable to stop revising its own plan.

You can see a long way if you climb. The scale is not intimate. From a ridge of freshly split bark—young country, unrounded, still showing the pale inner wood where growth tore itself a cliff—the settlement goes on as a single living mass to a horizon that is only more of it, district after district, each with a different habit of leaf and a different idea of height. There are low quarters of moss and liverwort packed into velvety cubes, a kind of botanical masonry that holds alleys so narrow the walls touch your shoulders and leave a green smear. There are mid-city belts of braided liana, streets that are also aqueducts for sap, platforms that are also leaves. There are towers. The towers are not trees in the ordinary sense. They are stacks of intention: ringed platforms of parasitic figs that have forgotten the host, spiral ramps of corkscrew palm, crown-gall palaces swollen into halls, all of it grown so recently the wounds have not browned. Between the towers the air is a green-gold ordinary daylight, then suddenly a shaft of unfiltered noon when a canopy fails, then green again as the gap knits. Light here is normal and still manages to feel like a guest.

The sound does not recede with distance. It is the growth itself. Cell walls stretching. Hydraulic pressure popping in stems thick as pillars. Leaves the size of sails filling and emptying in the warm convection, each fill a clap, each empty a long tearing exhalation. When two structures meet they do not politely graft. They grind. The grind is oceanic. Pollen is thrown up in sheets that hiss like rain on metal, though there is no metal. Seed-pods the size of rooms go off in the upper levels with a concussive unsealing; the released contents do not fall so much as get claimed midair by other growth. Under all of it, a bass you feel in the jaw: the root-mat rearranging, the whole valley’s underside crawling over itself, looking for a better civic arrangement. Conversation, if you attempted it, would be a mime. The city is deafening in the way a waterfall is deafening, except the waterfall is made of becoming.

Because there are no tools and never were, the urban quality is not imposed. It is an appetite the plants have for adjacency, for corridor, for repeated form. A “street” is a corridor the vegetation has agreed, for now, to leave unfilled, and the agreement is temporary. Doors are sphincters of living wood, or curtains of sensitive leaflets that collapse at a touch and then forget you. Windows are not openings so much as thinner places—membranes of pale leaf that pass the day in panes of veined amber. You can read a kind of zoning if you stay long enough to watch a district stabilize for a morning: here the city wants height and echo; here it wants density so complete that passage is a negotiation with stems; here it has grown a market that is only stalls of nectar and a floor of petals continually replaced, as if commerce were a shape plants could remember without ever having seen coin.

Then the agreement ends.

The weirdness is not an overlay. It is the weather of the place. Geometry does not keep its appointments. A lane that took a hundred steps to enter takes nine to leave, or does not leave, or leaves you on a balcony that was, a moment earlier, a culvert of wet ferns. Stair-roots climb and then decide that up was a rumor; you find yourself descending into a brighter canopy. Reflections appear in standing beads of condensation and show streets that are not the ones in front of you—older versions, or versions the city has not grown yet, or versions in which the leaves are a color no leaf is. Time does not flow evenly. In some courtyards a blossom opens, seed-sets, withers, and composts into the floor in the span of a held breath, and the floor is already sending up the next identical blossom as if practicing. In others, a single drop hangs on a tendril and has been hanging since you arrived, and the tendril has become a girder, and the girder has become a district, and the drop is still deciding whether to fall.

Water, scarce as free surface, becomes a kind of relic. You find it in pockets: a hollow stem cut by its own expansion, holding a dark column of warm rain; a depression in the moss-cubes where condensation has pooled to the depth of a palm and shakes continuously from the city’s vibration; a former river-course, still faintly lower than the rest of the basin, where the root-mat thins and the old dampness of the land breathes up as fog. The fog does not behave. It climbs the towers against the heat. It takes the shapes of rooms that are not there and then occupies them until the plants grow those rooms in fact, as if weather could draft. When the fog burns off, the rooms remain, already furnished with hanging roots and the first furniture of gall and burl.

There is a center, or there are several, or the idea of center is one of the things the city is arguing with itself about. In one of them the vegetation has achieved something like a forum: a wide, temporarily stable bowl of interlocking bracts, pale and thick as stone, ringing a space of almost-ordinary air. The roar drops half a tone. You can hear individual failures—a rib of wood giving, a leaf-sail splitting along its midvein. In the bowl’s floor the plants have grown a pattern that looks like paving until you realize it is a cross-section of a thousand nested growth-rings, all produced this week, all already being overwritten from beneath. Stand there long enough and the pattern shifts under your feet toward a different civic diagram. The city is not remembering a city. It is iterating one. Each iteration is sharp, unweathered, young in the erosional sense: corners that have not been softened, cuts that still show the wet pale of the last hour’s work, features that have not yet learned the stoop of age. The valley is low and the land is new and the plants are using both facts as a blank sheet they refuse to stop covering.

At the edges—if edges is the word—the mass does not taper so much as change its mind about density. The high-saturation core, where you cannot extend an arm without touching living wall, gives way to a more open weave, still total, still plant-as-ground and plant-as-sky, but with longer sightlines and a different noise, more hiss than thunder. Beyond that, only more basin, more of the same appetite, the regional body of it going on until the air whites out with distance and pollen and the heat-shimmer off a million transpiring surfaces. No smoke. No metal glint. No path that was cut by anything but the city’s own momentary mercy. No cry of bird, no insect-drill, no mammal hush in the undergrowth. The ecology is a single kingdom talking to itself at a volume that would flatten any other conversation.

Night, when it comes, is not a rest. The light goes to the ordinary dark of a cloudy lowland, and then the city provides its own: a residual green, the ghost of photosynthesis, and here and there the dull lamps of flowers that open only when the growth-roar changes pitch. You can see the turbulence better without the sun. Whole blocks heave. A tower undoes itself in a slow vegetable avalanche and is already a different tower before the debris has been digested back into the mat. Streets reroute like thoughts. The condensation thickens into a second weather under the canopies, a warm indoor rain that is only the city breathing. If you sleep, you sleep on a floor that will not be this floor, in a room that is rehearsing being a forest, under a sound that does not distinguish between making and unmaking.

The place does not want a name that lasts. Names last. This basin is a draft. It is a town the size of a valley written in a medium that cannot stop revising, in a climate that is only damp enough to keep the ink wet, under a light that insists, reasonably, that day is day. The unreason is everything else: the way distance folds, the way a leaf can be a law, the way silence has been so thoroughly evicted that even the idea of a still grove feels like a story from a drier, deader world. You are not in nature and you are not in a city. You are in the argument between them, conducted entirely by plants, at a volume that leaves a ringing in the skull for hours after you find—if you find—a pocket of older fog, a hollow stem, a pause in the roaring, and remember what quiet used to be for.
