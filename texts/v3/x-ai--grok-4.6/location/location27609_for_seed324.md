The pocket is not a valley. It is a cistern that learned the habits of one.

You come to it along a service catwalk that forgets to be a catwalk: the grating gives way to a poured apron, then to a shallow dish of dark ceramic, then you are inside a space no wider than a chapel and no longer than a barge, hung high on the weather side of the intake stack. The stack itself is the mountain now. Whatever ridge used to stand here was cut, keyed, and replaced with laminated concrete and ribbed plate until the old contour exists only as a remembered outline in the engineers’ drawings. There is no natural substrate left to touch. Every surface underfoot, every upright, every lip of every basin was mixed, cast, bolted, or fired.

It is cool in the way metal is cool after rain. Breath shows. The air is thick with a mineral damp that never quite becomes fog and never quite dries. Water is everywhere that is not a vertical. It films the walls. It stands in the lowest pans an inch deep, black and still until a pulse from the overflow header arrives and the surface flinches. It threads the designed weep-slots in the retaining plates—slots still sharp, still showing the mill-bright edge of a recent cut—and runs in hairline sheets down faces that were textured to look like cliff but are only formwork grain and release-agent stain. Condensation beads on the cold jackets of the riser pipes and drops with a regularity that is almost liturgical. The atmosphere itself is working: the stack draws wet air from the weather, wrings it against chilled coils behind the east wall, and returns the surplus here, into this hanging room, as if the building were sweating into its own palm.

Light is a remainder. A single slot, high and north, admits a slab of dimness rather than day. What illumination there is comes mostly from the underglow of a status bar along the header—amber, unblinking—and from the wet itself, which holds a dull pewter sheen. You see by reflection more than by source. Edges appear late. Distances collapse. The far wall, which is only twelve paces away, arrives as a rumor of rivets and then, suddenly, as a plane.

Vegetation has not been invited and has barely crashed the party. In the grout lines, in the shaded elbows of the weirs, a thin lime-green film has taken: algae, or something algae-adjacent, the industrial cousin of moss. A few stalks of something wiry have rooted in a drift of grit where the apron meets the north plate; they are the color of old brass and they do not move except when the overflow surges. That is the forest. It does not reach your ankle.

There are no animals. Nothing nests. Nothing skitters. The place is too new, too loud, too thoroughly itself.

The sound is the true climate. It is not one noise but a stacked weather of noises, all of them continuous, all of them too large for the room. Behind the east wall the main pumps labor at a frequency you feel in your teeth. The overflow does not trickle; it is dumped, at intervals just irregular enough to keep you from adapting, from a lip twelve feet up, and the fall hits the receiving basin like a door slammed in a cistern. The coils tick and slough. Valves in the header cycle with a hydraulic bark. Wind—true weather, the high ridge weather this stack was built to drink—finds the slot and the grate-seams and turns the whole pocket into a reed. The result is not roar exactly. It is a close, bodily deafening, the kind that lives in the bones of the face. Speech would be a pantomime. Thought arrives already shouted-down.

And yet the space is sparse. The processes are many; the objects are few. A single dogleg of stair, unpainted, still showing mill-scale where the galvanizing missed. Three basins in a shallow cascade, their overflows offset so the water must travel. One isolation wheel, red, the size of a serving platter, that has never been turned since commissioning. A coil of unused cable, still bound, hanging from a cleat like a sleeping animal that is not an animal. Between these things: wet air, and the sense of a room that was finished last season and has not yet decided what it will become when it weathers.

The youth is visible in the sharpness. Bolt-heads still have their hex. The ceramic has not spalled. Where two plates meet, the gasket still stands proud, black and uncrushed. You could cut yourself on a weir. The “erosion” here is only the first staining—the tea-colored bloom under each weep, the first lime crust at the waterline, the first bright rust-freckle on a fastener that was specified stainless and was not. The place has the look of a tool just unboxed, already being used hard.

It is active in cycles you can almost count. The header fills. The lip breaks. The basin receives, rings, settles. The weeps accelerate, then thin. The coils dump their condensate in a sudden curtain. Somewhere below, a turbine note changes pitch and the whole hanging room seems to lean a degree toward the weather, as if the stack were breathing in. Then the sequence begins again, not identical, never quite identical, the way weather is never quite identical. This is not landscape as rest. It is landscape as a machine that has been taught the manners of a spring, and has learned them too loudly, and too well, and in too small a space, high up where the cloud is already trying to be water, and the building has decided to finish the job.

Stand in the middle basin, if the surge will let you. The ceramic is glazed the color of wet slate. Your reflection arrives broken and is remade every few seconds. Above you the slot shows nothing but a brighter gray. Around you everything is made, and making, and slightly too new for the amount of water it already carries. The pocket does not care that you are in it. It has work. It wrings the air, and drops what it wrings, and the sound of that work is the whole of the weather here, and it does not stop.
