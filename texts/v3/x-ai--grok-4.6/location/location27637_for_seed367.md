The plateau has forgotten the shape of mountains. Whatever ranges stood here finished their work so long ago that even the memory of slope has been worn out of the stone; what remains is a high, warm table of pale grit and fused gravel, a surface so old it has the patience of a finished sentence. Wind still moves across it, but only as a habit. There is no gleam of water anywhere—no seep, no salt crust of a vanished lake, no dark stain under a ledge. The sky is a low, dusty pewter. Light arrives already tired.

You would walk this place for a day and think nothing lived. Then the ground opens by a single step.

It is not a canyon. It is a bowl, a collapse, a last remaining dimple in a world that has otherwise flattened itself into agreement. From the rim you could shout across it. From a hundred paces off you would not know it was there: the lip is flush with the plateau, fringed with the same colorless grit, and the interior has roofed itself.

The roof is plants.

They are not thirsty plants. They have never been offered a river and have stopped expecting one. What they have instead is thickness—layer on layer of succulent paddles, resinous scrub, trailing stems with leaves like hammered green coins, flowering towers the color of rust and wax. They pack the bowl so completely that soil is a rumor. Roots have married stone, stone has been split and re-split into terraces no wider than a forearm, and every terrace is stuffed. The air under that canopy is warm and still and sweet with sap and dust. Light comes down in coins and blades. It is always a kind of indoor dusk, even at noon.

Into this same thickness the settlement has been fitted, which is to say the settlement and the thicket are no longer separate projects. Dry-stone rooms lean into one another like people in a crowd. Walls are stacked from the plateau’s own exhausted plates—flat, pale, so thoroughly weathered they ring dull when struck. Lintels are single slabs. Roofs are lashed fiber over pole, then planted, so that from above the town is only a denser greening, a few smoke-holes, a few ladders of lashed bone and wood rising to the rim and stopping, as if ashamed to be seen. There are no streets. There are intervals. You turn sideways. You duck. You put your palm on a wall and feel the day’s heat stored in it, and the faint tremor of something living on the other side of the masonry—a nest, a hive, a sleeping weight.

No metal shows that was not carried here as a relic and worn thin. Tools are flake and edge, horn and fire-hardened point, fiber cord, a little beaten stuff the color of old blood that might once have been copper. People move the way the animals move: by habit through gaps that have always been gaps. The architecture has reached the state the plateau reached. Nothing new is being invented. Things are mended in the pattern of the last mending.

And the animals.

This hollow is the last cupboard on a bare shelf, and everything has found it. The sound is not a roar; it is a pressure. Wings against paddles. Claws on stone. A continuous small thunder of bodies changing their minds. Lizards densely tiled on every sun-warmed face that still catches a rag of light. Beetles like spilled seed in the leaf-litter, which is not wet litter but a dry, clicking mulch of shed skins and hollowed succulent. Birds the size of fists and birds the size of hands stitching the dim air between terraces. Things with too many legs in the wall-cracks. Things that only come out when a shadow crosses a threshold. The people live among them the way people live among weather. Nets are set. Traps are walked. Eggs are taken with a murmured count, never all. Something larger coughs, now and then, from the deepest green against the far wall—a sound with weight in it—and the whole bowl adjusts around that cough and then forgets it, which is how the place stays possible.

There is a metabolism to the hours. Morning (such as morning is, under the roof) is insect-loud, a bright sawing. Midday thickens; the warmth pools; reptiles flatten themselves into a single glittering hide across the stones and the people nap in the inner rooms where the masonry is coolest. Late day the birds take the air back, and the hollow fills with a sound like dry grain poured forever. Night is not quiet. Night is closer. Eyes. The tick of cooling rock. Something browsing the upper paddles with a slow tearing. The settlement’s few lamps—fat, wick, clay—make islands that the dark animals regard and then ignore.

Water is a private substance. It lives inside stems, inside fruits like stones, inside the bodies of the things that eat the fruits. Children learn early which paddle can be opened with a thumbnail and sucked, which vine weeps if wounded, which beetle, crushed, is a bead of moisture not worth the bitterness. There are cisterns, yes—carved, ancient, older than the present stacking of rooms—but they hold dust and a greenish memory, and no one expects them to change their minds. Fog, on rare nights, beads on the outermost leaves at the rim; by first light it is gone, and the leaves look as they always look.

What rules there are feel like geology. Do not cut the roof. Do not widen a gap that has decided its width. Leave the far-wall thicket to whatever coughs there. Mend in the old pattern. Take from the animals only what the animals can replace by evening. The plateau beyond the rim is not forbidden; it is simply finished, and nothing on it will answer you.

Stand in the very center—if a center can be found among the leaning rooms—and you can hear the whole machine of the bowl at once: sap, wing, voice, stone ticking, the dry friction of a hundred thousand small lives using the same air. Look up and the sky is a ragged coin. Look down and the floor is not floor but a compost of shed life so old and so continually renewed that it has the spring of something still deciding. The place is the size of a secret. It has been this size for longer than anyone bothers to measure. It will not grow. It does not need to. Everything that can still happen is already happening, pressed warm and dim and crowded into the last hollow the worn world had left to give.
