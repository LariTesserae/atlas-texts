The basin has no name that still works. Whatever first dug or settled it has been gone long enough that even the geology has forgotten the difference. It is a shallow dish in a world worn almost flat, a last low place where groundwater still finds a reason to collect. The rim is barely a rim: a wet shoulder of stone that weeps without stopping, a continuous seepage that beads, sheets, and becomes the sky’s own condensate before it ever quite decides to be rain. The air is warm and thick with it. Everything shines as if recently washed and never given time to dry.

What stands in the dish is a town that grew itself after the last builders left, or perhaps while they were still leaving. There are not many structures. You can count them. They sit far apart, each on its own island of living mass, with wide reaches of black water and root-skin between them, so that the place feels less like a city and more like a sentence with most of the words erased. The emptiness is not barren. The water is ankle-deep in some lanes, hip-deep in others, and it is never the same depth twice. Mats of pale, spongy vegetation float and knit and tear. The “streets” are suggestions the plants keep offering and then withdrawing.

Each building is plant all the way through. Not overgrown stone. Not vine on brick. The load-bearing members are trunks that learned, long ago, a grammar of cantilevers and vaults. Walls are living cambium, warm to the touch, slightly giving, weeping a clear sap that smells of copper and crushed leaf. Windows are not openings so much as decisions the tissue makes: a thinning, a transparency, a brief alignment of cells that lets ordinary daylight through. The light itself is unremarkable—cloud-filtered, puddle-bright, the kind of light that belongs to late morning after rain—but it falls on surfaces that refuse to stay still.

The turbulence is the town’s only remaining industry. A hall that was, an hour ago, a ribbed nave of dark wood will unzip along its grain. Fibers unbraid. The whole mass slumps into the water as a raft of enormous pale flowers, each bloom the size of a door, opening and closing with a wet sound like applause heard through a wall. Then the flowers darken, cork, and climb again, this time as a spiral stair to nowhere, or a bridge that spans a gap that did not exist yesterday. The process is loud. Lignin sets with a series of cracks like distant musketry. Roots argue in the mud with a continuous low grinding. When a structure chooses a shape that local physics finds inconvenient, there is a brief, bright tearing—air folding, a smell of ozone and green sap—and the new form occupies a volume that is slightly wrong, an interior that is larger than its skin, a corridor that arrives before you have finished entering it.

This is the old technology, still running. Whatever was engineered here no longer distinguishes between growth and computation. Information moves in the xylem as pulses of sugar and charge. Mycelial nets under the water table keep a kind of consensus about what the town is allowed to become next, and the consensus is unstable on purpose. The place has settled into its final form, which is not a shape but a weather: perpetual remodeling at the scale of a hamlet. It will do this until the basin fills or the sun changes its mind.

Animals use the flux as if it were weather they were born to. Schools of small, copper-sided fish occupy the temporary canals and vanish when a street becomes a wall. Things like deer, but jointed wrong and leafed along the spine, browse the older, slower façades; their hooves leave prints that immediately fill with seedlings. Birds the color of wet slate nest in sockets that will be windows by evening and mouths by morning. Insects in metallic greens and bruised purples stitch the air between structures, carrying pollen and, one suspects, packets of the town’s own revising code. There is a sound under all the other sounds, a mid-range roar that never quite resolves: water, growth, wings, the wet tear of a rooftop becoming a canopy, the splash of something heavy and living entering a pool that was a plaza. It is not deafening. It is simply always there, the way a market is always there, even when you cannot see the stalls.

Walk the long way around a single tower and the light will have shifted only a little, but the tower will have grown a skirt of aerial roots and then withdrawn them, leaving a ring of perfectly circular holes in the water, each hole holding a column of air that does not quite want to collapse. The warmth makes everything smell louder: tannin, rot that is also birth, the mineral breath of the weeping rim. Stand still long enough and the ground under you—itself a woven plate of roots and compressed leaf-mold—will begin to consider whether you are a feature worth incorporating. Usually it decides against it. The town is sparse with its attention. It has learned, over whatever span counts as ancient here, that emptiness is part of the architecture.

At the very center, if the paths hold, there is a space that has never successfully become a building. It remains a clearing of open water, circular, no deeper than a man’s chest. The surface trembles constantly, not from wind but from below: something in the substrate turning over, rewriting, failing to commit. Reflections break and reform. Occasionally a structure will send a tendril toward the clearing, taste it, and recoil as if the empty water were a law. The fauna avoid it too, except for a single species of pale eel that hangs vertical in the column, heads toward the ordinary sky, as if listening.

This is the place at the end of wearing-down: low, wet, warm, almost finished, and still unable to stop making itself. The last city, which is also a grove, which is also a machine that grew past the need for anyone to run it. You can cross it in the time it takes a storm to decide its mind. You cannot cross it the same way twice.
