The basin is no larger than a village green and has never needed to be. Low rock walls close it on every side, their faces dark with perpetual wet and so thoroughly weathered that every edge has forgotten it was ever sharp. What little sky shows above is a dull, unmoving pane the color of old pewter; the light that reaches the floor has already been wrung through mist and comes down as a brownish dusk that does not change hour to hour. There is no wind. There has not been, as far as the place itself is concerned.

Water occupies almost every interval. It stands in shallow, body-warm pools that occupy every hollow between the mineral masses. It sheets down the walls in slow, continuous films that never quite become falls. It hangs in the air as a warm, condensing breath that beads on stone, on leftover bone, on skin if anything living stays long enough. The heat is constant and close, the temperature of a held hand, coming up from somewhere beneath the packed floor rather than from any sun. You feel it first in the ankles.

There are no plants. What fills the space instead is the long, finished work of mineral water: a crowding of travertine shelves, cauliflower bosses, fused columns, and hanging draperies of flowstone so dense that open ground exists only as a network of narrow, ankle-deep channels. Formations lean into one another, interlock, roof over the smaller pools. You cannot take three steps in a straight line. The air between them is itself occupied—thick with vapor, with the faint mineral smell of wet calcite and a ghost of sulfur that never quite arrives or leaves. Everything glistens. Everything has been glistening for so long that the gleam itself feels like a settled property of the rock, not a process.

The place is in equilibrium and has been for longer than the scattered marks of people can remember. Drips fall at intervals so regular they have ceased to be events. Water levels do not rise or fall. The formations no longer grow in any way a visitor could witness; whatever slow chemistry still occurs has reached the point where addition and solution cancel. Time here is a held breath. A dropped pebble would remain exactly where it landed until the stone itself forgot it.

Here and there the packing of mineral is interrupted by older, smaller interruptions: three flat stones set in a rough triangle beside one of the clearer pools, their edges rounded by the same water that rounded everything else; a short run of steps cut into a low bench of rock, the treads now half-swallowed by later crust; a single ladle, carved from some dense pale wood that has gone almost mineral itself, left on a ledge as if the hand that set it down had only stepped away. These things are not ruins so much as pauses that never resumed. Whoever made them used only what could be shaped by hand and patience. Nothing metal remains, if anything metal was ever here.

Sound is a thin thing. Most of the time there is only the almost-inaudible tick of water finding the next lip of stone, and the softer, continuous whisper of films sliding over already-wet surfaces. Once in a great while something moves in one of the deeper pockets—a pale, thumb-sized crustacean turning over, or the brief dimple of a creature too small and too infrequent to name—and the splash is startling mainly because the rest of the world has agreed not to make noise. Then the agreement resumes.

The walls weep. The air condenses. The mineral bodies press close. Nothing arrives, nothing departs, and the dim warm wetness continues exactly as it has, occupying every available space, asking nothing, changing nothing. To stand in it is to feel the pressure of a finished sentence. The place is already as itself as it will ever be.
