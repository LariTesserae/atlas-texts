The highland does not yet know it is finished. The stone still has the look of something recently unwrapped: planes of pale basalt and younger andesite standing at angles that weather has not had time to argue with, joints still sharp enough to cut mist. Water uses every one of those unfinished edges. It does not so much fall as continuously arrive—sheet-flow over tilted pavements, threads bursting from weep-holes in the cliffs, braided shallows that have not decided on a single channel. The air itself is half water. You taste metal and chlorophyll. Breath condenses and does not quite leave.

Everything that is not moving water is plant, yet the place remains open. The substrate is living. A continuous, ankle-to-knee-deep pelt of sphagnum, liverwort, and a blue-green algal felt covers every surface that holds still for more than a season. You walk on it. The rock is only a rumor underneath, cool and slick, occasionally shouldering up in a raw fin or a table of unrounded scree. From this green skin rise the larger organisms, widely and irregularly spaced, like isolated thoughts. They are not trees in any ordinary sense. Each is a single architectural mass: a column of fused fronds or a hollow, fluted bole the color of wet jade, thirty or forty meters high, its crown a slowly rotating parasol of translucent tissue that sheds water in curtains. Between these presences lie hundreds of meters of glittering emptiness—shallow tarns, moss-meadow, the white noise of another unchannelled cataract. The vegetation is total and the saturation is low; the land is both filled and vacant.

Light here is a physical pressure. At this elevation the sun is unfiltered, and after each rain—rain that arrives as a fine, cold vertical smoke more often than as drops—the world becomes a field of exploding highlights. Every wet frond, every trembling meniscus, every sheet of water sliding off new stone throws glare. Shadows are rare and never dark; they are only cooler greens. The sky, when the weather tears, is a hard, almost white blue. You squint constantly. The plants have answered with a waxy cuticle that looks metallic at certain angles, so the whole highland sometimes appears plated.

Sound is the other climate. There is no quiet register. Water striking unfinished ledges produces a continuous, chest-deep roar that occupies the middle of hearing and never leaves it. Superimposed, from the scattered industrial points, comes a second, narrower violence: the turbine halls. They sit where the larger drops have been briefly captured—low, windowless blocks of poured concrete and riveted plate, already wearing the same moss pelt as the cliffs, pipes running out of them like roots that forgot they were metal. Inside, or just outside the intake grates, the generators add a hard, rotating scream that the water cannot quite drown. When the wind is wrong you feel both frequencies in your teeth. Conversation is a matter of putting your mouth against someone’s ear and still not being sure. Birds, on the rare days one appears, are seen long before they are heard, and then only as a dark hyphen against the glare.

The machines are few. A generating station every several kilometers, a pumping house whose purpose is no longer obvious, a single aerial tramway tower whose cables have become a trellis for a hanging garden of filmy ferns. They do not dominate. They are incidents, the way a lone boulder is an incident. Power lines sag between them, furred with the same living felt, humming their own thin note beneath the general deafness. Someone still maintains the nearer turbines; oil rainbows appear in the tailraces, and at night—though night here is only a brief, bright dimming—a few sodium lights burn inside the halls, making the wet concrete look like the interior of a fruit.

Change is almost too slow to be called weather. The mountains continue their millimeter-per-year lift; new fractures open and immediately begin to weep. The moss-film advances across fresh rock at the speed of a stain. The great plant-columns add a frond, lose a frond, re-balance. Seasons are long gradients of slightly more water and slightly less, the temperature never wandering far from the cool side of lukewarm, the kind of cold that lives in shade and in the marrow of the stone. A person could stand in one place for a decade and believe the highland had always been exactly this: raw, saturated, brilliantly lit, and loud.

Fauna is rumor. Once, a pale amphibian the size of a thumb, the same color as the algal felt, watching from a pool. Once, high up, something with long wings that did not flap so much as adjust. The plants do not seem to need them. Pollination, if it occurs, is a matter of water and wind and time. The industrial outposts have their own small, accidental ecologies—mosses that prefer warm effluent, a black mold that grows in the shape of cooling fins—but these are footnotes.

You can see a long way. The scale is the scale of a wide valley system or a small inland sea of stone and plant: ridge after unfinished ridge, each wearing its scattered green towers, each shedding water in the same patient, deafening way. In the farthest distance the geology is even younger, paler, still steaming faintly where it has only just finished arriving. The living skin has not reached it yet. It will. There is no hurry. The light will still be this bright when it does.
