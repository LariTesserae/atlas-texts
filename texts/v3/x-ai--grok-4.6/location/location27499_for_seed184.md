The first thing that is not water is brick.

You are already inside it when you notice: a warmth that does not belong to open sea, a mineral sweetness, iron and leaf-rot braided together. The water here does not surge. It occupies. It has the patience of something that filled a space a long time ago and then simply stayed, learning the rooms by heart.

This is not a coast and not a wreck. It is a basin, a low oval cut into living rock some uncounted fathoms beneath whatever surface still pretends to be the world. The rock is warm. Veins of paler stone run through the walls like old fat in meat; they weep a faint milk that never quite clouds the water and never quite clears it. Visibility is a few body-lengths of amber dusk. Beyond that, mass without outline.

The settlement sits in the basin the way a clot sits in a vein. It was never large. You could walk its longest street in three minutes if you could walk, if the street were empty, if the water were not a second architecture. Nothing is empty. Buildings shoulder one another until alleys become slots, slots become flues, flues become the interiors of pipes that were once meant to carry something else. Facades of fired clay and slag-brick lean together and have, in places, fused. Iron strapwork binds corners that began to slump a century ago and then decided slumping was a finished thought. Rooflines are a low, irregular saw of gables and truncated stacks, all of them lower than the lip of the basin, all of them drowning in the same unhurried dark.

The industry did not leave. It slowed until slowness became its method.

Heat-exchangers the size of cottages still tick. You feel them before you hear them: a pressure-change in the chest, a poultice of warmth blooming and fading on a twelve-breath cycle. Pistons in the old pump-houses travel their last few inches of travel as if arguing with molasses. Flywheels turn so gradually that a drifting frond may rest against a spoke for the length of a thought and then be carried, uninjured, a hand’s-width onward. There is electrification of a kind—cables fat with mineral crust, glass insulators grown over with a felt of red algae, junction boxes that leak a brownish glow no brighter than a held breath. The glow is not quite light. It is the memory of light, stained into the water.

What grows here has learned the buildings as soil.

Not coral, not quite weed. Something between liverwort and cable, a lushness that prefers verticality and enclosure. It hangs in curtains from lintels, packed so dense that doorways are only rumors of thinner green. It threads the brick, prying lime from mortar and replacing it with a living grout that holds better than the original. In the courtyards—there are three, none larger than a chapel nave—the growth rises in columns, braided, load-bearing, each column a hanging garden of overlapping blades the color of old bronze and wet tea. Between the blades: bladders that store the basin’s warmth and release it at night, though night here is only a dimming of an already dim thing, a shift from amber to the brown of steeped oak.

The plants occupy every interval. There is no “between.” Where two walls almost meet, the vegetation finishes the meeting. Where a stairwell descends into a cellar that is now only a deeper room of the same water, roots and holdfasts make a second stair, softer, intended for a weight the builders did not plan. Pipes that once vented steam now vent slow green. The green has a pulse. Not a heartbeat—slower. A tide that does not consult the moon. Over hours, a whole curtain will lean one way, then the other, combing silt from the water and laying it in even shawls across the cobbles. The cobbles have not been seen in a generation. They are a hypothesis under sediment and root.

Sound is a courtesy the place extends rarely. A valve seats somewhere in the northern stack with the politeness of a lid on a jar. Silt ticks against a window that has not been glass for a long time, only a pane of compressed weed, translucent as horn. Once in a long while the great flywheel in the central house completes a revolution and the water carries a single, rounded note, felt in the teeth, already fading as you recognize it. People who work here—and there are people, or were, or will be again in the next slow census—speak with their hands or not at all. Speech unravels. Words leave the mouth as shapes and are combed apart by the hanging gardens before they arrive.

The workers are few enough to count as weather. You do not meet them so much as notice that a valve you passed an hour ago has been eased a quarter-turn, that a curtain of bronze blades has been braided back from a doorway and pinned with a bone clip. Their tools are wooden, swollen, almost growing. Their lamps are not lamps; they carry jars of the same brownish residual glow, cultured from the junction boxes, fed on filings and patience. When they pass you in a slot-alley you smell iron and chlorophyll and the specific warmth of skin that has not been dry in years. They do not hurry. Hurrying is a surface habit. Down here a task is a season.

Fauna is a rumor that sometimes proves itself. A pale eel no longer than a finger will unspool from a pipe-mouth, taste the water, and return as if the sampling were a complete life. Once, in the eastern court, something with too many slow joints walked the length of a cornice and was gone; the plants closed behind it without comment. Shells exist, but they are inhabited by the same greenery that inhabits everything else, and whether that counts as animal is a doctrinal matter the settlement has tabled indefinitely. The water is too used, too occupied by root and rust and purpose, to support the ordinary appetite of fish. Hunger here is a plant’s hunger: for mineral, for the faint electric seepage, for the privilege of remaining.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is a quality of fit.

The machines and the gardens have reached an accord so complete that it is no longer possible to say which maintains which. A governor-arm in the pump-house is wound with living strap that contracts in the warm phase and eases in the cooler, doing the work the metal spring forgot how to do. In return the spring, oxidizing, feeds the strap a rust it prefers to any other food. Windows grow shut and then, over months, grow a thinner place that serves as window. The streets remember feet and shape themselves microscopically toward the paths still used, so that walking—swimming—the main way feels like being remembered by the ground. Time has a grain. You can see it in the brick: layers of mineral skin, each a decade, each a slightly different ochre, the whole wall a core sample of stillness.

There are rules, though no one has written them.

You do not break a column. The columns hold more than roofs. You do not start a fast machine. Fastness tears the water’s agreements and the gardens take a year to forgive a whirl. You do not take more glow than you can replace with filings and a week of dark. You do not go up. Up is a shaft in the western rock, grilled, furred with the same bronze blades, and beyond the grill the water turns suddenly cold and empty, a throat that leads toward a surface no one here has a use for. The basin keeps its weather. The surface can keep its weather. This has been the understanding since the flood that was not a disaster but a completion—since the day the pumps, running the other direction then, invited the aquifer in and the aquifer, being older, stayed to finish the design.

Light remains a rumor of industry and of the plants’ own dull photochemistry. No sun. No shaft of glory. Only the brown jars, the seepage from the cables, and, in the deepest cellars, a faint interior shine in the rock itself, as if the limestone were very slowly remembering a fire it once lay near. Eyes adjust into a seeing that is mostly touch and temperature. You navigate by warmth-maps: the living columns a degree kinder than the brick, the still-working engines a palm of heat, the unused rooms a stillness that reads as cool only by contrast. In that dimness color becomes a luxury spent on the gardens—bronze, tea, the occasional unaccountable smear of oxblood where a bladder has split and sealed again.

The whole place is the size of a hamlet that decided to become a single organism, or a factory that decided to become a grove, and then both decisions weathered into the same shape. You can know it. That is part of its scale and part of its mercy. After a few days the three courts, the northern stack, the pump-house with its molasses flywheel, the shaft you do not take, the alley where the brick fused into a single knuckle—these become a body you move inside of. After a few weeks you understand that the body is also moving, too slowly to startle, wearing its own streets the way a river wears a valley, except the river is warm and full of leaves that are not leaves, and the valley is a room, and the room has been finished for a long time, and is still, without contradiction, finishing itself.

At the center of the middle court the oldest column stands. It is thicker than the others, hollow, and if you press your ear to it—if you have an ear the water will still permit—you hear not a machine and not a plant but the basin’s own note: a held, almost subsonic vowel, the sound of stone and water and rust and chlorophyll agreeing, again, to continue. Nothing answers it. Nothing needs to. The agreement is the landscape. The landscape is the work. The work, for as far ahead as anyone here bothers to imagine, is already done, and is being done, and will, with the same unfrightened slowness, be done again.
