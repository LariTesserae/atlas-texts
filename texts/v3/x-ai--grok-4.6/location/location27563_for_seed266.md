The Calcined Heights do not begin at any shoreline or river-mouth; they have no memory of either. What you first take for a horizon is only the next terrace, a pale, heat-shimmered lip two hundred kilometres away and half a kilometre higher, and beyond that another, and another, the whole world stacked like the worn steps of a stair that forgot why it was climbing. The air is thin enough that distant factories appear as faint, rust-coloured smudges rather than solid architecture, yet it is never cold. Heat lives in the rock itself, a slow residual warmth left from whatever furnace once baked this crust dry. You feel it through boot-soles even at dusk.

Wind is the only water here, and it is a miser. It has had geological ages to do its work. Every surface is already the shape it intends to keep: knife-edged yardangs of gypsum and anhydrite, vast shallow bowls of wind-polished halite that gleam like frozen lakes under the ordinary ochre sun, fields of ventifacts so densely packed they look like a city of kneeling statues until you walk among them and discover each is only a single faceted stone the size of a fist. Nothing is sharp anymore. Edges have been rounded by centuries of grit, then rounded again. The land has finished becoming itself.

Cities occupy the rare places where the terraces fracture—along old fault scarps, inside collapsed salt domes, on the lee of the largest yardangs. They are never close. Between one settlement and the next you can walk for days and meet only the slow fauna and the patient machines that serve them. The cities themselves are layered rather than tall: concentric rings of foundries, ore-sintering halls, and dwelling-blocks built from the same brick-red sinter they produce. Conveyors the width of roads creep at the pace of cooling slag. Pistons the size of grain silos rise and fall once every few hours, their rhythm so regular it has become a kind of silence. Smoke is dry—iron oxide, sulphur dust, powdered glass—and it hangs in slow, horizontal banners that take a week to smear across a terrace. At night the banners catch the last light and look like frozen aurora.

The machines are old enough to have weather of their own. Their casings are pitted, their rivets worn egg-smooth. They were never designed to hurry. A single smelting cycle can last a local month; a finished girder may travel thirty kilometres of roller-bed before it is cool enough to touch. The people who tend them move at the same tempo. They speak little. When they do, it is in the intervals between piston-strokes, as though conversation itself were another industrial process that must not be rushed.

Fauna is everywhere and nowhere in particular. Lithovores the colour of the ground itself graze the mineral crust in loose, continent-scale herds. Most are no larger than a human torso: low, many-legged, their carapaces a mosaic of the same salts and oxides they ingest. They leave faint, glittering trails that the wind erases in a day. Larger forms exist—slow tripedal browsers whose joints are living geodes, flocks of membrane-wings that ride thermal columns for weeks without landing, colonial mats of something that is neither quite fungus nor quite crystal and that creeps across cooling slag-heaps at a centimetre a night. They do not drink. They do not photosynthesize. Their chemistry is a dry conversation between metal ions and trace atmospheric acids, a metabolism so gradual it can be mistaken for geology until you watch one long enough to see it change colour.

Some of the animals have begun to incorporate the cities. You will find a herd that has learned to wait beside a particular outfall of warm clinker, or a colony of membrane-wings that nests inside the cooling vanes of a generator, their bodies taking on the same pitted patina as the metal. The relationship is not parasitic. The machines run slower when the creatures are absent; the creatures grow denser, more geometrically precise, when they live among the forges. No one remembers which adapted first.

Sound is mostly the absence of sound. Wind combs the yardangs with a noise like distant paper. Once in a while a far conveyor chain will clink, or a lithovore herd will pass close enough that you hear the faint, dry click of their mouthparts. At the very centre of the largest cities there is a deeper quiet still: the pause between the great pistons, a held breath that can last ten minutes. In that interval you can hear the terrace itself settling, a subsonic creak older than any factory.

Nothing here is in a hurry to finish. Seasons are measured in the slow migration of dust-plumes from one terrace to the next. A city expands by the width of a single new sintering hall every handful of years. The fauna shift their ranges by a few kilometres per generation. Even the light seems uninterested in drama: the sun (or whatever ordinary star this is) rises and sets on a schedule so consistent it feels like another machine, one that has been running without maintenance since before the first brick was fired.

Walk far enough and the terraces keep rising. The air thins further, the minerals grow paler, the factories fewer. At the highest levels the cities are little more than maintenance outposts around machines so ancient their original purpose has been forgotten; they simply continue their cycles because stopping would require a decision no one is prepared to make. The fauna there are stranger still—almost geometric, their bodies suggesting crystals that decided to walk. They watch the visitors with eyes that are only facets, and they do not approach. There is no need. Everything that lives on the Heights has already arrived at the only destination that matters: a state of weathered, self-consistent continuance, dry and warm and almost silent, waiting for the next slow thing to happen.
