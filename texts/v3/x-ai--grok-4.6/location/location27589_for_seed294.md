The plateau was finished so long ago that finishing is the only weather it still has. What remains is not a city and not a mountain. It is a single worked table of stone, regional in reach, lifted into thin cold air and then drowned in itself. There is no bedrock underneath that anyone has ever found. There is only more fitting: blocks the size of hills, courses that run for days, channels cut so true that water still obeys them as if the last mason had only just stepped back.

You are always under water here. Not in a trench. Not on an abyssal plain. The elevation is high enough that the pressure never quite becomes the deep-sea kind of weight; it is the weight of a highland flood that never found a sea. The surface, when there is one, is a dim plate of slush and wind somewhere far above the lintels. More often there is no surface you can name—only stacked galleries of cold, gray-green volume, each gallery a drowned courtyard or a nave or a lock, all of them full, all of them moving.

The stone does not move. That is the settled fact of the place. Every edge has already been taken. Corners have gone to slow curves. Hieroglyph and joint-line have been planed to the same satin. The plateau has reached the form it will keep until the world ends, and the water has been using that form as a machine ever since. The turbulence is not weather arriving from elsewhere. It is the architecture working. Cataracts drop through wells no living thing designed for viewing. Lateral currents are pinched through throats until they scream in frequencies too low to hear and then bloom into white chaos in the next basin. Whole valleys of colonnade stand in standing waves. Ice forms in the slack water behind piers, calved by a change in the flow, and is driven like glass wreckage through halls that were already old when ice first learned this route.

There are no plants. There has not been a leaf or a frond or a smear of algal green in any memory the water keeps. The mineral is the only kingdom that sits still. What lives here lives on motion, on each other, on the fine flour the current still rasps from the oldest arrises. They are abundant and they are far apart. You can swim a long time in a single nave and meet no one, and then the water will thicken with bodies: pale, slow-eyed things the length of a forearm, hanging in the lee of a buttress; sheets of filterers stretched like banners across a throat; something larger and colder that uses the drop-shafts the way a bird uses thermals, appearing and gone in the same long groan of displaced volume. They do not make much sound. The place is mostly quiet in the way a cistern is quiet—sound folded into pressure, clicks and drumming arriving late and from the wrong direction. When the system changes its mind, the quiet becomes a single note you feel in the jaw.

Light is a rumor of the surface and a habit of the animals. What comes down through the slush is the color of wet pewter. What the fauna carry is spare: a seam of dull foxfire along a flank, a brief lantern in a mouth, enough to make a pier look nearer than it is. Distances lie. A colonnade that seems a street is a day’s transit. A ceiling that seems low is only a second floor, and above it the same cold rooms go on, and below it the same, all the way to whatever foundation the makers thought was enough.

No tool has been found. No metal. No glass that was not ice. The whole highland is constructed and none of it is technical. The blocks fit because they were made to fit, or grown to fit, or laid down in a slurry that forgot how to be anything but wall. There is no natural substrate to return to. If you chip the stone you get more stone that has already decided its shape. The flood is not an accident on top of a landscape. The flood is what the landscape was for, and it has been running so long that purpose and ruin are the same current.

Sometimes, in a slack gallery, the water holds still enough that silt draws the ghost of a floor-plan on the pavement—circles inside squares, a grammar of overflows. Then a pulse comes down from three basins away and the drawing is a cloud, and the cloud is animals turning, and the animals are gone into the next dark, and the stone, which has seen this exact vanishing more times than there are names, does not record it.
