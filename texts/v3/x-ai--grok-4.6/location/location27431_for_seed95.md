The plateau has no remaining ambition. Whatever folded it upward finished that work so long ago that the folds themselves have been planed into a single thought: a warm, high table of stone that remembers mountains only as a rumor in its grain. You can walk for a day and the horizon will not change its mind. The sky sits at an ordinary height. The light is ordinary too—neither thin nor golden, just the usual daylight of a world that has stopped rehearsing.

Underfoot the rock is not sharp. It has been handled by weather until every edge became a suggestion. The surface is a kind of old skin: pitted, satined in places, freckled with basins no deeper than a palm. Those basins hold the plateau’s water, such as it is. Rain comes rarely and without theater, a warm gray soaking that lasts an hour and then forgets itself. Afterward the basins brim for a week, then a film, then a dark stain, then dust the color of old tea. Between rains the only moisture is what the air condenses at dusk and dawn—beads along every flank and ridge, a brief dampness that the stone drinks without sound.

There are plants. They do not amount to a landscape. In the seams, a gray lichen that has the patience of the rock itself. In a few of the deeper basins, after rain, a thread of something like rush, never taller than a finger, never in company. Once in a long walk you may find a shrub that has claimed a crack and grown sideways, woody, almost mineral, its leaves the size of fingernails and the color of dried blood. Nothing here is trying to be a forest. The green has already lost.

What fills the space is not green.

The animals are the density. They occupy the plateau the way sediment occupies a lakebed: layered, adjacent, almost touching, generation upon generation pressed into the same shallow volume. They are not one kind. There are the low ones, broad as shields, that lie so still they take on the temperature of the stone and only the slow blink of a nictitating membrane tells you the ground has an eye. There are the upright ones, no taller than a child, that stand in loose congregations and lean until their shoulders meet, a living rubble with the patience of masonry. There are the long ones that braid through the gaps, pale-bellied, moving at the speed of shade. There are smaller lives in the basins after rain—things like tadpoles if tadpoles were made of smoked glass—and things that live under the lichen and taste of salt if you were ever to put a tongue there, which you should not.

They do not hurry. Nothing here has hurried in so long that hurry has become a theoretical animal, mentioned, if at all, the way one mentions glaciers. A crossing from one basin to another may take a morning. A courtship may take a season and consist of two bodies standing near enough to share dew. Predation exists, but it has the manners of geology. A larger presence settles beside a smaller one and remains. Days later the smaller one is simply less. There is no chase to hear.

That is the other law: the quiet. Wind moves, because wind is not local and has not signed the treaty. It makes a dry sound in the rare shrub and a softer one across the hides. Once in a while a basin, drying, will click as mud contracts. The animals themselves produce almost nothing. Breath is a warmth you feel if you stand too close. When they shift, the sound is the sound of a heavy cloth being folded in another room. At night—if you stay, and the warmth does not quite leave—you may hear a single, unused note, like a stone set down, and then nothing again for hours. The density is visual, tactile, olfactory (a faint, clean musk, like wet iron and dust). It is not acoustic. The plateau absorbs its own occupancy.

You will find marks that are not quite geology. Not tools—there are no tools—but habits worn into matter until they resemble decisions. Paths so old they are trenches, polished by the same shoulders, the same bellies, until the rock looks poured. Circles of smaller stones around certain basins, renewed whenever weather scatters them; no one is seen arranging them, and yet they return, the way a scar returns. In a few places the upright animals stand in alignments that last years, and the ground beneath them has been compacted into a kind of floor, slightly concave, slightly darker, as if the plateau were being taught a room. These are the scattered built things: not ruins, not architecture, only the faint insistence of bodies that have used the same world too long to leave it unshaped.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a saturation. After an hour you notice that many of the shield-beasts have the same scar, in the same place, as if they were printed. After three hours you notice that the long ones never cross a trench-path; they go around, even when the path is empty, as if the path were a river of a different element. The dew, in the hour it lasts, does not wet them evenly. It gathers in characters—not language, not quite, but repeating clusters, like the ghosts of words that never needed speaking. The light is normal, and still, at the edge of vision, the congregations seem to breathe in a rhythm older than lungs, a tide in the high air. Nothing leaps. Nothing calls. The overwhelming is the fact of so much life having agreed, without a meeting, to stay.

Walk far enough and the table does not end so much as forget to continue being interesting in a new way. Another basin. Another leaning congregation. Another polished trench. The elevation is real—the air has that slight thinness that makes distant things very sharp—but there is no drama of peaks, no falling away. This is the last shape the land consented to. The fauna are its final vegetation, its mobile stone, its weather that has learned to stand still. If there were ever a people here they have been sanded out of the record, or they became another layer in the leaning, another pair of shoulders in the quiet. The plateau does not distinguish. It is warm. It is high. It is almost finished, and has been almost finished for longer than finishing would take.
