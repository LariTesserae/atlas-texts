The first thing is not the dark. You expect dark, going under. What you get is noon.

It arrives as a pressure behind the eyes and a wet heat on the skin at the same moment: a chamber the size of a modest nave, cut or worn or both into pale stone, and filled with so much light that the air itself looks thick. The light is not a shaft. It does not fall from a hole and pool on the floor. It is *in* the room, evenly, almost insolently, as if the sun had been folded and packed into the rock and then told to stay. Your pupils argue with it. After a minute they lose.

You are perhaps four body-lengths below the surface. The way down was a throat of steps, slick, one wall weeping in a continuous film. At the bottom the steps simply end in water that is never still enough to be called a floor. Ankle-deep, shin-deep, then a trough you cannot see the bottom of because the surface is being rewritten too quickly. The chamber is not flooded so much as *occupied* by water that has decided motion is its job.

The stone is old in the way river-cut limestone is old. It has already had its argument with time and settled the terms. Grooves the width of a forearm run in long, patient curves from ceiling to basin, some of them still carrying sheets of water, some of them dry and stained tea-brown with iron. The corners are not corners anymore. Everything that could have been sharp has been spoken to for a very long time by mineral water and given up. You can put a palm on the wall and feel both warmth and a faint, continuous vibration, as if the rock were a drumhead with something enormous on the other side.

What is on the other side, or rather what is *in* the middle, is the reason the light is here.

A single vertical filament hangs in the chamber’s long axis. It is no thicker than a wrist. It does not look like metal, exactly, and it does not look like glass. In the brightness it is almost the same color as the air, a heat-shimmer given a spine. Where water touches it — and water touches it constantly — the water climbs. Not capillarity. Climbing, in braided ropes and sudden veils, against every ordinary rule, then flung outward in sheets that slap the walls and return as spray and rain and a fine hot mist that never quite lets you see the far end of the room as a single image. The filament sings. Not a metaphor. A high, load-bearing tone just under the roar, the sound of a thing doing work that current physics would prefer to file under *please explain*. No housing. No consoles. No cables. Whoever set it here — if *set* is even the verb — left almost nothing else. The tech is the weather.

Because there is weather. The chamber has a climate that will not sit down.

A sheet of water will travel the length of the west wall like a curtain being torn sideways, hit the north alcove, and explode into droplets so fine they become a white room inside the room. Ten seconds later the same wall is only sweating. Then a pulse comes up the filament — you feel it in your teeth — and a column of water stands, briefly, as a cylinder of glass, and the light inside it goes prismatic, and every wet surface in the chamber throws moving geometry across every other surface, and then the column collapses and the geometry becomes noise again. Steam lifts off the trough in sudden flowers. The air is bathhouse-warm, then a degree hotter, then back. You stop trying to predict. The stone does not. The stone has seen this for longer than any of the marks left on it, and the marks are few.

There is a bronze ring set in the east wall at chest height, green-black, the hole worn oval. A length of ceramic channel, broken, half-buried in a gravel of its own shards, still trying to lead water somewhere that no longer exists. In the south alcove, above the reach of the worst spray, a shallow niche holds a bowl of the same pale stone as the walls, the rim polished by hands or by dripping. Nothing in the bowl now except a skin of water that trembles when the filament pulses. On the steps, if you look back, three iron staples, one missing, the rock around them stained and slick. That is nearly all. People have been here. People did not stay, or did not stay as builders. The place kept its own architecture and allowed them a few courtesies.

Vegetation is an afterthought the room has not quite rejected. In the highest cracks, where the spray arrives as mist and the light is a little less punishing, a few dark mats of something like liverwort hold on, black-green, rubbery, looking cooked. A single pale fern occupies a ledge the size of a book, its fronds permanently beaded, trembling in the updrafts. Algae make faint rust and olive maps in the slower runnels and get scoured off whenever a sheet decides to use that path. Nothing tall. Nothing that would ask for soil. The green things here are stains that learned to live.

Life that moves is rarer. Once, in a lull that lasts perhaps half a minute — the water falling back into ordinary dripping, the filament’s tone dropping a fraction — a fish the color of weak tea appears in the trough, hanging as if the turbulence had been a rumor. It has no hurry. When the next pulse comes it is simply gone, or has become part of the flung water, and you do not see it again. Later a pale cricket picks its way along the dry upper lip of a groove, antennae flattened by humidity, and a sheet takes the wall beneath it and it does not fall. That is the census. The room is too loud and too busy for a population. What lives here lives in the gaps the storm leaves, and the gaps are short.

Sound is the other weather.

The base of it is a continuous shout: water hitting stone, water hitting water, water being torn up the filament and thrown. It fills the chamber the way the light does, with no quiet center. Over that, impacts — the slap of a sheet, the cough of steam, a hollow *whump* when a collapsing column finds the trough. The filament’s tone rides on top, sometimes a single note, sometimes a chord that seems to come from the bones of the room rather than the air. Drips, when they get a chance, are huge, amplified by the stone, each one a knuckle on a table. If you speak, you hear yourself as a thin thing laid over all of this, and you do not speak long. The place is not hostile to voices. It is simply already using the air.

And yet the whole of it is *settled*. That is the strangeness that is not quite strangeness, the unusual thing that does not tip into dream. The turbulence has a tenure. The grooves are the record of a thousand thousand identical storms. The mineral crusts grow and are blasted and grow again in the same maps. The filament has no rust, no flicker of failure; it has the patience of a geological feature that happens to be doing impossible work. You could believe the room has looked like this — bright, wet, roaring, almost empty — since before the language you brought down the steps. The chaos is the climate. The climate is the balance.

Stand in the lee of the east wall, near the bronze ring, and you can last a while. The spray still finds you. Your clothes are immediately a second skin. The light makes the inside of your eyelids coral when you blink. Look up: the ceiling is a low vault, not far, beaded, throwing back a thousand moving coins of brightness. Look along the filament and your depth perception fails; the thing has edges and also does not. Look at the trough and you are looking at a surface that will not be a surface for more than a breath.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to feel them rather than name them.

The pulses have a grain, not a clock. They come in clusters, then a stretch of lesser weather, then a cluster again. After a hard cluster the mist is so dense the far wall dissolves and the light becomes a white blindness with a note in it; then the mist falls out of the air as warm rain and the room reappears, smaller than you remembered, all pale stone and running metal-colored water. Do not put a hand on the filament. The air around it is hotter, and the water there is not water you would want on skin — too fast, too organized. The alcoves are older than the main volume, or feel older; their air is a degree less frantic, and the fern lives in one of them for a reason. The steps will take you out. The light will not follow more than a turn. Above, when you emerge, ordinary day will seem dim and the ordinary quiet will seem like a held breath.

What the filament wants, if it wants, is not available. It lifts water and throws light and sings and has been doing so long enough for the stone to agree. Perhaps it is a pump that became a star. Perhaps it is a lamp that became a spring. Perhaps it is only a leftover from a system whose other pieces are gone, still faithfully performing a function whose name no one in the hamlets above quite knows. They call the place, when they call it anything, the White Throat, or the Shouting Well, and they do not send children down, and sometimes in a dry year someone will fill a jar from the trough in a lull and carry it up, and the water in the jar will be ordinary, and warm, and taste of limestone and rain.

Down here it is never ordinary. It is a small country of wet light and old stone, sparsely inhabited, loudly alive, already finished becoming itself and still, every few breaths, tearing that self apart and putting it back with the same patient hands. You leave louder than you arrived, blinking, wrung, with the tone of the filament still going in the hinge of your jaw, and the surface world, when it takes you back, feels briefly like a place that has forgotten how to be weather.
