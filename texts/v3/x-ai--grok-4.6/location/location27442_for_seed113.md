The cavity is new enough that the cut-faces still remember the blade.

It is not a cave in the old sense. Caves accrue. This place was opened—or grown open—and the walls keep the grammar of that opening: planes meeting at angles that have not yet been argued with by time, ribs of stone left standing like unfinished buttresses, a ceiling that is almost flat in places and then drops, without warning, into a vault. You could walk the long axis in a quarter of an hour if the floor were simple. It is not simple. The floor is a sequence of shallow basins and raised walks, some poured, some extruded, some apparently precipitated in situ from the same wet air that keeps the whole chamber breathing.

Water is the first fact. It does not fall as weather so much as it is always arriving. The stone weeps. Fine films travel the fresh faces and gather in threads, and the threads become cords, and the cords find the engineered channels that were laid for them before anyone lived here. There is a sound like rain that is not rain: a continuous, many-throated pouring, loud in the way a cistern is loud, or a lock, or a room whose only occupant is plumbing. Condensation blooms on every cool surface and then lets go. In the dimness you see it as a grain in the air, a slow glitter without a source. The lower basins stand an inch or a foot deep, depending on the hour. Their edges are sharp. Marsh-plants have already found the shallows—reeds with dark, waxed stems, a kind of velvet moss that thickens on the walks wherever a drip-line persists, something like watercress in the warm slack water, paler than it should be.

No fish. Nothing moves in the basins but the surface, which wrinkles when a drop hits, and then forgets.

The settlement occupies the cavity the way a thought occupies a skull: fitted, incomplete, too much structure for what is present. Towers rise from the raised walks, not crowded, never crowded—thirty, perhaps forty of them, none taller than the vault will allow, which is not very tall. Between them the distances are wrong for a city and right for a ruin that was never lived in. Plazas of a pale, poured material, slightly cupped to shed water into slit-drains. Arcades that run along the dripping walls and then simply stop. A spine of infrastructure down the center: conduits braided as thick as bodies, some clear, some smoked, some carrying a light that is not quite illumination, more a residual knowledge of brightness. The light in the chamber as a whole is the color of a storm just after it has decided not to break. You can see the far wall. You cannot see it well.

The towers are occupied by systems. Their skins are neither masonry nor glass. In places they look grown—lamellar, faintly translucent, beaded with the same condensation as the stone. In places they look assembled from plates that do not quite touch, the gaps filled with a tension you feel in your teeth when you pass. Whatever physics holds those gaps has not been written down where you could read it. Stair-flights cling to exteriors and lead to doors that stand open on rooms of equipment whose purpose is only partly local: heat-exchangers exhaling a soft, tropical breath; lattices in which water is being unmade and remade, stripped of something, given something back; panels that dim and recover in a rhythm like weather. The rooms are empty of people. They have always, you suspect, been empty of people. The city was built at settlement scale for a population that was never the point.

Vegetation has taken the invitation. Not a jungle—there is too much designed emptiness for that—but a lushness that surprises you in the hollow. Vines find the drip-lines and the warm exhalations of the towers and climb with a patience that looks, in this light, like intention. Broad leaves hold beads. In the basins the reeds have already made stands dense enough to whisper when the air moves, which it does, regularly: a cycle of intake and release through vents along the vault, as if the cavity itself were a lung practicing. The plants are the wrong greens, slightly too blue, slightly too black, as if they had learned color from the stone and the dimness rather than from a sun. Some of the moss on the walks is faintly luminous, a light so low it is more a rumor of light, enough to mark the edges of the basins and no more. There are flowers the size of a thumbnail in the warmer alcoves, waxy, scentless. Nothing eats them. Nothing pollinates them that you can see. They open and close on a schedule that does not match any day.

The heat is even, body-warm, the heat of a closed hand. Combined with the wet it would be oppressive if the space were smaller or fuller. Instead it feels like a greenhouse that someone forgot to stock. Your clothes dampen from the inside and the outside at once. The air tastes of minerals and of something faintly sweet, like wet iron and crushed stems.

Sound layers. The pouring never stops. Under it, the infrastructure speaks: a mid-range hum that lives in the conduits, occasional deeper throbs when a lock somewhere cycles, the tick of cooling plates, a high thin note you only notice when it ceases and then returns. The plants contribute almost nothing until a vent-breath moves through a reed-stand, and then there is a dry rushing, loud because the chamber is a sounding-box and there is so little else to absorb it. Footsteps, if you make them, come back from the cut-faces delayed and clarified. The emptiness is acoustic. You hear how unoccupied the volume is.

Along one wall the stone is still changing. Whatever opened the cavity did not finish, or finished in a way that includes aftermath. Fresh planes catch the dimness differently from the older ones, which are perhaps only days older. A fine grit collects in the basins nearest that wall, pale, angular, not yet rounded by any long transport. The channels there have had to be recut already, or have recut themselves; you can see the palimpsest of a slightly different course, abandoned, already furred with moss. The city has been adjusting. A walkway has been extended three meters on a cantilever that should not hold and does. A tower nearest the active face has grown a skirt of new plates overnight, or over the last cycle, closing a gap the weeping had found.

There are no animals. This is not a silence you have to confirm. It is structural. No insects stitch the air above the basins. No larvae in the warm shallows. No bats, though the vault would hold them. The ecology is plants, water, stone, and the city’s unfinished physics. The plants seem to be in conversation with the infrastructure more than with one another: roots visible through the translucent skins of certain conduits, leaves oriented toward the residual-light braids, moss thickening wherever a heat-exchanger breathes. If there is a food web it is mineral and thermal. Growth is visible if you stay. A vine will have crossed a joint you were watching. A reed-stand will have leaned.

At what passes for the center there is a plaza slightly larger than the others, circular, its floor a shallow dish of that pale poured stuff. Water sheets across it in a film too even to be accidental and disappears into a ring-drain so fine you could miss it. Around the ring, at intervals too wide for conversation, stand plinths that might be seats or might be instruments. Their tops are wet. Condensation, or something the plaza is doing. If you stand in the middle the pouring-sound organizes itself; the chamber’s many throats resolve into a chord that is almost musical and then, as you move, is only loud again. This may be design. It may be the accident of an empty resonant volume and a great deal of water.

The far end of the cavity pinches. The vault comes down. The last towers are shorter, more like kiosks or shrines, and then there is only the walk, and the weeping wall, and a door that is not a door so much as a thinning: a place where the stone becomes less certain of itself, and the beyond-modern work has left a membrane you could, if you wished, put a hand through. On the other side of the membrane the sound changes—deeper water, or a larger lung. You do not have to go. The place you are in is already a complete sentence, hamlet-sized, city-shaped, hollow, newly cut, loudly alive with everything except animals and inhabitants.

It will be different when you come back. The active wall will have advanced or sealed. The moss will have taken another walk. A tower will have opened a new throat to the vault and the rain-that-is-not-rain will have found a new register. The film on the circular plaza will be thicker, or gone. The plants will have continued their quiet occupation of a settlement that was built as if people were a phase it could skip. Warmth, wet, dimness, the bright edges of young stone, the long gaps between built things, the systems talking to themselves in a language of pumps and almost-lights. A room the size of a town. A town the density of a held breath.
