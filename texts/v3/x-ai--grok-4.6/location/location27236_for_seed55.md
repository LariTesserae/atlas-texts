The water has been here longer than the memory of anything that could remember. It is not an ocean in the usual sense, with a surface that heaves and a floor that waits. It is the medium the world is made of now: a single cold body that fills every worked interval, every residual hollow, every interval between fitted stones. Pressure is modest. Depth is rarely more than a few dozen body-lengths to the nearest constructed plane. The light that reaches those planes is the color of old iron left in a well, and there is almost none of it.

What the water covers was never landscape. It was labor. For distances that would take weeks to walk if walking were possible, there is no unworked substrate. Every surface was cut, dressed, levered, and seated by hands that used only other stone. Basalt and a dull greenish granite predominate; they were chosen because they were what the vanished quarries gave, and they have been in contact with one another so long that the joints have the intimacy of bone. Edges that were once sharp have been rounded by an erosion so complete it has become a kind of courtesy. Corners have forgotten they were corners. Stair-flights that once rose a few meters now exist as faint undulations under carpets of silt and living tissue. The whole construction has settled into its last possible arrangement: low, interlocking, nearly flat, a continent of masonry that no longer aspires to height.

Through this finished geometry the plants have moved with the patience of the water itself. They are not trees and they are not quite weeds. Pale, strap-like fronds the width of a forearm rise from every horizontal plane and lean in the same direction, the direction of the oldest current. They are dense enough that a swimmer (if there were swimmers) would have to part them with both hands. Between the fronds grow mats of something finer, a cold moss that prefers the vertical faces and the undersides of lintels. In the deeper courtyards the two growths braid until the original architecture is only a rumor of regularity felt through the fingers. The vegetation is lush in the way a winter forest is lush: every available surface claimed, every claim held for centuries, almost no color except the faint yellow-green that appears when a rare photon finds a chloroplast. Growth is measured in the thickness of a fingernail per decade. Nothing eats the plants. Nothing pollinates them. They simply persist, converting the last usable light and the dissolved minerals that leach from the old mortar.

Sound is a private matter between water and stone. There is the long, almost subsonic sigh of the current as it threads the remaining channels. There is the occasional, widely spaced click of a joint adjusting itself by a fraction of a millimeter after another century of load. When a frond finally tears free it does not fall; it hangs in the water and rotates once, twice, then drifts until it lodges against another wall and begins, slowly, to rot into the next generation of moss. That is the loudest event most places will know.

Temperature never quite reaches the point of ice, but it lives next door. The water has the clarity of things that have been cold for a very long time. Silt, when it is disturbed, hangs in curtains that take days to settle. There are no seasons that a human nervous system would recognize, only a slower pulse: a faint strengthening of the current when some distant, unseen ice-shelf calves, a corresponding dimming of the already insufficient light. The world has weather, but the weather belongs to a scale larger than continents. One current system may take a human lifetime to reverse. The plants lean a little more one way, then, generations later, a little more the other.

No animals live here. The ecological niche that would have been occupied by grazers, filter-feeders, or scavengers is simply empty. The water is too cold, the light too thin, the chemistry too settled. What remains is a closed conversation between masonry, plant, and the patient solvent that holds them both.

It is possible to move through this place for days and encounter only variation in the same few themes: a wider plaza whose paving stones have been planed by water into a single undulating sheet; a colonnade whose columns have become indistinguishable from the kelp that uses them as trellis; a long, low aqueduct that now serves as a highway for the current and for the pale ribbons that trail from its lips. Everything is near, layered, almost touching. Density is the density of an overgrown library whose shelves have begun to grow into one another. You are never more than an arm’s length from worked stone or living tissue.

The builders, if the word still applies, left no metal and almost no timber. What they left is the shape of their effort, now so thoroughly occupied by later, slower lives that the distinction between made and grown has become a matter of archaeology rather than experience. The world has reached the state it was always going to reach: finished, filled, cold, and nearly silent. It will stay this way. Change continues, but only in the way a mountain continues after it has already become a plain.
