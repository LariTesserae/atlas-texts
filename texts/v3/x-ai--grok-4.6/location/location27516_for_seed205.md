The highland does not begin with stone. It begins with grain.

What you first take for a continent is the compressed body of something that never agreed to finish dying. For a thickness no one has successfully measured—deeper than wells, deeper than the memory of the people who live on it—the ground is lignified tissue: cork gone to strata, heartwood gone to canyon-wall, cambium pressed into seams that still, if you put your ear to a fresh cut, click with residual turgor. Heat has cooked the upper fathoms into a dark, resinous plateaux-country. The sun here is ordinary, a white-yellow disc that does what suns do, but the plateau sits high enough that the air arrives already thin and already hot, and the plant-ground drinks that heat the way other lands drink rain. By midmorning the bark-skin is too hot to cross barefoot. By afternoon it smells of varnish and cooked sap. Night does not truly cool it; it only lets the heat stand still.

Water is a rumor that keeps coming true in small ways. There are no proper rivers. There are runnels—black, glossy scars where last season’s storm found a fault in the grain and raced downhill for an hour, then vanished into the absorbent dark. There are cistern-galls, swollen cavities the living mass grows the way a body grows a blister, filled with a liquor that is not quite water and not quite sap, drinkable if you can stand the tannin and the faint pulse against the palate. There is condensation. In the shade-canyons, where the grain rears into walls the color of old blood, the air still carries enough leftover wet to bead on the cooler inner bark at dawn. People collect it on hides. Animals collect it on tongues. Then the sun finds the canyon floor and the beads are gone and the day is a kiln again.

The contradiction of this place—the thing that will not resolve until you have walked it for weeks—is that the world is made of plant and yet the world is mostly empty. Vegetation is the substrate, the architecture, the weather’s partner, the grave and the cradle. It is not a forest. A forest would be crowded. Here the living uprights, the meristem-towers, stand so far apart that each one owns its own horizon. You can walk a day on the open plate and see only one: a column so large that its shadow is a climate. The towers are not trees in any useful sense. They are the still-awake organs of the continental body, thrust up through the dead-and-living crust, each a mountain of cambium and leaf-scale and hanging atrium. Their crowns make private weather. Clouds snag on them and tear. In the heat they transpire so violently that a pale fog walks around their midsections like a skirt, and sometimes—rarely, loudly—that fog collapses into a local rain that never reaches the ground ten kilometers out. Between towers: the bark-desert. Weathered, established, in its own long balance. Wind has sanded the grain into dunes of fibrous dust. Old lightning-scars run for leagues, glassed and black. The erosion here is not the erosion of rock; it is the erosion of a body that has had geological time to become landscape and has done so without becoming anything else.

You hear the place before the towers resolve.

The sound is not one sound. It is occupancy. The fauna is overwhelming in the precise sense that there is no unoccupied register. The wood is tunneled. The tunnels have tenants. Xylophages the size of calves chew with a regularity that becomes, at distance, a single low note, a cello-drone of mastication that never stops because the generations never stop. Above that: stridulation. Things with rasps for legs, things that saw the air to speak, things that have turned the heat itself into a scraped hymn. Higher still: wing-thunder. The flyers here are not birds first. They are hinged, chitinous, some of them large enough that their shadows are cool rooms you can walk in for three breaths. When a flock turns, the sound is a door being torn off a cathedral. Under everything, always, the wood itself: growth-groan, the slow hydraulic shout of a continent still adding rings. In storm—when the rare rain actually arrives, a vertical argument between heat and the high thin air—the towers boom like struck drums and the cistern-galls overflow in sheets, and for an hour the deafening acquires a wet edge, a hiss over the chew, and then the ground drinks it and the hiss dies and the chew remains.

The animals are not visitors on a plant. They are the plant’s motion. Some are obviously themselves: the bark-wolves with jaws like adzes, the gall-otters that swim the cisterns and taste of tannin, the thread-deer that move in single-file lines so thin they look like stitches walking. Others fail the category. There are herds that photosynthesize along their spines and go still at noon to feed on light. There are colonial mats that flow like shadow and digest fallen chitin and leave the grain polished. There are things that fruit. In the lee of a tower, in the brief wet after a self-made rain, a slope will erupt in mobile bodies that were buds yesterday—legged, eyed, already hungry—and by evening the slope is grazed clean and the new animals have joined the general roar or been folded back into it. Predator and prey are less a chain than a weather. Everything eats. The wood eats the dead by growing through them. The dead eat the wood by having been born from it. You sleep, if you sleep, in a cavity you have smoked against the smaller tenants, and you wake with resin in your hair and a new hole in the wall that was not there at dusk, perfectly round, already occupied.

People are a minority weather.

They are not many. The built mark is faint because the ground heals and because there is not much to build with that is not already the house. You find platforms cut into the inner wall of a shade-canyon, the cuts shiny with old sap, the floors padded with beaten pith. You find ladders of lashed rib and vine that go nowhere now, the tower they once served having shrugged in its sleep and closed the door. You find cairns of spent tools: adzes of work-hardened resin, blades of a simple copper the plants themselves concentrate in certain galls (the metal comes out green and soft and is folded cold), awls of flyer-spine. No cities. A workshop under an overhang, a smoke-blackened ceiling, a rack of drying cistern-bladders. Carved signs that the living wall has half-swallowed, so that a prayer becomes a knot in the grain. The tech is hand and heat and patience. Fire is used the way a surgeon uses a knife: little, exactly, with an apology ready. Everything is fuel. Everything is also kin. A careless burn can run along a resin-seam for a week and take a tower’s weather with it, and the sound of that—the scream of sap flashing to steam in a confined gallery—is a sound the plateau remembers. The people remember it too. They have stories in which fire is a person who was asked to leave and did not.

The rules are the rules of a body that became a country.

Do not cut the white wood if you can cut the brown; the white still argues. Do not camp on a seam that is warm at night; something is moving there, or growing, which is the same. Water from a new gall must be left until the pulse slows; too-new and it is a hormone, and you will dream the tower’s dream, which is only hunger arranged as landscape. The flyers own the columns of rising heat; walk the flats at noon if you must move, when even they are grounded and stunned. If a herd of the photosynthesizers blocks a pass, wait. They will finish their light and become meat again and wander on. If you hear the chew-note change pitch, something large has died in the wood beneath you and the tenants are reorganizing. Move.

The strangeness is not a costume the place puts on. It is the logic followed all the way out. Time on the plateau is ring-time. A tower does not have years so much as episodes of swelling. People date events by which seam was still soft, by which gall had not yet opened. Direction is grain-direction; “north” is a word they know and do not need. Sometimes, in the deep pith-valleys where the compressed deadwood is paler and the heat pools strangely, sound behaves as if it had weight. A shout falls. A whisper travels the valley floor for a mile and arrives intact, a little cooler than when it left. The people use this. Messages are spoken into the pale wood and collected down-valley at dusk. Whether the wood is a medium or a participant is not a question they waste daylight on. The fauna uses it too. In those valleys the roar has structure—call and answer, territory, the long grammatical complaint of things that have never seen a sky without a tower in it.

Weather of its own, yes. The plateau is large enough to turn a storm into a nationality. Heat rises off the bark in sheets you can see, warping the ordinary light into water that is not there. That false water is how travelers die: they walk toward a cistern that is only air and the day finishes them. Real storms come from the far rim, where the living crust finally shears against whatever older geology still pretends to be underneath, and they arrive as a brown wall of fibrous dust and then, if the towers have been transpiring hard, as lightning that prefers the tall wet organs, and then as the brief, deafening rain. After, the whole highland darkens two shades and smells like a cupboard of spices and wounds. The runnels perform their one-hour rivers. The galls brim. The mobile fruiting bodies come out to drink and to be born and to be eaten. By the next afternoon the plate is pale again, the dust is a skin, the chew-note has not paused, and the towers stand in their private fogs as if nothing of scale had occurred.

You can walk until the first tower is a memory and the next is still only a discoloration of the heat, and the ground under you will still be the same continuous organism, weathered into the idea of a desert, inhabited past any silence, ringing. The emptiness is real. The fullness is real. They have had a long time to learn how to be the same fact.
