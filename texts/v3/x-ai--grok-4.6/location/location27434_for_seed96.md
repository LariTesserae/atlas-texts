The highland does not begin with a horizon of rock and then acquire walls. It is walls, continuously, as far as the basin holds. From the last uncut shoulder you see it as a single pale glare: a valley-sized work of edges, so densely abutted that the eye cannot find a courtyard, only rims sharing rims, tanks sharing lips, spillways that are also alleys that are also the next tank’s far bank. Nothing grows. Nothing walks. The light is hard and high, and every plane is wet.

They dressed the mountain into this. The stone is a fine, almost white calcareous stuff, still showing the grain of the chisel, still smelling faintly of the quarry when the air is cold enough. Mortar, where they used it, is paler than the block and has not yet taken the long stain of years; in many places there is no mortar at all, only fitted faces so close that capillary water occupies the joint and will not leave. The work is young. Corners have not rounded. Tool-pocks remain as a kind of writing on every riser. At the eastern margin the conversion is incomplete: half-freed blocks lean in their beds, wooden levers and fiber ropes lie where the last shift set them down, a ramp of compacted chips climbs a face that is still mountain on one side and already architecture on the other. No one is there now. The silence has the particular quality of a site that expects a return and has stopped expecting it.

Water is not an ornament here. It is the other material. It stands in every depression to within a finger of the lip. It sheets, almost without current, across pavements cut with a barely perceptible fall. It weeps from the porous faces as if the walls were lungs. In the covered runs—low, square tunnels you can hear only if you kneel—it moves so slowly that a leaf, had there been a leaf, would take a morning to pass from one grating to the next. There are no rivers in the ordinary sense. There is a highland sea disassembled into ten thousand shallow rooms and then reassembled by gravity so lazy it feels like intention. After rain the whole basin brightens further, each tank becoming a lidless mirror, and the glare is such that you walk by memory of edge rather than by sight. Between rains the weeping continues. The stone never dries. Condensation finds the underside of every overhang and gathers, and drops, and the drops are the loudest events for hours.

The air is cool and thin. You feel elevation in the lungs and in the way sound fails to carry: a slipped pebble, a palm on wet stone, then nothing. Wind arrives only at the upper rims, combed to almost nothing by the packing of the work. Down among the tanks the atmosphere is still, bright, faintly mineral. Breath shows. Fingertips go numb if you trail them too long in the water. The water itself is clear to a depth it does not have—most basins are no more than a person is tall—and cold enough to ache. In certain deep cisterns, rare and square and plumbed by shafts that look like wells that forgot to finish, the water is darker, and if you wait you can see the very slow rotation of silt that has nowhere else to go.

Density is the law of the place. There is no unmade ground. Whatever soil once softened this hanging valley has been stripped, burned out, packed into the cores of retaining walls, or washed through the system and deposited as a pale flour in the lowest tanks. You cannot stand between things; you stand on them. Circulation is a matter of choosing which rim is wide enough for a foot. Some rims are a handspan. Some are generous walkways scored with the same shallow grooves that keep water from sheeting into the next chamber too fast. Crossings are slabs, unrailed. The geometry is not a city plan. It is more like a single thought repeated until it filled the available basin: hold, pass, hold, pass, always a little downhill, always shared. Houses, if that is even the word, are not distinguishable from cisterns that happen to have a dry ledge. There are no doors. There are lintels over channels, and niches where a body might sit out of the brightest light, and that is the whole of shelter. Roofs are scarce—here and there a corbelled cap, primitive and careful, over a shaft—so the sky is the ceiling and the sky is too much.

Light behaves as if the valley had been built to break it. Wet stone returns the sun in fragments. A thousand shallow waters return it whole, then shiver. Midday is nearly uninhabitable without squinting; the pale rock seems to generate its own weather of brightness. Morning and late afternoon are the hours when the place becomes legible: long shadows rake the tanks, and you can read the slight differences in height that constitute the entire hydrology, the patient staircase of levels falling away toward the western outlet, which is not a gorge but another construction—a lip, a series of stepped pans, a last bright sheet that disappears under a dressed sill and is heard no more. At night, if you remained, the wetness would hold a leftover shine long after the peaks have gone black. There are no lamps. There is no one to light them.

What is unusual is not hidden. It is simply that the work has no outside once you are in it, and no purpose that resembles dwelling. The channels do not serve fields; there are no fields. They do not feed forges or baths or gardens. They feed one another. The whole basin is a catchment raised to the condition of a doctrine: water must be kept at the surface, divided, bright, almost still, and never allowed to become a river or a swamp or a loss. Overflow exists, but it is shy—a notch, a whisper into the next room. In a few places the builders inlaid the lips with a darker stone, a simple volcanic scrap, as if to mark a measure. The measures mean something sequential. Walk them in order and the water beside you falls by the width of a nail each time, and after an hour of walking you have descended the height of a person, and the air is no warmer.

Ecology, such as it is, is chemistry. A gel of minerals skins some of the oldest tanks—old in the sense of finished first, a few seasons ago—iridescent, thin, not alive in any way that answers to touch. Stains climb the walls in iron and copper ghosts, mapping where the weep is constant. In the quarry margin, rock flour and meltwater make a milk that dries to a crust and is rained off again. There are no insects to skate the surfaces. No birds mistake the glare for a lake, or if they do they do not come this high, or they came once and the emptiness taught them. The silence is therefore not the silence of wilderness. It is the silence of a tool laid down.

Time here is the time of seepage and of evaporative loss so slight you would need marks to prove it. A season will lift the levels a handsbreadth after the snows on the remaining peaks, and then the sun will take it back, and the weeping will even the difference from tank to tank until the whole basin agrees again. Construction, if it continues, continues at that speed: a course of blocks in a month, a new shaft in a year, the mountain turned inward upon itself by hand tools and patience. You can still see how they did it. Dressing floors. Spalls in drifts. A wooden mallet swollen with wet. Peg-holes in a face where a scaffold lived. The craft is pre-metal in spirit even where a rare copper dowel stains the stone green; most joins are gravity, friction, and the knowledge of how this particular rock likes to split. Everything that exists was lifted by bodies. The density is a record of how many times a stone can be asked to move.

If there is a rule, it is this: do not make a hole that does not hold, and do not leave a surface that is not a decision. The basin obeys. Even the unfinished eastern edge already weeps in the new cuts, as if the mountain had been waiting to be divided into rooms of water and had only needed the first accurate blow. Stand still long enough and you hear only the bright, small percussion of drops, widely spaced, falling into a fullness that does not change while you listen. The place is not ancient. It is merely complete enough to have begun its slower life—the life of a made highland that will, for a long time yet, remain sharp, saturated, uninhabited, and shining.
