The highland sits in a long, shallow dish of pale rock, high enough that the air has a thin, metallic taste and the sun comes in without haze. There is no soil. There is no green. From one rim of the dish to the other—far enough that the far side is only a brighter smear of the same thing—the ground is occupied by standing stone.

They are not columns in any architectural sense. They are the landscape itself, crowded together the way teeth crowd a jaw. Most are taller than a person, some twice that, a few much taller, all of them close enough that a body must turn sideways to pass. Their surfaces are the same bone-white or faint grey-green mineral, long-weathered into soft vertical flutings and shallow concavities. Every face weeps. Not dramatically, not in falls, but in a continuous, even film that beads, slides, and reforms without pause. The water is cold. It has the clarity of melt and the faint mineral bite of something that has lived inside rock for a very long time.

Between the stones the ground is a network of narrow, worn channels, all of them full, all of them moving at the same unhurried speed. The water does not pool and it does not flood. It simply occupies every low place and keeps moving, a single connected skin that has been doing this long enough to have forgotten any other arrangement. Where two channels meet they do not swirl or argue; they join and continue. The equilibrium is old. The shapes of the stones, the widths of the gaps, the depth of every groove have been negotiated over a span that no longer has weather in it. Rain, if it ever comes, would only be more of the same water already present.

Light arrives as glare. The wet stone throws it back in sheets and needles. There is no shade worth the name; the pillars stand too close for true shadow and too far for darkness. Everything is bright to the point of erasure. Highlights crawl continuously down every wet face. The air itself seems slightly thickened with fine droplets that never quite become mist and never quite vanish. You feel them on the skin as a cool, constant damp.

The sound is the water, multiplied past counting. A million thin films sliding, a million tiny impacts in the channels, the low hydraulic rush through constrictions too narrow to see—the whole of it adds into a single, unchanging roar. It is not rhythmic. It does not swell or fade. It occupies the air the way the stones occupy the ground: completely, without remainder. After a short time the roar ceases to be something you hear and becomes the condition of being there, a pressure in the chest and behind the eyes. Speech would be useless. Thought arrives already half-drowned.

Nothing grows. The chemistry that would allow even lichen has long since been rinsed away or never arrived. The only movement besides water is rare and small. Once, far off between distant pillars, something the colour of wet stone itself shifts position by a hand’s breadth and then is still again. It may be an animal that has learned to be indistinguishable from its surroundings, or it may be a slower settling of rock. Either way it does not repeat. The place does not encourage repetition of that kind.

There are no paths because every gap is already a path and none of them lead anywhere that is different from here. The dish extends, the stones continue, the water continues, the light continues, the roar continues. Elevation holds the whole arrangement in a cool, bright stasis. Whatever weather exists outside the rims does not reach in, or has been absorbed into the same perpetual seepage. Time here is the time of the water film: always now, always the same thickness, always the same temperature, always the same unhurtable descent.

If you stand still long enough the individual stones begin to feel like a single crowded body, breathing out moisture through every pore. The crowding is not hostile. It is simply the finished shape of the place—the form that erosion, water, and stillness agreed upon and then refused to revise. You can walk for a long time and the view will not change in kind, only in the particular arrangement of the nearest pale, glistening presences. Behind you the gaps close. Ahead they open onto more of the same. The roar does not mark distance. The light does not mark hour. The water does not mark season.

This is the entire country of it: a high, packed, brilliantly wet mineral field that has already become whatever it was going to be, and has no further intention of becoming anything else.
