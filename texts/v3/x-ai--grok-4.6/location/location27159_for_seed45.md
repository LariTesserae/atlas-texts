The highland does not yet know it is finished. It occupies a belt of extreme elevation, a regional expanse of raw upthrust so newly arrived that the surrounding world has not had time to name it or weather it into habit. From any vantage the distances remain open and uncluttered: kilometers of nearly featureless mineral plain separate one abrupt feature from the next. The air here is thin enough that sound travels poorly and the horizon, when it can be seen at all, feels both intimate and unreachable.

Light arrives as a residual dusk that never quite becomes day. A high, motionless veil of lofted dust and ionized grit hangs at the upper edge of the atmosphere, scattering what little stellar radiation reaches this altitude into a uniform, directionless gloom the color of old iron. Stars are occasionally visible as faint, trembling points, but they do not illuminate. Warmth does not come from above. It radiates from below, a steady geothermal bloom that keeps the rock itself at a comfortable, dry heat even while the thin air would otherwise freeze. The ground is never cold to the touch. In places it is almost feverish.

There is no water. Not as liquid, not as ice, not as vapor that could be seen or felt. The chemistry here is mineral and gaseous only: anhydrous salts that never deliquesce, metallic oxides, sheets of volcanic glass still cooling from recent extrusion, and a fine, electrostatic dust of pulverized feldspar and olivine that lifts in slow, almost silent veils whenever the land shifts. The absence is so complete that the very idea of rain or dew feels like a rumor from a lower, wetter epoch.

The land itself is embryonic. Entire ridges appear over the course of hours, pushed upward along fresh faults that have not yet decided their throw. A plateau that was level at one observation may, later, display a knife-edged hogback of black glass several hundred meters high, its faces still slick and unfractured, radiating residual heat. Elsewhere a circular depression sinks without warning, its walls stepping downward in concentric rings of unweathered basalt. These events are frequent and uncoordinated. The highland is in a state of continuous, chaotic orogeny; creation and local collapse occur side by side, yet the net motion is upward and outward. Erosion has barely begun. There are no rounded slopes, no alluvial fans, no talus that has had time to stabilize. Every surface looks freshly broken or freshly born.

Because the air is so thin, the turbulence is mostly silent. A nearby ridge may split with a sound no louder than a dry cough. Distant events register only as a faint, subsonic pressure against the inner ear or a momentary shimmer in the dust. The prevailing impression is of an immense, quiet restlessness. Occasionally a deeper vibration travels through the rock itself, felt in the bones more than heard: the mantle adjusting, or a new pluton shouldering toward the surface.

Life, in any familiar sense, has not arrived. There are no plants. In a few sheltered fractures, where geothermal warmth and certain metallic ions coincide, thin iridescent films of chemotrophic mineral accrete—neither quite biological nor purely geological. They grow in slow, radial plates the color of tarnished bronze or pale sulfur, then slough away when the next tremor arrives. Nothing moves of its own volition. No insects, no scavengers, no wind-borne seeds. The ecology is pre-life or post-life; the distinction has not yet been settled.

Scattered across the openness are faint, almost accidental marks of construction. A single low wall of fitted but unmortared stone, already half-swallowed by a later upthrust. A ring of standing slabs whose alignment no longer corresponds to any current celestial or magnetic reference. An isolated platform of fused glass, perfectly circular, its purpose unreadable. These artifacts are few and widely separated. They do not suggest occupation so much as brief, abandoned attempts to measure or to stand still. The land has already begun to incorporate them, tilting their foundations, encasing their edges in new crystal.

The rules here are those of a world still deciding its own grammar. Gravity is reliable but the ground is not. Time is locally inconsistent: a slope that required days to climb may, on the return, have steepened or vanished. Dust remembers the last several movements and will sometimes rise in the exact reverse sequence of a previous collapse, as if replaying it. The darkness itself seems to have weight and direction; it pools in new depressions and thins over rising ground. Nothing here is ancient. Everything is either just arriving or just departing. The highland continues to assemble itself in the dim, warm silence, ridge by unweathered ridge, toward a shape it has not yet chosen.
