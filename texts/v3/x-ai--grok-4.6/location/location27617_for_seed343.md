The plateau does not announce itself as high. It has been high for so long that height has become a kind of weather, a quality of light and of distance, not a drama of peaks. Whatever mountains once stood here finished their dying ages ago. Wind and a thin, intermittent rain sanded them into a single continuous table, so old and so thoroughly resolved that even the last ridges have the softness of something that has chosen its shape and will not change it again. You walk on the roof of a worn-down world. The horizon is very far and very level. Clouds form *below* the rim, in the gulf, and crawl along it like slow white animals that will never be allowed up.

The ground is not ground. Soil ended here. In its place there is a living felt, continuous, warm, faintly damp the way a working muscle is damp. Roots, rhizomes, compressed generations of leaf and thallus have become one another until there is no longer a difference between organism and terrain. It gives underfoot, not like mud, like the hide of something enormous and asleep. At dawn the whole surface beads with condensation; by midmorning the beads have been drunk back in. Rain, when it comes, is brief and almost polite—sheets that hiss and vanish, leaving the felt darker for an hour. Water does not run. It is held in shallow dark pools that form in the slow hollows of the mat, black mirrors the size of rooms, of houses, of small lakes, their edges furred with the same living tissue that made them. You can walk a day and never hear a stream.

Out of this floor the great plants rise, and they rise so rarely that each one is a destination. Kilometers of empty felt between them. They are not a forest. They are architecture that happened to be alive: single trunks with the girth of citadels, hollow, ribbed, exhaling a sweet vegetable heat; umbels that cast twilight over a valley’s worth of ground; leaves so large their veins are ridges you could lose a path in. They are the oldest standing things. Their bark is not bark so much as a weathered outer weather, lichenous, pitted, in places glassy where resins have wept and hardened for centuries. Between them the air is a warm vacancy. You can see the next titan as a dark vertical weather on the horizon and walk toward it until evening and still not arrive. The emptiness is not barren. It is simply unoccupied by mass. The sky is ordinary daylight, a clean high sun that the titans interrupt only locally, in islands of shade.

What fills the vacancy is animals.

They are the true weather of the place. The air is never empty of them. Midges and beetles and things with no common names move in densities that have weight; they dim the sun in traveling continents of bodies, not a plague so much as a second atmosphere with a pulse. Birds work those continents the way fish work a current. Things live *in* the felt—segmented, armored, soft—and come up in tides, so that a stretch of ground that was only damp green at noon is a moving pelt by late afternoon. None of this looks crowded from a distance. The distances are too large. Up close the air is a grain, a blur, a constant slight collision against skin and mouth and eyes.

And the sound.

The sound is a physical fact, like heat. It does not come from one direction. Wingbeat and stridulation and the dry rasp of too many legs on living fiber braid into a single pressure that sits in the skull and will not leave. It changes key with the hour—higher, frantic, at the hatches; a lower, grinding roar when the larger things move through the mat; a thin screaming edge when the wind finds the hollow titans and plays them. There is no silence to remember. People who stay learn to stuff their ears with the resin-wool that weeps from certain seams in the great trunks, and even then the sound arrives as vibration through the jaw, through the soles. At night the register drops and thickens. You sleep, if you sleep, inside a held chord.

The few structures that exist do not argue with any of this. They are slight: platforms woven from the titan-fibers and lashed into the lee of a trunk, black with old smoke; ladders of trained vine that have been persuaded, over years, to hold a particular shape; low windbreaks of stacked chitin and felt-brick, already being overgrown. Paths on the mat last a season and then the living ground closes. What tools there are are hand-scale—scrapers of flaked stone, awls of bone, cordage, a rare beaten ornament of soft metal that someone carried up from a lower world and then stopped needing. No one builds a wall that would pretend to keep the animals out. The work is smaller: harvest the sweet exudate that collects in the leaf-axils after rain; take the fat-bodied things that come to the dark pools at dusk; dry what can be dried in the brief hard sun; move when a titan sheds.

A shedding is the one catastrophe the plateau still permits itself. A leaf the size of a hillside lets go. It does not fall so much as lower, taking a day, booming when it finally meets the felt. Then there is a new country of rotting green, sweet and deafening with the things that come to eat it, and for a year the local sound is different, and then the mat has taken the leaf back into itself and the emptiness resumes.

At the rim—days out, if you walk toward the place where the light changes—the plateau does not break. It forgets how to be flat. The living ground thickens, then thins, then begins a long worn decline that has been rounding itself since before names. You can stand on that lip in ordinary afternoon light, warm wind coming up out of the white gulf, the roar of the insects suddenly given a direction because now there is an edge for the wind to work, and look down onto the tops of clouds. Nothing below is distinct. The old mountains spent themselves making this one finished surface, and the surface has no further ambition except to remain, damp, loud, nearly empty of objects, almost entirely full of life.
