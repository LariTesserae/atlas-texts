The first thing is not the dark. The first thing is that the rock is sweating.

It has been sweating for longer than anyone who lives here has a word for. The walls do not drip so much as they perspire in a fine, continuous film, and the film gathers in old channels that were first cut by water and later deepened by hands, then left alone until the water and the hands became the same motion. You cannot tell, in most places, where the carving stops and the seepage begins. Calcite has sealed the tool-marks. The tool-marks have guided the calcite. The chamber has arrived at a finished dampness.

It is a single pocket. That is the whole of it: a lentil of space, pinched at both ends, wide enough in the middle that three people might stand abreast if they did not mind touching, and high enough that a short person can walk without bowing if they keep to the midline. Everything else is stacked into that lentil. The settlement is not *in* a cave. The settlement *is* the cave, partitioned until there is almost no cave left.

They built by subtraction and by packing. Niches first, then shelves of packed clay and river-cobble, then walls of fitted stone so thin they ring if you tap them, then more niches cut into those walls, then hanging floors of split slate lashed with gut and root-fiber that is not root, only the memory of a material that used to be plant and is now a black, mineralized lace they still harvest from the oldest seams. There are no living plants. There have not been. Whatever green once sent filaments down this far has gone to stone, and the lace is gathered like ore. The floors hang one above another until the pocket is a hive of low rooms, each room the size of a lying-down, each ceiling another family’s floor. You smell your upstairs neighbor’s lamp-fat. You hear their knees.

The light is the color of old tallow and wet limestone. It does not travel. It sits on surfaces and dies there. A dozen small flames in the whole pocket, never more, because more would eat the air and the air is already a broth. Each flame has a hood of soot-blackened shell or hammered copper so thin the copper is almost a rumor. Primitive, yes: the metal is rare, beaten from a single seam they found once and have been stretching ever since. Most tools are flint and bone and the dense gray ceramic they fire in a kiln that is itself a niche, always warm, always the same warm, because the fire in it is never allowed to go out and never allowed to grow. Equilibrium as a craft.

Water is everywhere that people are not, and several places they are. It sheets the outer rock. It threads the joints. It stands in basins cut into every threshold so that you step over a black rectangle of yourself before you enter. The basins are not decorative. They keep the film from crossing the sleeping-ledges. They also keep the sound honest. Because the sound is the other architecture.

There is a throat at the pinched downstream end of the lentil, a slot no wider than a forearm, and through that slot the collected seep of the whole pocket falls six or seven body-lengths into a cistern no one has seen the bottom of in living memory. The fall is not a roar in the usual sense. It is a single, unvarying chord, a thick vertical note with a grain in it, like a voice speaking one word forever. It fills the pocket completely. There is no quiet corner. Infants are born into it and old people die in it and no one can remember a time when the pitch shifted. When someone new comes (rarely; the ways in are two, both crawls, both known only to those who already live here) they spend three days unable to hear speech, and then the chord becomes the floor of hearing, and talk happens on top of it the way lichen happens on top of stone, except there is no lichen.

The temperature is the temperature of a mouth. Cool. Not cold. Breath is visible if you stand near the upstream pinch, where a slower air comes in from a higher dark and condenses on the first surfaces it finds. Those surfaces are teeth of flowstone, old and blunt, the color of cheap butter. People hang their spare bindings there to take the damp in a useful way. Clothes here are never dry. They are a managed wet. Skin is a managed wet. The rule is not to be dry; the rule is not to let the wet become still on you. Still water on skin is how the white bloom starts, the chalky film that means the rock has begun to think of you as another surface to mineralize. They scrape it off with slate. They do not talk about the ones who stopped scraping.

Fauna is an event. Once in a long while a pale thing the length of a finger comes up the throat against the fall, working the film at the edge, and someone may catch it with a loop of fiber. It has no eyes worth naming. It tastes of cold iron and is eaten the same day, shared in slivers, because leaving it would be to invite the smell of it into a space that cannot afford another smell. Other than that: the small clicking in the highest seams, which might be a creature or might be the rock cooling by a fraction it will regain by morning. No one has seen the clicker. The clicker does not change its mind.

Time of day is a rumor from elsewhere. There is a discoloration on one upstream wall that some claim brightens, a bruise of lesser dark, and the lamps are trimmed by that bruise, or by habit that has outlasted the bruise. The work does not change. Stone is dressed. Fiber is twisted. The kiln is fed with a black cake they make from the mineral lace and a grease rendered from the rare catch and from themselves, carefully, in the way of people who have few surplus substances. Children are taught the crawls. The basins are scooped. The chord continues.

What is unusual is not any single object. It is the degree to which the pocket has finished itself. The hanging floors no longer sag; they have been shimmed with wedges that calcified in place. The paths of elbows and hips are polished into every corner. The air moves in a circuit so exact you can set a flake of ash on it and watch it return. Nothing is being built that was not already implied. Nothing is falling that has not already found the angle at which it will fall forever. The settlement is not dying and not growing. It is occupying its own shape the way water occupies a cup.

If you put your palm on the downstream wall, beside the throat, you feel the chord as a tremble in the bone. The wall is thin there. On the other side of it is more of the same wet dark, uninhabited, uncarved, continuing. They do not go. The pocket is the amount of world that can be held. Beyond the pocket the sound would be the same and there would be no basins, and that is not a life, that is only weather.

Sleep is layered. You lie in your slot and the person below you shifts and the person above you shifts and the chord does not shift, and the film on the outer rock keeps arriving, and the basins keep their black rectangles, and the copper rumor of the lamp-hoods holds its little fat-colored coins of light, and the kiln breathes the same breath it has breathed since before the oldest living memory, which is not very old, because memory here is short and the stone is long, and the two have reached an arrangement.

In the arrangement there is a kind of city: streets that are gaps, plazas that are the wider middle where the hanging floors stop short and six or seven faces can be seen at once, a well that is the throat, a gate that is a crawl. Density is the civic principle. You know everyone by the particular interruption they make in the chord when they speak. You know them by smell, which is limestone and tallow and the sweet-stale of crowded heat trapped in cool rock. You know them by the worn places their bodies have left. The urban fact is not size. It is that no surface is left unassigned.

And still the rock sweats. And still the fall speaks its one word. And still no green thing argues. The world, in this pocket, has already happened, and is happening as a maintenance of that fact, wet-handed, close-quartered, dim, and loud in a way that has forgotten how to begin or end.
