The world has already finished becoming stone. What remains is almost nothing you could call a surface: a continental flatness so complete, so long-ground, that “horizon” is a rumor passed between pockets of thinner mist. Elevation here is a memory the rock still keeps in its grain—old bedding planes, the ghosts of mountain roots—but nothing stands. The last ridges were worn to smears an age ago. You walk, when you can walk, on a floor that is the planet’s last sentence, spoken once and then left to weather.

It is wet the way a lung is wet. Not a sea exactly, not a marsh exactly, though both words try and fail. Water is the occupancy. It stands in sheets a finger deep across leagues. It hangs as a warm, condensing atmosphere so thick that breathing is a kind of drinking. It weeps from every exposed face of the ancient pavement, not because the stone is porous in any ordinary sense, but because the stone has been saturated for so long that saturation became its nature. There is no dry underside. Lift a flake of the old crust and the underside is already beaded, already streaming, already trying to become weather.

The light is the color of something heard through a wall. Dim, perpetual, without a disk you can point to. What illumination there is arrives pre-used, scattered through vapor until direction dies. Dawn and dusk have no jurisdiction; the sky is a low, warm lid of condensate that never quite rains and never quite stops raining. Visibility is a local accident. You see fifty paces, or five, or a sudden cathedral of clear air a mile across in which the wet pavement shines like dark glass—and then the cathedral folds, and you are in the close again.

Packed, and yet almost empty of the living things that usually pack a world. Vegetation is a rumor of green: mats of something algal in the standing sheets, the occasional pale reed that looks less grown than precipitated, a few low, rubbery cushions clinging to the rarest hints of slope. They do not compete. There is nothing to compete for except a slightly less drowning inch. Between them: occupancy of another kind. The air is full. The shallows are full. The sound is full. There is no interval in which a presence is not already standing.

The sound is the first architecture. It is not wind, though wind is in it. It is not surf, though the sheets of water are forever being lifted, slapped, dragged, and laid down again by a turbulence that has no storm-front and no calm. It is a continuous, deafening occupation—a roar with rooms inside it, a roar that has learned harmony and then forgotten the lesson, a roar that sometimes articulates, for a few seconds, the shape of a word no throat could hold. You do not hear it so much as stand inside it. After a while the bones accept it as weather. After a longer while you notice that the roar has weather of its own: cells of louder and less-loud, migrating fronts of pitch, sudden dropouts in which the silence is so complete it feels like a physical object, a slab of mute dropped onto the flats—and then the slab shatters and the world is noise again.

This is the paradox the place will not resolve. The lithic world is finished. It has reached the end-state of erosion, the oldest stable sentence, the nearly-flat. Nothing more will be carved from it that has not already been carved and recarved into meaninglessness. And yet nothing is still. The water, the air, the dim, the sound—these have not agreed to the ending. They are in a storm-state that does not pass. Continents of mist assemble, thicken into almost-terrain, walk a hundred miles on legs of rain, and dissolve. Sheets of standing water suddenly remember how to be a river and run, carving nothing (there is nothing left to carve), then forget and lie down again. The atmosphere condenses into temporary architecture: walls of droplet, halls, the suggestion of stairs descending into the pavement and finding no basement, only more wet light.

And then the breakdown, which is not an event but the local physics.

Reality here is thin in the way ice is thin when it has been breathed on from below. Distances disagree. You walk toward a darker smear on the flats—one of the scattered built things, perhaps—and it recedes at the speed of your approach, or arrives all at once against your shins, a low lintel of something that was once poured, now rounded by a million years of condensate, warm as skin. Edges fail. A pool will have a surface and then, without changing appearance, have two, and you cannot tell which one your foot should believe. Reflections sometimes arrive before the object. Reflections sometimes arrive without the object. In the densest weather, the mist briefly holds the shapes of rooms that were never here, furnished with the idea of furniture, occupied by the pressure of someone having just left. These are not ghosts in any consoling sense. They are the medium trying, the way a sleeping animal twitches, to complete patterns it almost remembers.

The built remnant is scarce and does not explain itself. A ring of fused material no higher than the knee, half-sunk, ringing with the roar as if it were a mouth. A straight groove a mile long and a hand wide, too true to be a crack, filling and emptying with the wandering sheets. A single upright, listing, its top lost in the dim—smooth, not carved, as if grown by a patience that is not plant and not tool. No devices. No metal that still knows it is metal. Whatever made marks here did it so long ago that the marks have been accepted into the geology, and the geology has been accepted into the water, and the water does not keep accounts.

Fauna is rare enough to feel like a rumor you tell yourself to stay oriented. Something moves in the deeper sheets—deeper being a relative term, a thigh’s worth of warm opacity—and the surface wrinkles in a way water should not wrinkle, a geometry of interference that suggests a body larger than the depth that holds it. Once, in a dropout of the roar, a sound like a huge wet cloth being slowly torn, and then nothing. Pale, eyeless things the size of a thumb sometimes bead on the undersides of the reed-cushions, drop, and are gone. Birds are a wrong idea. There is no up that would reward a wing. What lives here lives as a function of the medium, briefly, and does not accumulate into a population you could count.

The turbulence is not weather riding a stable world. It is the world continuing to happen after the stone has stopped. Creation and uncreation share the same gesture. A bank of mist will thicken into a range of hills that cast dimmer dimness, that shed their own condensate in sheets, that stand long enough for the sparse vegetation to seem to lean toward them—and then the hills inhale themselves, and the leaning is only leaning, and the roar laughs in a register you feel along the teeth. Plains of standing water will suddenly freeze into a skin of something that is not ice (too warm, too flexible) and you may walk on it for an hour, hearing the deafening from below as if from a crowded room, until the skin remembers it is water and you are simply in it, waist-deep, the pavement still there, still ancient, still finished, waiting.

Scale is the other occupancy. There is no edge you can reach that is not more of this. Storm-cells the size of nations form and unform in the condensate. The roar has climates. You can travel for days through a region where the sound is a single held note, unbearable, and then cross—without a border, without a change in the pavement—into a chop of overlapping crashes, like a coast that has lost its ocean and kept the idea of wreck. The place is large enough to have weather of its own and then weather of that weather: nested instabilities, a physics that is local and then suddenly planetary, as if the whole flattened world were a single instrument being bowed by something you will not meet.

At night—if the dim can be said to deepen into something you agree to call night—the weeping of the rock becomes visible as threads of warmer dark. The atmosphere close to the pavement beads and runs and beads again. You may find, then, that your own outline is slightly late, a second skin of moisture trying to keep up, and that the roar, for a few breaths, contains your footfalls from a mile ago, arriving now, arriving densely, as if the place had no room left for time to be thin.

There is no lesson in it. The ancient floor does not care that it is finished. The packed medium does not care that it cannot settle. Between them—between the last form and the form that will not be last—the warm dim water stands, and stands, and is never the same standing, and the sound goes on occupying every vacancy as if vacancy were the one thing this world has outlawed.
