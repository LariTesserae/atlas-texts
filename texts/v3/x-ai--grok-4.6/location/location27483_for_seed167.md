The water began as a kindness and stayed as a law. It filled the low bowl of the old valley so completely that the distinction between basin and flood has long since worn away; what remains is a single warm dark body, barely cooler than blood, that holds the drowned district the way a lung holds breath. There is no surface worth speaking of—only a gradual thinning of pressure and a rumor of less-dark that never quite arrives. Light is a memory the water itself seems to have forgotten. What little illumination exists is grown, not given: brief, living sparks that open and close in the bodies of the things that live here.

The city is still a city in its bones. Streets run in the old grid, though their paving has been planed by centuries of silt and the patient grinding of currents that know only one direction at a time. Buildings stand—some whole, most not—stacked limestone and whatever harder stone the first masons dragged from the surrounding low hills. Their faces are pocked, their corners rounded, their doorways softened into caves. No metal remains. No glass. No device of any kind. The place has been scoured back to mineral and habit. Cornices have become ledges. Stairwells have become chimneys of slower water. Entire blocks have slumped into themselves and then been re-inhabited as if the collapse were simply another kind of architecture.

Density is the ruling fact. Every surface that can hold a body holds many. Tube-worms the color of old ivory plume from cracks in the masonry like living smoke. Crustaceans the size of fists pack the mortar joints, their legs ticking against stone in a sound too small to travel far. Schools of pale, lantern-jawed fish hang in the “rooms” as if waiting for an announcement that will never come. Larger hunters—eel-shapes with jaws that unhinge sideways, slow-drifting rays whose undersides glow in slow pulses—move through the avenues with the unhurried confidence of things that have never needed to hurry. The water itself feels occupied, thickened by suspended life and the fine rain of organic spoil that never quite reaches the floor. Vegetation is almost absent; a few mats of chemosynthetic film cling to the warmest vents where the old city’s buried heat still leaks, and here and there a solitary, leafless stalk of something that is not quite plant and not quite animal waves in the current like a forgotten banner. That is all.

The warmth is constant and slightly wrong, as if the valley floor remembers a different climate and refuses to let it go. It rises from cracks, from the buried remnants of whatever fires or forges once burned here, from the slow chemistry of stone and water and the bodies that feed on both. It keeps the dark from becoming cold. It keeps the processes running. Predators take prey in silence broken only by a sudden rush of displaced water and then the long, muffled sound of something being unmade. Currents shift on a schedule older than the streets; they rearrange the silt into new dunes that bury a plaza for a decade and then uncover it again, perfectly swept. Schools migrate through the same intersections their ancestors used, turning as one body around the same crumbled fountain. The city is not dead. It is merely finished with the kind of life that required tools.

Sound stays close to the source. A jaw closing. The faint rasp of a shell against limestone. The distant, almost subsonic groan of a wall deciding, after another century, to lean a little farther. Most of the time there is only the pressure of water against water, a quiet so complete it has texture. When the larger animals pass, their bioluminescence paints the facades for a few seconds—arches, empty windows, the suggestion of a marketplace—and then the dark takes it all back, as if the city were being remembered and then immediately forgotten, over and over, by things that do not remember.

The place has been this way long enough to have reached its own balance. Nothing new is built. Nothing old is restored. The structures weather at the same rate the life colonizes them. The valley is wide enough that the far edges of the district dissolve into unfeatured dark, yet close enough that every current that enters eventually finds a wall to turn against. It is a city that has become landscape, a landscape that still insists, in the stubborn regularity of its remaining walls, that it was once something else. The water does not argue. It simply continues to hold everything in its warm, almost lightless palm, and the abundant lives inside it continue their quiet, necessary work of eating, fleeing, glowing, and dying in the rooms that no longer belong to anyone.
