The low country is a single artifact.

It was poured, bolted, and stacked until there was no remaining ground that had not already been decided, and then it was left running. What remains is not a ruin in the usual sense. A ruin has finished. This place has only finished becoming what it was always going to be: a flattened industrial hydrology the size of a river valley, worn so far toward its last possible shape that further collapse would be redundant. The horizon is a long, even smear. Nothing rises far. Whatever chimneys and cooling towers once stood have been planed down by weather and their own vibration until they are only stumps and rings, dark against a darker sky.

There is almost no light. Not night, exactly — night implies a day that will return. This is a standing dimness, the color of wet iron seen from under a lid. Cloud and condensate occupy the same altitude, a low ceiling that never quite rains and never quite lifts. Moisture beads on every surface and then does not fall so much as decide, slowly, to join the film already there. You walk in a cool that has no edges. Breath shows. Metal shows colder.

Water is everywhere and never cleanly itself. It stands in the expansion joints. It sheets across old pour-slabs that have sagged into shallow basins. It weeps from the faces of retaining walls where the aggregate has been washed out grain by grain until the walls are more hole than mass, and still they hold, because what they hold back is only more of the same wet dark. Culverts run under culverts. The original channels were cut for purpose; the later ones were cut by overflow; the latest are simply the paths water prefers through a landscape that has forgotten the difference. At the marsh-edges of the works — and the works have marsh-edges, soft aprons of silted slurry and pulverized concrete where reeds have taken the seams — the water is the color of tea left too long, and it does not reflect.

Underfoot there is no earth. Break the crust and you find strata: road, then roof, then the floor of a hall, then a run of cable thick as wrists, then another road. Mill scale and rust have become a kind of loam, fine and dark and faintly magnetic. In the lowest cuts, where excavation or collapse has opened a trench, you can read the city as geology — successive industrial epochs pressed flat, each one the bedrock of the next, until the distinction between foundation and fossil is a matter of courtesy.

The machines have not stopped. That is the sound, and the sound is the climate.

It is not one noise. It is a weather of noises stacked until they become a single pressure. Deep in the remaining basements the old pumps still take suction, vast centrifugal things that have outlived their buildings, drawing from flooded galleries and throwing water up into viaducts that no longer go anywhere in particular. Turbines turn in the weirs because the water still falls and falling is what they were for. Somewhere always a relief valve is lifting. Somewhere always a belt, long since without a load, is slapping a housing. Steam finds the cracks and becomes instant weather, a white that is gone before it can be looked at, leaving only the colder wet. The air shakes. Conversation, if anyone were here to attempt it, would be a theory. The body takes the sound as a second pulse. After an hour the silence between impacts starts to seem like the imaginary part.

And yet the place is sparse. The density is all in the noise and the wet; the structures themselves stand far apart, as if the original plan had been a city and time had edited it down to punctuation. Between pump-houses there are long reaches of open slab, pitted and puddled, big enough that the far side is only a suggestion. A gantry, stripped to its lattice, crosses nothing. A row of lamp standards leans the same way, every bulb long dead, their glass teeth still in the sockets. You can walk a kilometer and pass only one working thing: a brick cavern half-sunk in its own yard, doors gone, interior a single piston the size of a cottage, rising and falling in oil and black water, serving a system whose other end has not existed for longer than memory would reach if memory were here.

Nothing animal uses this. The absence is clean. No birds on the gantries, no rats in the cable runs, no insects skating the basins. The ecology, such as it is, is vegetal and slight and does not mind the dark. A rust-orange film of algae occupies the stillest water and the north faces of anything that holds still. In the joints, where grit has gathered, there are patches of a low, rubbery moss that looks like it was specified rather than grown — the same gray-green in every township of the valley, as if one planting had been enough for the world. Along the oldest sluices a reed has colonized the grout lines, thin and regularly spaced, more like a notation than a thicket. That is nearly all. The green does not compete. It annotates.

What the works still make is unclear, and may have become the making itself. Conveyors run, on some nights, carrying nothing from a hopper to a hopper. A stamping hall at the western margin still comes down on its dies at a pace like weather, and the dies are blank, or worn blank, and the sheet that feeds them is only the condensate caught and rolled and fed again. Electrification persists as a habit. You find substations humming in the wet, their yards a geometry of porcelain and chain-link, the fences bowed with age into soft catenaries. Cables sag between towers that are themselves only the idea of height. Sometimes a light — a true light, sodium or the older white — hangs on in a cage under an eave, and it does not illuminate so much as confess that illumination was once a policy. Around it the dark is thicker, insulted.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is the strangeness of a purpose that has outlived the need to be known. Walk long enough and the layout begins to suggest intention on a scale larger than a plant or a town: graded planes that tilt whole districts toward a central sump; repeating modules of basin, weir, house, empty apron, basin again, as if the valley were a single machine whose working fluid is the valley. In places the slab is stamped with numbers the size of courtyards, half-eroded, their meaning gone. In others the water, moving over ribbed metal, speaks in a frequency that the bones accept as language and the mind cannot parse. There are doors in the floors of the open yards that open onto stairs that go down into pump-galleries that open onto more yards, slightly older, slightly wetter, as if the country had been folded under itself and both layers left running.

Weather still happens. It is one of the remaining processes. A front will drag itself along the condensate ceiling and the dimness will deepen by a grade you feel in the teeth. Then the true rain comes, not a shower but a decision, and every surface that was already wet becomes a tributary. The deafening grows a new layer — impact on plate, impact on the skins of the basins, the sudden holler of overflows finding their old throats. Water sheets from the stump-towers in veils. The moss blackens and shines. For an hour the whole low country is one catchment, and you understand that this is what it was engineered to be after it was done being everything else: a machine for receiving the sky and putting it through iron and sending it on, slightly more tired, to the next basin, the next weir, the next dark.

When the rain eases, the quiet does not return, because there was no quiet. Only the previous roar, revealed again, like a floor after a flood.

No one tends it. That fact has had time to become structural. The last adjustments were made so long ago that the tools left beside the valves have their own patina, their own small weathering, and have become part of the inventory of the ground. The system keeps its levels the way an old body keeps a temperature: not perfectly, not for a reason you could write down, but with a persistence that has outlasted explanation. Gates still lift when the float rises. Alarms, if any still know how, have no one to tell, and have become simply more of the sound.

Stand on one of the higher slabs — higher by a man's height, no more — and the openness is a kind of vertigo in the horizontal. Feature after feature, all mid-scale, all equivalent: the dark glitter of a holding pond; the hunched shoulder of a turbine hall; a field of broken palettes that were never wood; the pale suggestion of a road that is only a less-ruined texture running toward a vanishing that the mist occupies. Behind you the same. The country does not climax. It extends until the idea of edge becomes another industrial rumor.

This is the oldest stable state of a made world. Not wilderness reclaiming. Not apocalypse. The end of a certain kind of life, settled into final form, still loud, still wet, still working the only job it has left, which is to continue being the ground.
