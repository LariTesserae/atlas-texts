The basin is no larger than a market square and a ring of alleys, and it has been full for so long that fullness is the only season it remembers. Cold water stands in it the way a held breath stands in a chest—complete, unwilling, almost gelled. You do not arrive at a shoreline. You are already inside the volume. The surface, if it exists, is a rumor several body-lengths up, a lid of darker dark, and everything that matters happens below it.

What remains of the town occupies the cup the way organs occupy a ribcage. Brick warehouses lean into one another until their eaves interleave. Iron galleries cross at three and four heights, so close that a drifting glove would catch. Between the buildings the water is not empty; it is piped. Cast mains, lead laterals, ceramic sleeves, flexing hose gone stiff as bone—layer on layer, near enough to touch with both palms at once. To move is to choose a seam. There are no streets anymore, only residual corridors the thickness of a person, and even those are threaded with cable and the pale roots of abandoned scaffolding.

It is industrial in the old sense: not screens, not clean current, but engines that still believe in mass. In the central block a bank of reciprocating pumps works without hurry. Each stroke is a decision made long ago and never rescinded. Pistons travel in oil that has emulsified with the flood until it is the color of weak tea. The water they shift does not leave the basin. It is lifted a few meters through heat-dull risers, cooled in a forest of finned tubes against the near-freezing ambient, and returned to the intakes as if the town’s only remaining labor were to circulate itself. The cycle has worn the metal to a kind of honesty. Seats are true. Valves seat with a courtesy that feels like manners. This is erosion as vocation: everything that could flake or seize has already done so, and what persists has the settled geometry of a riverbed.

Light is a local rumor. Here and there a porthole still holds a filament, sodium or something older, burning at a voltage that makes the glow brown and reluctant. The glass is thick, crazed, bearded with calcite. The light does not travel; it occupies. A few meters off it is only a stain. In the pump house the metal itself seems to remember heat—an infrared ghost along the cylinder heads, visible if you wait long enough for your eyes to invent it. Elsewhere there is only the dark of deep unused rooms, and the darker dark inside pipes.

Sound has been almost abolished. Water this cold, this laden with flour-fine rock, drinks vibration. What remains is interior and infrequent: the tiny percussion of a check-valve deciding, once every few minutes, to be a check-valve; the stretch of a mooring chain that still thinks a hull might tug; a grain of grit finding the one path through a bearing and then forgetting. Your own motion arrives as pressure on the inner ear, not as noise. If you stop, the place becomes a held chord of almost-nothing.

Vegetation is an afterthought the water has not quite erased. On the south-facing brick—south being a remembered direction, a slightly less lightless quadrant—there is a film, colorless, that is either algae or the idea of algae. It does not photosynthesize so much as endure. From the jib of a rusted crane a single thong of kelp hangs, not growing so much as remaining, its bladdered length aligned with a current so slow it might be a draft in a shut house. In the intake grates, where the returning water is forced through a comb of iron, a scum of sessile mouths works the silt. That is the garden.

Animals are rarer than that. Once in a long while a pale crab, small as a thumbnail, walks the vertical face of a boiler as if it were a shore. Its legs make no sound you could use. Deeper in the cooling labyrinth—inside the honeycomb of tubes no one has opened since the flood found its level—something longer keeps a territory. You know it by the way a hanging chain will sometimes lift and settle without current. You do not meet it. The basin is too small for hunting and too old for surprise; the creature and the town have arranged a truce of mutual scarcity.

The temperature is a fact with edges. Near-freezing is not a number here so much as a texture. Metal bites through any glove that is only cloth. The water has a viscosity that is almost instructive, a reluctance to be parted. Where a seal has failed in a high-pressure line, the leak does not bloom; it needles out and, expanding, sheds its heat so abruptly that a rind of industrial ice forms in the dark—geometric, layered, too hard, growing in plates and corners as if the leak were trying to remember architecture. These ice-structures last. They are one of the basin’s few new constructions. In the oldest of them you can see inclusions: a washer, a thread of hemp packing, a bubble that has been a bubble for decades.

The town was built when the cup was dry, or drier—a foundry hamlet in a limestone sink, elevation already a kind of apology. Chimneys were sited for a wind that no longer visits. A loading quay still has its bollards, now useless except as holds. After the water came and stayed, the settlement did not drown so much as continue, revised. Floors became unnecessary. Doors were left open until they were not doors. Stairwells turned into vertical streets; people, when there were people, learned to pull themselves from landing to landing along the same banisters their grandparents had descended on feet. The last painted notices survive as pale rectangles. The language is all stems and no flourish, verbs of maintenance: prime, vent, do not.

What feels strange is not the flood—the flood is merely complete—but the town’s composure inside it. Industry usually argues with water. Here the argument ended so long ago that the two parties have intermarried. The pumps do not fight the basin; they are a way the basin has of having a pulse. Sediment lifts on the outstroke, hangs, and writes the same low dune across the old cobbles every forty minutes, a dune with a perfect lee and a perfect face, erased and rewritten like a thought the place cannot finish. In the former market the silt is finer. It makes a floor that looks almost swept. If you hover there long enough you will see the dune-cycle as a kind of clock, and you will understand that the town is not abandoned so much as staffed by processes.

Occasionally a figure in a pressure-suit moves through a gallery, so slowly that at first you take it for a hung coat. The suit is the color of the brick, patched with the same calcite. It does not hurry because hurry has no purchase. A gloved hand may rest on a valve-wheel and wait, as if listening through palm and iron for a permission that arrives on the next stroke of the distant pumps. Whether these are inhabitants or visitants or the town’s own way of growing a temporary limb is not a distinction the water bothers to keep.

Boundaries are close and certain. Swim any direction and within a minute you meet the limestone of the cup, fluted by an older river, now glazed with the same pale precipitate that coats the iron. The rock is the town’s last wall. Above, the lid. Below, a few more meters of rubble and then the original bedrock, drilled long ago for wells that now open uselessly into more of the same cold. The whole place could be a room if rooms were allowed to have weather. Its weather is internal: the pump-tide, the microscopic rain of calcite, the rare release of a gas bubble that climbs through the pipe-forest like a thought trying to reach a surface that will not care.

Nothing here is new and nothing is finishing. That is the established state. The flood is not an event; it is the climate. The engines are not a defiance; they are a metabolism. If you stay until your own heat becomes a problem the water must solve, you will feel the basin accept you the way it accepted the cranes and the notices and the long pale kelp—as another density in a volume that was already almost full, another quiet process among the ones that keep the dark circulating, and circulating, and never quite leaving.
