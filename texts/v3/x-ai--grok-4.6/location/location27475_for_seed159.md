You come down into it by a stair the rock has not finished deciding to be. The treads are still cooling in places, still wet with the last squeeze of water from the stone, still changing their minds about height so that your foot sometimes meets more step than you expected and sometimes meets air. Above, the old surface is a bright lip, a broken circle of ordinary daylight. Down here the same light arrives late and thickened, as if it has had to think about the descent.

This is not a cave. It is a country that has fallen inward and has not yet agreed to stay fallen. The hollow is valley-wide, then wider than that: a regional slump, a slow catastrophe still finding its floor. Terraces of raw basalt and pale evaporite step away in every direction, some of them only hours old, their edges sharp enough to cut, their faces still beaded with the moisture that attended their birth. Between the terraces the ground buckles. New ridges lift while you watch, not quickly, not slowly—at the speed of a held breath that refuses to end. Elsewhere the same ground sighs and goes down, taking with it whatever had begun to settle. The air is warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. Condensation stands on every upright face and then chooses, without pattern, to become a thread of water or a faint mineral crust or nothing.

There are no plants. There has not been time, or this place does not know that particular trick. What grows here is simpler and hungrier: felts of colorless film that drink the seeps, mats that look like wet suede until they twitch, veils of something that is not quite fungus strung between fresh pillars like abandoned sewing. These are not vegetation. They are the first arguments the chemistry is having with itself. They slick the stone and make the warmth smell faintly of iron and old rain.

The fauna do not wait for an ecosystem to be ready. They are the density. They are the loudness. They are most of what the place is doing.

They occupy every register of the hollow at once. On the newest ledges, things like wet fists pull themselves out of cooling mud and immediately begin to eat the mud, converting it into more of themselves with an audible ticking. In the mid-air—there is a great deal of mid-air; the slump has made a volume—flocks turn so tightly they look like smoke that has learned anger. Their wings are not quite feather, not quite membrane; when the light from the rim catches them they flash the dull color of tarnished spoons. Their calling is a single note hit by ten thousand throats a half-beat apart, so the air itself seems to vibrate at the frequency of a struck rail. Lower, in the damp runs between rising walls, longer bodies move in braided rivers of muscle, climbing one another, vanishing into holes that did not exist this morning, emerging from holes that will not exist tonight. They are warm. Everything here is a little too warm. When two currents of them meet they do not so much fight as reorganize, a sudden local storm of limbs and wet sound, and then they are going somewhere else, or they are the same animal in a different arrangement. It is difficult to count them. It is difficult to be sure they are separate.

The sound is not one sound. It is the rock finding new joints (a deep, infrequent cannon-crack that you feel in the teeth), the constant silk-tear of the flying things, the ticking of the mud-eaters, the slither-and-slap of the braided runners, and underneath all of it a watery undertone: drip magnified by new acoustics, the brief underground rains that occur when a whole terrace sheds its condensation at once, the hush-and-roar of a temporary river that appears from a crack, runs white for a hundred body-lengths, and is swallowed by a newer crack. The hollow is loud the way a market is loud, or a hatchery, or a weather system that has been folded until it fits underground.

Scattered through this—never centered, never claiming—are the marks of hands.

They are primitive in the way a first thought is primitive. A ring of stacked plates, already half-drowned by a fresh seep, the plates themselves pried from a wall with something that left parallel scratches. A lean-to of cooling slag against a pillar, its interior darkened by the grease of bodies that are not the ordinary fauna. A few long grooves cut into a face of salt, too regular to be claw, too shallow to be writing, perhaps only the record of someone testing whether the new rock would hold an edge. You find a tool once: a split cobble with a single purposeful flake gone from it, still sharp, already being climbed by the colorless film. Whoever made these things is either gone or is simply one more moving density among the rest, using what the hollow discards in the hour it discards it. There is no village. There is no path that lasts. There are only these faint insistences that someone, briefly, tried to make a stay.

The light is ordinary and therefore strange. It comes down the great rent in a clean column at the center and then breaks itself across the moving terraces, so that you walk through bars of day and sudden indoor shadow as the land rearranges. In the deeper side-slumps, where the sun does not reach, the warmth itself seems to carry a dull mineral glow, not enough to see by until your eyes have been lied to for a while. Occasional vents exhale not fire but a wet brightness, a brief daylight-colored steam, as if the underworld were trying to remember the surface and getting only the color right.

The rules here are few and they do not keep.

What is high becomes low. What is solid becomes a route. What is a creature becomes, for a while, landscape: the braided runners will pack a new sag in the floor so densely that later arrivals walk on their backs without noticing the difference, and the ones underneath do not die so much as wait, metabolizing the pressure, until the sag lifts again and they spill outward like a delayed tide. Water is never a feature; it is a mood. It beads, it runs, it is inhaled by a thirst the stone has not yet satisfied, it returns as mist when a ridge is born too hot. The film-life writes and erases itself in a cycle faster than decay. The flying things nest in cavities that are still opening, so that whole generations hatch into a space that is already becoming something else, and their dead make a fine dark silt that the next terrace will lift into the light and call soil, though nothing green will use it.

You can stand on a vantage that did not exist at dawn and look across a distance that feels regional—steam-lines, the pale scars of evaporite, the dark moving seams of fauna, the far wall of the slump rising toward the bright lip of the world that has not fallen—and feel that the place is still being invented by its own weather. The turbulence is not weather exactly. It is ontogeny. The hollow is embryonic and already overcrowded, a geography in the act of being born with too many occupants already inside it, all of them loud, all of them warm, all of them eating the unfinished mineral of their own home. A new pillar punches up through a flock and the flock parts around it without offense, already calling from the other side. A pool forms, is drunk by a hundred small mouths, becomes a white crust, is shattered by the next lift of stone. Somewhere a stacked ring of plates loses another course to a seep and the plates clatter down into a run of bodies that do not pause.

If there is a center it is only the column of light, and even that wanders as the rent above slowly, almost shyly, continues to widen. The old surface watches. Down here the new country does not look up. It is too busy becoming, and un-becoming, and becoming again, wet-handed, swarming, not yet a landscape so much as the argument that will someday, if the rock ever cools enough to keep a shape, be one.
