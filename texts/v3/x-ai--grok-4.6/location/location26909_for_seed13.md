The basin sits inside a crown of unfinished mountains. Their ridges are still faceted, still catching light on planes that have never been rounded; the rock looks quarried rather than weathered, as though the range had been lifted into place and then simply left. Nothing since has been permitted to alter the angles. The same pale sun hangs at the same height, always a little west of noon, and the shadows it throws—long, precise, unmoving—are part of the architecture now.

Cool air fills the basin without ever arriving or departing. It carries a constant, fine dampness that beads on every surface and stays there: a skin of moisture on the stone, on the moss, on the motionless leaves. There is no wind to dry it and no further rain to add to it. In the lowest folds of the ground, small dark tarns hold perfectly still water; their surfaces are unwrinkled mirrors. A few narrow channels cut through the younger scree, but the water in them does not run. It simply occupies the same length of bed it has always occupied, a glassy filament connecting one pocket to the next.

Vegetation is thicker than the altitude and the coolness should allow. Deep, saturated mosses cover the flatter ground in continuous green pelts, so lush they look almost wetter than the air. Ferns stand uncurled in the lee of every boulder and every wall, their fronds fixed in the exact attitude of having just opened. Low shrubs and pale-barked trees grow in scattered groves rather than forest; their leaves are broad and slightly succulent, always beaded, never falling. Between the groves the moss gives way to open sward, the kind of emptiness that makes the built things feel farther apart than they are.

The city occupies the basin the way a careful thought occupies a page: deliberately, with room around each idea. Buildings of fitted pale stone—no mortar, only the precision of the cut—stand in loose clusters and long, formal alignments. They are low for the most part, their roofs either slabbed or slightly pitched and also mossed. A few taller halls rise with slit windows and heavy lintels; their interiors stay in permanent half-light. Streets are more suggestion than necessity: packed earth or fitted flagstones that have never been worn into ruts. Wide courts and empty lawns separate one precinct from another. The whole settlement could be crossed in an hour’s walk, yet it never quite becomes dense; space is left between everything, as if the builders had been instructed to keep the silence intact.

What they built with is what the young mountains gave them: stone, and the rarer timber of those pale trees, and fiber, and bone. Doorposts are lashed or pegged. Steps are simply stacked. There is the occasional gleam of beaten native copper or a knife of flaked glass, but metal is scarce and ornamental. Everything that needed making was made by hand and then, at some unrecorded instant, finished forever.

Animals are everywhere and nowhere in motion. Herds of short-horned, thick-coated creatures stand in the open sward exactly as they stood, heads lowered or turned. Birds occupy every cornice and every branch in the same postures, wings folded or half-extended. Smaller things—lizards the color of wet stone, insects like chips of amber—occupy crevices and the undersides of leaves. None of them startle. None of them proceed. Their abundance is a kind of furnishing; the city is as much theirs as anyone’s, and they have been arranged within it with the same finality as the masonry.

Sound is almost absent. The air does not move enough to make the ferns whisper. Water does not fall or lap. What remains is a low, continuous hush, the auditory equivalent of the unchanging light—something you notice only because you keep expecting it to break and it never does. Footsteps, if anyone still walked here, would be loud.

The rule of the place is simply that it does not continue. Erosion was arrested while the peaks were still sharp. Seasons were never allowed to begin. The dampness neither increases nor dries. The city neither grows nor crumbles. The animals neither hunger nor age. Whatever process once lifted the mountains and settled the people and the moss and the herds inside them reached an equilibrium so complete that time itself stepped aside and left the arrangement standing. It is not a ruin and not a living town. It is a single, perfectly held afternoon at high elevation, damp and cool and quiet, repeating nothing because there is nothing left that needs to happen.
