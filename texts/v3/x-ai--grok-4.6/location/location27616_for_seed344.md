The hollow is smaller than it first feels. You come down into it by a stair of raw ledges that still show the grain of the break, a collapse no older than a season or two, the lips of rock unrounded, the fracture-faces pale where they have not yet taken stain. The whole basin might be thirty paces across. The sky sits over it like a lid that does not quite fit.

Heat arrives before anything else. It is not the dry heat of open ground. It is wet heat, a thickness that coats the inside of the mouth and makes the eyes water. Water is everywhere and almost nowhere as a standing body. It weeps from the walls in sheets so thin they look like varnish. It beads on the undersides of overhangs and falls in irregular threads. It condenses on every cooler surface and then, finding none that stay cool, simply hangs in the air as a bright haze that never quite becomes fog and never quite becomes rain. The floor is not soil. It is a skin of precipitated mineral—pale, chalky, still soft at the edges—laid over angular rubble. Where a boot presses, the skin darkens and then slowly, over minutes, sweats the print back out.

There is almost no green. What grows does so as if by accident: a few blades of something like rush, yellowed, in a crack that holds a puddle the size of a palm; a mat of dark, heat-tolerant algae on the wetter rock, more stain than plant; one skeletal shrub on the highest remaining shoulder of the collapse, its leaves reduced to scales. The space between these things is large. Emptiness is the main occupant.

Sound is not emptiness. The hollow is loud in a way that does not belong to weather. A buried main—cast iron, flanged, older than the collapse—has been sheared and left running. It discharges into the far wall with a continuous, pressurized roar that the stone cups and throws back. Superheated water hits cooler rock and flashes; the flash is a hard, wet percussion under the roar. Somewhere out of sight a relief valve cycles on a long, slow period, perhaps once or twice in an hour: a rising scream of steam, a slam of metal, then the same roar again, slightly changed in pitch as if the throat of the pipe had been scoured a little wider. Heat-expansion ticks in the remaining pipework. Drips, when they gather enough mass, strike with a report out of all proportion to their size. There are no birds, no insects, no small moving lives to thread quieter noises through this. The loudness is industrial and lonely.

Light is ordinary daylight, but ordinary daylight doing unusual work. It comes in over the broken rim and turns the haze into a pale, shadowless glare. Metal surfaces—the exposed run of pipe, a single valve-house no larger than a closet, a ladder bolted to nothing that now ends in air—throw small, fierce reflections. The valve-house is brick, already sweating salt, one window gone, the interior a gloom in which a pressure gauge still twitches. The needle moves so slowly you would have to watch it for a long time to be sure it moved at all. That is the pace of the place. The water table is rising by fractions. Mineral crusts thicken by the day, not the hour. The new rock is taking its first stains. Rust blooms in orange fans along every weld and then darkens, patient as lichen. Nothing here hurries except the steam, and even the steam is only the audible edge of processes that began before the ground opened and will continue after the hollow has filled to a hot, still pool and then, much later, after that pool has been roofed by its own deposits.

You can stand in the middle and turn once and have seen all of it: the weeping walls, the single dead-ended ladder, the roaring wound in the stone, the shy vegetation, the absence of anything that walks or flies or burrows. The slightly wrong thing—the thing that keeps the eye from resting—is the cleanliness of the break. The collapse did not pulverize. It unzipped. Faces of rock fit their opposites across the gap as if they might, given enough quiet centuries, decide to close again. In the meantime the pipes keep working, the heat keeps arriving from somewhere deeper and farther than this pocket, and the air keeps condensing on itself, making the hollow a lung that never quite finishes exhaling.

At the lowest point a shallow saucer of water has formed, too hot to touch for long, filmed with oil-sheen and white floc. Nothing drinks from it. Nothing will, for a long time. The place is too new, too loud, too much the property of engines that no longer have a building around them and of water that has only just learned the shape of this air.
