The basin has not finished deciding it is land.

It sits low, a shallow dish of country maybe a day’s walk across if walking were possible, rimmed by walls of rock that still show the grain of whatever lifted or dropped them. The stone is young in the way a scab is young: angular, unpolished, full of edges that have not yet learned weather. Rain does not stay. What falls—seldom, hard, and gone in an hour—vanishes into a lace of fresh fractures, leaving the surface merely damp, as if the ground itself were sweating. In the hour after, every leaf wears a film. By midday the film is air again. Pockets of wet persist only where the rock has not yet cracked: a seep behind a fern-wall, a dark smear in a crease, the cupped base of a fallen bole filling and emptying with the same impatient rhythm as the sky.

There is almost no sky.

What grows here has grown too fast and too close for light to keep a path. The canopy is not a ceiling so much as a series of stacked nights, each layer eating the one above it. Broad, rubbery paddles of leaf overlap until the color green becomes a rumor. Below them, a second forest of pale stems and veined umbrellas, then a third of things that are not sure whether they are plant or rot: shelves, ropes, bells of tissue that swell in the damp and collapse when the air turns thirsty. The warmth never quite becomes heat. It is a held breath, a greenhouse that forgot the glass. Everything is a little too close to everything else. You cannot put a hand out without touching living surface, and the living surface is already touching back.

The dark is not the dark of caves. It is the dark of surplus. Photons arrive at the upper leaves and are spent before they finish falling. What remains at ground level is a brown-violet dusk in which shapes are known first by sound and second by the way they displace other shapes. Occasional points of light exist, but they are biological and untrustworthy: a smear of foxfire on a trunk, a pulse in the throat of something watching, a whole standing pool of beetles whose wing-cases catch what little there is and throw it around like cheap coin. None of it helps you see. It only proves the dark has furniture.

The furniture is animals.

They are not a population so much as a weather. The air is thickened with bodies. Midges in sheets that have weight. Things the size of thumbs that travel in ropes, linking and unlinking over stems. Ground-walkers in overlapping territories so tight that every path is a negotiation and most negotiations fail. Above, through the stacked night, something heavy and winged keeps changing its mind about direction; the downdraft smells of crushed fruit and iron. There are no clear species-counts because the counts will not hold still. A hollow that was empty at one breath is a knot of fur and clicking at the next. A trunk that was bark becomes a vertical river of ants, then bark again, the ants having been a decision the wood briefly made.

The sound is the basin thinking out loud, and the basin cannot stop thinking.

It is not one noise. It is all of them, unsynchronized, each insisting. Stridulation so dense it has a temperature. Wet percussion of bodies dropping through leaves. A low, continuous tearing, which is either roots or something using roots as a road. Periodically a cry goes up that is too large for the throat that made it, and for a few heartbeats the lesser sounds flinch, then climb back over the cry and bury it. There is no silence to measure the noise against. If you cover your ears you hear the blood in your head keeping time with the outside, which is worse.

The turbulence is not weather, though weather participates. The young rock still settles. A slope that was passable in the morning is a raw cut by afternoon, pale stone showing like bone, already being stitched by the first opportunistic roots. Colonies form and are eaten in the same hour. A flowering happens in a rush—pale, almost luminous cups forcing themselves open in the dark—and is stripped by tongues and mandibles before the scent has finished crossing the next thicket. Streams of migrants come through, not from far countries but from the next fold of the same basin, because the folds themselves keep changing what they offer. The land is in the first violent draft of being a place. Nothing has tenure. Everything behaves as if the lease is hourly.

There are no houses. There are no paths that were intended. Once, in a place where the damp gathers a little longer, you might find a stick that has been bitten to a point, or a stone with an edge that is slightly too useful to be accident. That is all. Whatever intelligence moves here moves with the same impatience as the roots and the swarms: it does not stay to stack stone. It uses, discards, is used. The basin has not offered the kind of stillness that lets a wall occur.

What is unusual is not any single creature or plant. It is the speed at which categories fail. A vine that was drinking from a seep begins, by evening, to drink from the animals that come to the seep; by night the animals have learned to avoid that crease, and the vine has already started a new mouth elsewhere. A chorus that you took for insects is, if you get close enough to regret it, the friction of seeded pods rasping in a wind that does not exist—the pods shaking themselves. Eyes open on bark and then are not eyes, only patterned wet. The darkness makes all of this adjacent. You do not see the transformation; you stand in the middle of it and the middle moves.

At the lowest point of the dish, where the last of the water should pool and does not, the ground is a mosaic of young mud-cracks and thread-roots racing to seal them. Condensation beads on every hair of every surface and is drunk before it can fall. The air tastes of copper, leaf-blood, and the sweet rot of things that lived too briefly to name. Something very large changes position a long way off; the sound arrives as a rearrangement of all the nearer sounds, a brief hierarchy, then the hierarchy collapses again into the same deafening equality.

The basin will be older tomorrow, but only by a day, and a day here is not a unit of rest. It is another draft. The rock will crack a little further. The leaves will take a little more of the sky. The bodies will trade places. The damp will come and go like a thought that cannot quite finish. And the dark, which is only the surplus of living, will remain exactly as full as it is now—so full there is no room left in it for a horizon, or a quiet, or a thing that lasts.
