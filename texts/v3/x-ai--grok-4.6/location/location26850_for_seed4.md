The hollow is easy to miss because it does not look like an entrance. At the cold lip of the marsh the ground simply fails—an oval wound the size of a modest hall, its rim still raw where the peat tore yesterday or last week. Nothing has rounded the edges yet. The plants that line it are new: pale, unweathered, still showing the knife-marks of their own emergence. Water stands in the wound at ankle depth and weeps continuously from the cut walls, a slow bright film that never quite becomes a stream.

You go down because the dark is already complete three steps in. The sky, what little of it there was, pinches shut behind a living lintel of interlaced stems. After that there is no daylight, only the memory of it and the faint, wet shine of surfaces that know they are being looked at. The air is marsh-cold and holds its own weather: breath condenses, hangs, is taken back by leaves.

This is the city.

It occupies the single chamber the collapse made and a few narrow galleries the roots have since opened. Nothing here was quarried. Every wall, stair, partition and cistern is still alive. The builders—whoever first noticed the hollow and decided it could be a settlement—work by training, binding, and patient wounding. They split a shoot, pin it with thorn-pegs, wait. They braid two vines until the braid lignifies into a load-bearing rib. They encourage moss to brick itself in the wet angles until the brick holds a man’s weight. The technology is thumbs, bone awls, twisted-fiber cord, and an inherited sense of which cuts a plant will forgive. There is almost no metal. What metal exists is a few salvaged fish-hooks and a single bronze needle passed from hand to hand like a relic.

Because the chamber is small the city is vertical and sparse. Habitations cling to the living walls in isolated clusters—three or four rooms grown around a central taproot, a hanging gallery reached by a ladder of trained ivy, a cistern-leaf the size of a boat that collects the perpetual weep. Between these clusters there is a great deal of empty dark. You can stand in the middle of the floor-water and hear your own pulse. The architecture does not fill space so much as punctuate it. The plants themselves are the substrate: floor of compressed sphagnum over a web of white roots, ceiling a low vault of overlapping fronds that pulse, very slowly, as if the whole hollow were breathing.

It is always wet. Water does not fall so much as exist in every state at once—bead, film, drip, standing pool, exhaled mist. The rocks (there are a few, freshly broken, still sharp) weep. The leaves sweat. Footsteps leave temporary white wounds in the moss that refill within an hour. Everything smells of chilled chlorophyll and iron.

The quiet is not emptiness. It is occupancy that has agreed not to speak. The fauna is total. Every interstice holds something. Pale amphipods graze the algal skin of the walls in herds so dense they look like moving frost. Hair-thin worms stitch the moss. Moths the color of wet bark hang in curtains from the undersides of the living roofs, wings never quite still. In the deeper galleries, larger shapes—something between salamander and hare, skin the same cold green as the plants—move along trained runways, pausing to drink from the leaf-cisterns. They do not call. Their feet are padded or webbed or simply careful. When they hunt one another the only sound is a brief rearrangement of water. You become aware of them the way you become aware of your own blood: continuously, and only if you attend.

The city is active the way a body is active. Growth never stops. A partition that was a curtain of stems last night may be a wall by morning, the internodes having swollen and locked. New stairs appear where someone scored a vine and weighted the wound. Old rooms are slowly digested—leaves yellow, collapse, are composted in situ by the same amphipods, and the space is reopened or left as a soft depression. Weather happens indoors: a sudden cooling makes the vault contract and shed a fine rain; a rare warming (never much) starts the fronds respiring so hard the air turns to soup. Predators and prey complete their cycles in the walls without ever needing the open floor. The human (or nearly-human) inhabitants move through this the way tenants move through a house that is also a digestive tract—respectfully, with small tools, repairing what they must, accepting what they cannot.

Light is a rumor. A few cultivated fungi give off a glow no brighter than the phosphene you see when you press your eyelids. Most work is done by touch and by the plants’ own faint thermal differences—a warm vein means this way, a cold hollow means a cistern or a nest. Eyes exist but are not trusted. Children are taught the city’s map as a sequence of textures and temperatures. Visitors, on the rare occasions there are any, are given a living staff that leans, very slightly, toward water or toward the nearest inhabited cluster.

The strangeness is quiet and structural. The plants remember being trained; a vine that was once a ladder will, if cut, try to become a ladder again. Some of the larger rooms have begun to grow furnishings no one designed—benches of fused petiole, sleeping-niches the exact shape of a curled body. It is not clear whether the city is being built or whether it is building its builders. The animals treat the human structures as just another layer of habitat; a family of the green hare-things will occupy a newly grown loft before the intended tenants arrive, and no one evicts them. Property is a conversation with living matter that can simply refuse.

Outside, the marsh continues its own cold business. Rain comes often and is added to the weep. The hollow’s rim, still sharp, is already being colonized by the same species that built the city—scouts, really, laying down the first trained stems in case the settlement ever needs to climb out. For now it does not. The pocket is enough. It holds a town, a weather, an ecology, and a darkness that has learned how to be inhabited. If you climb back out you will find the wound already smaller than when you entered, the plants knitting, the water still coming, the silence underneath everything like a second ground.
