The place is a hanging kettle of stone, no larger than a nave, cupped just under the last ridge of a mountain that has only recently finished arriving.

You feel the youth of it in the edges. The walls are not rounded. They are faceted, glassy in places, still showing the planes along which they split when the ground heaved them up. Fresh talus lies in uncompacted fans—angular, ringing if you kick it, not yet worn into the mute gravel of older heights. The rim is a knife. Cloud does not so much blow over it as pour, continuously, a slow white overspill that never quite empties the sky and never quite fills the bowl. Inside, the air is warm. Not jungle-warm, not steam-room: a held, even warmth, like breath under wool, maintained by water and by something under the rock that has not yet learned to be cold at this altitude.

Everything weeps.

The stone itself condenses. Beads form along the fresh fracture-lines and fatten and run. Where the walls step inward there are hanging gardens of seep—thin silver sheets, never quite streams, never quite dry. The floor of the kettle is not earth so much as a soaked, springy mat of peat and root and rusted grit, pocked with shallow lenses of water that hold the sky in fragments. After every pulse of cloud the whole amphitheatre ticks and drips, a thousand small percussion points, and the sound gathers because the space is small and hard-walled and the air is dense with vapor. It is loud the way a bathroom is loud when the tap is left on, or a foundry is loud when the boilers have been banked but not shut down. Water, and metal remembering water.

Vegetation has taken the wet and the warmth as permission. It is lush in the concentrated way of a conservatory, not a forest: everything near everything else, layered, touching. Tree-ferns the height of a person stand in clumps where the seeps braid. Their croziers are still unrolling in slow motion—you can watch a frond decide, over an afternoon, to become a plane. Moss is not a covering here so much as a climate. It hangs in beards from every ledge, stuffed into every crack, overgrowing the last angular corners of the young rock as if trying to hide how new the mountain is. Orchids and something like bromeliads have found the thermal seams and packed them; their roots drink from the stone’s sweat. There are leaves with the thickness of leather and leaves like wet paper. In the deepest shade, under the ferns, a second, smaller forest of filmy things lives on the drip alone. The green is high-saturated, almost edible, and it smells of chlorophyll and warm iron.

Among all this, industrial remains sit as if they were a second geology.

Someone tried to use the heat. Pipes—cast, flanged, still faintly warm to the touch—enter the cliff at three places and re-emerge lower, sweating at every joint. A single turbine housing, brick and corrugated iron, occupies the only truly level shelf, no larger than a cottage. One of its riveted doors hangs open. Inside, in the dim that is still ordinary daylight thinned by mist, a generator turns with the patience of a process that no longer has anywhere to send its current. The note it makes is a low, continuous vowel, industrial, slightly unsound, a bearing that has been dying for years and has decided to take its time. A few bulbs still glow along a run of ceramic insulators spiked into the rock—normal light, the color of late afternoon even at noon, because the cloud never quite lifts. Cables sag between two lattice masts that have begun to grow moss like everything else. A winch, a stack of sleepers, a cast-iron valve wheel the size of a table, half-swallowed by fern. Nothing here was built to last at this height and everything has lasted anyway, because change in the kettle is slow. Rust is a season. Peat accumulates by the thickness of a fingernail in a decade. The mountain is still rising, but you would need instruments, and patience measured in generations, to know.

Sound stacks. The drip. The overspill of cloud hissing along the rim. The generator’s long vowel. Water finding the pipes and singing in them, a thin metallic rain from the inside. Wind, when it shears the knife-edge, arrives as a single hard syllable and then is gone; the bowl does not hold wind, only moisture and machine-noise. Occasionally a sheet of seep lets go all at once and slaps the peat, and the amphitheatre answers itself.

Animals are almost an absence. You might wait a day and see only midges stitching the warm air above the seeps, or a single dark bird—too high, too brief—crossing the open side of the kettle where the floor simply ends and the drop begins, thousands of meters of unformed air. Once, if you are lucky or unlucky, a pale amphibian the length of a thumb hangs in a film of water on the rock, perfectly still, as if the warmth had invented it and then forgotten to invent more. The lushness is vegetal, not faunal. The density is of leaves, of droplets, of pipes and fronds occupying the same volumes, of sound folded on sound. There is little that moves of its own will.

The open side is the only horizon. Stand at the lip of the peat and the world falls away through cloud. You cannot see the lower slopes. You see only the white overspill continuing downward, and, on clear rare minutes, the suggestion of other, older ridges, already worn, already cold, which this young upthrust has not yet joined. Behind you the kettle remains: a room the mountain made by accident, then heated, then wet, then slowly filled with green and with the leftover grammar of engines. The light is the light of an ordinary overcast day, only closer, only wetter. Nothing here is hurried. The croziers unroll. The bearing complains. The cloud pours. The stone, still sharp, still new, weeps as if it had always known how.
