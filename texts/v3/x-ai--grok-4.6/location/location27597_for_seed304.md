The hollow is no older than a generation of roofs. You can still see the tear in the ground where it opened: a shallow oval no wider than a long shout, banks still knife-cut, clay and pale stone showing their layers like a fresh wound that has not yet decided to scab. Water found it immediately. Water is still finding it. The air itself condenses on every cool face and runs.

They built into the tear before the banks had time to slump. There was nowhere else and the hollow was already a room. Now there is no unused surface. Houses of wattle, rammed silt, and uncut river cobble share walls the way knuckles share a hand. Eaves overlap. Gutters are other people’s doorways. What would be alleys are troughs of standing water the colour of weak tea, crossed by single planks that bow and never quite dry. Circulation is mostly overhead—along the continuous, slightly canted roof-plane of packed reed and clay, where smoke-holes, drying racks, and the occasional ladder interrupt a surface that is more village than any one dwelling. From the rim you see it as a single low organism, glistening, densely faceted, breathing steam.

It is cool always. Not the clean cold of height; a low, wet cool that lives in the joints. Breath shows. Fingertips go white if you work stone too long. The rock of the banks weeps in steady threads—not springs exactly, more like the stone remembering it was seabed or riverbed and refusing to forget. The water is near the surface everywhere. Step off a plank and you are ankle-deep in something that is not quite mud and not quite water, a cold emulsion that holds a footprint for a day and then slowly fills it from below. After rain—which is frequent, soft, untheatrical—the whole hollow becomes a single shallow lens. Children pole flat baskets. Adults simply wade and keep working.

There is almost no green. A few yellow reeds in the wettest north corner, blackened at the base. Liverwort in the perpetual drip-line under the eastern bank. One alder, stunted, growing out of a collapsed wall someone has not yet rebuilt; its roots have the look of someone clinging. That is the vegetation. Everything else that might have grown was used, or drowned, or never had the interval of stillness required. The density is of made things and wet mineral, not of life in the leafy sense.

They work with what the hollow gives and what the last flood left: split willow, antler picks, fire-hardened stakes, a little beaten copper the colour of old blood that arrived from somewhere up-catchment and is hoarded for awls and fish-hooks. No wheel that you can hear. Stone is dressed by pecking; you hear it only if you are close, a dull regular tap that the damp air swallows after a few houses. Clay is packed wet into forms of woven withy and left to take the cool. Walls go up slightly out of true because the ground is still settling—young ground, unconsolidated, a little drunk. Every season someone loses a corner into the trough and rebuilds it a handspan higher, so the oldest houses have a geological look, successive watermarks and patch-courses climbing like a slow tide of habitation.

Sound is mostly water deciding. Drip from eaves onto already-wet clay. The soft gulp of a plank lifted. Someone wringing a cloth. A cough. At the rim, if the wind is right, the thin continuous hiss of the weeping rock. Voices stay low; the hollow is a bowl and everything reflects, so people have learned the volume that does not come back at them from the opposite bank. At night the quiet is nearly complete except for the water, which never quite stops, and the occasional deep slump of bank material giving up, a sound like a held breath released. Then the repair begins again at first light.

Light is ordinary and a little grey, the kind that makes colours honest and unkind. No drama of shafts or gloom; just the even illumination of a low overcast that the hollow seems to generate for itself, even on clearer days, because the wet surfaces give the sky back as a paler, colder version of itself. Shadows are short and filled with reflected cool. You can always see what you are doing. You can always see how temporary it is.

Animals are scarce and treat the place as a brief mistake. Eels move in the troughs after heavy rain and are taken with bone gorges; they are the closest thing to abundance. A grebe once wintered in the north reeds and was discussed for a year. Something small and dark—mustelid, perhaps—leaves prints on the highest roofs after frost, then is gone. Insects are present the way damp is present, not as fauna so much as weather. The scarcity feels like a rule the hollow made when it opened: this is for the packed human thing and the water, and little else is invited.

The unusual sits in the timing. The ground is too new for the density it holds. You feel it in the way floors are never quite level two years running, in the way a wall that was true at planting is bowed by harvest, in the faint, constant sensation that the whole settlement is still in the act of arriving. People speak of the opening the way other places speak of a founder—an event, almost a person. Some say the hollow is still widening, a finger’s width a decade, and they build accordingly, leaving a slack of withy and a habit of not loving any particular corner too much. Others say it is done, and the weeping is only memory. Both might be true. The clay does not argue.

Processes continue without needing an audience. Rain cycles through: the troughs rise, the lower thresholds take on a dark tide-line, the planks are shifted to the next pair of sockets already worn into the sills. Banks calve small fresh faces; children are sent to collect the fallen cobbles before they vanish into the emulsion. Smoke from the roof-holes shears sideways in the cool and never quite clears the rim, so the hollow lives under a thin, moving lid of its own breath. People mend, pack, wring, lift, reset. The alder loses another leaf. A new drip-line finds a path down a wall that was dry yesterday and begins the long work of making a stain, then a groove, then a problem. Nothing here is finished. The place is the activity of staying in a wound that has not closed, packed so tightly that absence itself would be a kind of collapse.

Stand on the rim at the hour when the light goes even and colourless. The roofs gleam like the skin of something that lives in water. No space between anything. The air tastes of silt and woodsmoke and the mineral chill of stone that will not stop bleeding. Below, a figure moves a plank two sockets over, tests it with a heel, and goes on. The hollow holds. For now it holds.
