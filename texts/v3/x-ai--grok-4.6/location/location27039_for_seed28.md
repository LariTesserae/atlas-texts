The bowl does not announce itself. From the plateau it is only a brighter patch in the already-bright stone, a paleness you could walk past for a lifetime. Then the ground slants, the horizon folds inward, and you are already below the lip before you understand you have entered something that was made to be missed.

It is not deep as canyons are deep. It is low the way a held breath is low: a depression worn into itself over so long that the wearing has become a kind of rest. The walls are pale and stepped, not by water — there has been no water here in any living memory, and the stone does not remember any — but by wind and by hands, and it is no longer easy to say which cut first. The wind came and stayed. The hands learned its grammar and built accordingly.

What they built occupies every surface and almost none of the volume. That is the first strangeness that settles in the eyes. Terraces, stairs, lintels, dry-fitted towers no thicker than a man is wide, bridges that leap the empty air between wall and wall: a whole town of pale masonry clinging to the inner skin of the bowl, complete with cisterns that have never held anything but dust, markets whose stalls are permanent stone shelves facing other stone shelves, a ring-road halfway up that could take a procession if a procession still happened. And between all of it, space. Courtyards the size of the houses that frame them. Rooms with a single bench and a window that is most of a wall. Streets that are more interval than path. The city is dense with intention and vacant of stuff. You can stand in the central plaza — a circle of flagstones so old the joints have rounded into something like bone — and turn a full slow circle and count fewer objects than fingers. The architecture is the population.

The plants do not contradict the emptiness. They occupy a different register.

They grow from every joint the masons left, and from joints the masons never made. They are not green. Green would imply a generosity this chemistry does not offer. They are the color of old brass, of tea left in sun, of the underside of a beetle. Their leaves are thin as paper and twice as dry, stacked in rosettes and fans and hanging curtains that should weigh nothing and somehow roof whole terraces. Stems are hollow, segmented, fluted — not like bamboo so much as like instruments that grew before anyone thought to blow through them. Roots do not seek moisture. There is none to seek. They thread the stone for mineral and for purchase and for the faint metallic taste the rock sweats when the heat peaks, and this is enough. The forest — it is a forest, lush in the way a crowded attic is lush, every volume claimed by surface — drinks light and warmth the way other forests drink rain. At noon the leaves turn their pale backs upward and the whole bowl becomes a second sun, intolerable, a white-gold glare that erases edges. You navigate by touch and by the memory of steps.

There is almost no one to navigate for.

A few inhabitants remain. You hear them before you see them, which is to say you do not hear them at all, because hearing here is a ruined sense, and so you notice a movement in the periphery: a figure in a wrap the same brass-tea color as the foliage, crossing a high bridge with a bundle of cut stems. They work with stone blades and copper beaten thin over river-cobbles that were last wet in another age, with cordage twisted from the inner fiber of the sounding-plants, with awls of the plants’ own harder nodes. They recarve a worn step. They restring a wind-baffle. They do not speak. Speech is a private motion of the mouth, a habit kept for the insides of the deepest rooms, where the roar drops from deafening to merely vast. Children, if there are still children, learn the city’s signs first: a grammar of hands developed because the air will not carry words.

The air carries everything else.

The sound is the city’s true weather, and it never stops. The bowl is a resonator. The plants are reeds. The architecture is a deliberate set of throats, bells, and baffles — some carved as such, many simply the inevitable result of stacking hollow rooms in a wind-funnel and then letting a dry forest grow through them. Morning wind comes over the western lip and the upper terraces begin, a high continuous tearing, like canvas ripping in a loop. By midmorning the mid-slopes join: a chord so thick it has texture, a pressure on the sternum, the feeling of standing inside a struck gong that does not decay. Afternoon is the worst and the most beautiful. Heat opens the plants’ sounding-chambers — slit valves along the stems, tuned by generations of pruning — and the city becomes a single instrument playing itself. You cannot think in sentences. You think in the pauses between gusts, if pauses come. Night should help and does not; the stone gives back the day’s heat, the wind settles into a lower register, a grinding, a vast dry surf, and the leaves, cooling, tick and snap by the tens of thousands like a fire that will not admit it has nothing to burn.

This is not noise in the sense of accident. The city was built to be this loud, or became willing to be, and the two histories have weathered into one. The oldest lintels are carved with mouth-shapes and radiating lines. Whether the sound is offering, ward, or simply the only honest thing the geology would permit is a question the remaining people answer by continuing to cut the stems to length and to recut the wall-flutes when erosion dulls them. Erosion here is a partner. Everything has been itself long enough to know how to go on being itself. Edges are softened. Repairs are indistinguishable from originals. The dry forest and the masonry have intercalated until a tower is also a trunk and a root-mass is also a foundation, and no one bothers to say which.

Animals are a rumor with occasional proof. Something small and jointed lives in the deepest unused cistern, a well of shadow in a place that otherwise refuses shade; it clicks, perhaps, though clicking is hard to certify. Pale moths — or moth-shaped seeds — lift from the highest fans at dusk and are gone by the time the eye commits. Tracks appear after the rarest nights, when the wind drops just enough for dust to hold a mark, and the marks are the size of a child’s hand and lead nowhere a person would go. That is the census.

The rules are simple because the place will not bear complexity.

Do not look for water. The word survives as a kind of curse or a kind of prayer, unused in ordinary hours. The plants will not abide it; a spilled drop, on the vanishingly rare occasion someone has carried a drop in from outside, blackens a stem as if burned. The stone drinks nothing and offers nothing.

Move on the off-beats. The wind has a cycle inside the roar, a pulse you learn with the soles of the feet. The old paths are laid along the quieter nodes. Stray from them and the sound will take your balance.

Do not try to make the city quiet. People have tried. The attempts are visible as sealed windows, as baffles mortared (with plant-resin, never slurry) across alleys, as one entire neighborhood on the eastern wall whose doors were stacked with masonry until the rooms became solid. The roar simply found other throats. The sealed quarter is now the loudest, a trapped column of air that booms.

Light is a substance. It has weight at noon. The inhabitants wrap their eyes and work by the feel of edges. The few interior rooms that face away from the glare are treasured not for coolness — it is not cool anywhere; the warmth is a constant hand — but for the mercy of dimness, a brown-gold dusk in which the carved walls can be seen as walls and not as sources.

And the last rule, which is not carved anywhere because it does not need to be: the city is a pocket, and pockets have outsides. No one forbids leaving. The lip is climbable in an hour. But the sound follows a little way onto the plateau, a halo of itself, and then cuts off as if a door had shut, and the silence out there is said to be worse than the roar — not because it is silent, but because it is the wrong size, an unshaped quiet in which a person can hear their own blood and mistake it for the city calling them back. Most who go come back before dark. A few do not, and the forest grows through their stoops, and the wind learns a new note in the gap.

Toward evening the light goes copper, then the hard white of bone, then — briefly, if the dust is right — a green that does not belong to any leaf here. The plants close their valves in a long ragged descent of pitch. The bridges empty. Someone, somewhere, draws a stone shutter that does almost nothing. The bowl holds the last of the heat against its chest. In the plaza the flagstones tick as they cool, a sound too small to exist and yet, in a trough of the wind, audible, like a clock invented by the ground.

You could live here a year and not fill a single room. You could also, without contradiction, never find a surface that had not already been touched, planted, carved, or left empty on purpose. The city is finished and still happening. The forest is a masonry that grew. The silence is a myth they keep in the deepest rooms the way other towns keep a well.
