The plateau does not announce the hollow. It is a high, wind-scraped table of black basalt and pale evaporite, so dry the dust does not cling but circulates as a fine, almost electrostatic grit. Heat comes up through the soles. The sun, when it is up, is a white coin that never quite reaches the floor of the cut you are looking for; the cut itself is a narrow chimney, a wound in the stone no wider than a chapel aisle, dropping into a dark that feels older than the mountain.

You go down because the heat is worse in the open, and because the stone around the lip is polished in a way that has nothing to do with weather. Fingermarks, if they were ever here, have been worn into a single continuous glaze. The chimney turns once, twice. Light dies in stages. First the sky becomes a slot, then a memory of blue, then nothing. What remains is infrared: a low, uneven warmth radiating from the walls, from the floor you cannot yet see, from the air itself, which has the density of a held breath.

The chamber opens without ceremony. It is not large. A single vault, perhaps the length of a nave, the height of three stacked houses, irregular as a lung. Every surface is occupied.

There is no interval. The basalt is a substrate only in the technical sense. Over it, in it, replacing it in places, is a living pavement of bodies so continuous that the mind first refuses the category “animal” and reaches for “texture,” “growth,” “furniture.” Then the texture shifts. A plate the size of a palm lifts and resettles. A ridge that looked like cooling lava flexes. What you took for a mineral streak is a stacked column of flattened, heat-dark organisms pressed edge to edge, their dorsal surfaces sintered into a false stone that only the temperature betrays. They are not still. They are never still. The motion is the motion of a packed market seen from a great height, or of blood under skin: a slow, total rearrangement that never leaves a gap.

There is almost no water. What moisture exists is interior, locked in hemolymph and in the thin, recycled film that the lowest tier rasps from the rock. The air drinks it the instant it is exposed. You taste salt and something like burnt keratin. The heat is not the dry bake of a desert noon; it is closer, more specific, rising from vents no wider than a wrist that breathe in long, irregular cycles. When a vent opens, the entire living floor performs a single coordinated drift, a meter or two toward the new heat, a settling, a new equilibrium. Predators — longer, paler, with legs that never quite touch the basal layer — reposition in the same motion, as if the hunt were a weather.

There are no plants. There is a film. It is black-iridescent, neither quite mineral nor quite microbial, a chemosynthetic glaze that lives on the hydrogen seeping from the hot rock and on the faintest traces of sulfur. The grazers, which are most of what you see, keep it cropped to a thickness you could not measure with a fingernail. They do not look up. Their mouths are on their undersides. Their eyes, when they have them, are simple heat-pits. Higher in the stack — on the walls, in the vault’s irregularities, in the few ledges that were once, perhaps, something else — hang the others: filterers of shed cuticle, drinkers of the almost-nothing that evaporates from corpses, things like articulated hooks that wait in the seams and take whatever brushes them. The food web is all consumer. It has been so for a long time. The rock itself is scored with old paths, grooves worn by generations of the same traffic, the same silent rerouting around a vent that closed a century ago and opened again last season.

Sound is scarce. The chamber should roar. It does not. Chitin on chitin produces a ticking so fine it is more felt in the teeth than heard. Once in a long while there is a dry crack — a molt, a predation, a body giving way — and then the tick resumes, slightly altered, as the vacancy is filled from all sides at once. They do not call. Whatever they use instead of calling is already in the air: a chemistry of alarm and invitation that you register only as a change in the heat against your face, a sudden attention that has no eyes.

The darkness is not empty. After a time the heat-pits of the upper animals become visible as a dim, shifting constellation, each one a dull coal. Some of the larger wall-dwellers carry a faint internal phosphor, not for seeing but for something else — a signature, a territorial stain of light so weak you doubt it until you look away and the afterimage remains. The vault’s highest point is occupied by a single mass that may be one animal or a fused colony; it pulses on a slower clock than the floor, and when it pulses the whole chamber’s temperature ticks up a fraction, as if the room were exhaling.

Scattered through this are the marks that are not animals. They are few. A ring of fitted stones around a cooler vent, the joints packed now with nest-material and shed plates, the purpose of the ring no longer readable. A single iron spike driven into a calcite seam and then overgrown, the metal blooming with the same black film as the rock. A shallow niche, man-high, whose back wall has been scraped smooth by something that was not claws; the niche is full, of course, stacked with the heat-tile creatures to the lip, but the shape of the emptiness it once held is still there, a rumor of intention. Nothing here was built to last, and nothing has been added in a long time. The animals have simply continued, using the old geometries as they use the new ones, without preference.

The ecology does not pause. A vent cycle completes. The floor’s living glaze shears along an invisible line; a pale hunter drops, takes, is itself taken two body-lengths later by something that had been indistinguishable from the wall. The vacancy closes. Higher up, a chain of the hooked things releases a rain of dry fragments that the grazers accept without looking. The air’s chemistry shifts. You feel it as a pressure change behind the eyes. The large mass in the vault pulses. Heat. Drift. Resettlement.

It has the feel of a place that found its shape a long time ago and has been practicing that shape ever since. The erosion is not weather. It is occupancy. The stone has been handled by so many bodies that its original fractures are gone, replaced by a topography of use: ramps that are only ramps because they have been climbed, basins that hold nothing but the memory of a cooler pocket, a single standing blade of rock in the chamber’s center that the traffic parts around as if it were load-bearing, though it bears nothing but its own slow polish.

You can walk, if you must, but not without becoming part of the count. They do not flee. They accommodate by the smallest possible margin, a living density that treats you as another fluctuation in the heat. When you stop, they close. The quiet, then, is complete enough that you hear your own pulse against the ticking, and the ticking begins to sound like a language you almost recognize — not words, not rhythm even, but the grammar of a closed system that has no outside, that has been, for longer than the plateau above it has had its present name, sufficient unto itself.

The chimney you came down is still there. The slot of sky, when you look up, is the color of a bruise. Nothing follows you out. The heat of the chamber stays on your skin for a long time after, a map of a place that does not need you and will not keep a space.
