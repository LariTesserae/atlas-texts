The highland does not begin as a view. It begins as a pressure change in the ears and a dimming that has nothing to do with weather. You have already been climbing for longer than the body likes to admit, and the last real color left you some hours below, down where the cloud-roof still remembers the idea of a sun. Up here the sky is a close, bruise-dark lid. It is not night exactly. Night would imply a day that had ended. This is the standing shade of a place that rose until the light could no longer afford the fare.

The stone is old in the way a tool is old after it has been used for one task so long the task and the tool have become the same shape. Shoulders of mountain, not peaks. Cirques that finished their work in some prior age and now simply hold their emptiness with a kind of courtesy. The rock is a dark metamorphic, fine-grained, polished not by hands but by a patience of thin wind and intermittent frost. It has the sheen of something worn past drama. You can walk a long time on it and feel that the mountain is no longer becoming. It is maintaining.

There is almost nothing on it.

That is the first true fact, and it has to sit for a while before the second fact is allowed in. The second fact is that the nothing is not barren. It is spaced.

Moisture arrives the way a rumor does. Not as weather you can point to. A seam of warmer air from far below will, once or twice in a long while, find a pass and well up through it, and the cold takes that air apart on contact. What falls is not quite rain. It is a brief, indecisive damp — a film on the stone, a bloom of rime that lasts an hour, a taste of metal and distance on the tongue. In the lee of every lip of rock there are pockets no larger than a basin or a palm, holding melt that never quite becomes a stream. A few threads of water do exist, appearing from under rime, crossing fifty body-lengths of open stone, and vanishing again into a crack as if embarrassed. The whole highland is like that: water as an intermittent loyalty, not a resident.

Where those loyalties collect — on the inner rims of the old cirques, in the long shallow troughs that used to be valley floors, along the one escarpment that still catches the upwelling — the forests stand.

They are not a forest in the continuous sense. They are lushness as an islanding. Each mass is thick past reason, a stacked darkness of frond and blade and hanging tissue, so internally crowded that the eye cannot find a trunk on the first look. Leaves the color of wet iron. Mosses that have learned to be structural. Things like ferns that have grown patient and enormous, their undersides permanently beaded with the condensation that is this country’s only reliable river. The air inside a grove is a degree less impossible than the air outside it. You feel that as a softness on the face, and as a smell: cold chlorophyll, and something mineral, like a battery left in snow.

Between one grove and the next there may be an hour of walking, or three, over stone that holds only frost-bloom and the faint polished paths of whatever crosses in the dark. The lushness is real. The emptiness is also real. They have agreed to share the highland by never quite touching.

Inside the groves the quiet is not the same as the quiet of the open stone. The open stone has the silence of thin air, which cannot be bothered to carry much. A footfall dies at your ankle. The groves have a silence that is full — a working silence. Drip. The almost inaudible unpeeling of a frond. A tick, once in a long while, like cooling metal. If you put your hand on certain stems you feel a tremor that is not wind. It is regular. It has shifts.

The plants are mining.

Not as metaphor. The root-mass of each island goes down through the old rock in sheets and pipes and fine electrolytic felts, and what it brings up is not only water. The highland is an exhausted orogeny; it is full of leftover veins, of ores that finished their geological argument ages ago and have been sitting in the dark like unclaimed tools. The groves have found them. They draw metals the way other forests draw sugar. They sort. They concentrate. Along the inner wood — if it is wood — run hairline currents, faint enough that you would miss them if the air were not this still and your skin not this cold. At night, which is to say in the slightly deeper register of the standing dark, some of the larger stems show a dull industrial glow, the color of a forge seen through flesh, or of a wire just too shy to be called lit. Nothing hums loudly. The work is chemical, capillary, patient as a factory that learned long ago that noise is a waste of heat.

When a stem dies it does not fall as timber. It sloughs a crust of purified slag and strange alloys, rose-copper and a blue-black glass that was never sand, and the grove grows around the deposit the way an ordinary forest would grow around a nurse-log. There are whole inner rooms of the larger islands that are nothing but these residues, terraced, drip-shined, cold as the stone outside and twice as intentional. No one stacked them. They are the byproduct of a production that has no loading dock and no whistle. The mountain is being refined by its own gardens. The gardens have been at it long enough that the process and the place have the same weathered look as the cirques: established, in-balance, not finished.

Life uses this.

The fauna is abundant in the way a city is abundant if you only count what happens after the lights go out and you wait. You will not see a crowd. You will see that every niche has been filled by something that does not wish to be heard. In the mid-canopy, things like sloths if sloths had been designed by a watchmaker — slow, gripping, their pelts beaded with the same condensation as the fronds, their mouths working steadily at the metal-rich tissues. They do not call. They taste. Lower, in the slag-rooms, pale grazers with mouths like spoons move along the alloy crusts, scraping, swallowing, laying down droppings that steam for a surprising time in the cold and then freeze into little glassy coins. Higher, almost at the roof of each island where the fronds thin toward the dark sky, there are fliers the size of hands, membranous, navigating by the faint electric weather of the stems. A predator will take one without sound: a long animal, furred in the same wet-iron color as the leaves, that unfolds from a vertical rest and is upon the flier and folded again before you have decided you saw it. The body is carried inward, toward the warmer currents near the big stems. Nothing cries.

Out on the open stone the census changes. Here the abundant life is a matter of crossing, not of dwelling. There are walkers. They are large enough that you feel them first as a change in the way the frost-dust sits on the rock, a regularity of print. Six-limbed, or four, depending on the lineage; the highland has had time to try both. Their chests are barrelled for the thin air. Their feet are padded and slightly adhesive, made for polished metamorphic and for the sudden damp. They move between groves on schedules that are not quite seasonal — season is a weak idea here — but metabolic. They go to drink from the plant-condensate. They go to lick salts from the slag. They go, some of them, to die in a particular island, as if the body were a shipment being returned. Between one walker and the next there may be a long emptiness. You can stand in that emptiness and believe you are alone, and be wrong only in the technical sense. The technical sense matters. The place is full of distances that have animals in them.

A few of the walkers carry light the way the stems do, a deep-body glow, banked. Others are dark and use the first kind as lanterns, trailing at a courteous remove. Predation on the open stone is a rare, almost formal event. There is too much space and not enough cover, and the air is too thin for a long chase. When it happens it is a meeting more than a hunt: two shapes arriving at the same pocket of melt, a brief rearrangement, one shape leaving heavier. Blood freezes in scallops. The silence after is the same as the silence before. The highland does not punctuate.

Weather is a cycle with a long radius. For days, nothing. The dark sits. Rime grows and is sanded off by the thin wind. Then the upwelling: a softness in the air that you feel in the teeth, a sudden beading on every surface, a brief indecision between rain and snow that resolves into damp and then into a million quiet ticks as the groves take what they have been waiting for. In the deepest cold that follows, the pockets glaze. The walkers’ prints become lacquered. Deep in the rock, far below the root-felts, something older than the groves still turns over — not a structure, not a machine anyone made, but a geothermal circulation that has the regularity of shifts. Heat rises in flues the plants have found and collared. It is why the inner groves never quite freeze. It is why the currents in the stems have something to work with. If you lie on certain patches of open stone you can feel, through all that worn cold, a remote and civil warmth, like a wall with a factory on the other side of it. There is no factory. There is only the mountain, still doing the one job it knows, and the gardens clocked to that job.

No path is cut. No cairn. No wire. The prints vanish under the next rime. What looks like a terrace is a slag-fall. What looks like a chimney is a stem that learned to vent. The industrial is everywhere and has never been built. It is a metabolism that scaled up until it borrowed the manners of manufacture — regular, extractive, electrified, producing more than any one body could use — and then kept the manners after it forgot the need for walls.

The rules are not written, but they are consistent, which is a kind of writing.

Light is a guest and should not be expected to stay. What you navigate by is texture, and the faint forge-color in the groves, and the rarer animal-glow out on the stone, and the memory of the last upwelling’s damp on your clothes. Sound is almost a courtesy: used sparingly, mostly by ice and drip. Distance is honest in one direction (the open stone will not lie to your legs) and dishonest in another (inside a grove, the stacked life folds space until a hundred paces can contain a climate). Water is a visit. Heat is a loan from below. Metal is a fruit.

The highland is not waiting to be discovered. It is in the middle of its work. The worn cirques hold their emptiness. The islands hold their crowds. The walkers continue their long, quiet deliveries across a country that is both nearly vacant and densely employed. If you stay long enough you will stop listening for a sound that would make the place make sense, and you will start attending to the small regularities instead — the tick of a stem, the scheduled glow, the way the next film of moisture always knows which leaves to find. That is the only language here, and it has been spoken long enough to have an accent of stone.
