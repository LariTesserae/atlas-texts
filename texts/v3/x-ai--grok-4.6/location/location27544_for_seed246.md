The plateau has already finished becoming what it is. Whatever mountain stood here has been ground down to a single remaining gesture: a shallow stone dish, high enough that the air thins in the chest, low enough in relief that you could mistake the horizon for a table if you did not know how far the world drops beyond the rim. The rim itself is barely a rim. Time has sanded it into a lip of rounded blocks and long, sleeping slopes. Nothing here is sharp. Even the fractures have been handled until they feel intended.

The dish is small. A person can walk the diameter between one change of weather and the next. It holds, at most, the plan of a town—and it does hold one. Streets radiate and cross with a patience that is either civic or mineral; after long enough the distinction fails. Walls remain, but they are not walls in the ordinary sense. They are the last competent thicknesses of whatever was stacked or grown or precipitated here, worn to waist-height in most places, to the shoulder in a few, and in three or four chambers still closed overhead where the stone was of a harder persuasion. The chambers have no doors. Their openings face the prevailing wind as if that were the only visitor still expected.

There was never metal in this place. No slag, no nail-shadow in the rock, no cut that remembers a toothed blade. The masonry, if it is masonry, is keyed by weight and by the memory of freeze. Blocks sit in one another the way old teeth sit. In the plaza—the one unmistakably open space, slightly dished, its floor a single pale pavement of fitted slabs—the joints have been caulked by centuries of grit and by a black-green moss that has the look of writing if you do not look directly at it.

Moisture arrives as a habit, not a river. At this height the cold is a constant low instruction, and the air, when it holds water, holds it as rime, as a grease of dew on the lee faces, as a fog that walks in over the western shoulder and sits in the dish until the dish is a bowl of milk. Then the fog condenses on every upright thing and begins, very quietly, to drip. The drips find the old gutters. There are gutters. They were cut as architecture and have been recut as creekbed; they carry the melt of rime and the occasional honest rain in thin, fast sheets that vanish into seams before they can become a stream with a name. After a bright hour the water is gone again, leaving only darker stone and a smell like iron and wet wool. In the lowest knuckle of the plaza a tarn persists, no wider than a room, black, filmed with ice at the edges even when the rest of the basin has thawed. It is less a pond than a held breath.

Where the damp lingers—north-facing wall-foots, the inner corners of the standing chambers, the seams of the pavement—the vegetation is out of proportion to the poverty of the rest. It is not forest. Nothing here has the confidence of a trunk. It is a lushness of the low and the stubborn: cushions of moss thick as bedding, liverworts in overlapping scales, a dwarf willow that crawls rather than stands, and, in the most sheltered cracks, a crowding of pale saxifrage and something like sorrel that flushes red when the temperature drops. In those pockets the green is padded, almost tropical in its insistence, a stuffed richness pressed into a climate that should not permit it. Step out of the pocket and the dish is nearly bare: lichen in maps, a few wind-combed grasses, stone. The volume of the place is empty. The sky occupies most of what you would call the town. Between one standing chamber and the next there is enough air to lose a shout, and often the shout is already there, belonging to no one.

The wind is the town’s remaining infrastructure. It does not pass through so much as inhabit. It arrives over the worn lip already organized, then finds the radial streets and is amplified, split, braided, slammed into the hard faces and sent back as a second and third weather. On ordinary days it is a continuous low roar with syllables in it—cloth-tearing, a distant choir that never resolves, the long groan of air being forced through apertures that were once doors or never were. On the days when a front crosses the plateau the sound becomes physical. It presses the ears from inside. Loose grit travels a handspan above the pavement in hissing sheets. The standing chambers boom like cisterns. Meltwater, if it has chosen that hour to run, is whipped out of the gutters and flung glittering and then is gone. There is no hush in the inventory of this place. Even the fog, when it is in, carries a faint, continuous sizzle of droplets striking moss.

Light is ordinary and therefore startling. At this elevation the sun, when the fog opens, is a clean instrument. Shadows of the low walls are knife-blue and very short at noon, then lengthen into the streets as if the town were being redrawn from one direction only. The pale pavement holds the light and gives it back without warmth. In the moss pockets the green goes emerald, wet, indecent. In the tarn the sky is a colder copy of itself. Night, when it comes, is the same geometry in negative: the dish still readable, the chambers still three or four black mouths, the wind still employed.

Animals are a rumor the place tells infrequently. Once in a while a hare the color of dirty snow will sit on the rim and decide against descent. Small birds use the standing chambers as momentary lee and leave; their droppings are the only recent amendment to the floors. In the tarn, under the skim of ice, something dark occasionally adjusts itself and is still again—larva, or a leaf, or a decision not to be either. The ecology here is mostly process without population: freeze and release, wet and unwet, the slow farming of lichen, the louder farming of weather. The town is an instrument the climate plays. The climate is not finished, even though the rock is.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a consistency that will not agree to be only one thing. Walk the streets long enough and the civic plan continues past the last wall and into the living shoulder of the plateau, where no block was ever set, and yet the same intervals appear as joints in the bedrock, as parallel runnels, as a suggestion of rooms that were never excavated. Or were never built. It is possible the town is what the mountain came to, given enough seasons—the hardest ribs left standing after everything softer went to grit and left. It is possible someone arranged the ribs and then had the patience to wait until arrangement and erosion became the same labor. There are places in the pavement where the fitted slabs are not fitted but continuous, a single rock persuaded into the idea of a floor. There are places in the standing chambers where the ceiling is a natural bedding plane and the walls are stacked, and the two meet without apology. No tool-mark argues the case either way. The place does not offer a verdict. It offers a finished shape that still works: channels that still channel, rooms that still hold a little stillness against the roar, a plaza that still gathers the fog as if gathering were its civic duty.

What moves, moves on short clocks. Fog in, fog out. Rime growing feathered on the windward edges and then sloughing in soft collapses you hear even through the larger noise, like books shut in another room. The tarn filming and clearing. The moss taking on water until it darkens and swells, then giving the water back to the air when the wind scours the dish dry. A rare bird. A rarer thaw that finds every gutter at once and makes of the town, for an hour, a single descending voice of water, street to street to the black mouth of the tarn, after which the voice stops as if cut, and there is only wind again, and the green in the corners brighter for having been used.

You can stand in the plaza and feel the plateau’s age as a kind of silence underneath the loudness—an old completion. The slopes beyond the lip go down for a very long time, but they go down smoothly, without drama, the work already done. This dish is what remains when a height has been allowed to finish its own sentence. That the sentence includes a town, or the image of a town, or the mineral habit of one, is the only unsettled thing, and it is not very unsettled. The wind has been through every room. The moss has signed every joint. The water, when it comes, knows the way. Nothing here is being invented now except weather, and the weather has been practicing on this same small, high, emptied, carefully organized hollow for longer than naming lasts.
