The highland does not cool. Heat stays in the water and the water stays in the air, so the first thing that happens when you come over the last rise is that the world goes white-green and thick, as if someone has wrung a jungle out at altitude and left the juice hanging. You can see a long way because almost nothing occupies the middle distances. What occupies them is weather.

Underfoot the ground is not ground. It is a poured, living pavement—corky, faintly warm, sweating through a thousand pinholes—so that every step finds a film of water already there, already running. The pavement is continuous and also unfinished. In places it is only a skin over older skin, a succession of lids. In places it has not yet decided to be solid and you walk in warm shallows that have no bottom you can trust, only a give, a flex, a sudden braid of roots like cables just under the glare. The glare is ordinary daylight doing extraordinary work: it hits wet everything and comes back at you doubled, silvered, slightly sick. There is no stone that has not been covered. If there was ever a mineral plateau it has been eaten, replaced, and then replaced again. The architecture is the geology.

Far apart—far enough that each one has its own weather—stand the masses.

They are not forests. A forest would fill. These are solitary, complete, and wrong in the way a building is wrong when it has no doors and no one who would use a door. Each mass is a construction of plant as if plant had been given the problem of shelter, of height, of catchment, and had solved it too many times. Walls of fused petiole, ribbed like vaulting. Buttresses that are themselves hollow stems, organ-pipes the thickness of houses, bleeding a clear sap that never quite dries. Terraces of leaf-plate locked edge to edge until they make floors you could lose a town on, except there is no town and no one to lose. The interiors, when the light finds a rent, are not rooms so much as hydraulic arguments: cisterns, sluices, hanging reservoirs, columns that are only xylem under enormous pressure. Water is always being lifted. You hear it. The sound is not a trickle. It is a continuous interior river, a roar held inside wood, as if the structure were a cliff with its waterfall inverted and caged.

Between the masses the flats run for miles. Open, regional, a kind of vegetal polder that does not stay put. Sheets of warm water, ankle to knee, ruffled by wind that has nowhere high enough nearby to break it. The water is not a lake with a name. It is a condition. It condenses out of the hot air as fast as it evaporates, beads on every surface, weeps from the undersides of the distant architectures in ropes and veils. The air itself is a marsh. Breathing is work. Your clothes are immediately the same temperature as your skin and then wetter.

Nothing animal lives here. The silence that should follow from that does not arrive. What arrives instead is plant-noise at a volume the ear keeps misfiling as industry or weather. Rapid lignification sounds like ice on a lake, a sharp report, then another, then a whole façade deciding to be hardwood in the space of a minute and cracking as it does. Transpiration at this heat is not invisible. It fountains. Whole towers stand in their own steam, white at the crown, and the steam collapses back as rain that never had a cloud, only a plant. When a gallery overbuilds—and they overbuild constantly—the failure is not quiet. A wing shears. A colonnade of living pillars goes over as one thought. The impact on the flats is a long, wet thunder you feel in the pavement, and then the fallen timber does not lie. It roots where it lands, or it is already being mined by neighboring tissue, drawn back into the nearest mass like a sentence being revised. By evening the wreckage has a floor. By the next hard rain it has walls again, but not the same walls. The plan does not repeat. The place has no memory that looks like ours. It has only urgency.

This is why the land is young. Nothing is given time to weather. Edges stay knife-bright: new bark like wet ceramic, cuticles still glossy, thorns that have not yet learned to blunt because they will be shed and remade before they can dull. Scarps of plant-matter stand as if quarried this morning, leaking, sharp enough to see the light catch their lips. There are no old hills of humus, no rounded anything. The highland is still emerging, but what is emerging is not rock. It is a construction event that has mistaken itself for a landscape. You can watch a ridge happen. A line of shoots punches the pavement in a file a kilometer long, thickens, locks, becomes a windbreak, becomes a dam, becomes a hanging garden whose weight the dam cannot take, becomes a new shallow sea, becomes a mud of shredded cellulose that the heat ferments into a skin, becomes pavement again. The cycle is not seasonal. It is hourly in places, daily in others. The whole region is in storm-state even when the sky is only the ordinary pale of high country. The storm is metabolic.

Light remains normal, which makes the strangeness worse. This is not a twilight world or a phosphorescent one. It is day, with a sun you could name, falling through steam and returning from water, and in that honest light the architectures rearrange like thoughts you can see. A staircase of bracket growth extends from a wall, finds no landing, retracts, extends elsewhere. Hollow trunks lean until they kiss, fuse, become an arch, become a tunnel that leads to a cistern, become a cistern that drains itself overnight into a bright new river aiming for a neighbor mass it will never reach because the neighbor is drinking the river from below. Purposes proliferate and cancel. You walk a causeway that is a single flattened trunk, warm, pulsing faintly with sap, and halfway across it begins to lift—not to throw you, only to seek a better angle on the light—and you are briefly on a rising road to nowhere, the flats falling away, the heat rising with you, the sound of interior water growing louder as the stem takes load.

There are no tools and no one who would use them. The built quality is total and biological. Every channel, every terrace, every load-bearing wall is a decision made in cambium and turgor. If there is a mind it is distributed through pressure differentials and the simple rule that height plus water plus heat equals more self. Competition is architectural. The sparse spacing is not peace; it is the distance required for each mass to throw weather at the others—shadow, rain-shadow, stolen humidity, allelopathic mist. Between them the emptiness is real and shining and loud with the flats’ own small violences: sheets of water arriving from a collapse up-slope, the pavement blistering into a dome and splitting, a sudden stand of reeds like spears that were not there at noon and will be timber by dusk and mulch by midnight.

At the edges of vision other masses stand in the haze, each a dark, complicated weather-system of its own, town-sized, valley-sized, enough of them to make a region and not a scene. You could spend a day walking the wet open and pass only two. You would hear the third before you saw it: that caged-waterfall sound, the cracking, the applause of a thousand leaves hitting their own rain. Closer, the smell is not flower. It is wet cellulose, hot tannin, a greenhouse left sealed. Sap on the wind is sticky. The pavement drinks your footprints and then forgets them, or incorporates them as a shallow that becomes a pool that becomes a mouth for a root.

Night, if you stay, does not empty the noise. Growth does not clock off. The masses creak and drink and fall and rise in the dark the way they do in the day, only now the steam shows as a dimmer white against a sky that is merely sky. Heat does not leave the water. The water does not leave. In the morning the map is different and the pavement is still warm and the nearest architecture has grown a nave it did not have, already flooding, already beautiful in the ordinary light, already about to fail.
