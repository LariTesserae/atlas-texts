The cleft you came down through still smells of broken stone. It is a recent wound: the plateau above sheared along a joint and dropped its floor twenty or thirty fathoms in a single season of thaw and seepage, and the edges have not yet learned to be old. The rock that forms the walls is pale, almost white where it is not already sheened with water, and it breaks in clean, knife-edged plates. Nothing has rounded them. Water has only just begun its work.

You are below the world’s ordinary surface, in a long, irregular trough that opens and closes like a valley that forgot the sky for a while and then remembered it in pieces. Daylight reaches you in ordinary strength through the collapsed stretches of ceiling and the many fissures that still drain the plateau’s rain. It is not cave-dark. It is the light of a wet, overcast afternoon that never quite ends, filtered through mist and the pale undersides of leaves. In the still-roofed galleries the same light arrives thinner, bouncing off wet calcite and the constant film of condensation, so that the whole place exists in one continuous, slightly greenish noon.

Water is the first fact and the last. It does not fall as weather so much as it weeps, beads, sheets, and stands. Every rock face is a spring. The air itself is heavy enough to drink; it condenses on your skin and on every needle and frond the moment the temperature slips another fraction. The floor is never simply ground. It is a mosaic of shallow, ice-edged pools, saturated peat, gravel bars still sharp from the collapse, and channels that braid and rejoin around every obstruction. In the wider reaches the water lies in broad, still meres whose surfaces are broken only by the slow rise of bubbles from below and the dimpling of rain that finds its way down from the plateau. The meres are not deep. The whole basin sits just under the water table; you are walking on a drowned country that has only lately been given air.

Because the rock is young and the water is everywhere, life has packed itself into every available plane with a kind of frantic, silent greed. There is no bare surface larger than a hand. Vertical walls are felted in liverwort and sponge-moss that hold water like a sponge and drip it in slow, regular pulses. Horizontal ledges carry cushions of sphagnum, then sedge, then thickets of dwarf willow and alder so densely intergrown that they form walls themselves. In the wetter bottoms, horsetails stand in ranks as tight as grain, their jointed stems clicking faintly when a current moves them. Ferns uncoil in every crevice that offers a spoonful of soil. The vegetation is lush in the way of a cold bog after a long thaw: not tall, not tropical, but utterly complete, every cubic foot claimed. You cannot put a boot down without compressing living matter. You cannot look more than a few body-lengths in any direction without the view being closed by a hanging curtain of roots, a dripping buttress of moss, or a palisade of pale stems.

The same crowding gives the place its unexpected urban quality. Water and plant and mineral have begun, in the short time since the floor dropped, to organize themselves into something that reads as streets, courts, and stacked dwellings. Travertine and tufa precipitate wherever a seep meets air; they build terraces, half-walls, and hollow cones that already look like the first storeys of houses. Willow and alder send aerial roots and suckers that knit these mineral walls into galleries and tunnels just large enough to walk. Beaver-work and the communal dams of smaller rodents have multiplied the channels into a hydraulic grid: narrow alleys of black water, broader “avenues” where several streams coincide, little plazas of still water where the animals have gnawed clearings. The larger willows have been coppiced by browsing and by the simple pressure of their own density until they stand as multi-trunked pillars with rooms of shadow between them. None of it was planned. None of it required a tool. It is only what happens when too many living things and too much water are forced into a fresh, sharp-edged basin and given a single growing season of unobstructed light. The result is a town that has no builders and no empty lots.

Animals are as tightly fitted as the plants. The water is full of newts and small trout that have come down with the springs; they hang in the clearer pools like suspended glass. Voles and water shrews stitch the moss. Insects are mostly the quiet kinds: springtails in uncountable numbers on the water film, crane-flies whose larvae live in the peat, midges that do not bite so much as simply occupy the air. Birds are present but subdued—dippers working the riffles, wrens threading the willow galleries, the occasional heron standing one-legged in a mere as if it had always been there. Larger shapes move at the edges of sight: something elk-sized that comes down at dusk to browse the new willow, something smaller and quicker that leaves only a V-wake in the shallows. They do not call much. The whole basin seems to have agreed on a lower volume. Sound is absorbed by moss, by water, by the sheer packed biomass. What you hear is a continuous soft dripping, the occasional metallic tick of ice forming on a shaded ledge, the almost inaudible rustle of a thousand stems adjusting to a change in current. When a breeze finds its way down a fissure it does not howl; it is combed into silence by the vegetation before it reaches the floor.

The cold is constant and specific. It is the cold of meltwater and of stone that has never seen a full summer. Your breath stays visible. The water is painful if you stand in it long. At night, and in the deeper galleries, thin skins of ice grow across the quieter pools and then dissolve again when the seepage warms a fraction. The plants are all species that accept this: they grow fast in the long wet light and simply stop when the temperature drops, holding their water like stored time. There is no frost-heave yet to tear the new soil; the place is too recently made, the water too continuously present. Everything is still in the first flush of colonization, every process running at once: precipitation of mineral, extension of root, the slow rearrangement of gravel by current, the nightly freeze and daily thaw of the uppermost film, the eating and being-eaten that never pauses even though it makes almost no noise.

If you climb one of the sharper residual towers of uncollapsed rock you can see the basin’s true size. It is not a single chamber. It is a chain of linked depressions, some still roofed, some open, stretching farther than the light will let you count. Each depression is its own crowded quarter, its own wet parish of moss-towers and water-streets, yet they communicate through low arches and drowned passages so that the whole is one continuous, saturated city of the newly exposed earth. Nothing here is ancient. The sharpest ledges still show the conchoidal scars of the break. The oldest tree is a sapling. The oldest dam is last season’s. You are walking through a place that has only just become possible, and it has wasted no time filling itself to the brim. 

The air tastes of wet limestone and chlorophyll and the faint iron of new springs. Stand still long enough and the dripping will map the architecture onto your skin. There is no dry place, no empty interval, no silence deeper than this quiet, and no edge that is not already being overwritten by the next root, the next film of calcite, the next quiet animal passing through.
