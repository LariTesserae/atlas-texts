The Hollow sits in a crease no map would bother to mark. From any distance the land reads as a single weathered plane of ochre and bone-pale mineral, heat-shimmered and featureless; only when you are already inside the lip do the walls declare themselves. They are not high. They do not need to be. The basin is a shallow, ancient spoon-bowl scoured by nothing wetter than wind and the slow crawl of thermal fracture, its rim worn into a continuous, sagging horizon that keeps the rest of the world out and the dimness in. Light arrives already exhausted, filtered through a standing haze of flour-fine grit that never quite settles. What reaches the floor is the colour of old brass left in a cupboard.

The air itself is the first architecture. It is thick, motionless except where it is violently not, and dry enough that breath leaves no ghost. Heat radiates upward from the packed mineral pan as if the ground were still remembering a fire it finished centuries ago. There is no moisture to carry scent; instead the nose fills with a faint, persistent tang of heated stone and something faintly sweet, like roasted dust. Sound, when it comes, is louder than the emptiness should allow. Wind finds every gap between the widely spaced structures and turns them into flutes and organ pipes. It does not howl so much as speak in overlapping registers: a low drone through the long colonnades, a higher rattle where loose plates of sintered sand still cling to walls, sudden gunshot cracks when a face of rock finally yields to the day’s expansion. The plants add their own percussion. Their fronds, broad and waxy and the dull green of oxidised copper, clap together like dry hands whenever a thermal gust arrives.

Those plants are the only lushness. They occupy discrete thickets, never a continuous cover, each grove separated from the next by expanses of empty mineral pavement wide enough that the groves feel like isolated rooms. The vegetation does not drink. It has never needed to. Its chemistry is a closed, non-aqueous bargain struck with the heat and the airborne mineral load: the fronds comb the haze, secrete a glassy varnish that traps micronutrients, and slowly mineralise their own tissues from the inside out. New growth is soft and almost fleshy for a season; older limbs become living stone, still photosynthetic, still flexing in the wind with a sound like distant pottery being stacked. In the dimness the varnish catches what little light there is and returns it as a dull, internal gleam, so that each grove appears faintly lit from within, a cluster of lanterns no one tends. There are no insects, no birds, no burrowers. The only motion besides wind and heat-shimmer is the plants themselves, which grow and shed and re-sinter on a pace just fast enough to notice if you watch one thicket from dawn-gloom to dusk-gloom. A fallen frond will be half-stone by the time the next thermal cycle begins.

The built things keep the same unhurried schedule. This is a city by organisation, not by crowding. Structures of rammed mineral, stacked plates of the local sinter, and occasional blocks of a darker, almost glassy rock stand far apart, each compound or tower or long low hall given its own generous emptiness. Streets, if they can be called that, are simply the unoccupied ground that happens to run between them; they are wider than any traffic this place ever required. The construction is entirely hand-scale: no metal, no fired brick in the wet sense, only material gathered, broken, fitted, and left to bake into permanence under the same heat that made the basin. Corners have been rounded by centuries of grit. Walls lean in minute, established ways, their slumps long ago accepted into the design. Doorways are simple rectangles or corbelled arches; some still hold woven screens of the mineralised plant fibre, brittle and musical when the wind worries them. There are cisterns that have never held liquid, only dust, their interiors polished by nothing but the passage of air. Stairways climb the sides of the larger halls and simply stop, as if the next storey were a thought that dissolved in the heat.

Everything here has been in this state long enough to have forgotten any other. The erosion is not active destruction but a finished conversation: wind, thermal crack, the slow rain of grit, the plants’ own mineral shedding. The city and the basin have reached an agreement. Dust dunes form in the lee of walls, migrate a few body-lengths over a season, then collapse and reform elsewhere. A tower that began to list did so generations ago and has remained at that exact angle, its shadow a fixed sundial in a place that has no clear sun. The emptiness between buildings is not neglect; it is the native density of the place. Sound travels the distances without obstruction, so that a single falling plate of sinter on the far side of the Hollow arrives as a sharp, delayed report, then echoes twice more among the groves.

At the very centre, where the basin floor is lowest and the heat most concentrated, one larger structure sits slightly apart: a broad, stepped platform of darker stone, its upper surface etched with shallow channels that lead nowhere. Around it the plant groves grow thickest, their mineralised limbs almost touching the lowest step. The wind is loudest here, funnelling across the open pavement and rising in a standing roar that never quite becomes a voice. The light, such as it is, pools in the etched channels and makes them look briefly like something that once flowed. Nothing does. The only cycles are heat and grit and the unhurried conversion of living tissue into more of the landscape. The Hollow keeps its own counsel, a pocket of organised vacancy held in perfect, ringing dryness, loud with the noises of a world that finished becoming itself a very long time ago and has been practising the same few motions ever since.
