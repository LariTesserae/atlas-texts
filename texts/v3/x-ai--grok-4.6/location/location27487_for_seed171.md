The highland does not yet have a proper name because it does not yet have a proper edge. What you would call a continent is still deciding whether it is stone or leaf. From orbit it would read as a single dark-green weather system, a bruise of living pressure sitting too high on a young crust, generating its own rain and eating its own light. Up close there is no “up close.” There is only inside.

The substrate is plant. Not soil with plants on it. Plant all the way down until plant gives way to something that is still learning to be rock. Roots have not so much anchored as precipitated: they exude mineral in thin, cold films, a slow sweated geology that has not finished hardening. Walk—if walking is even the word—and the ground yields like wet cork, then firms, then pulses once as a deeper vascular bundle adjusts. You are standing on a cambium the size of a weather front. Beneath that, more cambium. Beneath that, a cool darkness of compressed xylem older than the local mountains, which are not mountains yet, only lift, only the forest’s own mass dragging the crust upward as it thickens.

There is almost no empty air. Density here is not crowding; it is occupation. Stems have flattened into walls. Leaves have stacked into ceilings and then into further floors. Petioles have braided into load-bearing cables that never heard of tensile strength as engineers mean it, because the math they run is older and wetter and does not publish. Every gap that might have been a path has been filled by a younger generation growing in the damp. You move by displacement, by the forest making a temporary vacancy around you and then closing it, the way flesh closes around a splinter it has decided to keep.

Water is not a feature. Water is the medium the plants have agreed to be. It hangs in the air as a standing mist that never quite rains and never quite clears, condensing on every surface until every surface weeps. The weeping is structural. Guttation at this scale is a climate: droplets the size of fists well from hydathodes you could crawl into, run in sheets down living bark, collect in cisterns that are themselves hollowed petioles, then get pulled back up by a transpiration engine so large it has its own barometric identity. The whole highland is a single cool lung, inhaling vapor, exhaling vapor, never dry, never warm. Temperature sits in that narrow band where breath shows and joints ache and sap moves like syrup just before it sets.

Light arrives already used. Whatever sun this world has is filtered through kilometers of stacked chlorenchyma and a sky that is mostly the forest’s own exhalation. The result is a green-black dimness, not night, not day, a perpetual understory even at what should be the canopy, because the canopy has no top. Photons are currency and they are hoarded. Some leaves have gone nearly black to catch the last of them. Some have become translucent panels, shunting what little remains deeper, a living fiber optics no one designed in a lab. You see by residual glow, by the faint chemiluminescence of overworked tissue, by the occasional pale bloom that opens only to dump heat and then shuts again.

There are no animals. The niche is full. Anything that might have chewed or nested or flown was outcompeted by surface area itself. The forest does not hunt. It occupies. What might once have been insects are now just more leaf, more hair, more gland. Silence would be the natural result except the opposite is true.

The sound is total. It is not one sound. It is every hydraulic event at once. Cavitation in xylem the width of rivers pops like continuous artillery. Stomata in their billions hiss. Growth itself is audible: cell walls stretching, a low wet tearing that never finishes. Water sheets strike water sheets in the dark. The packed mass conducts everything; a rupture a hundred kilometers away arrives as a pressure change in your teeth. There is a register below hearing that you feel in the sternum, the forest’s own pulse, the planetary-scale movement of sugar and signal. People—if people ever came, and they have not built so much as a cairn here—would not converse. They would endure. The roar is the place thinking.

And it is thinking. This is the part that does not fit the old physics. The vegetation computes. Not metaphorically. Phloem is a bus. Electrical potentials propagate not as simple action but as coordinated, error-correcting fronts that adjust growth, weather, mineral deposition, even local gravity wells so slight you only notice when a hanging vine hangs a few degrees off true. The forest is assembling itself according to a program that includes the bedrock it has not finished making. It is embryonic on purpose. Erosion has barely begun because there has not been time, and because the plants will not permit waste: every loosened grain is recaptured, bound in mucilage, laid down as the next course of proto-stone. The highland is under construction and the contractor is the building.

Weather here is biological. Cells swell and the pressure aloft changes; a bloom-front a thousand kilometers wide opens in the same hour and the resulting evaporative drop pulls a cold front out of nothing. Mist becomes rain becomes mist again without ever quite falling, a closed cycle the size of a nation. At the margins—there are margins, theoretically, somewhere the living mass thins toward older, deader crust—the forest is still arriving, a green tide climbing, rooting, lifting, filling. Behind that tide the world is already finished being empty.

Nothing here was poured or cut or stacked by hands. There are no roads, no towers, no instruments left to rust. The technology is the tissue. It runs on gradients and quantum-coherent excitons in antenna complexes that should not remain coherent this long, this wet, this cold. It does anyway. The unusual thing is how ordinary it has made the impossible feel: of course the ground is alive, of course the sky is a leaf, of course the noise never stops. You stop noticing the contradiction the way you stop noticing your own blood.

Stand still long enough and the vacancy around you begins to close. Not hostile. Just complete. A tendril tastes the salts on your skin and files the data. A leaf turns a fraction to steal the heat of your throat. Water finds you and weeps down you as it weeps down everything else. The dim goes on. The roar goes on. Far below, something that is not yet granite takes another micron of shape, secreted, cool, packed, and already full of roots.
