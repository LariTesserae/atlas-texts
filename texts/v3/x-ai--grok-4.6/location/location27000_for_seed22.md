The sound arrives first, and it never leaves.

It is not one sound. It is a weather of sound: a low load-bearing groan that seems to come from the ground’s own unfinished joints, overlaid with a high continuous tearing, as if silk the size of a province were being ripped along a single endless seam. Between those two registers live clatters, wing-roars, hydraulic syllables, the periodic slam of something vast seating itself into something vaster. There is almost no quiet to measure it against. In the rare thinning, you do not hear silence; you hear the shape of the last noise still occupying the air, like a heat-shimmer of the ear.

Only after the hearing has been occupied does the basin admit it is a place.

It is low country, newly so. The land has the look of something that finished arriving last season and has not yet decided how to weather. Ridges stand as sharp as broken crockery. Channels are cut too cleanly, their walls unsoftened, their beds paved with plates of mineral that still show the stress-lines of their cooling or their lifting. In places the ground is tiled with hexagonal joints, pale and precise, as if a giant kiln had been emptied and left to star. Dust lies in the seams but has not yet had centuries to become soil in the old sense. Where moisture has worked—briefly, locally—the edges are already failing in bright flakes. Everything looks recent. Everything looks slightly too exact to have been worn.

The elevation never amounts to much. You can stand on a blade of this young stone and see a very long way, not because you are high, but because there is so little in the way. The basin is a region, not a room: a town-or-valley of distances, a mid-country of repeated emptiness. Far off, other edges of the same unfinished geology lift and fall like the lips of a wound that has not scabbed. The sky sits close and dim above it, not night, not day—an uncommitted bronze-gray, as if the light were being rationed or as if it had to travel through a medium that tired it. Warmth holds steady in the dimness. The air on the skin is the air of a closed mouth, of a greenhouse after the sun has gone: damp-capable, never quite wet.

Water here is an event, not a resident. It comes as night-sweat on the stone. It comes as a sudden ten-minute rain that hisses on still-hot mineral and vanishes into vapor before it can argue with the dust. It comes as beads along the undersides of leaves and along the bellies of the structures. In the lowest fractures, dark pockets stand for a day or two, skinning over with iridescence, then sink. There are no rivers worthy of the name—only suggestions, dry calligraphy waiting for a season that may not be on the calendar yet. The plants have made their arrangements with this. They are not thirsty in any familiar way.

And yet the green, where it is allowed, is indecent.

It does not carpet the basin. That would be a different world, a filled world. Here the vegetation is lush the way a shout is loud in an empty hall: concentrated, almost violent, and surrounded by nothing. At the feet of the structures, in the long condensation-shadows they throw, in hanging galleries the buildings themselves seem to exhale, the growth is thick enough to lose a body in. Leaves the size of doors. Stems with the girth of columns, already woody though the land under them is barely older than a rumor. Mosses that construct rooms, layer on layer, as if practicing architecture. Vines that do not climb so much as scribble, writing the same urgent sentence over every upright thing. The color is not a single green. It is greens with fever in them—copper-green, black-green, a green that looks wet even when it is not. When the brief rains come, the leaves move as if applauding. You can hear them. Of course you can. You can hear everything.

Between these thickets: almost no one, almost nothing. Kilometers of bare young lithic skin. Heat-shimmer. The dim bronze light lying down in sheets. A person walking the interval between two inhabited places will go for an hour seeing only their own hands and the next structure hanging in the air like a thought that has not yet found a skull. The emptiness is not desert in the old, romantic sense. It is more like the interior of a lung between breaths, or the pause inside a struck bell. Volume without occupancy. The city planned this, or failed into it, or requires it.

Because there is a city. You would not call it a city if you came from a street-grid and a crowd. You would call it a collection of decisions, spaced as if they disliked one another. The structures stand at settlement scale across the basin—enough of them, organized enough, that the region is urban in the way a constellation is a drawing. Towers, yes, but also rings laid on their sides, and inverted wells whose lips are gardens, and long low bars that might be walls if walls were allowed to forget what they were containing. They are not brick. They are not quite metal. Their surfaces take the dim light and hold it under the skin, so that at a distance each building is a dull lantern, a bruise of illumination rather than a source. Up close, the material shows grain that rearranges while you look, not quickly, not as a trick, but the way a muscle rearranges under work. Walkways exist, then don’t; apertures iris; a staircase will be there in the morning because last night someone needed it, or because the building did.

The interiors are mostly hollow. That is the other emptiness, the one inside the urban fact. You enter expecting streets stacked on streets and find instead a nave, a shaft, a volume so large the far wall is weather. Habitation clings to the inner surfaces the way the lushness clings to the outer ones: galleries, nests of rooms, farms of condensate, workshops where the light is a little less dim because something in the walls has been asked to remember noon. People live here. Not a crush of them. A city’s worth if you counted across the whole basin, a village’s worth if you counted in any single monument. They move along the inner skins and call to each other across the voids and do not seem lonely, which is either culture or damage. Their tools do not always have edges you can see. A woman will set her palm on a bulkhead and the bulkhead will become a window onto a depth that is not outside. A child will spill a cup and the spill will run uphill, tidying itself, humming. No one explains this as magic. They explain it, when they explain it at all, as courtesy—the building’s, or the substrate’s, or the courtesy of whatever physics was negotiated when the land was raised.

That raising is still audible. It may be the groan under everything else. The basin is not finished being land. Processes continue that older worlds would have had the manners to finish in the dark, underground, across eras. Here they happen in the open, in the warm dim, at a biological pace as much as a geological one. A ridge will lift a handsbreadth in a week, shedding plates. A channel will find a new grammar after a rain. The structures adjust; you feel them take a new stance in the night, a slow flex, and the sound-seam tears a little higher. Some of the inhabitants say the city is scaffolding. Some say the city is a splint. Some say the city is what called the ground up and is now obliged to stay and listen to it heal.

The animals agree with no one and are everywhere the emptiness can still be called abundant.

They do not pack the air in the way a jungle packs itself. There is too much air. But they use it. Flocks move like weather systems, darkening the already dim light in passing, their wing-noise a second architecture. Things with too many joints pour down the sharp new gullies when the stone sweats, shining, tasting mineral, gone by what passes for morning. In the lush pockets the density of life becomes briefly ordinary—ordinary if you accept mouths on the undersides of leaves, and pollinators that are more temperature than body, and grazers the size of benches that eat moss-rooms from the inside and leave the walls standing. Predators follow. You hear the predation more than you see it. A scream will start in a hanging garden three monuments away and arrive intact, unsoftened by distance, because the basin is a single acoustic chamber and the air has nothing better to do than carry.

There are other fauna that do not fit the word well. Pale ribbons that live in the tearing-seam of the sound itself; you see them only when the pitch changes and they are shaken loose, drifting, tasting of ozone, then threading back in. Soft drums in the hollow buildings that are animals if a lung is an animal, swelling and emptying with a thunder you feel in the floor. Night-things—though night is only a dimming of the dim—that walk the bare lithic stretches on too few feet and leave prints which fill, by morning, with a jelly the plants will drink. The ecology is in motion the way a wound is in motion. Pioneer, overclocked, slightly wrong in its haste. Seeds take on stone that should not feed them. Fruiting bodies open with a sound like wet cloth and are emptied by mouths that should not have found them yet. The food webs have the brightness and fragility of something invented this year.

Cycles keep the activity from becoming mere thrash. The moisture-tide is the clearest. Once in a rotation the structures release what they have been holding: fog sheets from vents you had not noticed, a sweating of the outer skins, a brief generosity. The dim light goes milky. The lush zones riot—audible growth, a crepe of new leaf, the moss-rooms adding a story. The channel-pockets fill. The jointed bright things arrive in their pouring. For an hour the basin is almost a wet place, almost a filled place. Then the warmth takes it back. Vapor lifts. The tearing-seam of sound changes key, as if a tool had been set down and another picked up. The emptiness reasserts, steaming a little, smelling of iron and nectar and the inside of a charged machine.

There are other rhythms. The buildings’ flex. The flocks’ clockwise then counterclockwise years, if years are what they are. A predation-pulse that seems to travel the basin as a rumor of teeth. Weather that is not weather: bands of thicker dimness that drift, and within them the sound muffles in a way that makes people uneasy, because they have come to need the roar the way other cities need streetlight. When the muffling bands pass, children are kept from the outer galleries. Something hunts by the relief of quiet. No one has a good picture of it. The pictures they do have do not agree about legs.

Rules emerge if you stay, the way paths emerge in grass, except there is almost no grass in the open.

Sound is public property and also a kind of sight. People navigate by pitch as much as by the dull-lantern silhouettes of home. A change in the groan means the ground has lifted; a change in the tear means the air’s grain has shifted, and with it the way the buildings will listen to a hand. Distance is not entirely honest. Walk toward a monument and it will sometimes recede for a while, not as mirage but as policy, until you have thought about why you are coming. The inhabitants shrug at this. They say the city is allowed to be shy. They say the new land is still learning what a visitor is.

Light remains dim even at what should be noon, and the plants do not appear to mind. Whatever they drink from the damp, they drink something from the bronze-gray as well. Stand in a hanging garden and you will see the undersides of leaves faintly lit, as if each blade kept a private dusk. The warmth never quite becomes heat, never quite becomes comfort. It is the temperature of work.

If there is a center, it is not a plaza. It is a confluence of louder sound, where three of the greatest hollows stand within sight of one another and the interval between them is planted more thickly, almost a forest, almost a street of green. There the emptiness relents by a degree. You can see other people without shouting. You can hear, under the roar, actual voices—barter, prayer, complaint, the ordinary urban music at last, nearly drowned. Condensate runs in guided films down the inner walls into cisterns that smell of metal and leaf-rot. Someone has tried to teach a vine to make letters. The vine has made something else, looping, wet, which the birds that are not birds prefer.

Beyond that confluence the basin continues, monument after monument, green clot after green clot, bare new stone, warm dim, the seam tearing, the ground occasionally remembering to rise. It extends past any single day’s walk, past the idea of a town and into the idea of a country that is all one unfinished civic act. No wall encloses it. The urban condition simply thins, the way a note thins, until the last structure is a rumor of lantern-color on the horizon and the lushness is only a stain at its foot and the fauna is whatever can live on distance.

You could call it desolate if you closed your eyes and attended only to the spaces between. You would be wrong the moment you opened them, or the moment a flock erased the sky, or the moment the moisture-tide came and the young ground shone as if it had been planned to shine. It is a hollow place densely used. It is a quiet geometry that will not stop screaming. It is a city that has not yet been given enough world, and a world that has not yet been given enough time, and both of them busy, warm, dim, damp at the edges, green where they can stand to be, and strange with the particular strangeness of things that are still being decided.

Stay until the buildings flex. Feel the floor take a new angle, polite, enormous. The groan deepens. Somewhere a channel finds a sharper letter to cut. In the gardens the leaves applaud, or brace. The pale ribbons in the sound-seam flicker as if laughing. Then the stance is taken, the land is a little more land than it was, and the roar—unchanged to a visitor, changed in a way the inhabitants hear the way one hears a familiar voice enter a room—goes on, filling every empty cubic measure it can find, which is nearly all of them.
