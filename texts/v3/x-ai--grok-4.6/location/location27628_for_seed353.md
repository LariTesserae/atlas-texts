The plateau has no name that still functions as a name. It is simply the high place that remains after every other high place was ground away. Whatever mountains once stood here were reduced, over intervals that make geology feel impatient, to a single worn table of stone. Then the stone itself was eaten. What you walk on now is not ground. It is a continuous, living slab several meters thick, a rhizome-foundry that long ago digested the last basalt and recast it as itself. The elevation is still there—thin air, a sky that should be enormous—but the sky is gone. In its place is the underside of everything.

There is no water. There has been none for so long that the chemistry of life here never learned the habit. The plants (the word is a convenience, not a description) run on heat and mineral grit and a dark, viscous solvent that is more cousin to liquid glass than to sap. Their vascular systems are kiln-tubes and slag-channels. What they circulate is a suspension of metallic salts, polymerized hydrocarbons, and catalytic spores, kept just below the temperature at which it would flash into vapor. You can put your hand against a trunk and feel the slow, pressurized surge of it, hotter than blood, utterly dry.

The entire volume is occupied. There are no clearings, no gaps, no unused cubic meters. Trunks the diameter of silos stand so close their fused bark forms continuous walls. Above them the canopy is not a canopy but a second terrain: interlocked heat-exchanger plates, some the size of courtyards, folded and refolded until they make a solid roof that admits neither light nor weather. The plates are the color of old rust, of bone left in a furnace, of cooling slag. They radiate in the infrared. What little visible light exists is the dull cherry of internal processes leaking through translucent sections of living ceramic. You navigate by heat on the skin and by the faint, directional glow of those leaks. It is not night. Night implies a day that will return. This is simply the optical condition of a world that metabolizes in the dark.

Everything is made. There is no “natural” substrate left to contrast with the constructed. The plants are the architecture, the machines, the power grid, the warehouses, the streets. A corridor is the space between two vascular bundles that have not yet anastomosed. A factory floor is a horizontal platform of compressed, lignified tissue that grew itself level, then sprouted rows of reaction chambers the way other forests sprout mushrooms. Pistons of living wood-metal rise and fall inside trunks. Valves open and close with a motion too slow to startle. Conductive fibers—black, glassy, warm—run through the bark like veins and carry current generated from thermal gradients. In some districts the plants have grown electromagnetic coils as casually as they grow leaves. The coils hum at a frequency you feel in your teeth more than hear.

The heat is constant and graded. Near the geothermal vents—circular, lipped openings that may once have been volcanic and are now lined with specialized, heat-harvesting epithelium—the air is nearly unbreathable, a dry 70 degrees that smells of scorched mineral and something faintly sweet, like overheated resin. Farther out, in the “cooler” production zones, it drops to a merely oppressive 50. There is no rain to moderate it, no standing water to evaporate. Convection happens through designated chimney-plants: hollow, fluted trunks that rise through the canopy and vent the excess thermal load into whatever still exists above. The resulting winds are slow, circling, laden with metallic dust and spores. They are the only weather.

It is ancient in the way a finished sentence is ancient. This configuration was reached so long ago that the system no longer remembers another way to be. The original erosion of the plateau is complete; the plants simply continued the work, grinding, incorporating, rebuilding the topography according to hydraulic and thermal logics that have no outside reference. The place has settled into its final form, which happens to be a state of perpetual, local catastrophe. That is the turbulence. Nothing is still. A trunk will swell over the course of hours, splitting neighboring structures along old graft-lines. The damaged tissue is immediately colonized by runner-plants that germinate from industrial spores—each spore a seed and a schematic, carrying not just genetics but the encoded processes for a specific machine-organ. By the next thermal cycle a new distillation column or heat-sink array or structural brace has already begun to lignify. Entire districts undergo programmed senescence, collapsing in a whisper of dry fibers and cooling metal. The collapse is not failure. It is feedstock. Neighboring masses overrun the wreckage before it finishes settling, converting it into the next iteration of themselves.

The sound of all this is mostly absence. The air is too thin, too dry, too packed with living matter for acoustics to travel far. What remains is a subsonic pressure, a collective vibration of a billion slow pistons and valves, felt more than heard. Occasionally a sharp, dry crack as a load-bearing member fails and is instantly shored by new growth. A hiss of superheated gas from a pore. Then the hush again. The chaos is visual and thermal and structural. You feel the landscape rearranging itself around your body: a wall that was two meters to your left is now three meters and has sprouted a row of filtration membranes; the floor has risen half a centimeter and grown warmer. Distances do not stay honest. The living architecture inserts new rooms, new mezzanines, new production floors faster than any map could be drawn. The plateau is larger on the inside, or at least more folded, a single organism that has been subdividing and consuming itself since before the concept of “before” applied.

There are no animals. The niches fauna would occupy were absorbed so thoroughly that the idea of a separate mobile kingdom never arose, or arose and was metabolized. Pollination is accomplished by electrostatic discharge and heat-current. Decomposition is internal, a closed-loop composting that happens inside specialized trunk-chambers. Nothing walks or flies or burrows that is not plant. The only movement that is not growth or collapse is the slow, tidal shifting of the entire mass as it equalizes pressure and heat across kilometers.

The strangeness is not decorative. It is the logic taken to completion. A wall respires, but what it exchanges is not oxygen; the gas is something sharper, perhaps a chlorine compound or sulfur vapor, the waste product of a chemistry that never needed water. Structures that look like flowers open and close with thermal cycles rather than light, revealing inner surfaces traced with mineral circuit-patterns that grew there the way other plants grow veins. In the deepest production strata the plants have begun to grow optical fibers of living glass, carrying not images but thermal data, so that one part of the plateau “knows” the heat-state of another without any nervous system that a zoologist would recognize. You can stand in a narrow channel between two reactor-trees and watch a faint, heat-haze aurora crawl across the undersides of the exchanger plates overhead—waste photons from processes that have no use for visible light and therefore leak it like a forgotten byproduct.

The scale is regional, not intimate and not planetary. You can travel for days. There are long, shallow depressions that function as collection valleys, their floors paved with a smoother, more compressed rhizome that still yields slightly underfoot, like walking on the back of something that is only pretending to sleep. There are ridges of stacked vertical factories, each trunk a city of photosynthesis and metallurgy, linked by aerial vascular bridges that sway, very slowly, in the convection winds. The whole high place—perhaps eighty kilometers across, perhaps more, the edges lost in the same darkness that fills the interior—is one continuous made thing. It produces. What it produces is more of itself, and components that other sectors require, and heat, always more heat. The loop is so old it has no exterior. If there was once a purpose, a commission, an original seed planted by someone or something, that information has been composted along with everything else.

You do not leave tracks. The rhizome closes behind you, not quickly, but with the unhurried certainty of a process that has been practicing the same motion since the last ocean died. The air tastes of hot dust and a sweetness that might be the solvent itself, volatilizing in trace amounts. Your skin prickles with the infrared. Somewhere far off, too far for the sound to arrive as sound, a major trunk begins its collapse, and the entire plateau, in its darkness and its silence and its packed, ancient, industrial life, leans a fraction of a degree to compensate, then grows the difference away.
