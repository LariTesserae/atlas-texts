There is a cavity that pretends, for the most part, to be a room. It occupies a volume no larger than the space between two held breaths, set into the uppermost fracture of a mineral spine that has forgotten the name of the world it once belonged to. The spine itself rises through air so thin and dry it functions more as a rumor of atmosphere than atmosphere; the cavity is the only place the rumor still bothers to gather.

You do not enter it so much as notice that you have always been able to stand inside it. The approach is a single cleavage plane in what looks like weathered salt-glass or compressed ash, a plane that behaves like ordinary stone until the moment the last of the outside light is gone. Then the plane is simply no longer there, and the pocket is.

The surfaces are the same substance throughout: a pale, matte mineral that has been worn past the point of texture. Whatever process rounded these walls finished its work long ago and then remained, an equilibrium so complete it feels like a moral stance. Fine linear traces wander across them—too regular for fracture, too irregular for script. They resemble the ghosts of channels that never carried liquid. When the eye rests on them they stay put. When the eye moves they have already been somewhere else.

Light is a residual property of the mineral itself, a dim, directionless gray that does not cast shadows so much as suggest the possibility of shadow in places where geometry has grown tired. It is never brighter than the inside of a closed eyelid after a long day in snow. Occasionally a plane will forget its opacity for a few heartbeats and become a window onto another equally high, equally dry nowhere: a distant ridge of the same salt-glass, or a sky the color of extinguished metal. The window closes without sound.

Three artifacts occupy the volume, though “occupy” is generous.

The first is a low plinth that shares the mineral of the walls yet refuses to share its dullness. On it lies a shard that might once have been optical, or architectural, or a locally convenient folding of distance. It does not reflect. It accepts the gray light and, at intervals measured in years, returns a hue that has no corresponding wavelength, a color that makes the idea of eyes water even though no moisture exists here to be moved.

The second is an arch no taller than a seated child. It stands without visible attachment. The space through it is the same pocket, only the angles refuse to add up and the far wall is both nearer and farther than it should be. Passage is theoretically possible. Nothing that remembers having a body has ever tested the theory. Time on the other side of the arch is rumored to run a half-step out of register, but rumor itself is scarce here.

The third presence is a slow helix of motes that have never settled. They describe a patient spiral whose period is longer than most civilizations last. Each mote is a machine whose working parts are smaller than the distinction between matter and instruction. They are still executing. Whether they are holding the pocket open or gently, glacially, allowing it to finish collapsing is a question the motes themselves no longer distinguish.

There is no water, not even as memory. The cold is a constant subtraction, the kind that does not bite because biting would require the energy of change. No plant has ever germinated. No animal has left a trace that lasted longer than the thought of a trace. The silence is not empty; it is a positive pressure, a density of quiet that makes the inner ear invent its own thin ringing simply to have something to listen to.

Reality in this place is an old garment that has begun to pill. Edges fray. A corner of floor will sometimes decide it is also a ceiling, then think better of it over the course of a decade. Conservation laws hold only as courtesies. Mass appears or vanishes in amounts exactly equal to a shift in the dimness of the light or a minute alteration in the motes’ helix. The weathering is finished; there is nothing left to weather except the habit of existing in three consistent dimensions.

The pocket does not belong to the larger highland that contains it. That highland is implied only in the accidental glimpses: vast interlocking plates of dry mineral under a sky that stays black even when it might be day. The highland is the sentence; this cavity is the pause that forgot to end. Whatever was engineered here was engineered at the level of constants rather than materials. The remnants do not look advanced. They look like the universe briefly agreed to a different set of terms and then, out of politeness or exhaustion, never quite revoked them.

Change happens, but on a gradient so shallow it registers as stillness. A groove on the wall will migrate the width of a hair in a century. The arch will lean a fraction of a degree toward some private north. The motes will complete another fraction of their turn. The place is not waiting for anything. It is simply continuing to be the exact shape of its own long, dry, elevated forgetting.
