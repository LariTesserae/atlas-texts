The plateau does not remember rain. That is the first thing it will tell you, if you learn to hear it the way its inhabitants do: not as metaphor, not as weather-lore, but as a structural fact, like load-bearing or the direction of grain. Whatever sea once stood over this stone has been gone longer than the stone has been stone. The high country was worn down after the water left, which is the wrong order for most worlds and the only order this one has ever admitted.

You come up onto it through a long, shallow climb that never quite becomes a pass. The last real relief died out of the land so long ago that even the idea of a summit feels ornamental. What remains is a table of unthinkable age, lifted and then sanded by time until it is nearly a plane, with only the faintest residual swells—like the memory of a shoulder under a very old sheet. The air is thin and cool and permanently dim. The sun, if that is still the right word, hangs as a pale coin behind a sky the color of tarnished pewter. There is no haze of moisture. The dimness is drier than that: a high, fine particulate that the living ground continually sheds and continually reclaims, a weather of dust that is also a weather of skin.

Because the ground is alive. This is not a figure of speech and it is not evenly true.

The entire plateau is one continuous organism, or one continuous argument among organisms that stopped distinguishing themselves before language was invented. It is plant in the way a reef is animal: not a forest standing on soil, but a body that became the substrate. There is no dirt. There is cambium the color of old ivory and old iron, silicified in places, still metabolizing in others, cool under the palm, faintly giving, like very dense cork or the interior of a horn. It photosynthesizes without green. The chemistry is solid-state and almost waterless—photon to lattice, oxide to sugar-analogue, ion exchanged through dry contact the way a battery exchanges charge. Where a tree of the wet worlds would have run sap, these tissues run emptiness. They are chambered. They are, by nature and by extreme age, hollow.

That hollowness is why the place is loud.

Wind is not dramatic here. It does not need to be. The thin air moves almost constantly across a highland the size of a small nation, and every meter of living ground is an instrument. The chambers—millions, billions, nested and cross-braced and worn as smooth as river rock though no river remains—take the wind and turn it into a standing chord that never resolves. It is not a howl, exactly, and not a drone. It is closer to a choir that has been rehearsing since the first cooling of the world and has forgotten there was ever a piece of music, only this. On still days (there are few) you hear the smaller voices: the tick of contraction as the temperature slips a degree; the dry percussion of fauna in the distance; the cities, which hum.

The verticals stand far apart.

Density is a courtesy the plateau declined. For kilometers the living floor runs almost featureless, a pale, faintly undulating expanse, and then a column. The columns are what is left of the organism’s old ambition toward height. They are worn as everything here is worn—shoulders rounded, fluting softened, bases flared into the floor as if they had been poured rather than grown. Some are no thicker than a keep. Some have the footprint of a hill. All of them are hollow, and all of them have been inhabited for so long that habitation and growth can no longer be sequenced.

These are the towns.

They are not built so much as conducted. The people who live in them—and it is not always clear that “people” is the first category that applies—practice an engineering that does not distinguish between horticulture, architecture, and a kind of applied topology. Rooms are larger inside than their skins. Corridors remember. A stair that follows the old vascular helix of a column will, if you walk it with the right attention, deliver you not only upward but *later*, or into a chamber that exists primarily as a standing wave in the column’s song. Heat moves without fluid. Information moves without wire. The whole plateau is a single dry nervous system, and each town is a thickening of that nerve, a ganglion grown into streets.

At dusk—which is most of the time, the dimness only deepening by degrees—the towns show as a low internal light, the color of cooling metal, leaking from apertures that were never cut. You hear them before you see them. The hum is part machine and part metabolism: harmonic engines seated in the oldest wood, or what wood became when it learned to do without water. They pump nothing wet. They pump temperature, charge, and sound. The sound is infrastructure. A message between towns travels as a modulation in the plateau’s chord, a phrase the floor conducts the way old timber conducts a footfall. You can feel it in your teeth if you lie down.

Fauna are everywhere and never crowded.

They do not drink. They have the same disrespect for solvent that the ground does. There are flocks of things like paper kites with too many articulations, grazing the shed dust, their wing-edges ringing faintly as they pass; you hear a flock two valleys over as a shimmer in the chord, a brightening. There are grazers the size of cattle and the texture of weathered basketry, moving in slow bands across the open, cropping the living floor down to a paler nap which then grows back over a season of cool weeks. There are predators for the grazers: long, jointed, almost ceremonial animals that run with a dry clatter like thrown sticks, and whose hunting calls are one of the plateau’s louder facts—a falling scale that the columns take up and return, so that a single hunt can be heard, delayed and transfigured, for an afternoon. There are smaller lives in the chambers themselves, clicking, rasping, singing in registers the wind does not use. None of them are wet inside. If you opened one (the towns have laws about this, old laws, almost geological) you would find structures like smoked glass and compacted pollen, and a lymph that is more like warm resin than blood.

They are abundant the way stars are abundant: not in a heap, but in a sky.

The ecosystem has been finished for an age. That is the strangeness that sits under all the other strangenesses. Nothing here is evolving toward anything. The last speciation event is a fossil in the language. What you are walking on is an oldest-stable-state, a metabolism that found its final form when the world still had mountain-shadows worth mentioning and has been running that form, without revision, ever since. The activity is not progress. It is clockwork at biological scale: dust-seasons and static-seasons; a “flowering” that is actually a release of conductive spores, when the air for a few days becomes a glittering circuit and the chord rises until conversation in the towns has to be conducted by touch; migrations of the kite-flocks timed to a rhythm no one had to invent. Predator and prey have been the same conversation for so long that their bodies look designed for one another, which they were, and then the designing stopped.

Even the light participates in the finishedness. It does not change its mind. Cool, dim, directionless except for that one pale coin. Shadows are soft and few. At what passes for night the spore-weather sometimes phosphors, a slow dim crawling in the air, and the floor answers with a light of its own, very low, the color of a bruise seen through paper. The columns stand in it like the pipes of an instrument no one is large enough to play, except the wind, which is always playing.

If you stay, certain things become apparent that a first crossing will not give you.

The plateau is not flat in the way a map is flat. Elevation here is a habit of mind as much as a measurement. There are places—never marked—where the living ground thins over older stone, and the chord drops out, and you stand in a sudden awful quiet that the ear refuses, and you understand that the organism has edges, or at least attenuations, and that beyond them there is only the mineral it once ate and then became too complete to need. There are other places where the floor thickens into slow reefs, barely waist-high, worn as everything is worn, and the towns harvest these for repair, cutting living plugs and setting them into the columns, where they take without fuss, without wet, knitting dry to dry.

The people of the columns have a technology for distance that is not travel. A room in the third town can be, for an hour, a room in the seventh. This is not a door. It is a negotiated identity of interiors, held by the same topological horticulture that makes the stairs misbehave. They use it the way other cultures use roads: sparingly, for need, because the holding costs something in the chord, a tax the whole plateau pays, a brief flattening of the music that every living thing feels as a change in weather. Children are taught the etiquette of this before they are taught the names of the flocks.

No one here has a word that means thirst. They have a word that means the memory of a solvent, and it is a liturgical word, used in the way some peoples use the names of dead gods: carefully, and not in the house.

Walk out from a town at the dim day’s brightest. The column falls behind you more slowly than its distance would suggest; the air is clear in the dry way, but the eye keeps wanting mist that will not arrive. The floor gives a little. The chord is in your ribs. A band of grazers moves along a swell so ancient it is almost only an idea of height, and their cropping sounds like rain on a roof, which is a sound no one living has heard from the sky. Far off, a hunt-call starts its falling scale, and the nearest column takes it, and the next, and for a while the whole open country is one sentence being said by a mouth too large to see.

You are very high. You can feel it in the thinness, in the cool that does not come from shade. The world drops away at the plateau’s remote rims, not into valleys but into a receding of the organism, a long pale decline toward whatever the low country became when the water ended and the plants refused to end with it. Up here the refusal succeeded so completely that success is the only weather.

The dust lifts, the dust settles, the chambers sing, the towns hum in their worn throats. Nothing is young. Nothing is finished becoming, because it finished so long ago that becoming is no longer a local custom. And still it is not still. The kites turn on their many joints. The resin-blooded runners pass, clattering. A spore-season is coming; the air has that faint metallic taste that means the circuit will close and the music will rise and the rooms will hold their distances more cheaply for a while. The pale coin slides a hand’s width. The floor, which is the forest, which is the body, which is the oldest city, conducts every footstep into the chord and does not distinguish your weight from any other season of pressure it has known.

There is no water. There has been no water. The plants did not mind. They became the ground, and the ground became a throat, and the throat has not stopped sounding since the mountains lay down.
