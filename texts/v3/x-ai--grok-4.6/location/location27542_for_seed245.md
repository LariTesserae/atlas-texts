The air here is always just after rain that never quite arrived. It hangs low and warm and salted with mineral, a dim broth you walk through rather than breathe. Everything sweats. Not metaphorically: the surfaces weep. Mortar beads. Fired clay gives up a film. Hammered sheets of dull copper run with condensate that traces the dimples of whatever primitive hammer last touched them, then falls, then is replaced. There is no sky distinguishable from ceiling, only a long lowering of the same grey-amber dusk, and from it a continuous, almost silent precipitation of hot droplets that do not cool the world so much as redistribute its heat.

This is a made place. There is no substrate underneath the making. Dig and you find more brick, more packed clay, more courses of uncut stone fitted by patience rather than geometry. Dig further and the courses forget their orientation; they begin to lean into vaults that were never planned, or into the empty sockets of cisterns that have not yet decided to hold water. The construction is total and embryonic at once. It is being laid now. It has always been being laid. Nothing here is finished, and nothing is natural.

The basin goes on at a scale that invents its own weather. You can stand on a plaza of unglazed tile the color of old blood and watch a storm of steam form three days' walk away, a pale column rising from some district of kilns that are lighting themselves. The storm will drift. It will rain hot lime-water onto walls that have not set. It will slump a colonnade that was only a suggestion of a colonnade, a line of uprights waiting for an entablature that may never be invented. Then the steam will thin, and the quiet will come back, which is most of what there is: drip, the tick of cooling ceramic, once in a long while the remote report of a collapse, like a book closing in another room.

No plants. The vocabulary of growth has been replaced by the vocabulary of stacking. What looks at a distance like a copse is a grove of unfinished chimneys, each one a throat of coiled clay, some smoking faintly from firings that have no potter. What looks like moss in the seams is only efflorescence, salt blooming out of wet brick, crumbling at a touch into a grit that tastes of lime and old fire. There are no animals. There is no one. The tools exist without hands: mallets of stone left on ledges, adzes with edges of chipped basalt, wooden forms (the wood itself is a problem — it is there, and it is not grown; it is a dense, grainless stuff, more like cooled pitch, carved into molds for mud-brick and then abandoned when the mud decided to take another shape). The technology is the technology of the first cities and the last ditches. Everything is packed, coiled, fired, hammered, wedged. Nothing is machined. The vastness of the result is therefore a kind of blasphemy against its own means. Someone, or something, or the place itself, has been doing simple work for a length of time that simple work was not meant to survive.

Low country. You feel it in the water table, which is not below you so much as among you. Channels have been scratched and then formalized and then forgotten: shallow cuts lined with broken tile, leading from nowhere to cisterns the size of lakes, the cisterns themselves only half-walled, their rims dropping into a glare of still, hot water that holds the dim ceiling like a second, slightly greener dusk. Walk the margins and the made ground gives in a way ground should not. It is not mud. It is courses of brick with water between them, a masonry marsh, and your weight sends a slow pulse out through the lattice that arrives, much later, as a faint lift in a distant floor. The elevation never commits to a hill. What rises does so as ziggurat and fails as ruin in the same hour. Terraces step down toward more terraces. Retaining walls retain only the idea of retention. The whole continent, if continent is the word, is a floodplain of intention.

Sparse. The made things do not crowd. Between the great unfinished courts there are distances of almost-empty paving, a skin of tile and rammed earth going on until perspective itself gets tired and begins to invent mirages of more gates. A single tower will stand for a day's travel in any direction, not tall, never tall — the world does not like height — but thick, a drum of cyclopean blocks sweating in the dim, with a stair that winds to a platform where nothing has been installed. Another day's walk: a field of kilns, hundreds of them, most cold, a few breathing the dull orange that is the brightest this place ever gets, and around them the failed pots, the slumping jars, the tiles that cracked in the heat and were left where they lay because there is no economy here, only the continuation of the gesture. Then emptiness again. Then a wall that runs to the horizon and stops for no reason, its last stones still stacked beside it as if the work-gang had only just set them down, though there has never been a work-gang that you could find.

The heat is not a climate so much as a medium. It comes up through the brick. It comes down with the condensate. It lives in the copper, which is always too warm to keep your palm on. There is a smell like a potter's yard after the rain, like wet ash, like the inside of a jar that held something fermented and was emptied a long time ago. In the deeper cuttings, where the masonry goes down through older masonry, the heat thickens until the air shimmers and the edges of things become negotiable. This is where the weirdness is least polite.

Reality here is a joint that will not stay true. Count the sides of a brick and the number will not repeat. Walk a cloister that is square and arrive having turned only two corners. A channel that ran left yesterday runs inward today, into the thickness of a wall, and if you follow it you find a room that should not fit, a chamber whose floor is a single fired slab still ticking with heat, whose ceiling is so low you must crawl, and at the far end a window that opens onto a different weather: a white, roaring fall of water that cannot exist, because there are no heights from which water could fall, and when you look away and look back the window is only a niche with a damp stain. Distances mis-add. A plaza that took an hour to cross takes three to recross, or takes no time, and you are on the far side before you have decided to start, your feet dry, the tiles behind you already re-patterning. The construction is not only unfinished; it is undecided. Vaults invert into basins. Stairs become aqueducts and then, while you are standing on them, remember they were stairs. Mortar sets around absences — the outline of a tool, of a hand that was never there, of a word in a script that has no language — and then the absences fill with water and become eyes that do not look.

The turbulence is the world's work. This is not a ruin and not a building site. It is a storm of making. Walls go up in the far dimness with a speed that should require a thousand bodies and produces only a change in the silhouette, a new shoulder of masonry against the low light, and then, equally without spectacle, a section sloughs, not into rubble exactly but into a new intention: a ramp, a quay, a long stepped descent into a pool that was not there. The Solaris quality is exact and unkind. The place dreams in brick. It tries a form, inhabits it, abandons it. Sometimes the trying is violent. A whole district will shudder, the way a sleeper shudders, and when the shudder passes the street-plan has been rewritten in wet clay, the old lines visible only as ghosts of darker tile beneath the new skin. You can watch, if you have the patience and the stomach, a colonnade assemble itself: drums of stone rolling into alignment as if downhill, though the ground is flat, stacking, the capitals flowering into crude volutes that look like the memory of a plant this world has never grown, and then the whole thing deciding against the idea of columns and sagging into a covered drain, water already finding it, already speaking in the dark.

Sound stays mostly at the edge of hearing. The great motions happen in a hush that feels like respect or like shame. What you hear is local and small: the continuous weep, the occasional sharp tick of a glaze giving way, your own steps coming back to you late and from the wrong direction. Once, a long way off, something like a choir of cooling kilns, a chord of contracting clay that lasts a minute and is gone. Wind is rare; when it comes it is only the heat deciding to move, and it carries no dust of life, only the fine grit of eroded mortar, which is not erosion in the old sense. Erosion has barely begun. The world is too new in its surfaces, too continuously renewed. What fails is not worn down; it is redesigned. The rawness is everywhere — arrises still sharp, tool-marks still speaking, the wet shine of clay that has not decided to be brick yet. And yet the scale is ancestral. You feel both truths at once: that nothing here has finished becoming, and that it has been becoming longer than mountains usually last.

At night — if the dimming can be called night, a further subtraction of an already-thrifty light — the water holds what glow there is. The cisterns become the only skies. You look down into them and see the suggestion of the ceiling, and behind the suggestion, sometimes, a deeper construction, inverted, continuing, as if the work goes on below as above, a second basin, a second set of empty courts, and in those courts the same sparse towers, the same patient kilns, the same weather of steam. Whether that is reflection or a real descent is one of the questions the place will not hold still long enough to answer. Drop a shard of tile and the ripples go out and do not come back the way ripples should. They square themselves. They find the corners of the cistern and climb.

There are no names that stick. You can try to call a district by the color of its clay or the habit of its walls, and by the time you return the clay has been recast, the habit broken. Navigation is a practice of attending to the present tense of masonry: this wetness, this heat coming up through these particular courses, this alignment of a distant drum-tower with a nearer gap in a wall that may be a gate tomorrow. The world is large enough that you will not run out of it. It is empty enough that you will not be delayed by company. It is quiet enough that you will hear your own thinking start to take the shapes of brick, start to stack, start to slump. Stay long enough and you will leave something — a arrangement of stones, a scratch in a drying slab — and when you look again it will have been accepted into the work, or corrected, or used as the seed of a room that was not in you when you made the mark.

The rules, such as they are: water seeks the made hollows and invents more of them. Heat is the kiln that never cools. Nothing grows and nothing hunts. Everything that exists has been put, and is being put, by a craft that does not know itself as craft. Geometry is a rumor the walls keep trying to remember. Time is the interval between one decision of the clay and the next. And the quiet, which is almost kind, is only the sound of a continent thinking with its hands.
