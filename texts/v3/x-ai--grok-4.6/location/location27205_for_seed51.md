The basin has been finishing itself for longer than the ridgelines remember.

It sits low, not in a dramatic sink but in the kind of long, worn-down country that rain prefers: a regional trough of old floodplain and older lakebed, barely convex, barely concave, the sort of land that water treats as a conversation rather than a slope. From any slight rise you can see a great deal of it—hours of walking, a town’s worth of distance if towns were a thing that happened here—and what you see is never empty. The openness is real. So is the crowding. The two have learned to occupy the same sentence.

Light arrives already used. It is not night. It is the color of wet pewter, of the inside of a shell, of sky after it has been wrung through cloud and then through the standing vapor that never quite lifts. You can see far. You cannot see clearly. Edges of stands and channels and pale stone shoulders keep their identities at a distance and then, as you approach, become more themselves than the dimness promised.

The air is water’s unfinished thought. It condenses on everything: on the dark leather of the ferns, on the undersides of leaves that look as if they have been varnished by a patient hand, on your skin if you stand still long enough to be counted as landscape. The rocks weep. They have been weeping so long that weeping is a geology. Some of the low outcrops are fluted and hollowed, not by wind so much as by the continuous downgrade of vapor into film into drip. The stone is cool. The water leaving it is cooler. Where the drips have fallen in the same places for a duration that has no honest unit, the receiving ground has dimpled into basins the size of palms, then bowls, then dark little cisterns that never go dry and never quite fill.

This is not a swamp in the ordinary sense, though maps made by people who only visited once would call it that. There is soil. There are hummocks. There are stretches of black peat that take a footprint and keep a version of it, slowly, as if considering. Water stands in sheets and in threads and in lenses just under the surface, so that walking is always a negotiation with a second ground. In places the second ground is louder than the first. Step, and something answers—not an echo exactly, more a confirmation, as if the water table had been waiting to be included.

The vegetation is lush in the way of places that never fully dry and never fully warm. It is not a riot. It is a saturation. Mosses occupy every register: tight dark cushions on the wetter stones, long drowned greens in the runnels, hanging beards on whatever will hold still. Ferns shoulder one another in the dim. There are trees, but they have the posture of things that decided long ago that height was a rumor. Their trunks are short, their roots extravagant, their bark mapped with lichens that look phosphorescent until you get close and find they are only very cleanly pale. In the more open reaches—those deceptive meadows of sedge and low shrub—the green is almost black, a cool, stuffed, wet color that drinks what little light arrives and gives back a dull sheen, like oiled iron.

You would expect birds. You would expect insects in clouds. There are neither, not in any ordinary density. The basin is loud without them.

The loudness is structural. It has several voices and they do not take turns.

First the water, which is never doing only one thing. It beads and releases from a million edges. It sheets off the hanging moss in sudden unzippings. It moves underground in channels you can hear the way you hear blood in your own head if the night is quiet—except here it is not quiet, and the sound is of a great many slow slides, a regional plumbing that has had geologic time to perfect its gossip. When a condensation front comes through (they do; this is an active country, not a still one), the air thickens another degree and the dripping becomes a rain that cannot decide whether it is falling or assembling in place. The sound then is like applause heard through a wall, or like a forest of small bells made of tin and old glass, never quite rhythmic, never quite not.

Under that, and through it, there is the other noise: the one that does not belong to weather as weather is usually employed.

It is a low, continuous, many-throated pressure, sometimes mistaken at first for wind in a gorge, except there is no gorge and the air is barely moving. It rises and falls on a cycle longer than weather and shorter than seasons. When it swells, the standing water takes on interference—rings that do not originate from any thrown stone, nested geometries that hold for a few breaths and then slacken into ordinary ripple. The moss leans. Not toward or away; it leans as if remembering a direction that is not on the compass. The dimness deepens by a fraction, as if the light were being spent on something else.

Whatever is happening is not built.

There are no walls. No pylons. No roads, no cairns that were clearly cairns, no cut stone that admits a chisel. The basin does not contain artifacts in the way a ruin contains them. If there was ever an engineering, it finished by becoming climate. What remains is a condition: a hydrology that behaves as if it were still coupled to a process current physics would not fully countersign. Water here does not only obey the usual persuasions. In the long, established grooves of the basin it also—rarely, locally, as if by an old habit it has not entirely retired—runs for a few body-lengths up a face of rock, or hangs in a curtain that does not fall, or occupies the same volume twice, two skins of wetness slightly out of register, so that looking into a pool is briefly like looking at a thought and the thought’s correction.

This has been true long enough to have weather of its own. The impossible has eroded. It has found its stable forms. The uphill rivulets have worn shining tracks in the weeping stone; moss grows in the lee of the hanging curtains and nowhere else; the double-pools have shores that account for both surfaces, a slightly drunken braiding of peat and grit. The strangeness is not a tear. It is a terrain. Reality here is worn thin the way a step is worn, in the places of most use, and the thinness has a polish, a settled usefulness, as if the world had discovered a cheaper way to be continuous and then lived in that discovery until it grew lichens.

You feel the density even when the sightlines are long. The air is occupied. Layers of mist do not stack so much as interleave, cool strata you walk through like rooms without walls. Sounds occupy volume the way bodies would. Distances misbehave with the patience of old liars: a dark copse that is clearly an hour off is also, in a way you will not be able to explain later, immediately to your left, its wet bark smell arriving without travel. The two facts do not cancel. They coexist the way the two skins of water coexist, and the basin has been in-balance with this for so long that the game trails—when you find them, rare, pressed by something heavy and infrequent—dogleg for reasons that have nothing to do with contour.

The fauna, when it occurs, does not correct the loneliness. It deepens it. Once in a long walk you may see a pale back moving in a deeper channel, too smooth, too extensive, the suggestion of an animal that is mostly an event of displacement. Or a clustering of small gelatinous bodies in the dim meres, briefly, like a thought the water had and then recanted. Or, at the loudest swell of that unweather sound, a lifting of something from the peat that is not a bird and does not become a bird, a shape that uses the extra room the thinness provides and then is not using it. Tracks persist longer than the creatures. The peat keeps versions.

None of this feels newly broken. That is the quietest and most important fact. The basin is not a disaster mid-sentence. It is a completed climate. The fronts move, the hanging water forms and slacks, the double-pools breathe their slight offset, the ferns drink, the rocks continue their inexhaustible weeping, the great low sound works through its cycle like a tide that forgot the moon and found another reason. Cool. Dim. Crowded with absences that have weight. Open enough that you can watch a change arrive from a long way off, a darkening of the already-dark air, a brightening of the wet on every surface, the applause-through-a-wall rising as the condensation front walks the length of the country and the country, which has practiced this, answers in all its worn and willing throats.

If you stay until the swell passes, the light returns to its used pewter. The hanging curtains thin to threads and then to the ordinary business of drip. The second ground quiets to a rumor. You can hear, then, the smaller wetnesses—the individual beads releasing, the cisterns receiving, a single fern letting go a load it can no longer justify. The basin does not empty. It only resumes the version of fullness that it can sustain between events. You are still in a place that is mostly water, some of it decided, some of it still thinking. The land remains low enough to keep every thought. The air remains cool enough to make each one visible, for a moment, on the next thing it touches.
