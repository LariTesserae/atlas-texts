The plateau does not rise so much as it remains. Whatever ranges once stood here have been worked down to a single decision: a shelf of pale mineral so high the sky has no middle distance, only a hard, close blue and then the curvature of the world showing as a faint whitening at the rim. You feel the height in your teeth and in the way your breath thins before it is finished. The air is cool the way stone is cool after a long night that never quite ended. Light is ordinary and merciless—sun, shadow, the honest grammar of day—but it has nothing wet to catch on. There is no haze. There is no glitter of distant water. Distance itself looks dry.

Underfoot the ground is a packed flour of salt, gypsum, and something older that the wind no longer bothers to name. It does not drift much. The grains have found their angle of rest and intend to keep it. What erosion could do, erosion has already done. The last of the great cutting happened so long ago that the channels are only suggestions now, broad shallow troughs like the memory of rivers that decided they were a mistake. The land is not jagged. It is not dramatic in the young way. It is finished. An ancient, near-flat finality at an altitude that still feels like an argument with the planet.

Sparse things grow. They do not look like they are trying. Pale crusts of lichen the color of old bone occupy the lee of every low rise. A few wiry, leafless shrubs stand at intervals so regular they seem placed, their roots mining whatever trace chemistry still moves in the mineral. Once in a long walk you find a plant that is not a plant: a branching structure of translucent salt that has copied the idea of a bush and then stopped, satisfied. Nothing here is lush. Nothing here is even hopeful. The vegetation is a punctuation, not a sentence.

And yet the place is dense.

It is dense the way a held breath is dense. The open goes on and on—valley-scale, region-scale, a tableland that keeps offering another horizon after the one you thought was last—but every volume of it is occupied. The occupation is animals.

They are everywhere. They do not part for you. They do not startle. They stand, sit, crouch, hang, overlap, interleave. Herds that are also flocks that are also colonies. Bodies packed to touching. A thousand flanks against a thousand other flanks, a layered saturation of hides and carapaces and feathers that have gone the color of the ground. Some are the size of fists. Some are the size of houses. They occupy the same spaces in ways that should be impossible: a rank of antelope-things standing inside a rank of larger, slower shapes, both sets equally present, neither casting the correct shadow. Wings that do not beat fill the air in still veils. Things with too many joints are folded into the salt like handwriting. The density is not a stampede frozen mid-panic. It is an equilibrium. This is how many living forms the plateau decided it could hold, and then it held them, and then it stopped.

They make almost no sound. What sound there is is the small, dry tick of salt contracting in the cool, and once, if you wait long enough, the suggestion of a breath that might belong to you. The animals do not vocalize. Their throats are open or closed in permanent attitudes. Eyes are open. Eyes are shut. Some eyes are not eyes. The silence is not empty; it is full of withheld noise, as if the world inhaled a long time ago and found no reason to exhale.

Nothing changes. The light moves, technically—the sun performs its ordinary arc—but the plateau does not participate. Shadows lengthen across flanks that do not twitch. A grain of gypsum may roll, eventually, and then it will not roll again. There are no seasons here that matter. There is no weather that arrives as news. The temperature stays in its cool register. The fauna stay in theirs. Time has the quality of a held photograph that is also the actual place. You can walk for a day and the arrangement will be the same arrangement, only seen from a slightly different angle, which is not the same as change.

Scattered through this stillness are the built things, and they are few enough to feel like errors, or like apologies. A ring of upright slabs no taller than a person, their inner faces scored with marks that might be counting or might be a map of something that was never water. A low wall of fitted salt-blocks half-swallowed by the pale ground, its corners rounded by the same ancient patience that flattened the mountains. Here and there a cairn, or the idea of a cairn: stones stacked by hands that understood levers and lashings and not much else. A few shallow pits lined with beaten metal so thin it is almost foil, stained with residues of some dry chemistry—offerings, or attempts to catch the night’s frost, or both. The technology is hand-scale, pre-confident. Whoever made these things did not expect to stay. They marked, they counted, they left a tool or two of bone and hammered copper, and the plateau absorbed the gesture without comment.

The rules are simple until they are not.

You may walk. The animals will not move out of your way; you move through them the way you move through a crowd that has agreed, collectively, not to be a crowd. Sometimes your shoulder passes through a shoulder. Sometimes it does not, and you feel hide as dry as paper, as cold as the air. You may not drink. There is nothing to drink. The chemistry of this place is non-aqueous and faintly wrong; if you put your tongue to the salt it tastes of iron and old batteries and a sweetness that is not sugar. Vegetation will not feed you. The fauna will not flee, will not charge, will not die further. They have already arrived at their last form.

The weirdness is not a spectacle. It is a wear in the fabric. At the edges of vision the packed bodies refuse a single ontology: a creature is a creature and also a fold in the plateau and also a hole shaped like a creature through which you can see more plateau, more animals, stacked in a depth the land does not have. Distances lie in a polite way. A structure that was a mile off is under your hand without the interval of approach. Sounds you did not hear arrive late as a pressure in the sinuses. Once, the entire herd-field seems to inhale together and the sky darkens a half-shade, then returns, and nothing has moved. Reality here is not broken so much as overused. Too many presences per volume, for too long, in a place that finished changing before minds were invented. The plateau has settled into its oldest stable state, and that state includes a saturation of life that life cannot, strictly, support. So the support is provided by something else—by stillness, by height, by the refusal of water, by an agreement older than weather.

If you stay, you will begin to understand the density as a kind of courtesy. There is room for you because there is room for everything, and the room is made of the fact that nothing needs to happen next. The animals have been arriving for geological ages and they have all, now, arrived. The mountains have lain down. The rivers have taken their names with them. What remains is this cool, open, crowded silence: a high desert of forms, mineral and once-alive and not quite either, extending past every reasonable edge, holding its last shape in ordinary light, forever.
