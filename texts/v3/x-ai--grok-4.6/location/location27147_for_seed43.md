The way in is never a door so much as a surrender of height. You come off the dry glare of the upper flats and drop, first by a stair of fired brick so worn the treads are scoops, then by a ramp of rammed earth polished to a dull skin by centuries of heels and hooves. The sky contracts to a rectangle. Heat does not leave you; it only changes its source. What was sun becomes clay radiating back the day, and the breath of many bodies, and the low continuous work of fire in alcoves.

You are below the world’s surface, but you are not in a cave. There is no living rock here. Every plane that meets your hand was mixed, stacked, beaten, or woven. The outer shell is a thick bowl of adobe and lime, repaired so often that its original geometry has gone soft—corners rounded by palms, thresholds slumped into gentle saddles, walls re-plastered until they look poured. Inside that bowl someone long ago built a second landscape, and then another, and then filled the interstices until the whole volume is a single saturated object: rooms that are also corridors, benches that are also cisterns, roofs that are floors for the next occupancy up. Cathedral-tall in the central well, hamlet-small at the edges. You can shout and be answered from any far niche. You can walk the entire circuit between one meal and the next.

The central well is the only true open. It is a roughly oval shaft, perhaps thirty paces on the long axis, its floor a shallow dish of packed lime that darkens where water is allowed to stand. Around it the built world rises in stacked galleries—timber joists black with smoke and oil, rails of lashed reed, platforms of sun-brick cantilevered on corbels that have sagged into smiles. Light arrives normally, which here means specifically: three light-wells punched through the overburden, their throats lined with white plaster so the day comes down as a milky column rather than a blade. At noon a disk of sun lies on the lime like a coin. In late afternoon it crawls up a wall and sets the ochre plaster on fire for an hour. Night is lamps—clay saucers, fat, floating wicks—hundreds of them, because the density of occupation will not tolerate dark corners. The light is ordinary and yet always slightly wrong in color, stained by soot and by the yellow of the clay itself.

Water is not a presence so much as a habit. There is no river. There is a captured seep, led in through a buried channel of fitted stone (the only work here that feels like masonry rather than modeling) and voiced into a trough that never quite fills. The trough sweats. Condensation beads on the cooler north faces after dusk and is wiped off with rags into jars. After the rare rain, the light-wells deliver a brief, startling percussion—water hitting lime, water finding the old cracks, water being shouted at as people run with pots. For a day the air tastes of wet plaster. Then the place drinks it. Damp lives in the lowest courses, in the animal pens, in the undersides of the hanging stores. You can press a palm to a wall at knee-height and feel cool; at shoulder-height the same wall is warm and dry as bread.

Almost nothing green volunteers. A few pots of something leafy are dragged on sledges into the noon disk and dragged out again before the leaves scorch. Pale roots hair the seep-stone. In one neglected gallery a film of moss has decided the condensation is enough, and it makes a dark map that no one scrapes away because it is useful as a clock of the seasons. That is the vegetation: tended, portable, or accidental. The rest of the biomass is animal and human and the things they make.

The fauna is the second architecture. Pigeons own the highest joists and the lips of the light-wells; their droppings are harvested with the same seriousness as the seep. Goats are folded into a ring of waist-high walls near the trough, their heat a second hearth, their voices a continuous complaint that the space amplifies into a braided roar. Chickens stitch the floor. There are lizards the color of plaster who hunt the insects that hunt the grain. There are cats who are not quite owned. There are beetles in the grain jars and moths in the wool and, at night, something smaller and many-legged that ticks across the lime when the lamps dip. People move through this the way current moves through a crowded channel—always touching, always heard. Density here is not a crowd so much as a packing: bodies, beasts, hanging baskets, stacked jars, spare timbers, hides on stretchers, the vertical library of tools. Near-touching is the ordinary state. To stand alone you would have to climb into a light-well.

Everything is made, and almost everything was made by hand with the same short list of means. Stone is used as found and as battered—hammerstones, querns, the lined channel. Metal is rare enough to have names; a copper knife is inherited. The rest is earth, fiber, fire, and time. Bricks are still formed in wooden molds in the upper yard and lowered on slings. Reed is cut far away and carried down to be split and woven into walls that are more screen than barrier, so that air and sound pass and the galleries breathe. Wood is ancient, reused, scarred with old joinery that no longer matches any present need. The craft is primitive in the sense that no machine larger than a bow-drill exists, and sophisticated in the sense that a thousand years of repair have taught every surface how to fail slowly. You can read the place like a midden of gestures: the thumb-prints in the plaster, the soot-fan above each niche-hearth, the grooves worn by ropes, the shine on the corners people always grab.

Sound is the weather. It never stops. The well takes every noise and gives it back thicker—bleat, grind, laugh, the wet slap of washing, the wooden thunder of a dropped shutter, the high continuous argument of birds, the irregular clock of mallets on copper and on hide. When the goats are restless the whole bowl seems to vibrate. When someone sings (they do, against the noise, not over it) the note finds the plaster and travels. There is no silence to remember. Even in the supposed deep of night the place ticks: water, animals shifting, the settling of old timber, someone still at a quern because the grain will not wait.

The activity is layered, not sequential. Fires are banked and woken. Dough is slapped. A sick goat is walked. Children run the galleries in a game whose rules require touching every hanging thing. Someone is always plastering, because plaster is the place’s skin and it sheds. After rain there is a day of urgent catchment and of patching. In the heat of afternoon work migrates into the shadowed rings and the central floor empties except for the animals and the light. Predation is small and constant: cats and lizards and the quick hands that steal from jars. Nothing here is wilderness, and nothing is still. The ecosystem is the settlement. The settlement is the ecosystem. They have been wearing each other into a single fitted shape for so long that the distinction has the quality of a polite lie.

What is slightly unusual—never spectacular, only persistently off—is the way the builders refused the idea of a substrate. There is no “behind” the walls. If you chip the plaster you find more plaster, then brick, then rubble, then the packed fill of earlier rooms that were sacrificed to make this volume. The place is thick with its own history the way a pot is thick with clay. People speak of the first bowl as if it were a person who lay down and became useful. They do not go “outside” so much as they ascend, and even the upper flats are understood as a lid, not a land. The rules are practical and old: water is counted; fire is shared and watched; the light-wells are common and must be kept clear; an animal that dies in the well is a public problem; a new room may be added only by thickening, never by breaking the outer shell. Repair is worship and also just what Tuesday looks like.

By evening the lime floor holds the day’s warmth and the lamps multiply until the galleries look lined with low stars. Condensation starts its quiet work on the north plaster. The pigeons settle into a rustling that is almost like rain. Someone leans on a rail that has been leaned on since the rail was a different tree. The air smells of goat, of fat-smoke, of wet lime, of the green nothing of the potted plants finally exhaling. You are inside a made world that has had time enough to weather into something that feels inevitable—settled, loud, warm, slightly damp, crowded past reason, and still, in every scraped and mended surface, obviously, stubbornly constructed. The earth above is only the thickness of a roof. The life is all down here, touching itself, making more of itself out of clay and noise and the little water it can keep.
