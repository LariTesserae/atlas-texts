The place is a hollow, and it has been a hollow longer than anyone still working it has been alive. You come into it by a stair cut into wet limestone, then by a second stair of living root that has been trimmed and bound so often the treads have the sheen of old banisters. After the second turn there is no more sky, only a greened-over throat of air, and the heat rises to meet you like breath from a cellar that has never once gone cold.

What you enter is not a cavern in the mineral sense. The stone is there, yes—low ribs of it, stained black-green, weeping in slow sheets that never quite become a fall—but the stone is only the cup. Everything that occupies the cup is plant, and has been plant so long that the distinction between crop, timber, wall, and machinery-housing has worn down to a habit. The floor is a packed mat of rhizome and peat and old leaf-mold, springy, always wet to the sock. Water stands in the low places in films rather than puddles; it is never deep and never dry. Condensation beads on every underside. You wipe a stem and your palm comes away warm.

The settlement is the size of a large nave, or of a market square that has been roofed and then forgotten by the city that once owned it. You can walk from the pump-house wall to the far press in under two minutes if the aisles are clear, which they almost never are. There are no streets. There are corridors of living willow and giant reed, trained into barrel vaults and kept that way by annual binding. The bindings themselves have become part of the architecture: old hemp, then wire, then hemp again over the wire, all of it greened, all of it dripping. Where a vault has been allowed to thicken, the interior is nearly dark and the air is a degree warmer. People have hung lamps there—oil, mostly, a few carbon-filament bulbs on a single greased cable that hums when the generator turns. The light is the color of weak tea. It does not reach the crowns.

Those crowns are not far overhead. This is not a forest. The canopy is a working ceiling: fig and rubber and a kind of aerial yam whose tubers are harvested from ladders, all of it intergrown with the original collapse-tangle that roofed the sink in the first place. Daylight arrives as a rumor, a green pressure rather than a beam. On the rare afternoon when the upper weather clears, a few coins of actual sun wander the floor for an hour and then go out. Nobody stops work for them.

The work is industrial and it is loud. There is a beam engine at the wet end of the hollow, its house grown rather than built: a cylinder of packed living bamboo around a brick firebox, the brick sweating, the bamboo blackened on the inner face and still putting out shoots on the outer. The engine does not hurry. It has the same unhurried stroke it had when the grandparents of the present crew were children. It drives, through a wet leather belt that has to be dressed twice a day, a bank of fiber-breakers and a pair of screw-presses. What goes into them is whatever the hollow grows in surplus—bast, pith, latex, a bitter oil-seed that is mashed for lamp-fuel and soap. The smell is of cooked chlorophyll and hot iron and the sweet rot of crushed stem. The sound is continuous: the thud of the beam, the slap of the belt, the presses complaining, water finding its way down the limestone and arriving as a bright hiss where it hits the boiler-jacket. Conversation happens by leaning in. There are no animals to hear. There have not been animals in the hollow in living memory—no insects in the peat, no worms even, or none that anyone bothers to look for. The warmth and the wet and the constant tannins, or the soot, or simply the fact of no gap left in the living mass: something has closed that door. The plants do not seem to mind. They occupy every interval.

Density here is not a metaphor. You cannot put out a hand without touching something growing, or something that grew and was worked and then grew again through the working. Walkways are tunnels. Sleeping niches are pruned cavities in the thicker hedges, lined with dry reed that does not stay dry. A family of four occupies a volume that would be a single room elsewhere; their walls leaf in the night and have to be cut back from the bedding. Tools hang from living hooks. The water-main is a split liana, bound, leaking at every joint, patched with clay and more liana. When it fails—slowly, over weeks, a dampness that becomes a trickle that becomes a problem—the repair is horticultural as much as mechanical. Someone grafts. Someone waits.

The people move as if the place were a body they were inside of, which it is. They prune as they walk. A hook-knife is as ordinary as a pocket. Cuttings are stuck into any wound in the peat and most of them take. The hollow has been in this equilibrium so long that novelty would be a kind of vandalism. The engine is repaired with parts filed from older parts. The willow vaults are the great-grandchildren of the first willow vaults. Even the wet has a settled character: it does not flood and it does not cease. It beads, it sheets, it is taken up, it falls again from the ceiling as a fine warm rain that is not weather so much as the hollow breathing.

At the dryish end—still wet, only less so—there is a packing floor where the pressed cakes and coiled fiber are wrapped in living leaf and bound for the hoist. The hoist is a wet rope and a counterweight of stone, rising through a shaft that is itself a hollowed trunk, or many trunks fused. What is above the shaft is not described from below. The goods go up. Sometimes supplies come down: coal for the firebox, a new belt, salt, needle and thread, a newspaper that is already soft before it is read. No one from outside stays. The heat and the noise and the way the air sits in the lungs are enough. The crew rotates on a long count—weeks in, a stretch out in the upper weather—but the hollow itself does not rotate. It has the patience of something that found its shape and then kept it.

Evening, if the word applies, is only a further dimming. The oil lamps are lit from the last of the day-glow. The engine is not banked; it runs. The water continues. In the sleeping niches the walls tick as cells swell. Somewhere a press eases and takes up again. The sound is a single thing, layered but not various: water, iron, leaf against leaf in the absence of any wind, the generator’s low complaint. If you stand in the center aisle and close your eyes, the hollow is as loud as a millrace in a closed room, and as warm, and as inarguable. Nothing here is trying to become anything else. The plants fill what can be filled. The people keep the belts dressed and the vaults bound. The stone weeps. The light stays the color of steeped leaf. This is the whole of it, and it has been the whole of it for a long time.
