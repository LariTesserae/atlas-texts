The basin has no floor that was not laid. Whatever once sat here—if anything did—has been broken, dressed, and restacked until the original contour is only a rumor in the way water still wants to collect. You stand in a shallowness that extends past any single night’s walking: a regional dish of masonry, low enough that the horizon is a lip of more masonry, open enough that sound travels in long, wet sheets.

It is always nearly dark. Not the dark of a closed room but the dark of a place that has never negotiated with a sun. Occasional condensations bead on every surface and fall as if the air itself were sweating. When the intermittent rains come they do not refresh; they simply add another film of moisture that the stones drink or shed according to how recently they were set. Puddles form in the unfinished depressions, black mirrors no deeper than a palm, then drain through joints that have not yet been mortared. The air stays cool, a mineral coolness that never quite becomes cold, heavy with stone-dust and the iron smell of wet chisel-work.

There are no plants. There have never been plants here. What grows is only the construction itself, and it grows by hand. Every block, every course, every corbel and lintel and false arch has been shaped with hammerstones, antler picks, copper chisels already mushroomed from use. The builders—few, widely spaced, almost never seen whole—move like slow accidents through the density they have already made. You hear them more than you meet them. The sound is constant and loud: the dry clack of stone on stone, the wetter thunk when a face is being pecked under drizzle, the long groan of a levered slab being walked into place, the sudden bright shatter when a block fails and the fragments skitter down already-finished walls. Echoes stack inside the packed geometry until a single strike becomes a small thunderstorm that rolls for minutes through corridors that were never meant to carry weather.

The structures touch. They lean. They share walls the way lungs share a chest. Passageways pinch to shoulder-width and then bloom into chambers whose ceilings are the undersides of someone else’s floor. Stair-flights end in blank masonry because the next level has not been decided. Niches hold nothing except the tools left overnight—stone mauls, fiber cordage twisted from the sinews of the rare animals that sometimes blunder in from whatever still exists beyond the lip. Those animals do not last. A hoof-print in wet mortar is already an artifact by morning; the creature itself is bone and hide before the next rain, both of which are promptly worked into more tools, more lashings, more wedges.

Everything is recent. Edges have not yet been softened by traffic or weather. Tool-marks stand up like writing. In places the work is so new the blocks still sweat quarry-damp, and you can see the decision that was made an hour ago: this wall will turn, that opening will be closed, a drainage runnel will be cut along the base because last week’s rain found a way in and stayed. Settling has already begun—hairline cracks, a slight tilt, a course that dropped a finger’s width when the ground (which is only more blocks) compacted. The builders treat these as further instructions. They chisel the crack into a channel, they undercut the tilt and pack it with smaller stones, they accept the drop and build the next layer to match. The place is embryonic and already dense, a city that has never been finished because finishing is not the rule. The rule is coverage. The rule is that nothing remains that was not first broken and then remade by hand.

Water is the only thing that still arrives uninvited. It beads, it sheets, it finds the low spots they have not yet raised. In the larger cisterns—themselves just deeper rooms with better joints—the surface is black and still except when a drip strikes it, each impact ringing the masonry like a dull bell. Those sounds layer over the percussion of work until the whole basin seems to be speaking in a single hoarse voice that never quite forms words. Sometimes the rain is heavier, a brief vertical weather that turns every unfinished roof into a waterfall and every corridor into a millrace. Then the activity changes: the distant figures hurry, not to shelter (there is no shelter that is not also more work) but to divert, to raise temporary weirs of stacked rubble, to open channels they had sealed the week before. The water is used and then released; it leaves silt that will be scraped up and mixed into the next mortar. Nothing is wasted except time, and time here is measured only in courses laid.

At the scale of a valley you begin to notice the larger pattern, if pattern is the word. The masonry does not radiate from a center. It accretes. Neighborhoods of different bond—cyclopean, coursed rubble, near-ashlar—abut without transition, as though separate crews had simply kept going until they met and then kept going through each other. Some districts are already three and four layers deep, a vertical slum of rooms stacked on rooms, reached by ladders of notched logs (the logs themselves imported, irreplaceable, already fossilizing under the damp). Other stretches are still only a pavement, a single skin of flags over whatever they first buried. Between them run the residual channels, half canal and half street, where the rare living things other than the builders sometimes appear: pale, elongated shapes that hug the wet joints, feeding on the mineral slime that condenses there. They are not hunted so much as harvested when the work requires organic material. Their absence is noted only as a shortage of cordage.

There is a strangeness that is not quite architectural. Distances misbehave in the dark. A corridor you have walked twice already opens onto a court you do not remember, and the court is already half-filled with new walls. Sound arrives from directions that have no line of sight. Occasionally a finished surface will weep a water that is warmer than the air, or a block will ring when struck as if it were hollow, though the next course proves it solid. The builders do not remark on these things. They incorporate them. A warm seep becomes the site of a new cistern. A ringing stone is left proud of the wall so that later hammers can use it as a signal. The place is teaching itself how to be a place, using hands that may or may not understand the lesson.

Stand still long enough and the density presses in even without contact. The air is thick with the memory of impacts. Dust and droplets hang together, a slow glittering if there were light to catch it. There is none. What illumination exists is accidental: the brief orange of a friction-spark when two stones glance, the faint foxfire of the mineral slime, the pallid suggestion of a living body moving far off between pillars. Most work is done by touch and by the shape of the echoes. A good mason can tell the quality of a joint by how the sound of the next strike changes. The whole basin is an instrument being tuned by the act of building it, and it is always slightly out of tune, always requiring one more adjustment, one more course, one more night of loud, wet, necessary labor under a darkness that has no intention of lifting.

The lip of the basin is not an end. It is only where the work has not yet reached, or where it reached and then folded back on itself. Beyond, presumably, something else continues—another construction, or the last unmade ground, or simply more of the same cool, damp, ringing dark. No one here has gone to look. There is still too much to cover, and the rains will come again before the current walls have set.
