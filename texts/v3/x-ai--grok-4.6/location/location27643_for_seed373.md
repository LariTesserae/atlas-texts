The high col is no larger than a ruined nave, a shallow stone basin hung between two worn shoulders of mountain that have forgotten how to be peaks. Whatever water once carved it departed so long ago the memory has been scoured out of the rock itself; the place is mineral, desiccated, a bowl of pale feldspar and iron-stained quartz that rings underfoot like poorly fired ceramic. Cool air slides through it in thin, directionless sheets. There is light—ordinary, slightly diluted daylight that seems to arrive already tired from the altitude—but it never quite warms the stone.

What grows here does not drink. The vegetation is a mineral lushness: great hanging curtains and freestanding candelabra of living crystal and silicate frond, some the colour of verdigris, some the dull gold of weathered pyrite, some a bruised amethyst that drinks the light and gives back only a cooler shade. They sprout from hairline fractures, from the undersides of overhangs, from the few standing monoliths that the wind has not yet planed away. Their growth is slow in any given hour and yet, because the basin is so empty, they dominate. Between them lie metres, sometimes tens of metres, of unoccupied air and bare, polished bedrock. You can walk from one grove-pillar to the next and feel you have crossed a small country. The plants themselves are dense, overlapping, almost indecently abundant on the surfaces they claim; the volume they occupy is a near-void.

The fauna will not let the void stay quiet. They are everywhere and nowhere, a constantly reconfiguring storm of bodies that treat the empty air as both highway and battlefield. Clouds of thumbnail-sized flyers with mica wings and voices like tearing foil; larger, slower things that look like living kites of cartilage and dust-fur, booming as they gyre; iridescent crawlers that erupt from the crystal roots in sudden, boiling mats then vanish again into the stone as if the rock itself were exhaling them. They do not perch for long. The entire population is in motion, a deafening, granular roar that never resolves into individual cries. It is the sound of a market and a war and a hive collapsing and reforming in the same instant. You feel it in your teeth and in the hollows of your lungs. The crystal fronds shiver and ring in sympathy, adding a high, glassy overtone that the animals seem to answer, or perhaps to feed upon.

Nothing here is allowed to finish becoming. The basin is old—its shapes are the settled, weary shapes of a landscape that has already decided what it is—but the decision is under constant, furious revision. Spurs of new crystal erupt overnight and are ground back to powder by midday. Whole thickets of fronds fold themselves inward like closing fists and then unfurl elsewhere, metres away, as if the plant had simply changed its mind about location. The animals are born, swarm, and are unmade in cycles too fast to watch comfortably; their empty husks sift down as a fine, glittering talc that the next generation immediately stirs back into flight. Even the light seems to hesitate, brightening and dimming in irregular pulses that have nothing to do with any sun you can see.

Scattered through this are the built things, never many, never whole. A single canted arch of something that is not quite metal, its surface crawling with script that rearranges itself when you look away. A low dais of fused glass that is always a few degrees colder than the air and that sometimes displays, for a few heartbeats, a map of a place that cannot exist in three dimensions. Thin guy-lines of force, visible only as a faint polarisation of the dust, that hold one of the larger crystal pillars at an angle the mountain itself would never have permitted. The technology does not announce itself as machinery. It simply refuses the physics you brought with you. Gravity eddies around the dais. Sound arrives a half-second before the event that should have caused it. Once in a long while a section of bare stone will briefly become transparent, revealing a depth of layered galleries that cannot fit inside the mountain, then seal again as if embarrassed.

The rules are local and impatient. Moisture is an insult; any trace of it on skin or breath is immediately stolen and converted into more of the dry, ringing dust. Time does not pass evenly: stand too long beside one of the force-lines and you will watch the crystal forest complete a century of growth and collapse while your own pulse stays ordinary. The animals will not touch you unless you remain still, at which point they treat you as another mineral outcrop and begin to colonise. The plants, if you break a frond, bleed a scent like burnt ozone and then grow a perfect replica of your hand overnight, cool and faceted and slightly too large.

All of it is happening at once, inside a space you could shout across if shouting were possible. The roar never drops. The crystal never quite stops rearranging. The few artefacts continue their private, physics-violating work. The mountain holds the basin the way an old hand holds a coin it has carried for decades: worn, unsurprised, and entirely willing to let the coin keep changing value. You are not a visitor here so much as another brief density the place has decided to tolerate, for now, in the roaring, glittering, waterless air.
