The basin is new. You can still see the way the ground tore when it settled: a low oval of mineral plate, edges lifted like a badly healed lid, the floor not yet worn to anything a foot would call dust. Whatever opened it did so recently and without weather’s help. The walls of the depression are sharp, stair-stepped, still bleeding pale powder from their corners. There is almost no water here and never has been. The chemistry is dry—salt, gypsum, something finer that tastes of tin if you put a grain on your tongue and wait. Cool air pools in the hollow and does not leave. Light arrives already tired, a dim pewter that never quite decides on a source.

In the center of this small place—small enough that you can walk its longest street twice and still see both rims—stands a settlement that no one built. The structures are the same mineral as the basin, extruded upward in planes and hollows that read, against all sense, as streets, courts, façades, rooms. Walls meet at angles that feel decided. Openings align. There are thresholds. There are even what the eye insists on calling windows, though they open onto more of the same pale interior, as if the buildings were grown as empty thoughts. Nothing here was cut or stacked. Nothing was planned with a tool. The city assembled itself the way a crystal does, only the lattice preferred corridors and stacked chambers and sudden plazas the size of a single held breath.

It is almost empty. Density is a rumor. Between one wall and the next there is often more air than structure; whole blocks are a single thin partition standing in a field of its own fallen grit. You can stand in what ought to be a crowded square and hear only the faint, continuous settling of powder—so quiet it is more a change in the pressure of the ear than a sound. The processes are many and they do not rest. New edges lift overnight. A doorway that was a slit at dusk is a proper arch by the dim morning, its lintel still sharp enough to cut cloth. Fine mineral weather moves without wind: a silent rain of particles that chooses certain surfaces and thickens them, as if the city were being redrawn from inside its own dust. Angles correct themselves. A crooked lane will, over hours, become true.

Sparse life has found the cracks. Along the bases of walls grow thin, rust-colored stalks that are not quite plants—brittle, jointed, tasting of the same tin, putting out no seed you can find. They appear where the powder has been still for more than a day and they do not crowd. Farther out, on the youngest plates, nothing grows at all. Fauna is rarer still. Once in a long while something the size of a thumb moves in a high aperture: a dry, pale animal with too many slow limbs, feeding on the stalks or on the dust itself. It makes no noise. When it vanishes you are not sure it was not a trick of the dimness and the repeating geometry.

The strangeness is not decoration. It is the rule the place obeys. Streets do not quite connect the way streets should; follow one long enough and you will pass the same empty courtyard twice without having turned. Interiors are larger than their shells, or smaller, or the same volume folded until it hurts to look. Some rooms have no floor, only a continuation of wall that becomes walkable if you do not think about it. The city has no center and several. At the rim of the basin the structures simply stop, as if a thought had ended, and beyond them the young mineral field runs out to the lifted lip and the dimmer world past it. There is no gate. There is no need of one. Nothing manufactured has ever stood here. The only infrastructure is the habit of form itself—the way emptiness, given a dry chemistry and time enough, prefers to become rooms.

It is cool enough that breath does not fog. The light never strengthens. Activity continues: plates emerging, powder choosing, the rare creature crossing a sill, the stalks leaning a fraction as if toward a sun that is not present. All of it happens at the edge of hearing, which is to say it happens in silence. The basin holds its city the way a mouth holds a word it has not yet decided to say. You can walk the whole of it before the light changes, which it barely does, and when you leave by the same torn rim you entered, the powder on your clothes is already organizing itself into tiny, useless, perfect corners.
