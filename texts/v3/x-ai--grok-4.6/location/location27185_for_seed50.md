The notch does not look like an entrance until you are already in it. From the ridge it is only a crease in stone that has not yet decided to be a mountain—raw faces still bright, still wet along their newest edges, as if the rock had been pressed up from below an hour ago and the air has not finished cooling it. Elevation here is not a view. It is a thinning. The sky sits close. What weather there is arrives sideways.

You step through the crease and the world drops its volume into a single room.

It is not large. A person could throw a stone across it, if there were anywhere dry to stand and a stone that would stay a stone. The pocket is a hanging basin, open overhead to ordinary daylight—the flat, uncolored light of high places—and almost entirely occupied by water that has not agreed to be a lake. The water is the room. It occupies the volume the way a lung occupies a chest: completely, then not, then more than completely. It climbs the unfinished walls in sheets, hangs, collapses, is thrown again. Spray does not fall so much as occupy. There is no still surface. There is a body of liquid in the act of becoming several bodies, and the sound of that becoming is total.

You do not hear the water so much as live inside its sentence. It is a continuous detonation without climax—white, physical, a pressure in the teeth and the hinge of the jaw. Thought arrives already shouted-down. If there is wind it has been recruited; if there is stone speaking as it grows, the water has taken that voice and made it louder. Silence is a theory you remember from lower elevations.

The geology is younger than the noise. This is not erosion’s work. Nothing here has been worn. The walls are still arriving—facets too clean, joints that have not learned to leak in only one place. Along a north curve the rock is actively precipitating, a pale rind thickening in pulses you can watch if you brace and stare, mineral bloom like frost run in reverse, eating the water that feeds it and then surrendering a slab that slumps back into the boil. Opposite, a fresh cleft opens with a sound that is only more of the same roar, and a vein of colder water, almost gelid, jets in, changing the color of the chaos for a few seconds from green-white to something nearer iron. The basin is under construction. The mountain is using this throat to finish itself, and the work is not careful.

There is almost nothing else. Density here is a kind of politeness: vast intervals of spray and vacant air between any two solid facts. A ledge the width of a forearm, slick, appears and is later not quite where you left it. In a crack that stays dry for minutes at a time, a few threads of vegetation—black-green, more lichen than plant, a single fern the size of a child’s hand that looks as if it were invented this morning and has not yet been told the rules. That is the forest. Nothing knits. Nothing holds a slope.

Built things, such as they are, have the same provisional character. Someone—people with hands, with the early arithmetic of stacking—has tried to keep a platform. Unshaped blocks, no metal, a lashing of twisted fiber gone dark with wet, rebuilt whenever the water decides the old arrangement was an insult. The platform is never finished and never quite erased. On it, when the heave allows, a single artifact: a staff of pale wood, bark still on, one end fire-hardened, the other wrapped in what might be gut. A tool for testing depth that has no meaning here, or for insisting that a floor exists. Nearby, three stones in a cairn so small it is almost a grammar rather than a structure, and a smear of charcoal on a wall that the newest rind is already beginning to overgrow. Marks, not dwellings. The suggestion that a body slept here once and was not entirely believed.

Light is ordinary and therefore strange. No underworld tint, no cathedral shaft. Daylight, cool and even, coming in through the open roof as if this were a courtyard and not a machine for unmaking courtyards. You can see the grain of the newest stone. You can see how clear the water is in the rare half-second when a slab of it hangs intact: not mountain-tea, not glacial milk, but a clarity that has not had time to dissolve anything. Then it breaks and the light goes to pieces with it.

Temperature is the high-place kind of cold that does not announce itself until you have stopped moving. The water is colder than the air. Spray finds the gaps in whatever you wear and stays. Breath shows. The fern, improbably, does not freeze. Neither does the one animal you may or may not have seen—something pale and brief in a trough of the chaos, not a fish exactly, more a muscle of water that chose a spine for a moment and then thought better of it. Rare is the correct word. Life here is an exception the room has not finished reviewing.

The strangeness is not decorative. It is the operating condition. Paths do not persist. The crease you entered by will still be a crease, probably, but it will not open onto the same arrangement of ledge and roar. The water is writing the stone and the stone is editing the water and neither keeps minutes. Time in the pocket is turbulent in the same way the surface is: not fast so much as unwilling to stay a sequence. A slab grows, calving a sound you feel in your knees; the cairn loses a stone and gains a different one; the staff is still there, or a staff is there, and you cannot swear it is the same wood. The place is embryonic and hyperactive at once—an unborn mountain having a tantrum in a room the size of a secret.

Rules, such as they will admit:

The water is not trying to drown you. It is trying to finish a sentence that includes you only if you insist. Stay on whatever rises. Do not trust a silence; there isn’t one. Do not drink unless you mean to carry a little of the unfinished geology away in your blood. If you speak, do it against the bone of your own skull; the air will not take delivery. Leave the staff if you move it. The cairn is not a shrine so much as a claim that counting is still possible. The fern is not for picking. The exit is a crease, not a promise.

When you leave—if the notch still corresponds to the idea of leaving—the ridge will look slightly more like a mountain than it did. Or slightly less. Behind you the roar continues, constructing a high, hidden, nearly empty room out of water and the first draft of stone, over and over, as if the world had only this one place left in which to practice being born.
