The highland did not finish rising. That is the first thing anyone learns who lives there long enough to think in generations rather than seasons. The rock is still new: pale, angular, ringing when struck, its bedding planes still clean enough that a person can split a block along them with a hammerstone and a night of patience. Ridges come up like the backs of animals that have only just stood. Cirques hang unfinished. Whole hanging valleys look as if the ice withdrew last week and left the gouges wet.

Across all of it, for distances that weather can form inside, people have been stacking the stone.

It is not a city placed on a mountain. It is a mountain that has been persuaded, slowly, into rooms. From a high saddle you do not see a skyline so much as a continuation: walls climbing other walls, stairs climbing ridges, courtyards filling every hollow that will hold a courtyard, then more walls beyond those, paler where they are newer, darker where lichen has had a century. The built thing and the young range share an outline. Fresh scarps become the backs of granaries. A fault that opened in someone’s grandfather’s time is now a street with a lip of fitted coping so no one walks off the drop in white weather.

Water is a guest. It arrives as a thin rain that lasts an afternoon and is gone, or as snow that sits in the inner courts like a second pavement and then thins to nothing without ever quite melting in the ordinary way. At night the stone sweats. You wake to find the inner faces of the rooms filmed with beads that run into grooves cut for them long ago, grooves that lead to jars, to basins, to the dark cisterns under the oldest terraces. In the high slots between buildings there are places where breath and cook-steam and the damp of wool have made a climate of their own: a permanent cool wetness, green only with the smallest things, a smell of stone and fleece. Rivers, where they exist, are brief. They come off the unfinished snowfields, clatter through chutes the masons have lined so the water will not take the foundations, and disappear into swallow-holes that the range has not yet decided whether to keep. People speak of the water as something that visits and must be asked to stay.

There is not much that grows. South faces wear a crust of rust and sulphur lichen that the children scrape for dye. In the most sheltered kinks of wall, where a courtyard faces the noon and the wind cannot turn around, there are plots no larger than a sleeping-mat: bitter leaves, a starchy knuckle of root that can be stored through the long cold, a shrub kept low by knives and used to the last twig. Timber is an inheritance. A beam in a door is older than the family that oils it. Most everything else is stone, hide, bone, hair-rope, and the rare, treasured length of something that was once a tree in a valley no one now living has walked.

The cold is the condition of the place, not an event. It lives in the shade of every wall. It comes up through the floors. It makes the fitted joints sing when the sun leaves them. People dress in layers that have no fashion, only thickness; they sleep against the animals; they keep the inner rooms small so the air a body has already used can be used again. At midday, if the sky is clean, the light is ordinary and hard—high-country light, no mystery in it, only thinness—and the shadows of the stacked roofs cut the alleys into strips of glare and ink. You can see your breath at noon in the north-facing lanes. You can see it at night everywhere.

And yet the highland is crowded.

Not only with people. The masonry is a cliff that never ends, and every cliff-thing has come to live in it. The walls are holed with nests. At certain hours the air above a courtyard is a turning of wings so dense it looks like weather, though the sound is only a dry rush, quickly gone. Wool-beasts stand in the communal stables that are simply the ground floors of whole districts, shoulder to shoulder, their breath a fog that freezes on the lintels. Dogs sleep in heaps on the sunny steps. Small browsers—rock-bodied, sure, almost silent—use the roofs as pasture, cropping the lichen, leaving dark pellets in the gutters. At night the lanes belong to other feet: pad and claw, the click of keratin on flag, things that come down from the unfinished peaks to drink at the leaky cisterns and then go up again. No one counts them. There are always more presences than rooms. You feel it in the way a passage is never quite empty, in the warmth that lingers in a stair because something slept there, in the layered smell under the cold—fleece, dung, bird, people, wet stone.

The work is hand work and has always been. Blocks are walked into place on rollers of bone and green wood. A team will spend a season on a single corner because the corner must take the weight of three more storeys that do not exist yet. Copper, where it appears, is a thin edge on a chisel, an ornament, a thing that came from very far away and will not be replaced. Most edges are stone on stone. The famous joints of the highland—the ones travelers (who are few) remember—are not magic. They are time. A mason sits with two faces of rock and a bag of grit and a patience that looks like idleness until the faces consent to disappear into each other. Mortar is almost unused. The range is still making new cracks; mortar would only tell you where the next one will run. Better to let the building breathe with the mountain and be taken down, block by numbered block, when a fresh scarp comes up through someone’s floor.

Sound falls into the density and does not come out whole. Wind, which ought to own a place this high, is baffled into a long, low pressing in the outer streets and almost nothing in the inner ones. Voices stay near the mouth. A hammer three terraces away is a click. The animals, for all their numbers, have the hush of cold-country herds: the shift of weight, the brief complaint, the enormous quiet of rumination. Snow, when it comes, finishes the muffling. There are weeks when the whole vast fabric of walls seems to have decided not to speak. You hear your own blood. You hear melt starting in a hidden pipe. You hear, very far off, a flock turning in the light and then not hearing them.

Change is the same work as staying. The plateau lifts. A stair that was true is, after twenty winters, a little drunk. A cistern that held now sweats through a new hairline and must be lined again. Districts are not founded; they accrete, a room against a room against a retaining wall that was a cliff last century. Maps are jokes told by old people. You navigate by the color of the lichen, by which stable-smell is which, by the particular way the afternoon light hits a certain stack of untrimmed roofs that everyone agrees has always been there and that, if you found an older person still, was a bare ridge in their youth. Seasons turn slowly. The high snowfields give up their water on a schedule that has nothing to do with calendars. A child can grow up between one notable rain and the next. The people match this pace. They do not hurry a wall. They do not hurry a grief. They sit in the thin sun on the steps with their animals and watch the next ridge, which is also a city, which is also still becoming rock, and they do not expect it to be finished.

At evening the light goes ordinary and gold and then ordinary and blue. Smoke from dung-fires finds the gaps and lies in the lanes like another kind of water. Somewhere a basin ticks as the day’s melt settles. Wings come home to the holes. The vastness does not dwindle with dark; it only becomes a density you move through by touch and habit, wall, then opening, then the living heat of a doorway, then wall again, on and on, a highland that has learned to hold itself, damp in its joints, sharp at its edges, quiet as a held breath, still rising.
