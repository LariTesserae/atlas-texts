The first thing that registers is not height but the absence of ground. Everything underfoot has been poured, stacked, bolted, or grown in a tray. There is no native stone. What looks like cliff is retaining wall; what looks like ridgeline is a long spine of engine-houses and cistern-towers whose roofs have been rebuilt so many times the original profile is a rumor. You are already high when you notice this. The air has that thin, warm brightness of altitude—sun coming in hard, shadows cut as if with a rule—and the whole mass of the place sits above a haze you never quite reach, a pale sea of lower weather that the Heights refuse.

They call the region the Circum, or the Made Heights, or simply Up, depending which terrace they were born on. It is not a peak. It is a constructed highland the size of a long valley, a system of stacked precincts, cuttings, and hanging yards that has been added to for so long the additions have weather and citizenship. Brick the color of dried blood. Slag-block the color of storm. Iron painted, rusted, painted again until the paint itself is a geological layer. Walkways of poured clinker that still hold the marks of the boards they were struck from. Everything is in its finished age. Nothing here is new and nothing here is falling; the erosion has already happened and been accepted. Corners are soft. Bolt-heads have become smooth knobs. Rain-channels cut a century ago have deepened into grooves you could lay a thumb in, then stopped deepening, as if the water and the work agreed on a shape.

Water is the argument the Heights never quite win. There is not much of it. What there is arrives as weather that does not stay: brief warm rains that sheet the upper decks and vanish into the designed throats of the downpipes, night-condensations that bead on the cold sides of the high tanks and run in threads to the moss, leakages, seepage, the slow sweating of old masonry on the shaded faces. You learn the damp places the way you learn a language. North walls. The undersides of flyovers. The mouths of overflow weirs that only speak after rain. Between these, dryness—warm brick, dust in the joints, the faint mineral smell of heated iron. The plants have been taught this grammar, or have taught themselves.

Vegetation is not a decoration laid on top of industry. It is a second industry, older now than some of the machines. Wherever moisture lingers even a little, the green is extravagant. Fig and rubber and a dozen nameless broadleafs packed into courtyard tanks whose original purpose was quenching. Ferns in the spray-shadow of a cooling tower that still turns, slowly, on a bearing the size of a cartwheel. Vines braided through lattice that was once scaffolding and is now load-bearing in a different sense. On the bright upper terraces the plantings are more formal and more thirsty: citrus in boxed earth, tomatoes against south walls, a kind of succulent hedge that drinks almost nothing and still manages to look like wealth. In the deep cuttings, where the stacked architecture makes its own weather, the growth is jungle-thick and almost indecent—lianas crossing from gallery to gallery, bromeliads plugged into every bolt-hole that holds a drip, a continuous wet fur of moss on the pipes. You can walk for an hour in shade that never touches the sun and hear only leaves and the patient tick of condensation.

The heat is not desert heat. It is the heat of altitude and of residual work. The Circum sits in a warm belt; the sun at this elevation is a white authority; and under the decks the old processes still idle. Kilns that fire one month in four. Dye-houses whose vats are kept just off the boil. Engine rooms that turn because turning is cheaper than stopping, the great belts slapping in a rhythm so settled it has become silence to the people who live beside it. Warmth comes up through the floors at night. Cats know this. So do the lizards, which are everywhere, the color of whatever they last sat on, throat-fans opening in the iron glare of noon.

Light is nearly total. High, clean, minimally filtered. Whitewash and pale render throw it back. Glass—factory panes, lantern roofs, the long clerestories of the weaving sheds—breaks it into slabs. At midday the Heights are almost painful; people work the undersides and the inner yards then, and the upper walks empty except for laundry and birds. Morning and late afternoon the place becomes itself: every cornice edged, every tank a mirror, steam from the remaining stacks going gold and then invisible. There is little night-dark in the densely built cores. Electric lamps, old and yellowish, hang in cages along the main cuttings. Where the wiring has failed, oil and carbide still hold the corners. The brightness is not only the sun. It is a habit.

Sound occupies the middle distances. Not a roar. A continuous, layered occupancy. Wind in the high trestles, which never quite stops—this is extreme elevation, and the air has room to move. Belt-slap and the slow complaint of gears from the works that still run. Water finding tin. The dense talk of birds: swifts in the updrafts off the heat-walls, pigeons in their uncountable ledges, something like a crow that has learned to nest in the mouths of unused stacks and comes and goes with a sound like tearing cloth. Goats on the uppermost batter, their bells irregular, their hooves a dry clatter on slag. Human noise folded into all of it—calls across a courtyard, a tram’s bell two terraces down, someone beating a carpet, the deep regular thump of a fulling mill that has kept the same hour for longer than anyone’s grandmother. Loud enough that you raise your voice on the open decks. Not so loud that you cannot hear a drip, or a lizard leaving a wall.

The animals are not visitors. They are census. Besides the goats (milk, meat, the unofficial postal service of the high paths) there are dogs of no particular breed sleeping on warm iron, chickens in the vine-yards, fish in the darker cisterns—carp and something silver that flashes when a child drops crumbs. Insects in biblical numbers after rain, then gone. Bats at the first cooling, pouring out of a vent that was once a flue. In the wet cuttings, frogs. On the bright stone, a hawk that belongs to no one and is seen often enough to have names. The density of life matches the density of structure: there is very little empty volume. A shaft has ferns, cables, a nest, a drip-catcher, a ladder, a faded notice, a cat. A roof has tanks, solar-darkened pitch, a line of gourds, a sleeping man, antennae of rusted rod, a whole linear garden in salvaged troughs. Near-touching. Layered. You move through occupancy, not past it.

The industrial is not a spectacle. It is the substrate, and it is tired in the way a good tool is tired. Factories occupy whole mid-levels: spinning, pressing, the slow chemistry of dyes and soaps, a brickworks that still feeds the endless small repairs, a foundry whose pours are ceremonial now, seasonal, attended like a harvest. Electrification reached here and stayed in a practical condition—light, a few motors, the tram that climbs the long east incline on a cable you can hear singing when the wind is right. No one is inventing the future. They are keeping the present from undoing itself. Production happens at the pace of cooling and drying and the growth of flax on the outer batters. You can stand in a doorway and watch a process that will not finish today.

Because everything is made, the rules are made as well, and they have had time to become custom rather than law. Rain belongs to the tank whose gutter caught it, unless the overflow is marked for the common cistern, in which case it is watched with a seriousness out of proportion to the trickle. A plant that roots in a wall is the wall-owner’s until it crosses a joint; then it is argued. Smoke rights, sun rights, the right to drip. The High Paths are kept clear because the goats and the messengers use them and because falling is not theoretical. Children learn early which gratings are load-bearing and which are only memory. There is no wilderness to exile anyone into. Punishment is relocation—down a terrace, into a noisier cutting, away from a favored drip. Kindness is a share of condensate, a cutting, a warm floor in the old engine basement when the brief rains make the upper rooms sweat.

The scale is the scale of a city that became a landform, or a landform that was always a city. From the western battery you can see most of it: the long stacked valley of the Circum falling away in shelves, each shelf a neighborhood of sheds and courts and green-black cuttings, the pale thread of the incline, the brighter upper works where the tanks flash, the far eastern massif of the oldest kilns, hunched and grown over, still faintly breathing. Beyond the last constructed edge there is only the drop and the haze. No foothills. The Made Heights end as a wall. People do not often speak of what is below. It is not forbidden. It is simply not the subject. Their weather is this weather. Their ground is this ground.

Time here is a thick thing. The dynamic is almost geological, which is a joke the masons tell, because there is no geology, only work done slowly enough to resemble it. Seasons are a change in the angle of the light and in how often the tanks sing. A vine takes a decade to close an alley. A machine is repaired with parts of its grandparent. Paint fails in a pattern you could map. The great belts are replaced one section at a time, so that no belt has ever been entirely new. People measure years by which court the figs took, by which overflow was recut, by the last time the foundry drew a crowd. Nothing lurches. Even the rains, when they come, are brief and then gone, and the Circum drinks, and the moss darkens, and the frogs start, and then the bright dry days resume as if they had only stepped out.

If there is an oddness, it is only this, and it has been lived in until it is ordinary: that a mountain was assembled, and then kept, and then inhabited so completely that the assembly became a climate. You can put your hand on a wall and feel warmth that is sun and warmth that is engine and warmth that is composting leaf in a trough three stories up, conducted down through iron you could not name the age of. The wall was made. The heat was made. The leaf was planted. The iron was poured. And still the lizards arrive at noon, and the swifts write the same sentences in the updraft, and the condensate finds the same groove it found last year, and the Heights, entirely invented, continue in the only way they know—which is to stay, and to thicken, and to let the light do most of the talking.
