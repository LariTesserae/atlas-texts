The highland does not begin with rock. Rock is underneath, yes—old, planed, a plateau that finished its argument with ice a long time ago—but you do not walk on it. You walk on a living rind that has been thickening since the last of the great colds withdrew. It is plant all the way down to a depth you never quite find: compressed mosses gone woody, rhizomes braided into something like cable, leaves that have learned to be shingles, stems that have learned to be beams and then forgotten they were ever stems. The form of the country is settled. The ridges hold. The shallow basins hold. Nothing here is newly carved. The land has the patience of something that already decided its shape and then let weather and time sand the decision smooth.

It is cold enough that breath hangs. Not alpine-cruel, not the white knife of the true summits, but a persistent, high cold that never quite leaves the shade. The plant-mass answers by making its own weather at skin-scale. Dark tissues drink the ordinary daylight and hold it as a thin, metabolic warmth. Where the living layers fold, condensation beads and runs. You can put a palm to a bole—if bole is even the word—and feel it faintly hotter than the air, like a machine that has been left on overnight. There are no machines. There are no walls, no roads, no cut timber, no stacked stone. The industrial thing is only the work itself: the plateau processing itself at a scale that would look, from far enough away, like a factory district seen through fog. Sap is under pressure. Valves of living tissue open and close. Sugars are broken and rebuilt. Heat is exchanged through mats of capillary leaf. Occasional pale vents of vapor lift from seams where the rind is thickest, silent, smelling faintly of tannin and something sharper, almost solvent. Then the vapor thins and is gone.

Water arrives the way a shy animal arrives. A rain that does not last. A rime at first light that the upper leaves take before it can become ice worth naming. Pockets—never lakes—of melt held in waxed hollows, darkened by dissolved plant, cold enough to ache the teeth. Underground, if underground is the right direction, the mass is damp the way a cellar is damp: not wet, not dry, a held moisture that never quite commits. Rivers in the ordinary sense are rare. What drainage there is follows old, established grooves in the living surface, lined with plants that have specialized in being channels, their faces glazed, their margins sending finer roots down into the flow to drink without breaking it. After rain the whole highland ticks and weeps for an hour. Then it is only damp again.

You can see a long way. That is the openness: not emptiness, but distance. The plateau rolls in a sequence of vegetated swells the size of towns, each swell a single continuous organism or a congress so old the distinction has worn off. Between them, saddles. Beyond them, more of the same, paling into the kind of blue that cold air makes of far green. The vegetation fills every volume it is allowed. It hangs. It stacks. It occupies the air in layers—low cushions, midstorey fans, high ragged combs that are not trees so much as the plateau reaching up to comb the wind. Things nearly touch. Light comes through in ordinary amounts, sifted, greenish in the hollows, clean on the crests. There is nothing theatrical about the illumination. Day is day.

And yet the stillness is a lie you are allowed to keep only if you do not watch.

Watch, and the highland is in continuous, nearly silent riot. Growth here is not the polite increment of a temperate wood. It is weather. A whole face of a swell will flush a new color in the time it takes you to cross a basin—pale, then tea, then a bruised purple—as some internal season turns over. Sheets of tissue slough. They do not crash. They settle, muffled, into the lower layers, and the lower layers begin at once to take them apart. In another hour the sloughed sheet is a stain. In a day it is food. In a week it is architecture again, slightly elsewhere. Stems lengthen with a slowness that is still too fast, a visible creeping if you set your eye and refuse to blink. Flowering, when it happens, is a sudden industrial output: whole hectares going over to the same pale, closed bloom as if a switch had been thrown, the air filling with a cold, waxy sweetness that is gone by evening. Nothing sings. Nothing calls. The sound of all this change is a pressure in the ears, a suggestion of cellulose flexing, the tick of a droplet finding a new path, and then the peculiar acoustic deadness of a country that has padded itself against its own weather. Wind arrives and is absorbed. You hear it as a change in temperature on your face, not as a voice.

Fauna is a rumor you sometimes confirm. A moth the color of wet bark, too still. A worm-thick thing withdrawing into a seam. Once, in a long hour of watching a vapor vent, a pale bird—if it was a bird—crossing very high, making no sound, not staying. The plant-mass does not need them. It pollinates itself, or the wind does the work in its muffled way, or the blooms are not for anyone. The rare animals seem like accidents the highland has not yet bothered to exclude.

There is a slight wrongness, never enough to break the place, only enough to keep your attention from going slack. The geometry is a little too consistent. Ridges repeat a curve as if they had been worn by the same thought. Certain leaves, if you turn them, show a venation that looks like a diagram of something that should have wires. At dusk the metabolic warmth becomes visible as a low, even haze in the basins, and for a few minutes the highland looks inhabited by an industry that has no workers and no roofs. Then the light goes ordinary-cold again, and it is only plants, only the old plateau under its living coat, only the work continuing in silence—growth and collapse and growth, the form holding, the contents never still.

If you sleep on it—and you can sleep on it; the upper rind is springy, dry enough on the crests, faintly warm—you will wake to a surface that is not quite the one you lay down on. A new lip of tissue at your shoulder. A hollow that has closed. A vent that has opened a body-length away, breathing out that tannin-and-solvent ghost. The country will not have moved. It will have remained exactly itself, which is the more unsettling thing: a finished land, long-existed, in balance, conducting a private storm that does not raise its voice and does not end.
