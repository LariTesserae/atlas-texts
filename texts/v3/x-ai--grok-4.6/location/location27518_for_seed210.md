The cirque is no older than a wound. It sits in a fold of the high country where the ridgeline tore itself open yesterday or a thousand years ago—there is no difference here—and the rock still remembers the snap. The walls are not rounded. They are facets: new basalt and younger schist, glass-sharp, still shedding flakes the size of palms. Water comes out of them the way blood comes out of a cut that will not close. Not streams. Seepage. Every face weeps. The air is cool enough that the moisture never quite decides whether to be rain or fog; it condenses on your skin, on the lichen, on the iron-dark veins that run through the stone like wiring that no one laid.

There are no buildings. There has never been a building. What there is, instead, is industry without an author.

The veins are not metaphor. They pulse. Slow, hydraulic, the same stroke forever. You can put your hand on a wet slab and feel the regular give of something that is both mineral and piston. Condensation beads, runs, is drawn back into hairline seams, is expelled again as a fine warm spray that smells of ozone and crushed fern. The sound is constant and mid-loud: a turbine note that is also the sound of a thousand small waterfalls that never change pitch, never flood, never dry. It occupies the space the way a factory occupies a valley, except the factory is the valley and the valley is the size of a nave. You can walk the whole of it in four minutes if you do not stop. Most people stop.

Vegetation is lush and lonely. Thick carpets of sphagnum and haircap moss hold the seeps; they are so waterlogged they quiver like muscle. Between them, emptiness—bare planes of slick rock, black and reflective, large enough to lie down on and see nothing but your own dim shape. From the wetter ledges hang gardens of filmy fern and a pale, almost translucent saxifrage that grows in exact rows, as if planted, though nothing here was planted. The plants do not colonize the empty stone. They respect the gaps. The whole place is a balance of saturation and vacancy, like a machine that needs clearance.

Light is the color of a storm that has forgotten how to break. It comes from nowhere in particular: a dim, even, gray-green illumination that might be the sky or might be the rock itself sweating a kind of residual day. There are no shadows that lengthen. There is no noon.

Fauna is everywhere and also spaced, like the plants. You hear them before you see them because they are loud in the way a foundry is loud—layered, purposeful, never quite resolving into individual voices. Amphibians the size of a thumb live in the moss and click in perfect time with the hydraulic pulse of the walls. Something with too many joints walks the bare rock on stilted, careful legs and leaves no tracks; when it pauses, steam lifts from its joints. Birds that are not exactly birds hang in the spray. Their wings do not flap so much as cycle. They drink the condensation and excrete tiny bright beads of metal that roll into the seams and are taken up again. The air is full of midges that spark, faintly, when they touch. None of this is predation. Nothing here is hungry. The ecology is a closed loop running at equilibrium, a production line that manufactures only its own continuation.

At the lowest point of the cirque there is a tarn no larger than a chapel floor. The water is black and still and does not reflect the walls. It reflects a different set of walls, slightly wrong, as if the basin were a window left open onto the same place a half-second out of register. If you stare too long the discrepancy grows: a fern that is and is not there, a pulse in the rock that arrives before the sound. This is as close as the place comes to weather. The rest is invariant. The weeping does not increase. The turbines do not speed or slow. A flake of schist detaches, falls, strikes the moss, and is already part of the moss; you cannot find the moment of impact afterward. Time here is a held note.

The weirdness is not decoration. It is load-bearing. Distance inside the cirque is politely inaccurate. Walk toward a weeping face and it recedes at the same rate you approach until you stop trying; then it is suddenly against your chest, cold and cycling. Sound arrives from the direction you are facing, always. The abundant life does not acknowledge you as an interruption. You are weather that did not happen. If you shout, the shout is absorbed into the turbine-note and returned to you a moment later as a slightly industrial version of your own voice, already forgotten.

There is a sense—never confirmed—that the whole hanging valley is a single exposed component of something larger that has no outside. The young rock, the unweathered edges, the industrial rhythm that was never installed: this is not a landscape that was built and then abandoned. It is a landscape that is the machine, freshly faulted into view, running the only process it will ever run. Cool. Wet. Dim. Loud in a way that becomes silence if you stay. Full of lives that are also functions. Empty enough to breathe.

You can leave by the same notch you entered. Behind you the sound does not fade. It simply ceases to be your problem, the way a factory you walked past at night ceases, though you know it is still doing whatever it does in the dark.
