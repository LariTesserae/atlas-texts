The basin formed last winter, or the winter before—no one quite agrees, and the ground itself has not yet decided. A shallow oval of slumped earth, no more than four hundred paces across, ringed by raw scarps of pale clay that still hold the knife-marks of their collapse. The scarps are sharp, unsoftened, weeping a constant film of condensation that never quite becomes a rivulet. Everything down here is new in the geological sense: the clay still plastic, the few exposed stones angular, the air carrying that mineral tang of freshly broken ground.

Four structures occupy the floor, widely spaced, as if each were afraid of the others. They are industrial in the old, heavy way—brick and riveted iron, high clerestory windows, roofs of corrugated sheet that tick and groan with every change in the cool air. A mill-house with a single tall stack that never smokes so much as breathes a pale, slow vapour. A long shed whose sides are more shutter than wall. A squat powerhouse whose dynamo turns with the patience of a millstone. And, at the far end, a row of workers’ cottages so few they feel like an afterthought, their doors always standing open to the damp. Between them lie acres of emptiness that the plants have claimed with startling speed.

The vegetation is lush in the way of places that have only just been given permission to grow. Giant horsetails rise in pale-green columns, their joints still supple. Ferns unfurl from every crevice, their fronds beaded with the same condensation that films the iron. Mosses have already knitted thick carpets over the clay, swallowing the rails that once connected the buildings; the rails themselves are still bright, barely oxidised, but already half-buried. Young alders and willows have sprung up in loose groves, their leaves a wet, dark green that seems to drink the cool light. There is no true understorey, only this sudden, vertical exuberance spaced by patches of bare, glistening mud.

Water is never abundant and never absent. It arrives as mist that beads on every surface, as the occasional thin rain that the basin seems to hoard rather than shed, as dark pockets that collect in the lowest hollows and last for days. A narrow channel—more a damp crease than a stream—wanders between the mill and the powerhouse, sometimes holding a finger’s depth of water the colour of weak tea, sometimes only a darker stain. The air itself is a kind of water: cool, heavy, tasting of leaf-mould and wet brick.

The light is ordinary daylight, the kind that arrives through high, even cloud and makes every surface equally visible, equally without shadow. It is a light that reveals rather than flatters: the fresh rust blooming on a bolt, the exact green of a fern spore-case, the way condensation maps the temperature of every pipe.

Sound fills the basin the way water refuses to. The dynamo’s note is a low, continuous roar that the brick walls throw back and forth until it becomes a physical pressure in the chest. Superimposed on it are slower, heavier sounds: the measured thud of a press that cycles only a few times an hour, the long metallic sigh of a flywheel losing and regaining speed, the occasional deep clang when something large is shifted. These industrial voices do not drown the living ones; they braid with them. Birds are everywhere—thrushes, warblers, a pair of herons that have claimed the mill-pond puddle—and their calls are startlingly loud, as if they have decided the only way to be heard is to out-shout the machines. Insects form a second, higher layer: midges in trembling columns, the dry rattle of grasshoppers in the horsetails, the wet slap of frogs whenever a pocket of water is disturbed. At dusk the volume does not drop so much as change key; the dynamo’s note seems to deepen, the frogs take over the lower register, and something—no one has agreed what—makes a slow, rhythmic knocking from inside the long shed, like a heart the size of a room.

The animals treat the place as if it has always been here. Deer paths already thread the moss between the cottages and the mill. A family of otters appears whenever the channel holds enough water to justify the name. Bats pour out of the powerhouse eaves at the same unhurried hour each evening. There is an unusual density of life, but it is not frantic; everything moves with the same patient tempo as the machinery. Even the midges seem to hover in deliberate clouds rather than frenzy.

The processes here are industrial yet glacial. Whatever is being made—or unmade—inside the mill happens across shifts that last weeks. Raw material arrives on a single narrow-gauge truck that appears perhaps twice a month, pulled by a small locomotive whose whistle is almost apologetic. The product, when it finally leaves, is wrapped in oilcloth and loaded with the same lack of hurry. The workers themselves (there are never more than a dozen visible at once) move through the lush gaps like figures in a painting that is being painted very slowly. They do not hurry because nothing here requires haste. The clay is still settling. The plants are still deciding how tall they will become. The machines have been designed, or have simply chosen, to keep time with the basin rather than the other way around.

At the very centre, where the four buildings’ sight-lines almost meet, the ground is a little lower and a little wetter. Here the horsetails grow tallest, and here the condensation gathers into a single dark mirror no larger than a dining table. Stand beside it long enough and you notice that the surface never quite stills. It trembles, faintly, in time with the dynamo, and the reflection of the sky in it is always a fraction of a second behind the real sky. No one comments on this. It is simply one more slow fact of the place, like the way rust blooms in perfect circles on the newest iron, or the way the herons have begun to nest in the rafters of the mill itself, as if the building were just another kind of tree that happens to make a louder sound.
