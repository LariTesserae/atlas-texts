The highland does not end. It buckles once, far behind the oldest foundries, into a lip of darker stone, and then it simply continues—plateau after plateau, each one paved, each one drained, each one still the same city. Cloud comes in at the height of the second-story lintels and stays. It does not storm so much as it remembers rain, a patient, warm precipitation that has been falling across the same gutters for so long the iron lips of them have worn to the softness of bone.

There are no trees. There has not been a tree in living record, nor grass, nor anything that roots. The official explanation, printed on the enamel plaques outside the waterworks, is that the plateau was stripped for the first foundations and the soil never returned; the unofficial one is that the stone itself will not have it. The bedrock here sweats. Cut a new cellar and by morning the walls are beaded; leave a tool overnight and it wakes in a skin of condensate. The city drinks from this as much as from the sky. Every façade is streaked with the mineral tracks of old weeping, pale fans of calcite and rust that have been mapped, named, and left alone. Children learn the streets by those stains the way other children learn by landmarks of green.

What grows instead is animal.

They are not a side-effect. They are the saturation. The density of bodies is the other weather. In the long covered markets the air is a warm broth of breath and wet fur and the peppery musk of whatever nests in the rafters of the dye-houses. The gutters are not empty water; they are occupied water, a continuous traffic of pale crustaceans the length of a thumb, of eel-things that have learned the schedule of the sluice-gates, of something with too many joints that clicks along the undersides of the grates and is considered, by custom, municipal. Above, the sky between the stacks is never clear of wings. Not songbirds—there is nothing for songbirds—but the heavy, industrial species: soot-hawks with faces like leather bellows, loft-rats that glide more than they fly, the great slow kites that ride the heat off the smelter roofs and drop only to drink from the cistern lips. At dusk their shadows braid until the streetlamps come on through a moving mesh.

The factories have been running long enough to have become geology. Brick has slumped. Iron has chosen a color and kept it. The engines are not new and are not dying; they are in the middle of a sentence that began before anyone now working was born. Shifts change on bells that are themselves two centuries old, and the sound of those bells—bronze, rain-dulled, slightly flat—travels farther than it should because the city is a single acoustic body, a continent of wet courtyards and open sheds. Under the bells: the continuous argument of water in pipes, the slap of drive-belts, the layered shouting of animals that have claims on certain alleys and will not be moved. It is loud the way a river is loud. You stop hearing the separate parts after an hour and live inside the one noise.

People live here. They have always lived here. Their rooms are high-ceilinged against the damp and lined with glazed tile that can be wiped. They eat from the vats—yeast, pressed cake, the pale oil that comes off the rendering floors—and they do not keep gardens because there is nothing to garden. Windows stay shut against the mist and against the smaller fauna that will otherwise treat a sill as public land. At night the stairwells tick and rustle. This is considered ordinary. A household that hears nothing is a household that should check the drains.

The scale does its own work. Weather forms over the city the way weather forms over a sea. A district of tanneries will spend three weeks inside its own low yellow cloud; two days’ walk east the foundry quarter will be in a clearer, hotter drizzle that smells of wet coke and the sweet rot of the grease-traps. Maps are not of streets so much as of watersheds and of the old animal roads—the routes the larger herds still use when they move, once or twice a generation, from one feeding ground of offal and warmth to another. Those migrations are slow. A column of the big gray browsers, shoulder-high, rain-slick, might take a month to cross a single borough, grazing the bacterial dark that grows in every unattended trough. Traffic parts around them. It always has. The tramlines were laid with that parting already in mind.

Nothing here is in a hurry to become something else. Paint is not refreshed so much as allowed to finish its conversion into stain. A chimney that began to lean in one century finishes leaning in the next and is then shored, not rebuilt. The water finds the same paths. The animals inherit the same ledges. Even the light is patient: a normal daylight, white-gray, that arrives late through the cloud and leaves early, never dramatic, never quite enough to dry a stone. In that light the city looks less built than precipitated—an enormous, inhabited mineral, still condensing, still being walked on by more lives than it was designed to hold, and long past the age when anyone thought to design it differently.

Stand on a high roof—there is always a higher roof—and the stacks go on until they become a darker band of weather. Between them the wings turn and turn. Below, the weeping never quite stops. The bells, when they go, sound like something being told the time by a very old and very certain machine.
