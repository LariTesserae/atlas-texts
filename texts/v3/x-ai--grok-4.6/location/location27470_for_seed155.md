The plateau has forgotten it was once mountains. What remains is a single worn table of living matter, raised two vertical miles above the last true lowlands, planed so thoroughly by time that its horizon is a clean, unhurried line. Cool air slides across it in long, uninterrupted sheets. Moisture arrives only as sudden cold rain that the ground drinks before puddles can form, or as a fine internal dew that beads for an hour along the undersides of leaves the size of barge-hulls, then is reabsorbed. There are no standing lakes. There are only the slow, dark capillaries that thread the substrate itself.

That substrate is plant. Not soil with plants in it: plant all the way down, a compressed, stratified organism whose upper centimeters are still photosynthetic and whose lower kilometers have become something denser and slower than wood. You walk on it. It gives a little, like very old cork, and it is never quite dry. Every structure you see growing out of it is the same body, only expressed at a different tempo and scale.

The city is sparse in the way a late-season orchard is sparse. Habitable masses stand kilometers apart: towers of braided heartwood the height of office blocks, long galleries whose roofs are single continuous leaves held taut by tension wood, factory-halls whose walls pulse with slow hydraulic pressure. Between them the living ground stretches open and almost empty, a regional emptiness of low, bronze-green lichen-meadow and the occasional dark seam where an older vascular bundle still carries water from one district to another. You can see the next cluster of structures as a distant, irregular silhouette, slightly wavering, because the air here is thin and the light is ordinary daylight made slightly metallic by altitude. There is always wind.

The wind is loud. It finds every hollow chamber the plants have grown for themselves over geologic time and turns them into organ-pipes. Added to that is the industrial layer: not grafted on, but metabolized. Brass conduits and iron pistons were introduced so many centuries ago that the wood grew around them, through them, and finally in them. Steam is raised in chambers of living xylem. Flywheels turn inside rings of compression wood that tighten or slacken with the weather. Electricity—where it exists—travels along conductive resins that the plant itself secretes in response to load. The sound is a layered, continuous thing: a low mechanical thud that you feel in your teeth, a higher hiss of vented vapor, and underneath both the older, wetter groan of the plant adjusting its own weight. When rain comes it is briefly deafening, a white roar on the canopy-leaves, then silence again except for the wind and the engines.

Almost nothing else moves. Fauna is a rumor: a pale moth the size of a hand that lives its entire life inside one gallery and never sees the sky; once, perhaps, a bird whose nest was found high in a ventilation flue, empty. The ecosystem is the plant and the machines it has decided to keep running. Processes still occur—sap is refined, timber is harvested from sacrificial limbs that the structure then regrows over decades, condensers collect the dawn dew and pipe it to the few still-functioning foundries—but they occur at the pace of weather and of very old wood. A factory cycle might last three days or thirty years; both feel equally natural here.

The oldest cores refuse ordinary geometry. In the central pith of the largest halls the corridors do not quite connect. A stair that has always led to the pressing-room will, on certain afternoons, open onto a courtyard that existed two centuries earlier, complete with the same steam-plumes and the same unfinished gears lying in the same puddles of resin. Conversations soak into the vascular tissue and leach out again, slightly delayed, slightly warmer. Some of the machines no longer process matter. They process sequence: a hopper that was built to take wood-chips now accepts hours, compresses them, and extrudes a dark, fragrant cake that, if burned, smells like a specific autumn that no one present remembers living through. No one finds this alarming. It is simply what very old industrial plant does once it has finished becoming architecture and has started becoming the place itself.

At the plateau’s far, still-higher rim the living ground thins. The last plant-towers stand farther apart, their lower trunks pale and wind-scoured. Beyond them the substrate finally yields to the original, almost-vanished rock: a few remnant mesas, themselves colonized only at the crown, dropping away into air so clear you can see the curvature of the world and the pale, uninhabited flats that used to be everything else. The wind is louder there. The condensation never quite stops. The city, such as it is, does not end so much as forget to continue.
