The plateau has no settled name. Cartographers still argue over the contour lines; the older sheets show a saddle two hundred meters lower, and the correction is written in a hand that looks hurried, as if the mountain itself were still arriving while the ink dried. You feel the unfinished quality first in the soles of the feet. The rock is warm, not from sun but from below, a steady geothermal seep that makes the altitude’s thin air contradictory: it should be cold enough to hurt the lungs, yet the stone radiates like the wall of a kiln left banked overnight. Heat here is not weather. It is the land’s own metabolism, slow and patient, the same force that is still lifting the entire mass a few millimeters each decade.

Nothing green has taken. The world is mineral and made. Where soil might have been there are sheets of black volcanic glass, some of it pahoehoe that froze in mid-fold, some of it already broken into hexagonal flags that ring like struck porcelain when a rare gust finds them. Newer flows still glisten, skins not yet fully cooled, and the newest of all are still arriving: thin, bright tongues that emerge from fissures no one mapped last season and then halt, as if listening. The glass is quarried. It is also growing. The two processes have not yet agreed which is in charge.

The city occupies the center of this rising plate like a thought that came too soon. It is dense the way a crystal lattice is dense: surfaces almost touching, walkways stacked three and four deep, pipe-runs and truss-work filling every interval so that the air itself feels occupied. Yet the whole thing remains open because the plateau is vast. From the highest gantry you can see twenty kilometers of stacked industry before the structures thin into the raw horizon. Between here and there the city layers itself according to temperature rather than plan. Lowest are the foundries that simply lean against the warmest rock, their floors cut directly into the living stone so that heat rises through them without fuel. Mid-level halls process whatever the rock yields: glass, metals that have been sweated out of the new basalt, condensates. Highest are the catwalks and the slow turbines, their blades turning once a minute, once an hour, geared down until motion itself becomes almost geological.

The industry is quiet because it does not fight the heat; it borrows it. There are no roaring furnaces. The stone is already the furnace. What you hear, when you hear anything, is the deep pressure of those turbines, a sound more felt in the sternum than registered by the ear, and the intermittent tick of condensation. Water exists here mostly as that faint, perpetual damp. Pipes that carry the cooler side of the heat-exchange bloom with droplets; the droplets run, gather in shallow gutters, evaporate, gather again. Real rain is an event. It arrives thin and high, hisses where it meets the warmer streets, and leaves dark maps that last an hour before the stone drinks them or the air takes them back. In the lowest alleys the air stays humid enough that metal sweats and the glass underfoot is always faintly slick.

The streets are new-cut. Saw marks still show. Corners have not yet been rounded by weather because weather has not yet had time. The city and the mountain appear to be growing at the same unhurried rate; neither is finished, and it is no longer obvious which is the scaffold for the other. Some walls show flow-lines, as if they were extruded rather than assembled. Leave a steel tool on the warm stone overnight and by morning it will have grown a skin of the same black glass, thin as vernix, that must be chipped away. The land is still adding itself to the city even as the city cuts and stacks the land.

Daylight is ordinary in color but thinned by altitude, the shadows razor-sharp, the sky a hard, almost metallic blue. At certain hours the glass fields throw the sun back in broken sheets so that walking the lower districts means moving through sudden white flares and then sudden dark. Night, when it comes, is not much cooler; the rock holds its heat the way a banked oven does. The few lights that burn are industrial, sodium or the dull red of cooling metal, and they do not travel far.

Life that is not machinery is rare. Once in a long while something pale and long-legged picks its way across the glass as if the surface were a frozen lake. No one agrees what it eats. It does not stay. Inside the condensation pipes there are films that are not quite bacterial, not quite mineral; the maintenance crews scrape them out and do not discuss them. That is the extent of fauna.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is in the clocks, all of which run a fraction slow, as if time itself were still settling into the new elevation. It is in the way the newest buildings seem to have been exhaled by the rock rather than built upon it. It is in the silence that is not empty but simply patient, the sound of a place that expects to be here for centuries and is in no hurry to finish becoming itself. Far off, a crane eases a girder into place with the unhurried care of geology. The wind, when it comes, is too thin to carry voices. You hear your own breath. You hear the stone considering another millimeter.
