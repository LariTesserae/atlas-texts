The high country is still arriving.

It did not finish forming. Whatever lifted it—plate, pulse, argument in the mantle—has not receded. Ridges come up overnight as clean as kiln-work, unrounded, edges that would cut a hand if a hand were the right kind of thing to bring here. The stone is young in the way a scab is young: glossy, too tight, faintly warm to stand on. Heat lives in the rock more than in the air. You feel it through whatever you are wearing. The air itself is only moderately warm, a held breath, but the ground keeps offering more, as if the planet has not yet decided whether this is a mountain range or a cooling foundry.

Light is scarce and sideways. Not night. A dim that has lasted. The sky is a thick, low lid the color of wet iron, and what illumination there is seems to sweat out of the stone itself—a dull internal shine, the glow of metal just below red. Shadows do not fall so much as pool. In that dimness the distances refuse to agree. A knife-ridge ten miles off will suddenly be a wall you could spit on; a valley under your feet will yawn into a province. The scale is wrong for walking and right for weather. Cells of cloud form, live, and die inside single cirques. Storms have zip codes. The plateau—if it is a plateau and not simply the planet showing a new face—goes on past any horizon that will hold still long enough to be trusted.

Water is a guest, not a resident. It arrives as film. Condensation beads on the fresh faces of basalt and something paler, almost ceramic, that keeps extruding along the fault-lines. Brief rains walk through like someone testing a house. They last minutes. The thirsty new stone drinks most of it before it can become a stream; what remains collects in sharp cups and hanging tarns that were not there yesterday and may be vents tomorrow. In the damp seams, vegetation has become indecently lush for so little moisture. Mosses the thickness of felt. Ferns that uncoil in an hour and then lignify, as if trying to become trees before the next lift. Stands of something like bamboo or reed, hollow, warm to the touch, growing so densely in the wetter clefts that the rock disappears and you walk on a living grate. The green is not continuous. It is concentrated, saturated, a series of hanging gardens and wet galleries in a largely mineral country. Between them the stone stays bare and sharp, still steaming a little at dawn, if dawn is the word.

Nothing here was built. There are no walls, no roads, no cut marks that remember a tool. And yet the place behaves industrially. Veins of ore do not merely exist; they sort themselves. You watch, if you stay still long enough in the quiet, a dark seam brighten, clarify, extrude a skin of slag that sloughs and is reabsorbed. Fumaroles do not only vent; they cycle, inhale, stamp a regular rhythm into the mist. In the deeper clefts there are chambers where heat and pressure and the wrong kind of time are doing something that looks like smelting, like rolling, like the planet practicing manufacture without a product in mind. The “factories” have no roofs and no owners. They are geological moods. Occasionally a finished thing is left on a ledge—a plate of laminated stone too even, a rod of cooled glass with a thread of copper through its heart—and then the next tremor or the next rewrite takes it back. Industrial, yes. Constructed, no. The distinction matters and also does not, because the rules that would keep those words apart are among the things still being decided.

Sound stays low. The turbulence is almost silent. Rock rises with a pressure you feel in your teeth more than hear. When a new ridge shears up it does so like a held note, a subsonic complaint. Rain, when it comes, is a hiss quickly drunk. The abundant animals do not waste voice. There are too many of them for the volume of the country: things like goats with too many joints, picking their way along edges that did not exist at first light; flocks of dark gliders that hang in the dim as if pinned to the air, then peel off in sheets when a thermal—or a revision—passes; insects the size of thumbs that live in the hollow reeds and tick, very softly, in time with the fumaroles. In the wet galleries, something pale and many-legged farms the moss, turning it, aerating, a husbandry without farmers. You smell them before you hear them. Density is layered. Life occupies the cracks, the films of water, the undersides of new overhangs, the warm skins of the industrial vents. Near-touching. If you stand in a lush cleft you can feel breath that is not yours on both sides of your body.

The quiet makes the instability worse. Change does not announce itself. A terrace you have been crossing will simply be a different altitude when you look down; the drop was always there, or never, or is still arriving. Colors slip. A stand of ferns will be copper for a while, then the green returns as if embarrassed. Gravity has local habits. In some basins you are slightly too heavy, and the condensation runs uphill for a few yards before remembering itself. In others, loose scree hangs in the air like a held breath and then chooses a direction. The weirdness is not decoration. It is the weather of a place whose physics is still in draft. Sometimes you see the draft: a double image of a peak, one sharp and one already beginning to erode, superimposed, and then one of them wins. Sometimes the industrial process leaks into living things—an animal whose bones have the regular spacing of rivets, a plant that sheds perfectly hexagonal leaves. Sometimes the living leaks the other way, and a cooling flow of stone will briefly have a pulse.

There are no ruins to explain any of this and no settlements to deny it. The country is too new for memory and too large for a single story. You can walk for what feels like days along a single rising wall, damp wind on one cheek, the faint factory-smell of hot mineral on the other, and the only artifacts you find are the ones the ground sets down and then reclaims: a gear of crystal, a length of wire that is also a root, a shallow pan of water so still it shows a sky that is not the sky above you. Then the next rain, or the next lift, and the ledge is gone, or higher, or has become a gallery of ferns ticking softly in the dim.

At the largest scale the highland has climates the way a continent has nations, and they do not stay allied. A warm wet cell will bloom into a hanging forest in a week, dense enough to lose yourself in, loud with the almost-silence of dripping and of many small bodies moving. A dry cell a valley over will stay mineral, sharp, the stone still so fresh it squeaks underfoot. Between them the boundaries walk. The whole mass keeps rising in pulses you can map by the generations of plants: the lowest, oldest galleries already starting to look like ordinary mountains, rounded a little, the green more patient; the newest crests still glossy, still deciding whether they are rock or machine or something that has not chosen a kingdom. Above it all the iron sky holds its dim. Below, whatever engine is lifting this place has not finished its shift.

You do not get used to it. The country will not hold still long enough for habit. It is a young, warm, quiet catastrophe the size of a world, damp in the seams, lush where it can be, crowded with lives that treat instability as climate, and underneath—or inside—or instead of—the geology, something that works like industry without ever having been built. The place is still coming into existence, and it does not particularly need you for that. It will keep arriving whether you find a way to leave or become, like the rest of the fauna, another density in the cracks.
