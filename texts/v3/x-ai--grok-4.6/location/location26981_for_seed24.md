The hollow sits in the low country as if the land had once drawn a quiet breath and never quite let it out. It is not a canyon and not quite a cave: a shallow, roofed depression perhaps eighty paces across, its ceiling of compacted siltstone hanging only a little higher than a raised arm. The opening that once admitted weather has long since slumped shut under its own weathered weight, leaving the space sealed from rain, from wind, from any cycle that might still move outside. What remains is a single, self-contained volume of warm dark.

The air does not stir. It holds a dry, even heat that seems to rise from the stone itself, the kind of warmth that never quite reaches discomfort and never recedes. Dust lies everywhere in a fine, pale film that has had centuries to settle; when a foot disturbs it the motes hang motionless, neither rising nor falling, as though the idea of convection has been forgotten. There is almost no water. A faint mineral dampness clings to certain fractures—enough to darken the rock in hairline veins, never enough to bead or drip. The chemistry here is all salt and silica and slow oxidation; nothing dissolves, nothing precipitates. The forms have finished changing.

Light is a rumor. What little there is arrives as a colorless, directionless gray that seems generated by the rock rather than admitted through it, a leftover glow too weak to cast a shadow or pick out color. Eyes adjust only enough to understand that the space is larger than it first feels and then stop adjusting. Edges remain suggestions.

Across the floor the vegetation keeps its distance. Isolated tufts of a gray, almost crystalline moss occupy the slightly lower pockets where the last traces of moisture collect. Each clump is no larger than a spread hand and stands several body-lengths from its nearest neighbor, so that most of the chamber is bare, swept stone. The moss does not green, does not flower, does not visibly grow. It simply persists, a sparse punctuation in an otherwise empty grammar. Nothing grazes it. Nothing pollinates it. The ecology is an afterthought that has already been thought.

Sound has likewise settled into near-absence. Occasionally a grain of silt detaches from the ceiling and meets the floor with a tick so soft it might be imagined. That is all. The silence is not expectant; it is complete, the acoustic equivalent of the heat and the dust: a finished state.

The stone itself shows every sign of having arrived at its final arrangement long ago. Surfaces are rounded, joints softened, fallen slabs half-buried in their own grit as if they had always lain exactly there. No fresh break, no sharp arris. The hollow has the patient geometry of something that finished eroding before memory began and has been keeping its shape ever since. There are no paths, no stacked stones, no scratches that could be read as marks. The place has never been arranged by any hand.

It does not change. Seasons do not reach it. The temperature does not cycle. The moss neither spreads nor dies back. The dust, once lifted, hangs and then returns to the same thin carpet. Time here is not a current but a still pool, and the hollow has been floating in it for so long that floating has become its only possible condition.
