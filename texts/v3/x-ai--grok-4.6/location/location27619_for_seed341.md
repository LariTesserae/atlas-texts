The basin sits no higher than a man’s shoulder above the surrounding flats, a shallow concavity perhaps two hundred paces across, its rim a low collar of sandstone that weeps without cease. Water does not fall here so much as arrive already present: it beads on every surface, sheets down the rock in slow curtains, stands in black channels between the structures, and hangs in the air as a warm, unmoving breath. The heat is not the dry sting of open sun but a close, soaking pressure that never lifts. Everything is slick. Brick sweats. Iron sweats. The air itself seems to sweat.

What occupies the basin is not a town so much as the memory of one, or the skeleton of one that decided to stay. A handful of buildings—engine house, two long sheds, a squat brick barracks on pilings, a cistern tower—stand well apart from one another, each large enough to feel civic yet few enough that the spaces between them remain the true rooms of the place. Those spaces are water and mud and sudden thickets. Raised iron walkways and fat, lagged pipes stitch the structures together at chest and eave height; beneath them the ground is a mosaic of steaming puddles, algal mats, and gravel beds that the water rearranges daily. Nothing here was built yesterday. Corners of brick have been rounded by decades of drip. Paint on the boilers has been renewed in overlapping skins of ochre and black, each layer cracked and colonized. The whole arrangement has the settled, unhurried look of something that long ago stopped arguing with the wet and simply incorporated it: oversized gutters, sloped floors, every joint designed to weep rather than hold.

Light arrives already tired. A low ceiling of vapor sits just above the chimneys, turning noon into a brownish dusk and dusk into something nearer night. What illumination there is comes from caged bulbs and the occasional unshielded arc that hisses inside its own halo of mist; their glow travels only a few metres before dissolving. Shadows are soft, multiple, and slightly wrong in direction, as though the steam itself were luminous. Colour is reduced to rust, wet brick, the deep green-black of saturated foliage, and the dull gleam of wet metal.

The vegetation is lush where it occurs and absent where it does not. In the open intervals between buildings, stands of giant horsetail and tree-fern rise three and four metres, their fronds heavy enough to drip continuously, their trunks furred with moss that holds water like a sponge. Pipes and railings wear living sleeves of liverwort and filmy fern. On the warmer southern faces of the engine house, climbing aroids have laced themselves through the brick courses until it is no longer clear whether the wall supports the plants or the plants have become the wall’s new mortar. The growth is vigorous, almost visibly impatient in the heat, yet it respects the sparseness of the plan: there are still broad sheets of open water, still mudflats that steam and crack, still stretches of bare, weeping stone. The plants do not choke; they occupy, densely and then stop.

There are no animals. Not a mosquito, not a frog, not the smallest moth drawn to the lamps. The silence of living bodies is absolute and, after a time, noticeable as a pressure of its own. The plants flower anyway—pale, waxy spathes that open and close on some internal schedule unrelated to pollinators. Seeds or spores or whatever they use simply appear downstream, or on the next season’s new brick. It is one of the quieter strangenesses: an ecology that has edited out the entire animal kingdom and continued, unbothered, at industrial volume.

Sound is the other occupying force. The central compound engine does not so much run as declare itself, a deep, wet thud that travels through the walkways and the water and the bones of anyone standing still. Steam valves lift and scream at irregular intervals, then settle into a long hiss that is almost a note. Water never stops moving: it pours from overflow lips in solid columns, chatters in the half-submerged flumes, and, every few minutes, lets go from the vapour ceiling and the undersides of leaves in a sudden, localized downpour that lasts half a minute and then ceases. Metal expands and complains. Pumps inhale and cough. The basin, being a bowl, keeps all of it and returns it as overlapping echoes, so that the place is never merely loud but polyphonic, a machine-and-water liturgy with no Off.

Everything is in motion and has been for a long time. Water levels rise and fall on a cycle that is part pump, part some slower seepage from the surrounding flats; walkways that are dry at one hour stand ankle-deep at the next. Steam plumes fatten and thin with the load on the engines. New fern croziers uncurl in a single hot afternoon. Condensation gathers on the underside of a pipe until its own weight tears it free and the droplet becomes, briefly, a vertical river. The industry itself—whatever it originally made or moved—now seems chiefly occupied with remaining in equilibrium with the wetness that wants to drown it. The pumps keep the basin from becoming a lake; the lake, or the idea of the lake, keeps the pumps necessary. The arrangement is old enough to have acquired the dignity of a natural law.

In the dimmest hours the arcs throw double and triple shadows that do not quite match the objects that cast them. The water in the deepest channels holds a faint, sourceless milkiness, as though something far below were still cooling. The plants nearest the hottest pipes have taken on a metallic sheen, not painted, not rusted, simply other. These are small deviations. They do not announce themselves. They wait, like the rest of the place, in the heat and the noise and the unending arrival of water, for the next cycle to begin.
