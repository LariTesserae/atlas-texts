The first thing is not a view. It is a pressure that arrives as hearing and then as bone.

The roar has no attack and no decay. It does not swell, does not idle, does not hunt for a harmonic and settle. It is already the entire air, a single industrial vowel held since the room was sealed, so even and so total that after a minute the mind begins to file it as silence and the body refuses. Teeth buzz. The inner ear offers a high, private whistle as if trying to cancel a frequency it cannot name. The gauges along the far bulkhead do not tremble. Nothing in the visible field moves. The noise is doing all the moving for everyone.

This is the whole place. There is no corridor beyond the hatch you do not remember using, no gantry, no weather. The chamber is a long loft of unpainted metal, perhaps forty meters, the ceiling a low vault of ribbed plate that never learned the word ornament. Every surface is made. There is no bedrock under the grating, no original stone, no soil that was here first. The floor is stamped decking in shallow, regular dunes — an engineered topography, each ridge still sharp enough to catch a sole. The walls are sandwich panels bolted on a grid so new the zinc on the fastener heads has not dulled. Seams have not been dressed. Burrs remain. You could cut a palm on the youth of it.

It is bitterly, dryly cold. Not the wet cold of a glacier tongue, not the cold that makes breath. There is almost no water here to make breath visible. The air is a processed gas, a thin industrial atmosphere with the humidity engineered out of it, and the cold sits in it like a law. Metal takes the temperature into itself and keeps it. If you put a tongue to a rail you would leave skin and learn nothing new. Frost does not feather the pipes; there is not enough vapor for frost. The cold is clean the way a store-room is clean: an absence that has been specified.

Light is ordinary and slightly green, the color of work. High-bay lamps hang in two ranks and do not flicker. They have the frankness of a loading dock at noon. Shadows are rectangular and stay where they are put. There is no sun-track, no dimming cycle. The illumination is a setting, not an event.

Three objects occupy the loft the way erratics occupy a high pass — far apart, deliberate, with a great deal of emptiness negotiated between them.

The first is the engine, if that is the word. It occupies the center third of the floor, a range of housings in pale ceramic and black oxide, each housing the size of a small hut, connected by trunks of insulated conduit as thick as thighs. Nothing on it turns that you can see. Inspection ports show only more stillness: a turbine disc like a frozen sunflower, a piston crown seated forever at the top of its travel. And yet the roar comes from it the way heat comes from a stove, as a condition of its being there. Perhaps the motion is elsewhere — in a frequency, in a working fluid that never changes phase, in a loop so perfectly closed that work and waste have agreed to stop accounting. The machine manufactures equilibrium and exports it as noise. Warning stencils along its flanks are still crisp. Someone painted them and then time, in this room, declined to continue.

The second presence is a row of three ceramic cylinders near the south panel, waist-high, each holding a single plant. The plants are the only concession to a biosphere and they look installed rather than grown: pale, succulent, almost glass, each with a few thick leaves arranged in a geometry that has not added a cell since the hatch was dogged. Their medium is a dry mineral wool the color of old bone. No reservoir, no drip-line. Vegetation as a clause in a contract, satisfied and then abandoned. A faint mineral dust has settled in the leaf-axils and will settle nowhere else, because nothing else here sheds.

The third is the window, if it is a window. A single oval porthole, double-glazed, set in a collar of frost-white polymer. Through it: whiteness and a sense of drop. Not cloud exactly, not snow. A high blank, the color of overexposed film. You understand elevation in the inner ear and in the way the cold feels thin, as if the room had been lifted and left. Whatever supports this loft is not offered to you. The structure is young — you can feel the newness in the unsoftened angles, the unused lifting lugs still welded to the ceiling ribs, the protective films someone forgot to peel from two of the lower panels, still clinging, still printed with a manufacturer’s chevron. Recently emerged. Assembled and then, immediately, held.

There are no animals. Not a moth at the lamp, not a spider in a corner (there are no proper corners, only radiused joints). The emptiness between the engine and the cylinders and the porthole is a worked emptiness, low-saturation, a designed sparseness. Footsteps on the grating would be inaudible under the roar and so they feel theoretical. You walk in a landscape that has already decided how much company it will tolerate.

Rules suggest themselves, the way frost-laws would if there were frost.

The sound will not change. Modulation is not in the vocabulary. If a bearing were to fail it would fail into the same vowel, or the room would not permit the failure. Time here is not a river. It is a clamped dimension. Gauges are painted at their readings. The plants will not grow and will not die; they occupy a third state that the specifications apparently allow. Water is a contaminant and has been excluded to a part-per of almost none, which is why the cold does not write on the metal, which is why your mouth tastes of tin and static. Everything you can touch was fabricated. The idea of a substrate — of geology, of a world that was here first — is a rumor from some other altitude.

And yet the loft has weather of a kind. Not change: texture. Standing nearer the engine the roar acquires grain, a roughness like crushed slag inside the vowel. Near the porthole it thins a fraction, as if the glass were a shore. The lamps have a faint insectile whine several octaves above the main note, a whine you only notice when you stop trying to hear the engine and let the engine hear you. The decking’s stamped dunes catch the greenish light in long parallel gleams, a dry tide-line repeating to the bulkhead. If you lie down in one of the troughs (the metal taking your heat at once, without drama) the ceiling ribs become a kind of sky: regular, bolted, young, a firmament with part numbers.

What the engine is for is not posted. Perhaps it keeps the cold. Perhaps the cold is the waste and the product is the unchangingness itself, piped out through those sealed trunks into a larger body you are not shown — a tower, a ridge of identical lofts, a high industrial reef recently jacked into a thin sky. The weirdness is quiet and structural. A factory that has achieved its purpose so completely that purpose and idling have become the same gesture. A pocket of made world that will not weather, will not drip, will not host a bird, will not dim. Deafening and still. High and sealed. You could grow old in the time it takes to accept that the next second will be this second, slightly louder for being noticed, and then the noticing goes, and the roar lays itself back down over the sharp new world like a second, heavier atmosphere, and nothing, including you, is required to continue.
