The plateau does not announce its height the way mountains usually do. There is no sudden wall, no last switchback into thin air. You come up through a long, soaking gradient—weeks of walking if you walked, though nothing here has been walked by anything that builds paths—and the air simply grows hotter as it thins. Heat rises from below more faithfully than it falls from the sun. The rock itself is a radiator that never cools.

What you stand in, when you finally stop, is a hanging basin several valleys wide, held between older rims that have already finished their violence. The caldera walls are not cliffs so much as slumped, rain-softened shoulders, black stone gone gray and then green, their faces permanently wet. Water does not run off them so much as leave them, bead by bead. Every overhang weeps. The droplets are warm. They taste faintly of metal and of something cooked.

Cloud lives at ankle height and at the height of the tallest trees at once. It is not fog exactly; it is the atmosphere condensing on itself, a low-grade, continuous rain that never quite decides to fall. Leaves do not drip so much as shine. The ground does not puddle so much as refuse to be dry. Between the rooted things the soil is a dark sponge, warm if you sink a hand in, sometimes almost uncomfortably so, as if a buried animal were breathing just under the peat.

The vegetation is not jungle in the lowland sense. It is denser than that and slower. Trunks are short, buttressed, furred with moss that holds water the way felt holds oil. Epiphytes do not perch so much as queue: fern stacked on fern, bromeliad on the corpse of last year’s bromeliad, everything facing the same patient work of catching what the air will give. There are stands of something like tree-fern that rise higher than the rest, their crowns a wet geometry against the ordinary daylight. Between them the understory is a single continuous animal of leaf, so close that walking would be a negotiation with stems, if walking were the point. Nothing here has been cleared. Nothing has been stacked or cut or aligned.

The heat is not the heat of a desert noon. It is wet heat with a mineral aftertaste, the heat of a kettle left on forever. You feel it in the sinuses and under the nails. Stones that look cool are not. Pools that look still are circulating from below; their surfaces shiver with the smallest convection. At the margins of the larger hot wetlands the marsh-edge is precise: a line where the water is too warm for one kind of root and exactly right for another. Pale, almost translucent sedges give way, in a single stride, to dark succulent mats that hiss faintly when the wind—what little wind there is—moves over them.

That hiss is the closest the place comes to industry, and it is not industry. Deep in the rock the old heat still organizes itself. Steam finds the same vents it has found for a very long time. The vents are not dramatic; they do not throw columns. They exhale in long, even breaths, a sound like distant boilers heard through wool, or like a factory on the far side of a hill that has already finished its century and is only cooling. Regular. Unhurried. The rhythm is older than any engine and has no product. Sometimes a vent will pulse three times and rest. The moss around its lip is bleached in a neat ring. Nothing has been built to capture this. Nothing has been piped. The earth simply continues a process that looks, if you insist on the comparison, like scaled production with no one to scale it for.

Because the sound is mostly that—and the drip, and the occasional heavy drop from a high frond—the basin feels quiet even when it is not silent. Birds exist in numbers. You see them more than you hear them: dark, compact things that move through the steam in short, economical flights and land without announcement. There are frogs whose throats are the same wet brown as the leaf litter; they sing, when they sing, in a register just under the hiss of the vents, so that you can mistake them for more geology. Insects are a constant soft presence, a density you feel on the skin rather than a roar. Something larger keeps to the thicker steam along the inner marshes—you catch the displacement of stems, the brief darker shape, then the vegetation closing as if it had never opened. Predator and prey are both here, but they work at the pace of the heat. Nothing sprints for long.

The light is ordinary overcast light, the kind that makes colors true and slightly tired. No golden hour arrives; the cloud is too even. Shadows are soft and multiple. You can see a long way across the basin—ridge to opposite ridge, a distance that would be a day’s walk through that density—and the view is not a vista so much as a repetition: wet shoulder, steaming flat, darker water, another shoulder. Features multiply without becoming various. The erosion finished its argument a long time ago. The forms are settled. Gullies are rounded. Boulders have the particular smoothness of stone that has been rained on in the same places since the last ash cooled. The landscape is in its own state. It is not becoming anything else in a hurry.

If there is a slight strangeness, it is only this: the heat should not live this high, and the water should not hang this thick, and yet the two have reached an old treaty. The plateau manufactures its own weather and consumes it. Rain forms, falls a few meters, is taken back into vapor by the ground’s warmth, forms again. The cycle is tight, almost mechanical, and almost silent. You could stand in one place until the light thinned and never see a structure, never see a straight line that was not a stem, never hear a voice that was not water finding the same path it found yesterday.

Toward the center of the largest wetland the water is deeper and clearer, stained the color of weak tea, and you can see the pale ghost of drowned wood standing upright on the bottom, not fallen, just finished. Bubbles rise through it in slow columns. The surface does not quite steam; it breathes. Around the edge, where the marsh is only ankle-deep and the roots knit a floating floor, the density of life is highest—larvae, small fish adapted to warmth, the layered shadows of wading things that do not startle. Everything is close. Everything is wet. Everything has been here long enough to look inevitable.

The rims hold the weather in. Beyond them, you sense rather than see the drop: the true mountains falling away, the air cooling as it should, the ordinary world resuming at a lower altitude. Here, for this one open measure of high ground, the earth has decided to be a kiln that grows a forest and a marsh at once, and to do it without witnesses, and to keep the boilers going for no reason except that the rock is still hot and the sky is still willing to condense.
