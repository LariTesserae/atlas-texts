The water is the first and last thing. It has no surface anyone here has ever reached, and the notion of “above” has long since lost its use. What remains is a single cool volume, dark enough that even the idea of a photon feels like a rumor from another age. Pressure is a constant handshake, neither crushing nor kind, simply the medium in which everything has always existed. Currents move through it the way blood once moved through something living: slow, directional, never arriving anywhere new. They have been doing this for so long that the word “current” itself has worn smooth.

What they move around are the structures.

They are not trees, though that is the nearest living word. They are the architecture that plants became when there was nothing left to grow toward and nothing left to grow from. Vast, flattened crowns the size of vanished cities sit at staggered depths, their upper surfaces worn into broad, almost-level plains of living timber. From those plains hang curtains of root and stipe, some of them hollow, some of them chambered, all of them the color of old ironwood left in brine for geological time. Between one such mass and the next there are kilometers of empty water. The gaps are not emptiness so much as interval: the measured silence that lets the structures remain themselves. You can travel a long time and see only two or three of them, each appearing first as a darker density in the dark, then as a cliff of bark and compressed leaf that has forgotten how to be leaf.

Everything is made. There is no bedrock, no sediment that did not once photosynthesize. The “floor,” where it exists at all, is a compressed peat of ancestral tissue, still faintly warm with the last metabolic agreements the world ever signed. The structures themselves were never assembled; they were grown into the only shapes that could persist after the last light failed and the last season ended. Chambers open into other chambers. Passageways follow the old vascular logic, wide enough for the largest residents, ribbed with what used to be xylem. Some halls are so tall their ceilings vanish into the same darkness that holds the water. The plants do not grow now. They do not die. They occupy the exact volume they have always occupied. Equilibrium is not a state they reached; it is the only state they remember.

The fauna treats this architecture as both cathedral and larder. Schools of pale, lantern-jawed things pass in numbers that would be a plague anywhere else; here they are simply the correct density. Larger shapes—slow, plated, the size of overturned hulls—graze the living walls without ever removing more than the walls have already agreed to give. Their mouths are not for tearing but for polite, endless rasping. Between the grazers and the filter-feeders and the things that live inside the hollow stipes there is a constant traffic, a living weather that never changes its forecast. They make noise. The water carries it farther than light ever could. Low-frequency rumbles travel from one structure to the next like conversation between mountains. Click-trains ricochet through the chambers. The currents themselves, forced through the older hollows, produce a standing roar that has no beginning and no decay, a single note held since the world finished becoming this.

Temperature is the same everywhere: the cool of a cellar that has forgotten the sun. No thermoclines, no vents, no gradients left to argue over. The darkness is not absence so much as the final material. Occasional faint glows appear—bioluminescent traces on a fluke, a brief constellation of dots along a feeding trail—but they are local, quickly swallowed, never enough to make a horizon. Sight is a luxury the place does not budget for. Everything that needs to know where it is already knows.

This is the oldest arrangement the world could settle into. Whatever mountains or forests or cities preceded it were metabolized, then grown over, then grown into, until only the plant-architecture remained, and then the water rose (or the land simply accepted submersion as its last honest shape) and the light went out and the clocks stopped. Nothing erodes anymore because the shapes that remain are the ones the water and the living wood have already finished negotiating. Time still passes, but it passes through a place that has no further alterations to offer it. The structures stand. The fauna move. The roar continues. The dark continues. The cool continues. There is no next version of this. There is only this, held open and sparse and complete, the final grammar of a world that learned how to stop becoming anything else.
