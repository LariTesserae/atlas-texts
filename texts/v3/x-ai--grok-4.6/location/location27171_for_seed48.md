The basin holds heat the way a kettle holds water: not dramatically, just completely. Whatever river or inlet first justified the mills has long since lost a clean name. It lies almost level with the streets, a brown sheet that never quite recedes, and the streets themselves sit so low that after any weather the water table comes up through the brick and stands in the gutters as a second, quieter pavement. You can walk a long time here without climbing more than a stoop.

There are no plants. Not weeds in the cracks, not moss on the north faces, not even the scum that usually claims still water. The ground is slag, poured clinker, salt-crusted concrete, and the old river silt baked into something like pottery. Whatever could photosynthesize died a generation or two back and was not replaced. The heat is part of it—afternoons sit in the high thirties even under the dim—and the rest is chemistry: runoff from the yards, the taste of metal in every puddle, a substrate that will not hold a seed. Life did not leave. It only stopped being green.

What remains is industrial and old enough to have found its shape. Foundries, pump-houses, long brick sheds with sawtooth roofs, cooling towers like truncated cones, a grid of rail cuts now filled with warm standing water. The buildings have settled. Lintels sag. Iron has gone to a stable brown and then stopped, as if rust itself grew tired. Nothing here looks recent and nothing looks about to fall. The city is in the state cities reach when they have been used hard and then left in a climate that weathers rather than destroys—stained, weeping, complete.

It is also empty in the volumetric sense. The streets are wide because they were laid for trucks and sidings. Between one occupied doorway and the next there can be a hundred meters of blank wall, a canal, a vacant lot of hardpan the size of a square. You see a long way: chimney-lines receding into vapor, the pale geometry of a warehouse district, the low dark bar of the next set of sheds. The scale is a town’s, or a small city’s, stretched across a valley floor that was never quite dry. Presence is scarce. Volume is cheap.

And yet the animals are everywhere, if you look at the right grain. The heat and the water and the absence of plants have not made a desert of flesh. They have made a world of things that eat detritus, each other, and the grease that still sweats from old machinery. Cockroaches in plates on the undersides of tanks. Midges in such numbers that a shaft of the dim light becomes a moving column. Frogs—or frog-shaped things—in every flooded cellar, so many that the water’s surface is a continuous small disturbance and yet almost silent, a wet ticking. Rats use the rail beds as roads. Feral dogs exist as rumor and as droppings; the distances between packs are large. Bats stitch the dusk, which is most of the day. Birds nest in the cooling towers in numbers that would be a marvel if the towers were not so tall and so hollow. None of this fills the air. The basin is too big, the buildings too vacant. Overwhelming is a count, not a crowd. You can stand in a square and feel alone while a million lives work the damp two meters of air above the water.

The light is always a little wrong for the heat. Vapor lifts off the canals and the weeping walls and hangs. The sun, when it can be placed, is a brighter region in a low white sky. Interiors are brown. Exteriors are the color of wet rust and wet brick. There is no sharp shadow and no full day. Lamps still burn in a few windows and along a few gantries—the grid was built to last, and something, somewhere, still feeds it—but they make islands, not a city of light.

Sound behaves the same way. The factories are not dead. A compressor kicks on two streets over and runs for twenty minutes, then stops. A pump in a riverside house works on a float switch, a slow regular gulp you feel in the soles before you hear it. Steam finds a valve. Water drops from a rusted eave into a larger water. The fauna, for all its mass, is not a chorus. Heat keeps mouths shut. What moves, moves wet-footed and close to surfaces. The loudest ordinary thing is often a single dog, very far, or the sudden small thunder of wings leaving a tower. Then the basin takes the noise and thins it.

The activity is real and mostly invisible as drama. Water rises and falls a hand’s width with weather and with the old industrial withdrawals that still, somehow, occur. Condensation runs its daily cycle on every pipe and window: beading, streaking, gathering in the same stains it has used for years. Animals shift with the dimness—out toward the open water when the air is slightly less crushing, back into brick when the heat pools. A conveyor in a packing shed jerks a meter and rests. Something large and unhurried uses the canal at night; by morning there are only the V of a wake already gone and a smell of mud. Processes continue. Nothing is in a hurry to conclude them.

People are not absent so much as diluted. A window with cloth in it. A boat tied where the street becomes channel. A man crossing a yard with a bucket, already small against the shed wall, and then the yard empty again. The city was built at a density of work and has kept the work’s skeleton while shedding the workers. What is left of habitation clings to the slightly higher ground—old offices, a row of terrace houses whose front steps now end in water—and even there the rooms are larger than the lives in them.

If you stay long enough the place stops reading as ruin. Ruin implies a story that ended. This is a climate that includes brick, a hydrology that includes basements, an ecology that ate the green and kept the rest. The heat does not break. The water does not leave. The animals do not thin. The machines, those that still turn, turn because they were made to, and the ones that do not have become geology with windows. You can walk to the edge of the built grid and look out across more of the same low wet land, unmarked except by the stumps of pilings and the heat-shimmer, and know that behind you the city goes on in the other direction just as far—street, canal, shed, empty air, the soft industry of things with too many legs—and that none of it is waiting to become anything else.
