The notch has no proper name because almost no one has stood in it long enough to need one. It is a single cut, cathedral-narrow, high enough that the sky at either mouth is a pale slit and the air arrives already used. The massif that holds it is still arriving. The stone has the look of something extruded rather than weathered: faces that meet in blades, bedding planes still glazed from the last shove, corners that have not yet learned to be pebbles. You can cut a palm open by leaning. Rubble, where it exists, is angular and recent, a scatter of fresh flakes that ring when they shift, as if the mountain were still cooling from a blow.

It is always dusk here. The neighboring teeth rose faster and stole the sun; what light remains is a gray that never quite decides to be morning. Condensation does the rest. At this height water does not run so much as decide, drop by drop. Rime feathers the lee edges overnight, a white fur that thaws in the dim and weeps down the walls in threads too thin to be called falls. The threads find the same grooves they found yesterday. Pools exist only as dark coins in hollows the size of a cupped hand, skimmed with grit, never more than a finger deep. The smell is wet iron and something faintly sweet, like crushed snow that has sat too long in a pocket.

There is almost no green. What lives as plant lives as a mistake: a beard of black lichen in the wettest seams, a few cushions of moss the color of old bruises, one wiry shrub no taller than a knuckle that has somehow kept a single leaf. The leaf ticks when the wind finds it. That is the whole forest.

The rest of the volume is animal.

They coat the walls in a second geology. From a distance—there is not much distance; the notch is perhaps forty strides long and you can touch both sides if you stretch—the life reads as texture, a living scree, pale and overlapping. Closer, it resolves into bodies. They are the size of a fist, then the size of a heart, then larger where the older ones have fused at the edges into mats. Each has a shell the same gray as the fresh stone, ridged, slightly translucent when a rare brighter hour leans in, so that you can see a slow dark fluid moving underneath. They cling with a circle of wet feet. They breathe through slits that open and shut out of phase with one another, a thousand small mouths, and the air they move is the air of the place: thin, cold, damp, used.

They are loud the way a hive is loud, or a river under ice. Not language. A stacked drone of clicks and wet inhalations and the minute scrape of shell on shell as they adjust. The sound does not peak. It has been this loud since the colony filled the available stone, which, as far as the stone is concerned, was last year or the year before. Newcomers settle in the gaps. The dead do not fall far; they are mortared in by the living, and the mats thicken by the thickness of a shell every few seasons. If you put your ear to a mature patch you hear a lower note, almost a tone, as if the mass had found a frequency in the rock and decided to keep it.

They eat the condensation and whatever the wind lifts this high: moths that should not have come, dust, the occasional seed that will never sprout, one another when a body fails. They do not hunt. There is nothing else to hunt. Sometimes a pale worm works the wet seams beneath them, blind, segmented, tasting mineral. Sometimes a bird the color of the dusk hangs in the mouth of the notch, riding the updraft, and screams once, a sound that does not belong, and is gone. The colony does not look up. Their eyes, if that is what the dark pits are, face the stone.

The weirdness is quiet and structural. The shells are not secreted so much as grown in register with the rock; break one free (it comes away with a sound like a wet plate) and the underside shows a perfect negative of the crystal it sat on, as if the animal had been trying to become a better version of the mountain. In the thickest mats the individuals have begun to share fluid through hairline bridges. Pulses travel a meter, then two. On the coldest days the whole south wall dimly fog-breathes in unison, a single slow lung. No one has stayed long enough to know whether this is a mind or only plumbing.

There is no path. There are scratches. In one place, at shoulder height if you are standing on the only flat flake large enough to be a floor, five parallel grooves have been cut into a fresh face. They are not claw marks. The tool was something harder than bone and not much more sophisticated: a flake of the same mountain, used until it crumbled. Beside the grooves, in a crack that weeps, someone once wedged a splinter of wood no longer than a finger. The wood is gray and furred with the same lichen as the walls. It might be a marker. It might be a mistake. Nothing else of hands remains. The colony has grown around the splinter without covering it, a courtesy or an indifference.

Change here is a patient argument. The massif lifts by amounts you would need a year and a string to notice. New hairline fractures open with a sound like ice in a glass, then do nothing for a season. A mat of the shell-beasts will, over many dim summers, creep a hand’s width toward the better drip. The rime comes and goes in the same pattern it has always used. The drone does not rest. If you sit until your own breathing falls into it, the notch stops being a place you entered and becomes a throat you are inside of—cool, wet, crowded, unfinished, speaking with a thousand small mouths in a language that has no hurry and no need of you.

At either end the world drops. One mouth looks into a gulf of the same dusk, ridged with other newborn blades. The other looks into cloud that never quite arrives, only condenses on your lashes and on the outermost shells, which click softly as they drink. Between the two mouths there is only this: sharp stone still learning how to be old, water that cannot commit to a river, a darkness that is not night so much as altitude, and a density of life so complete that the mountain seems, for this small length, to have grown a skin that breathes and sings and will not be shed.
