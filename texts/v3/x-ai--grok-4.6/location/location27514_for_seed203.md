The world beneath is not a cave. Caves imply stone that waited. This place grew itself empty.

You descend through a lid that was once soil and is now a compression of pale bark, fused leaf-plates, and ice-hard cambium thicker than any mountain range you have names for. The last meters of the descent are bright enough that your eyes water. Light does not leak down from a sun. It is generated in the walls: a cold, continuous luminance stored in translucent vascular crystal, flushed along ribbed corridors of xylem the width of river valleys, and thrown out into the hollow as a white-green glare with no source you can point to. Shadows are rare and brief. They occur only where living tissue has folded over itself in a recent spasm of growth, and even those last only until the fold thins and the glow works through.

Below the lid, the volume opens.

There is almost nothing in it. That is the first fact the body refuses. Vegetation here is not a carpet or a forest. It is the architecture and the substrate and the weather all at once, and it has arranged itself as a near-void: isolated megastructures of living mass standing, hanging, or drifting in a space large enough to hold its own climates. A single column may be a day’s walk around at the base and still be only a thread when you look up and lose it in the glare. Between columns there can be fifty kilometers of still, cold air. Sound dies in those gaps. A shout becomes a coin dropped into a well that has no bottom you will ever hear.

The plants are cold-adapted to the point of mineral. Their surfaces are matte white, celadon, the dull silver of hoarfrost on old paper. Leaves, where they exist, are stiff lancets plated with a wax that never quite melts. Many structures have abandoned leaves entirely and become ribbed towers of photosynthetic bark, or hanging curtains of root-hair packed so densely they look like frozen waterfalls, or continent-sized vaults of intergrown petiole that form ceilings you could mistake for sky if the light were warmer. Everything is damp in the way a cellar is damp. Water is not abundant. It lives as film, as breath, as occasional weather.

Pockets of it collect where a vascular rupture has wept and the sap has diluted with melt. These are the only true wetnesses: tarns of pale fluid held in the crotches of titanic forks, skeins of condensation that run for a week down a trunk and then stop, thin rivers that follow the undersides of aerial roots and never touch a floor because there is often no floor, only more air and then, far below, another layer of living span. Rain happens. It is not scheduled. A growth-storm far above will squeeze a reservoir; a freeze-thaw in the lid will weep; a hanging forest will shake itself and throw down a brief, localized cloudburst that hisses on cold bark and then is gone. You can walk for days and feel only the constant slight wet of air on skin. Then you can be soaked in an hour and watch the water freeze into a skin of clear ice on your sleeves before it can drip.

The temperature never quite becomes ice and never quite becomes comfort. Breath is always visible. Metal, where anyone still has any, burns the hand with cold. The plants do not mind. Their sap is an antifreeze chemistry, slightly sweet if you are foolish enough to taste it, slightly luminescent if you break a stem at night—which is a figure of speech; there is no night, only regions where a structure has temporarily occluded the glow, and even those are twilight, not dark.

This underworld is young. You can see it in the edges. Nothing has been rounded by a long weather. Trunks punch into the void with the abruptness of spears. Break-faces are clean. Where a column has split, the split is a geometry, not a ruin: pale wound-wood already sealing, sharp as a cut fruit. The whole volume is still deciding its shape. That is the turbulence, and it is almost silent until it is not.

Growth here is not the slow thickening of a temperate forest. It comes in storms. A region that has been empty for a season will, without useful warning, erupt. Buds the size of houses swell along a hanging keel. They rupture. New trunks lance downward or sideways at speeds you can watch, a visible crawl that becomes a roar of tearing fibers when two advancing fronts meet. The sound, when it comes, is enormous and local: a long tearing, a series of concussions as air is displaced, a rain of bark-scale the size of roofs. Then the quiet returns, thicker than before, and there is a new architecture in the glare—a bridge that was not there, a wall, a hanging basin already beginning to catch condensation.

Collapse is the same weather in reverse. A span that grew too fast, or grew hungry in a dry pocket, or was simply finished with itself, lets go. You hear it as a distant, single note, like a glacier calving, and then a long time later the air fills with pale dust and the smell of crushed green ice. The fallen mass does not lie as wreckage for long. It is eaten back into the living floor, or it becomes floor, or it germinates in place and a thicket of needle-spires rises from the corpse before the dust has settled. Creation and uncreation share a calendar. The Hollow is always mid-sentence.

Because the density is so low, these events have room to be weather. There are fronts of flowering that take a week to cross a gap, leaving the air full of colorless scale that catches the light like snow and does not melt. There are sap-floods: a vascular failure high in the lid that pours a slow cataract of luminous fluid down a column, freezing as it falls into a hanging glacier of amber glass, which later cracks in the cold and rains knives for a day. There are inversions, when the usual faint downdraft reverses and the damp of a thousand condensation-rivers lifts as fog, and for a while the glare becomes a blind white room the size of a country, and you walk by putting a hand on whatever living wall you can find.

Fauna is scarce enough that each encounter feels like a mistake. Things live in the wet pockets and in the hollow hearts of the oldest columns. Pale grazers the length of your arm, soft-mouthed, that rasp biofilm from damp bark and vanish into slits you did not see. Something with too many joints that travels the undersides of the great spans and leaves a trail of frost-flowers where its feet have been. Rarely, a flock—if that is the word—of thin, kite-like membranes that ride the growth-storms, feeding on the burst of spores, and then fold themselves into crevices and wait years. You can travel a month and see nothing that moves on purpose. The ecology is the plants. Everything else is a footnote that has not yet been erased.

People are here, in the way lichen is on a boulder: present, scattered, not in charge. Their marks are small against the grain of the place. A ring of living-wood platforms cinched around a column with woven root-cable, tightened each season as the column swells. A stair cut into wound-wood and kept open by scraping, so that the plant heals around the treads and the stair becomes a scar the structure has agreed to keep. Occasional artifacts of an older attempt: a ladder of lashed ribs reaching toward a hanging basin and ending in air; a circle of standing staves, each one a live cutting that has taken and grown into a palisade no one tends. No cities. Nothing that would survive a growth-storm by opposing it. The rule, insofar as there is one, is to lash to what is already alive and to move when it moves.

Their tools are bone, fired clay from the rare mineral seams the roots have not yet finished eating, and wood that is still technically alive, cut and bound before it can decide to become a wall. Edges are stone or ice-glass sap, flaked and re-flaked. Metal is rumor and heirloom: a single knife passed through a lineage, cold enough to stick to skin, used for surgery on the plants when a settlement must open a door in a trunk and keep it from sealing. They harvest without killing when they can—bleed a little sap, peel a little bark, take the ice that forms on the lee of a leaf-vault. When they must cut, they cut small, and they apologize in the same practical tone they use for weather.

The rules of the place are botanical and uninterested in being fair.

Light is a constant. Sleep is a cloth over the eyes or a crawl into the dim heartwood of a willing column, where the glow is only a suggestion and the air smells of sugar and frost. Direction is argued by the grain. The plants remember stress; their fibers align toward old wounds and old water. Follow the swirl in the bark long enough and you will find a damp pocket. Follow it the other way and you will find a desert of dry, ringing wood where nothing has wept in a year and the glare is so clean it feels like a blade.

Time is unreliable in the way weather is unreliable. A growth-storm can raise a barrier overnight and erase a route you had a name for. A collapse can open a passage into a volume no one living has seen, already bright, already faintly dripping, already growing the next version of itself. Maps are stories told in the present tense and discarded without bitterness.

There is a mild wrongness you notice only after the cold and the light have become ordinary. The plants do not quite obey the idea of individual. A column a hundred kilometers away will sometimes pulse in sympathy with a cut you make, a dimming, a flush of brighter sap. Condensation will run uphill for an hour along a root that has decided to drink from the air in reverse. Once in a long while the glow itself flickers—not off, never off—but as if something vast had blinked, and in that flicker you see, or think you see, the true scale: layer below layer of the same hollow, the same bright empty living, going down past any basement the surface world ever planned, a planet whose interior was eaten and replaced with a single, unfinished, radiant plant, still sharpening itself, still mostly air, still cold, still almost silent except when it decides, in some district the size of a nation, to become something else before morning.

You stand on a living span no thicker than a road and look out into a brightness that has weather in it. Far off, too far for sound, a new trunk is writing a vertical line down the white. Closer, a film of water is trying to become a river along the underside of the wood beneath your feet. It will freeze by evening, or it will find a pocket, or it will be shaken off when the span shivers and begins, without drama, to grow. The air smells of wet paper and winter fruit. Your hands ache from the cold. There is nothing per cubic kilometer, and everything you can see is alive.
