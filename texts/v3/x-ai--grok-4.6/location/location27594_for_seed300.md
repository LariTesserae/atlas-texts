The place is a sump. Not a valley—those have ambition—and not quite a courtyard. A rectangular hollow, maybe two hundred paces on the long side, cut a little below the surrounding flats so that everything that wants to leave the neighborhood ends here: rain, seepage, condensate, rust-water, the slow lateral creep of a water table that never learned a deeper bed. The lip is brick, then a berm of compacted clinker, then ordinary low country you forget as soon as you step down. From the berm the whole of it is visible at once. Cathedral scale only if the cathedral were a cistern.

What you notice first is not water but the refusal of dryness. Every horizontal has a film. The brick weeps in threads that never quite become drips; they travel, hesitate, rejoin. Ironwork is permanently beaded. The air itself is just below the point of rain, a cool saturation that makes the skin unsure whether it is wet or merely expected to be. Temperature sits in that narrow band where breath does not steam and hands do not ache, only become slightly less yours. Light is ordinary—overcast, directionless, the kind that makes noon and four o’clock indistinguishable—and it reaches everywhere because there is almost nothing tall enough to throw a true shadow. A few remaining stanchions. The stump of a stack, its throat stuffed with a nest of oxidized grating. One wall of a pump house, roofless, window sockets empty, standing as if it had always been a screen and not a room.

Vegetation has almost given up the argument. What grows does so as stain and afterthought: orange and black lichens mapping the wettest courses of the brick; a crust of algae in the shallows that looks more like a mineral than a plant; two or three reed clumps that have found a seam of actual soil and hold it with the grimness of claimants. No trees. Nothing that would require a root to go down and trust what it found. The green here is an accident of chemistry.

And yet the hollow is dense. Not with life. With presence. Pipes of three or four generations lie in the same beds, some nested, some simply laid across one another and left, so that the floor is a shallow braid of diameters—cast iron, riveted, then welded, then a later plastic that has gone the color of old bone. Valve wheels stand at shin and chest height, many seized into sculptural attitudes, a few still true. Walkways of perforated plate hang a hand-span above the wet, their bolts weeping rust fans down the supports. Tanks, open-topped, sit in a cluster at the far end like basins in a lavatory for something larger than people. Between all of this the water finds every available interval and occupies it. There is no single pool. There is a continuous, shallow, interlocking wet—sheet here, channel there, a trembling meniscus where two pipes almost kiss. You cannot take a step that is not a negotiation. The air between things is crowded with the things’ nearness. High saturation, the parameter said. Yes. The volume is spoken for.

The stone and the iron have been here long enough to finish becoming themselves. Corners are no longer corners. Rivet heads have worn to lozenges. Brick arrises are soft as knuckle. This is not ruin so much as settlement: the forms have reached the shapes they will keep until someone spends energy to change them. Erosion here is a completed sentence. The hollow does not look abandoned so much as arrived.

What has not arrived, what will not, is rest.

The water is not still. It should be—this is a sump, a dead end, a place of collection—and instead it is always in the middle of a rearrangement. Levels rise in one tank while another sighs down through a throat you cannot see. A sheet of water will suddenly find a new seam and evacuate, leaving a dark print that takes a minute to forget itself. Elsewhere a pipe that has been dry for an hour will cough, then run, then run harder, then stop as if embarrassed. Fog generates in pockets: a sudden local weather no larger than a room, blooming out of a flange or a warm plate, then collapsing back into wet air. The surface of the shallows is rarely glassy. It ticks, it crawls, it develops brief standing wrinkles that look like thought and then aren’t. None of this is loud. That is the unkindest part. The whole hydraulic argument conducts itself under a lid of quiet so complete it feels like a rule of the world rather than an accident of muffling. You hear, if you stand long enough, a very distant internal sound: not splash, not hiss, more the idea of water relocating behind brick. Once in a while a valve somewhere in the buried works seats itself with a single, soft, conclusive knock, as if a person in another room had set down a cup.

The machines, such as they remain, are not a factory. They are the factory’s circulatory leftovers, still subject to some timetable that no longer corresponds to production. Current still finds a few circuits. A lamp in a wire cage, bolted to the surviving pump-house wall, burns at a steady, slightly wrong white—not daylight, not quite tungsten—and has done so, you feel, through weathers and seasons without anyone replacing a bulb. A panel of gauges, glass intact, needles trembling in a range that means nothing you can use. Sometimes a pump deep in the wet starts, not with a roar but with a pressure-change you feel in the arches of your feet, and for a few minutes the entire hollow becomes a little more itself, every surface newly decisive, every meniscus newly tense. Then it stops. The water has to find its own politics again.

Fauna is rumor. A print in the silt that might be bird. A movement in the reeds that is probably the reeds. Once, at the edge of usefulness, something small and dark crosses from one pipe-shadow to another and does not come out. You do not wait. The place does not feel empty so much as already spoken for by processes that have no interest in being watched.

There is a mild wrongness that never quite condenses into a thesis. Condensation on the inner faces of the open tanks does not run down in honest streaks; it organizes, briefly, into networks that look like maps of other drainages, other hollows, then slurs and is only water again. The fog, when it blooms, sometimes holds a shape a half-second longer than physics would prefer—an impression of a wheel, a shoulder, a doorway—before becoming weather. The light is normal and the shadows are normal and still you have the sense that the hollow is slightly too exact a container, as if it had been poured to fit something that is not presently in it. Unusual, not impossible. You can leave. The berm is always right there.

What the place does, endlessly, quietly, is change its mind about its own interior. Channels silt and are flushed. A walkway that was a bridge at noon is a pier by the time the light has not changed. The tanks trade volumes like a slow argument among vessels that cannot see one another. Established form; turbulent content. The brick and iron have finished their becoming. The water and the leftover engines have not agreed to finish anything. You stand in a finished room in which the furniture is weather, and the weather is industrial, and the industry is a kind of memory that still has valves.

If there is a product, it is only this: a continuous, silent, low, cool remaking of wetness inside a box of old work. The surrounding flats do not care. The sky does not particularize. The hollow keeps its own weather and spends it and makes it again, densely, in a space small enough that every rearrangement is a local event, and every local event is, for a moment, the whole of the world you can see.
