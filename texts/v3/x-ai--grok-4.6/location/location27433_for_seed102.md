The chamber is no larger than a forgotten cistern and no older than a held breath that never ends. You come into it by a throat of unweathered salt-glass, a seam so recent the edges still cut the pads of the fingers, and then the space opens—not wide, not high, only *enough*. A pocket. A room the earth grew around and then forgot to finish.

There is no water. There has never been water. The air is a dry mineral heat, the temperature of bread just pulled from an oven and left on a stone. It does not cool. It does not rise or pool. It simply *is*, a constant warmth pressed evenly against skin, against the undersides of leaves, against the hollows of small skulls. What moisture the body brought with it beads once and is taken—wicked into the floor as a faint white film that is already part of the mineral, already ancient and already new.

The floor is a pavement of young crystals: hexagonal plates of pale amber and iron-dark glass, their faces unsoftened, their corners still capable of drawing blood. They look poured an hour ago. They have looked poured an hour ago since before there were hours. Nothing here weathers. Nothing abrades. The place is fixed at the instant of its own emergence, a formation that completed itself and then refused the rest of time.

Scattered across this sharp field, with generous emptiness between them, stand the groves.

They are lush the way a wound is lush. Each stand is a single organism or a conspiracy of organisms—it is difficult to tell—and each is a vertical riot of fronds, blades, hanging veils, and stacked cups, all of them dry as paper and thick as muscle. The tissue is neither leaf nor scale nor feather. It has the color of oxidized copper and old blood and the dull green of bronze left in a closed box. The surfaces are matte, velvety, slightly warm to the touch, and they drink heat the way other forests drink rain. They do not wilt. They do not grow. They occupy the exact lushness they have always occupied, every frond unfurled to the same degree, every hanging curtain at the same tension. Between the groves: nothing. Ten paces of empty crystal. Twenty. The eye travels a long way across vacancy and then meets another dense, motionless jungle the size of a standing person, or a bed, or a closed fist of trees.

Light is a low amber dusk that seems to sweat from the walls themselves, or from the groves, or from the heat. It is enough to see by and not enough to read a palm. Shadows are soft and permanent. Nothing casts a moving dark because nothing moves.

And yet the sound.

The sound is a roar without source and without mercy, a standing avalanche of noise that fills the pocket completely, the same amplitude in the ear as in the chest as in the teeth. It does not pulse. It does not fade in the corners. It is a single, unvarying chord of dry thunder, as if a great sheet of the world were being torn at a constant speed just beyond the walls and the tear had no beginning and would have no end. After a time the mind stops calling it loud and begins to call it *the air*. The groves vibrate in it, not visibly, but you feel the vibration in your jaw when you stand close. Some of the fauna seem to speak with it, or through it.

The fauna are many and they are apart.

They do not cluster. A thing like a long-legged cricket, large as a hand, stands on a crystal edge and works its legs against its body in a motion that produces no additional sound, only a continuation of the roar. Across the chamber, another, identical, does the same. Between them, emptiness. There are pale lizards with translucent lids that never close, posed on the lower fronds as if listening. There are moths the color of rust-flakes that do not fly so much as hang in the dim, wings open, and there are more of them than the eye first admits—dozens, scores—each occupying its own volume of air, none touching. Something heavier moves in the deepest grove: a suggestion of joints, a dry hide, the glint of an eye that has been open for a very long time. You do not see it leave. You do not see it arrive. The abundance is real and it is spaced like stars. Life here is a scattering of full intensities.

Near the far wall, where the crystal pavement buckles into a low ridge, someone has stacked plates of the floor-mineral into a ring no higher than the knee. The work is crude, the edges matched by eye and by the patience of hands. Beside it, a scraper of knapped black glass, its working edge still bright, and a length of something like tendon, dried to wire, wound around a pebble. These are the only marks. No smoke-stain. No path worn into the crystals—paths cannot be worn here. The ring and the tool and the cord have the same unaged sharpness as everything else. Whoever made them might have just set them down. Whoever made them has always just set them down.

The rules of the place are few and they do not negotiate.

Nothing here is wet, or will be. The chemistry is heat and mineral and a slow exchange of salts through membranes that were never designed for rain. The groves do not photosynthesize light; they seem to metabolize the roar itself, or the unchanging warmth, or the fact of being unfinished. Time does not pass so much as it occupies. A gesture begun is a gesture held. The fauna feed, if they feed, in ways that leave no remainder and no increase. The chamber does not breathe. The seam you entered by is still as sharp as it was, and will be.

If you stay long enough the sound stops being a sound and becomes a kind of weather you wear inside the skull. The dimness settles into the eyes as a second nature. You begin to understand the spacing: each presence, plant or animal or stacked stone, is allotted its own silence inside the roar, a private radius of not-touching. The lushness is not a forest. It is a series of arguments for forest, isolated, complete, and unrevisable.

There is a moment—there is always this moment—when you realize the pocket is not below the world so much as the world is a rind around this one small, loud, dry, eternal room. The young crystals will never dull. The groves will never shed. The cricket will never finish the motion of its legs. The heat will never slacken. You are standing in a place that finished arriving and then declined to become anything else.

When you leave, if you leave, the roar will cut off as if a door had been pulled shut on a furnace. The silence afterward is a violence of its own. Behind you the chamber continues, exactly, without witness, lush and empty and deafening and still.
