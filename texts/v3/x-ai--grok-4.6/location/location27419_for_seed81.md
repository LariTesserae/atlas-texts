The highland does not rise so much as it holds. You come up through switchbacks cut into pale sinter and find yourself not on a peak but in a broad, shallow dish of land, a plateau-basin several towns wide, its rim always a little lost in vapor. The air is a wet cloth. Heat sits in it the way heat sits in a closed kitchen after water has been left to boil all afternoon: not a surface warmth, not sunshine, but a fullness that occupies the lungs and the inner ear and the space behind the eyes.

There is almost no color that is not a mineral color. Iron-dark stone, brick the shade of old blood diluted by steam, pipes the green-black of long oxidation, pale crusts of carbonate where water has been allowed to dry and has never quite dried. The light is the color of a lantern seen through milk. It has a source, somewhere above the vapor lid, but the lid is low and continuous and the day never commits to being day. At what should be noon the plazas are the same dim pewter as at what should be late afternoon. Shadows are rare and when they appear they are soft as bruises.

The city occupies the whole dish. That is the first fact and the one that refuses to settle. Grid after grid, district after district, a complete urban argument in brick and iron and poured slag: tenement blocks four and five stories high, foundry sheds with sawtooth roofs, pumping houses, cooling towers like blunt teeth, viaducts that stride across the low places on arches wide enough for riverboats that were never here. Streets laid out with the confidence of a capital. Alleys. Courtyards. A civic spine of broader ways that run toward a center you can feel even when you cannot see it. All of it organized. All of it built to last, and it has lasted. The edges of steps are troughed. Lintels have been rewritten by drip. Iron has gone to scale and the scale has gone to a kind of skin. This is not a ruin in the romantic sense. It is a city that finished becoming itself a long time ago and then kept standing in the steam, weathering into an equilibrium with the water that will not leave it alone.

And it is empty.

Not empty the way a Sunday morning is empty. Empty the way a cistern is empty: as a condition of its volume. You can walk a boulevard for a long time and meet no one and pass no cart and see no object left in a window. The rooms, when you look in, are rooms. Walls, floors, the ghosts of fixtures, a stain where a tank once stood, a bolt-circle in a concrete pad. Air. A film of water on the floor that has crept in under the door and found its level. The tenements have stairwells that smell of hot mineral and old soot. Landings open onto galleries that overlook courts in which nothing is stored and no one dries anything and the only movement is the slow crawl of condensate down brick. Between the buildings the distances are wrong for the amount of life they were clearly meant to hold. The city was planned at a human grain and then executed at a scale of absence. Vast cubes of dim air. You become aware of cubic meters the way you become aware of silence after a clock stops.

The water is everywhere that absence is not. It does not fall, not often, not as rain you could name. It weeps. It beads. It runs in sheets so thin they are only visible when a stray thickening of light catches them. Every vertical surface is a spring. The stone itself seems to perspire. There are channels cut along every cornice and they have been cut again by the water’s own insistence until the architecture and the drainage are the same sentence. In the streets the camber has slumped; many of the ways are shallow, warm, slow rivers an inch or three deep, perfectly clear except where iron has stained them tea-brown, steaming faintly as if the pavement had a fever. You walk in them because there is no dry line. The heat of the water and the heat of the air are nearly the same heat. Stepping in does not cool you. It only makes the boundary of your body a little less certain.

Under the basin the ground is wrong in a useful way. This is why the city is here and why it is hot. Vents along the older districts breathe. Not dramatically — this is not a place of geysers and spectacle — but as a steady exhalation from grates, from the mouths of decorated outfalls, from cracks that were pointed and repointed and then left. The breath is wet and smells of struck flint and something sweetly metallic. In the lowest courts the water stands in pools that shiver without wind. Those pools are hotter than the rest. Stand near them long enough and the skin of your face tightens. The city’s remaining industry is arranged around this: not manufacture in the old sense, or not only that, but the management of heat and water as if they were a crop. Pumping stations still work. You find them by the one sound that regularly interrupts the hush — a deep, unhurried thud, felt more than heard, the stroke of something large and well-maintained in a basement. Then a long exhale of steam from a stack you had taken for a tower. Then quiet again, so complete that the drip from a thousand lintels becomes a kind of fabric.

The factories are mostly dark. A few still hold a coal-glow or the dull orange of resistance heat behind dirty glass, enough to make a window into a lantern in the vapor. Inside those, belts hang slack or move with the patience of tides. There are vats. There are galleries of pipe stamped with numbers no one reads. Whatever was being made, or refined, or separated, the process has narrowed to its thermal core: keep the water moving, keep the heat from pooling where the masonry would fail, keep the basin from becoming a single scalding lake. The city is, at this point, a machine for remaining a city in a place that wants to be a fumarole field. It is good at this. It has had time. The erosion parameter of the place is not decay so much as agreement. Corners have been rounded until they shed water the way a shoulder sheds a coat. Mortar has receded and the bricks present their fired faces like wet teeth. Iron straps have been added, long ago, and the straps have become part of the weathering, and newer straps have been added over those in a few places where someone, or something, still cares about a particular span.

There are no plants. This is not a metaphor and not quite a tragedy. The water is too hot in too many places, too mineral in all of them, and the light is a rumor. Nothing green has a thesis here. What grows is not growth. Along the lips of the hotter pools the sinter builds itself in small terraces, cream and rust, crisp as frozen foam and warm as blood. In some courtyards a black glass has been sweated out of the brick where leaks ran for decades, a volcanic sheen that is only chemistry taking its time. On the north faces of the pumping houses, where vapor condenses and never quite decides to drip, there is a film that might be life if you were willing to lower the word far enough: a mineral varnish with a faint, slow iridescence, more like tarnish than like moss. It does not spread so much as it occupies. You would not call it a garden. You would not call it anything, walking past. Only if you stopped and looked for a long time would you notice that yesterday’s iridescence is not quite today’s.

Fauna is rarer than reasons to look for it. Once in an afternoon you might see a pale thing the length of a finger lift from a gutter and move against the current, not swimming so much as testing the temperature with its whole body, then settling again under a flake of scale. In the high galleries of the empty blocks there are sometimes dry clicking sounds that are not water. Something small lives in the expansion joints, something that likes the heat that comes up through the masonry at night (night being a dimming of the already-dim, a thickening of the vapor, not a true dark). You will not see it if you try. The only animal impression that stays with you is negative: the knowledge, in your spine, that a place this large and this wet should have birds, insects, a stir of wings in the steam, and does not. The quiet is partly that missing stir.

The quiet is the other architecture. Sound dies in the wet air as if the air were padded. A dropped bolt two streets over arrives as a suggestion. Your own footsteps in the shallow street-rivers are a series of soft dislocations, quickly erased. When the big pumps stroke, the thud comes through the soles first. Between strokes there is a long, high, almost inaudible hiss that might be the basin breathing or might be the blood in your head. Voices, if there were voices, would not carry. There are almost none. Once, from a stack across a whole district, a valve lets go for half a minute and the city acquires a single bright note, a steam-scream suddenly musical in all that muffling, and then the note folds back into the fabric of drip and you can believe you imagined the city’s capacity to be loud.

The strangeness is not a single object. It is the way the parameters keep their promises at the same time.

Walk far enough toward the center and the blocks grow larger, the courts wider, the emptiness more formal. There is a plaza that must have been meant for gatherings. It is a sheet of warm water now, ankle-deep, perfectly still except where a bubble walks across it from some vent below the paving. The surrounding façades are civic in the old industrial manner — pilasters, a clock that has stopped at a heat-swollen hour, letters in relief that the drip has softened into almost-words. In the middle of the water, on a plinth, something that was a statue has been recast by mineral. You cannot tell what it honored. The sinter has given it a second, slower anatomy, dripstone garments, a face that is now a cascade. Heat-shimmer makes it move when you stand still. Refraction in the vapor makes the far side of the plaza nearer than the middle. Distances here are a negotiation. You learn not to trust how long a street will take. The city is open — you can see, or almost see, from rim to half-rim on a clearer hour — and yet every crossing is local, a sequence of wet rooms without ceilings.

The weather has cycles even though the lid never lifts. There are hours when the vapor slumps and the upper stories emerge, black and dripping, like piers at low tide. There are hours when it rises from the streets themselves and you walk in a white blindness that tastes of metal and is hot enough to make thought effortful. After those hours the buildings shine as if oiled and every ledge offers a row of perfectly spaced drops, and the drops fall, and the channels take them, and the pumps, somewhere, adjust their stroke by some increment you cannot hear. The basin is an instrument. The city is the fingering.

At the rim, if you climb a viaduct that no longer goes anywhere anyone needs, you can look back and understand the scale as a single thought: a wet, dim, hollow metropolis fitted into a highland like a cast into a mold, breathing, almost uninhabited, still working. Beyond the rim the land falls away into cooler distances you cannot see. The vapor stops being a lid and becomes a wall. You are aware, briefly, of height — of how much air is under this dish of heat — and then the awareness is useless, because the only world that has weight is the one you would have to walk back down into, street-river by street-river, past the empty windows and the sweating brick and the rare pale flicker in a gutter, toward whatever pump is keeping the agreement.

There are rooms, if you look for them, that are not empty in the ordinary way. Not furnished: occupied by process. A chamber of valves the size of people, each weeping at a gland, the floor a grate over moving black water. A hall in which the only furniture is a single running engine on a pad, flywheel taller than a door, so well balanced that its motion is a kind of stillness with a breeze. A basement the temperature of fever, where the city’s original purpose shows its bone: intakes from the deep, a smell of opened earth, heat that does not come from any furnace. You do not stay long. The rule of the place, insofar as a place can have a rule, is that you may pass through the volumes but you may not fill them. The city has room for you the way a lung has room for a breath. It does not have room for you to remain.

Toward what passes for evening the dim goes amber at the edges, a stain in the vapor like rust in water, and the rare lamps that still burn — cages of filament in the pumping houses, a line of bulbs along one working shed — become the only sharp things in the world. Their light does not travel. Each is an island of seeing. Between them the city returns to its true medium: heat, wet stone, the long memory of industry, and a silence so established it has weather of its own. You hear a drip. You hear it again. You understand that this is the sound the place has chosen, out of all possible sounds, to keep.
