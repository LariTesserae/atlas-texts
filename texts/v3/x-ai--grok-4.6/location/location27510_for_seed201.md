The bowl is smaller than the light makes it seem. You come over the last knife of ridge and the world drops inward only a few hundred paces, a nearly circular amphitheater of pale stone still bright as a fresh break, and the sun—unfiltered, almost vertical—hits every face at once. There is no shade that is not a sliver. The rock throws the light back as a hard, mineral glare; rime and thin snow throw it back again. You squint until your eyes water, and the water freezes on your lashes.

This place has not been open long. The walls are unsoftened. You can still read the way the mountain tore: long vertical sheets of feldspar and milky quartz, arêtes like new blades, a floor of angular rubble that has not yet learned to be a slope. No river has had time to invent itself. What moisture there is arrives as weather that barely deserves the name—thin clouds that graze the rim at dawn and leave a skin of rime, a few hours of melt when the sun is highest, seeps that darken the joints of the stone and then vanish. You hear the melt before you see it, if you hear it at all: a ticking, a thread. By late afternoon the threads have gone back to glass.

Along those joints, against all the cold and the thinness of the air, the green is extravagant. Not forest—nothing here has the patience or the soil for height—but a compression of life so dense it looks almost tropical from a few steps off. Cushion plants the size of oxen, packed so tight a boot does not sink. Mosses in stacked velvets, lime to nearly black. In the wettest seams, a fern that should not be at this elevation, its fronds rimed at the edges, unrolling anyway. The green is islanded. Between the islands the floor is only shattered stone and a glitter of frost. The eye, from the rim, reads emptiness: a pale room open to a white sky. Then it adjusts, and the islands declare themselves, and the emptiness becomes a kind of courtesy the stone extends around the living.

Sound behaves as if the bowl were padded. Wind comes over the rim and is instantly thinned, a dry whisper, more a change in the light on the rime than a noise. Melt ticks. Stone clicks as it warms a degree and then remembers the cold. That is nearly all. Even the animals—and there are many—seem to have agreed not to spend breath on calling.

They are everywhere once you stop expecting large things. Pale hares with winter coats that never quite finish changing, moving in slow stitches from cushion to cushion. Flocks of small ground-finches the color of dirty snow, feeding in a silence so complete you only notice them when a whole flock lifts and the air above a green island flickers. Insects in shocking numbers: black midges that hatch from the midday melt and last until the refreeze, nectar-flies working the tiny alpine flowers as if this were a low meadow in June. On the sunward walls, lizards no longer than a finger, dark as wet basalt, pressed flat to drink heat from stone that is still, to the hand, painfully cold. Raptors hang in the updrafts along the inner cliffs and do not cry. You count six, then nine, then lose them against the glare. Something like a fox, long-legged, almost white, works the rim at intervals; you find its neat scat full of bone and seed, and later the animal itself, sitting in the only true shadow the place owns, watching you without urgency. The air between all of this remains mostly air. The bowl is a volume. Life clings to the skin of it.

The light does not relent. There is a brief dimming when a rime-cloud forms—moisture lofted from the seeps, flash-frozen into a glittering fog that pours down one wall and dissolves before it reaches the floor—and then the sun is worse for having been interrupted. Colors bleach. The green goes metallic. If you stay through what ought to be evening, you learn the second strangeness: the bowl holds the day too long. The walls are a reflector. Long after the true sun has slid behind the western knife, a residual blaze walks around the inner faces, a circuit of borrowed noon, and the melt, which should have stopped, ticks for another hour. The plants take it. You can watch a cushion reorient, a fern uncurl a fraction more. The lizards stay out. Then, quite suddenly, the last reflected light dies, and the cold that has been waiting in the stone comes forward all at once. Breath becomes a solid thing. The seeps ring. Stars arrive as if poured.

People have used the place without living in it. On a terrace of rubble against the leeward wall: a ring of uncut stones, knee-high, the gaps stuffed with moss that has taken and gone lush, as if the ring were only another kind of seep. Inside, the ash of very old fires, and newer ones, charcoal still sharp. A cache under a leaning slab—dried meat sewn into hide, a bone needle, a blade of flaked pale quartz from these same walls, a twist of sinew. Cairns on the rim, not trail-markers so much as acknowledgments, each one a single stacked gesture against the tendency of the mountain to shrug things off. Someone has scratched a double spiral into a sun-facing panel, shallow, already filling with lichen the color of rust. That is the whole of the built world: a pause, a few tools that could have been made in an afternoon, the understanding that you do not sleep here if you can help it.

The mountain is still rising. You feel it as a rumor more than a fact—an occasional shiver through the rubble, a thread of rock-dust sifting from a crack that was not there yesterday, a new bright plane of unweathered stone high on the eastern wall, glaring even harder than the rest. The bowl is a wound that has not closed. Everything that lives here lives on the seepage of that wound: the melt of rime, the heat the raw mineral holds for a few hours, the insects that treat the glare as a season. Predators work the clock of the light. When the reflected evening-blaze begins, the finches go still. The fox comes down. The raptors stoop without sound, and the only evidence is a puff of white feathers that the thin wind cannot quite carry over the rim.

Stay long enough and the place trains you to its pace. You learn the hour by which seam is wet. You learn that the lushness is not kindness; it is speed. Everything here is in a hurry that does not look like hurry—plants packing a whole summer into the wet joints of a single bright day, midges spending a life between two freezes, stone cracking another fraction along a fault, the mountain lifting its shoulders another imperceptible amount. The emptiness is real and so is the crowding. You can stand in the center of the bowl and turn slowly and see the entire country of it: rim, seeps, green islands, stone ring, the fox’s path, the hanging birds, the new scar of quartz. It is a room. It is also an unfinished event. The light keeps arriving as if it has somewhere else to be and has forgotten, and the cold, when it comes, is absolute, and in the brief damp between those two facts the whole ecology spends itself, quietly, again.
