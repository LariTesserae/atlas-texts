The plain has been grinding itself flatter for longer than there have been names for time. What remains is a single, low, saturating expanse—neither valley nor basin, just the last honest geometry the world could settle into. You can walk for days and the horizon will not tilt. The ground is wet to the bone and colder than the idea of winter. Water does not fall so much as it is already here: a permanent, near-freezing film that weeps from every mineral face, condenses out of the dim air, and stands in black, motionless sheets between the bodies. There is no rain and no dry. The atmosphere itself is a slow leak.

Nothing green has ever taken. The chemistry will not permit it. What covers the mud is not soil and not stone but a packed, glittering slurry of oxides, frozen brines, and the shed carapaces of things that never quite decided whether they were animals or mechanisms. Density is total. There is no interval. Every square of surface is occupied by something that breathes, ticks, or simply occupies. The air is thick with them. The water is a crowded medium. Even the thin ice skins that form and shatter are colonized before they finish forming.

The fauna is industrial in the way a glacier is industrial: vast, patient, and indifferent to the distinction between organism and process. Herds of low, many-legged hulls—each the size of a collapsed foundry—drag themselves through the slurry on rusted, living treads. Their undersides are forests of siphoning mouths that drink the mineral water and excrete finished, still-hot components that immediately begin to rust and live. Smaller things swarm in their wakes: jointed, eyeless crawlers that unscrew one another for parts, then screw the parts into new configurations that walk away. Flocks of something like birds—thin, resonant, made of laminated ice and wire—pass in screaming sheets that never quite resolve into individuals. Their shadows are heavier than their bodies. When they collide they do not die; they become louder.

Sound is the primary weather. The plain is never quiet. There is the continuous low roar of millions of internal engines that are also metabolisms, the clatter of excreted gears locking into temporary machines that run for an hour and then dissolve back into the wet, the high, metallic keening of the ice-flocks, and underneath everything a subsonic grind—the sound of the planet still trying, after all this time, to become even flatter. Reality does not hold its shape here. Distances lengthen while you watch them. A creature will be fifty meters away and then it will be inside the space you occupy without having crossed the interval. Sounds arrive before their sources, or after, or from directions that do not exist on any compass. Occasionally a patch of the slurry will spontaneously organize into a working assembly line that no one built: raw ore crawling out of the mud, being sorted, heated by nothing, stamped, cooled, and then eaten by the next creature in line. The line runs until the weirdness eddies elsewhere and the whole apparatus slumps back into wet, ticking debris.

Light is a grey-blue dusk that never brightens or fully fails. There is no sun, or the sun has been worn as thin as everything else and now only stains the perpetual mist the color of old steel. Sparks jump between the larger hulls—static, or language, or both. Some of the smaller fauna carry their own faint internal furnaces, visible as dull orange seams in their plating; the light they throw is immediately swallowed. In the deepest channels, where the water is black and almost still, pale chemical glows pulse in time with nothing.

The ecology is closed, ancient, and cannibal. Everything eats the output of everything else. There are no producers in the old sense, only converters. A hull will graze for weeks across a field of smaller converters, ingesting their finished work, then stall and begin to be dismantled by the same smaller things it was eating. The water carries dissolved metals and the ghosts of previous configurations; drink it (you should not) and you taste copper, old electricity, and the memory of motion. Predator and prey are temporary job titles. The same organism will occupy both roles in the space of an hour, depending on which way the local physics is currently leaning.

Nothing here was built and nothing is abandoned. The industrial processes are as native as the cold. They are what the world does when it has been left alone long enough for its laws to forget they were ever laws. The plain is the factory and the factory is the plain. It has no edges you can reach. It has no center. It simply continues, packed and loud and wet and dim, wearing itself smoother by another molecule each century, while the things that live inside its density keep assembling and disassembling the same finite set of parts into new, temporary, screaming shapes. The only rule that still obtains is that there is no unused space, and even that rule frays at the corners if you look too long.
