The chamber is a single held breath beneath the crust, no deeper than a cellar stacked three times over, yet sealed enough that the surface exists only as a dull, even pressure in the stone. It is not a cave that grew. The rock here is new-cut, or new-torn: planes of dark basalt still bright along their fractures, edges that have not yet learned to round. You feel them before you see them. They catch the moisture that never quite becomes rain and hold it in trembling beads, so that every wall sweats without weeping.

Heat fills the volume the way water would fill a cistern. It does not radiate from one vent or one machine; it simply is, a thick, unmoving medium that makes the air itself feel viscous. Breathing is work. Skin films instantly. The temperature sits just below the point of pain and stays there, patient, as if the place has forgotten any other state. In this heat the sparse water behaves like a secret. Condensation gathers on cooler metal and on the newest stone faces, then releases in single, widely spaced drops that take minutes to fall. Where they land they make shallow, warm basins no larger than a man’s reach, dark mirrors filmed with mineral scum. A crack in the vault, high and hairline, sometimes lets a surface shower find its way down as a brief, warmer thread. Then nothing again for a long while.

Life has answered the heat and the damp with a lushness that feels almost indecent in so small and empty a room. It does not carpet the floor. The chamber is the size of a modest church or a village green, and most of it remains bare: slick stone, talus of fresh shards, stretches of powdery grit that the heat has baked into a faint crust. The plants occupy islands. A thicket of arborescent ferns occupies the wettest corner, their trunks buttressed by curtains of aerial roots that drink the air itself. Fronds hang heavy, never fully dry, beaded like the walls. Elsewhere mosses have built cushions deep enough to swallow a boot, releasing a puff of sweet, stewed vapor when compressed. Vines, thick as cable, travel the old conduits and then abandon them, looping through empty space as if remembering a map. The green is too vivid for the dark; it has the waxy, almost metallic sheen of leaves that have learned to hoard every molecule of moisture and every calorie of geothermal waste. There are no flowers. There is no need.

The machines are fewer than the plants and more lonely. A single vertical cylinder, twice a person’s height, stands bolted to a poured plinth now cracked by root and heat. Its skin is pitted iron, still faintly warm to the palm, as though some residual process continues somewhere inside or below. Pipes leave it and travel a little distance before ending in open mouths that exhale a constant, almost invisible steam; the ferns have claimed these orifices and grown into living mufflers. A short run of catwalk hangs from one remaining stanchion, its grating already lacy with rust. On the far wall a bank of gauges survives behind cracked glass, most needles frozen, one still creeping so slowly that only the patient would notice it has moved at all. Wires dangle like shed skins. Once in a great while a contact closes and a single filament somewhere in the dark remembers how to glow: a dull orange the size of a thumbnail that dies before it can name anything. The technology here was industrial, heavy, meant for extraction or circulation on a modest scale, and it has not entirely stopped. A vibration lives in the floor, too low to be sound, the ghost of a pump or a heat-exchanger that no one has told to rest.

Light is a rumor. The hairline crack admits a gray suggestion that might be day or might be the memory of day; it never strengthens. After a long time some of the mosses reveal a faint internal phosphorescence, a green that is less illumination than a quality of the dark itself, visible only when the eyes have given up looking. The rest is absence. Shapes resolve by heat and by the faint gleam of wet stone. Distances are felt rather than seen: the opposite wall is always closer than expected, the ceiling always lost.

Sound has almost left. The drops, when they fall, are distinct as clock-ticks in a stopped house. Metal expands and contracts with sounds like distant furniture shifting in another room. Leaves stir only when a rare thread of cooler air finds the crack and dies. There are no insects, no scurry, no wing. The ecology is plant, mineral, and the slow chemistry of rust. Whatever rules once governed this pocket have simplified to three: heat is constant, moisture is intermittent and precious, and change happens at the speed of seasons that have no names. The sharp stone will stay sharp for years yet. The vines will close another meter of empty floor in a decade. The last gauge will freeze. The place is not dying and not being born; it is simply continuing, a furnace-garden in a room the earth opened and then forgot to close.

Stand long enough and the chamber asserts its own weather, its own grammar. Condensation on the newest fractures sometimes arranges itself in lattices that echo the vanished pipe-runs, as if the stone is learning the old industrial pattern by heart. Roots follow the same routes with unnerving fidelity. The air tastes of hot iron, wet basalt, and a low sweet rot like compost left in an oven. Nothing here is in a hurry. The lushness and the emptiness hold each other in a truce that will last longer than any witness.
