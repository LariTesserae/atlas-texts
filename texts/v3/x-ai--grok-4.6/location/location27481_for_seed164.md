The highland does not recede. It does not grade down into foothills or forget itself in a coastal plain. It is the world that can be walked, a single upthrown slab so wide that weather forms on it the way weather forms on a planet, and so high that the true lowlands—if they exist—are only a rumor of pressure and a different color of cloud far under the rim.

It came up all at once. That is the only story the stone tells. Every scarp is a clean break, every ridgeline a shatter-edge still bright with unweathered crystal. Basalt and pale quartzite stand in plates and knives. Talus looks poured yesterday. There are no rounded summits, no softened saddles. The land has the geometry of an accident that finished and then refused to continue. Frost does not pry it. Rain does not plane it. The same fractures hold the same angles they held when the plateau first locked. Time here is a vertical thing: depth of mist, height of stack, thickness of moss. It is not a direction the rock can travel.

Water is everywhere and never in a hurry. It does not fall so much as occupy. The air is a cold emulsion, just above the point where breath becomes ice, and it condenses on every face as if the stone itself were exhaling. Cliffs weep in sheets that never freeze and never quite become waterfalls; they glaze, they thread, they soak the next ledge and begin again. In the young basins—flat, undrained, still the shape of the last collapse—the water stands in black marshes a hand’s depth over peat, or a body’s depth, or no depth at all, only a shining film that is the water table come up to look at the sky. You walk and the ground answers wetly from a long way down. There are no rivers with mouths. There are only levels: water seeking the next unbroken lip and hanging there, silver, loud.

The sound begins as water and does not stay that. Over a distance like this, with nothing to stop it, a thousand weeping faces become a single unending pour. Add wind over edges that have never been dulled. Add the industrial note, when it arrives. The plateau is never quiet. Even in the emptiest reaches the air is thick with a white noise of wet stone, a pressure in the ears like standing under a weir. It does not crest. It does not lull. It is the same hour of rain, held.

Between the water and the rock there is green, more green than the cold should allow. It is not a forest in the ordinary sense. It is a saturation. Sphagnum builds in the basins until the marshes are walking-soft and spring-fed from below, a lushness you sink through. On every vertical—every fresh wall, every iron trestle, every brick—there are mats of liverwort and ice-fern, fronds like wet glass, mosses so thick they hang in beards and tear under their own water-weight and grow back in the same beard, the same tear. In the long empty kilometers the green is a skin: not trees so much as a continuous cold garden of things that do not flower, do not fruit, do not brown. They persist. They drip. In the hollows they become almost jungle, a drowned understory of stems and pads, and then the plateau opens again and there is only the film of moss on a plain of broken plates, and ten miles of that, and the mist making the next ridge into a rumor.

There is nothing in it that runs or flies or burrows. No insect-tick. No bird-line. The lushness is a plant silence. If something once lived here that could leave a print, the prints were not kept; the peat is unmarked. The green occupies. It does not feed anyone.

You can walk a week and meet no structure. Density here is a cruelty of spacing. The air is huge. The light is ordinary overcast daylight, a high pale evenness without sun-disk, without true night in the usual way—only a slow dimming into the same grey, as if the plateau had a single lamp behind cloud and no switch. In that light the emptiness is not desert. It is interval. Presence is rare enough that when it comes it has the force of a coastline.

Then the towns.

They sit in the breaks and along the older (which is to say, still-young) fault-steps, not crowning the heights so much as bolted into them. Brick the color of dried blood and wet soot. Iron that never rusts past a certain brown, as if oxidation too had reached equilibrium. Streets that are also sluices. Roofs that are also gutters. Every town is a machine for remaining itself. Stacks stand in ranks and breathe a pale continuous exhaust that is the same color as the mist and indistinguishable from it a mile off. You hear them before the walls resolve: a low industrial chord, turbines and trip-hammers and the rhythmic shout of steam let go on a schedule that has no author. The sound does not vary. It has no shift-change. It has no Sunday. It fits into the pour of the rocks the way a second voice fits a drone.

Inside, the infrastructure is layered the way the moss is layered—pipe over pipe, catwalk over millrace, a town-thickness of wet iron. Dynamos hold a note. Looms, if they are looms, throw the same shuttle. Foundry light is the only warmth, and even that is a color more than a heat, a copper stagnant glow in windows that do not open because the air outside is the air inside, only louder. Rails leave each settlement and strike out across the plates, arrow-straight, gleaming with the perpetual wet. They do not branch toward a capital. They complete loops so vast the curve is invisible, or they simply continue until the mist takes them and they are still continuing. Trains run. You can stand at an unattended halt—a shed, a bench, a clock whose face is correct and meaningless—and the train will come on the minute it has always come, empty or loaded with the same pig-iron shapes, and it will leave, and the rails will tick back to cold.

No one is counted. That is part of the strangeness, and part of the emptiness. There are windows and there is laundry that is always the same laundry, wet, hanging, never quite dry. There are canteens with steam on the glass. There are boots by doors. Movement happens the way the hammers happen: as a function. If there are people they are not fauna in the way the parameter forbids; they do not flock or startle or vary. They are the towns’ way of being on. Or the towns have no one, and the laundry is another kind of moss, and the boots are a habit of the threshold. Either reading holds. The plateau does not decide.

What the factories make is less clear than that they make it. Ingots. Cloth the color of the overcast. Components that fit other components in the next town along the loop. Sometimes the output is only noise and water—the mills as organs for keeping the loudness constant, for giving the plateau a heartbeat that will not develop an arrhythmia. Smoke and vapor go up and become the weather they came from. Nothing accumulates. Slag is a hillside that has always been that hillside, sharp as the rest, already mossed, already weeping.

The rules are few and they do not loosen.

The height is not a climate you pass through. It is the only climate. Cold is the medium. Wet is the medium. You do not dry. Metal is slick. Wool is a liability. The water on your lips tastes of stone and a faint oil.

Nothing weathers forward. A broken thing remains the shape of its breaking. A new wall, if one were raised, would look the age of every other wall by morning—not older, not younger, only included. The plateau has a single tense.

There are no animals to mark a season, and there is no season. The ferns do not uncoil; they are uncoiled. The marshes do not freeze hard or thaw; they hold at the edge of ice like a held breath. The light does not migrate. If you stay long enough to feel that as horror, the horror does not peak. It too finds a level.

Sound occupies distance the way water occupies stone. A hammer in a town you cannot see is a fact in your ribs. The pour is a fact. When the wind comes it is the same wind, funneled by the same young canyons, and it does not howl in the sense of a voice discovering itself; it is a standing roar, an organ-pipe as long as a country.

And the strangeness, once you have walked far enough to be lonely for a law that changes, is this: the plateau is not waiting. Young land is usually a promise of ruin, of rounding, of soil. This youth is a finished product. The industry is not a phase. The lushness is not a bloom. The emptiness is not a pause before settlement fills it. The whole assembly—knife-ridges, hanging gardens, eternal towns, the loud wet air, the rails that complete their enormous circles—has the closed quality of a chord that has already been resolved and is only still being heard. You may cross it for a lifetime and the distances will remain distances. You may enter a foundry and the heat will be the same heat the first person felt, if there was a first person. The water will still be coming through the brick.

Far out, where even the loops of rail have not bothered to go, the plateau continues—sharp, dripping, green-black in the seams, loud with itself—until the mind refuses continental scale and starts to think of it as a kind of sky you can stand on. The mist thickens to a sea. Somewhere there is a rim. From the rim, if you reached it, there would be only more of the same weather generating itself, and the drop, and no animals crossing the drop, and the light holding steady on a world that rose once and then, perfectly, stopped.
