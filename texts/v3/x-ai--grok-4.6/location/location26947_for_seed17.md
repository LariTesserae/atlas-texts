The water is first and last. It occupies nearly everything, a single cold body so extensive that “ocean” becomes a local word, like calling a planet a hill. You are in it always. There is no gradual wading, no shoreline that recites itself; the medium simply is, clear as winter glass and only a few degrees above the point where it ought to lock into ice. Pressure does not crush. The height of the place cancels the weight: this sea sits on a newly risen highland, a continental roof that finished lifting only recently, so the water here is high water, thin-aired water, the kind that should have run off or frozen but instead remains, held in some quiet treaty with gravity. Light arrives undiminished, a flat, ordinary daylight that does not blue or fade with depth because depth itself is an unreliable measure. The surface, when it exists, is a pale lid far above or far to the side; more often there is only the even illumination and the knowledge that you could rise for days and still be inside the same liquid.

Between one solid thing and the next lie distances that feel planetary. The water is almost empty. A body can drift through cubic miles that contain nothing but the faint mineral taste of rock that has not yet learned to dissolve and the constant, bone-deep roar that is the true weather of this place. The roar never modulates. It is not storm and not machine alone; it is both and neither, a standing wave of vibration transmitted perfectly through the cold medium, so loud it becomes a second pressure, a second temperature. Engines contribute—great industrial hearts still turning on isolated platforms and cliff-bolted halls—but they are only punctuation. The larger sound is the world itself, the young stone still settling by millimeters that somehow produce thunder, the unchanging currents grinding along knife-edged ridges that refuse to round. Time here has no gradient. The roar was this loud yesterday in the only sense yesterday exists, and it will be this loud when the last heat leaves the last turbine. Equilibrium is not peace; it is a held note that never resolves.

When the emptiness finally breaks, the breaks are sharp. The seafloor, or the mid-water ridges that serve as seafloor, look freshly broken. Planes of dark glass and basalt meet at angles that have not been allowed to weather. Edges remain surgical. There are no beaches of rounded pebbles, no silt plains; sediment has not had time or permission to accumulate. These are the summits and high cols of a mountain system that rose too fast and too high and was immediately drowned, so that what should have been sky-islands became the rare solid interruptions in an otherwise vacant sea. A swimmer or a drifting hull can travel the length of a former nation and meet only three such interruptions: a vertical fin of stone, a hanging cirque now inverted into a hanging garden of nothing, a saddle still so new the fracture surfaces catch the light like mirrors.

On a few of these fins stand the works. They are not cities. They are not even towns. A single pumping house the size of a basilica, its intake pipes disappearing into a cleft that still weeps cold. A lattice of catwalks and pressure vessels bolted to a cliff that has never known rain, only this permanent immersion. A lonely generator hall whose turbines have not been shut down because shutting down is not a verb this place recognizes. The machinery is industrial in the old sense—pistons, dynamos, steam that is not steam but superheated brine forced through turbines by heat that still leaks from the recent orogeny. Some of the engines are clearly extracting something (minerals, charge, the very cold itself). Others appear only to maintain the equilibrium, as if the flood and the height and the silence-that-is-roar require constant mechanical consent. None of them are ruined. Rust exists as a thin, even bloom that does not progress. Bolts remain torqued. The works are as unchanging as the water that surrounds them.

Life occupies the same scattered, abundant logic. There are no forests of kelp, no algal veils; the chemistry or the newness or the cold forbids it. Instead there are animals, or animal-analogues, in numbers that startle after the long emptiness. Pale, elongated swimmers move in curtains that take hours to pass, their bodies faintly metallic, feeding on the electrical fringe of the machines or on the microscopic rock flour that the unweathered stone still sheds. Other forms cling to the sharp ridges in living reefs of chitin and carbonate that look almost architectural until they flex. They do not migrate in the usual sense; their routes are as fixed as the currents, great closed loops that have no beginning and will have no end. They add almost nothing to the sound. Their passage is a change in the quality of the roar, a Doppler that never arrives because nothing here is allowed to approach or recede for long.

The rules are few and absolute. The water will not freeze. The stone will not dull. The engines will not stop. Light will not fail. The distances will not fill. Whatever catastrophe or intention raised this highland and then filled it did so completely, then stepped away, leaving a system that has found its one permitted state and intends to occupy that state until the last proton forgets how to hold. You can swim the empty leagues, trace the knife-edges with a gloved hand, stand on a catwalk while the turbines sing their single note through the soles of your feet, and watch a living cloud eclipse a ridge for half a day. Nothing else will happen. The place has already happened, once, perfectly, and now simply continues.
