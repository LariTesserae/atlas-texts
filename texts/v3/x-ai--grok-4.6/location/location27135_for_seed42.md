The cirque holds its water the way a palm holds a coin: high, tight, complete. Granite walls rise around a basin no larger than a village green, their faces old and pale, streaked with the dark varnish of centuries of seep. There is no shore worth the name. The water comes to the stone and keeps coming, a cold lens pressed into the mountain’s socket, filled to a lip of polished rock where melt arrives in a single falling rope and never quite stops.

Below that rope the world is all one room of water.

It is not deep in the way of oceans. Light still knows the floor. Midday arrives as a green-gold thickness, grainy with silt so fine it behaves like weather. Morning is bluer; evening pulls a slow bruise down the western wall and the whole basin goes the color of old bottle glass. The temperature never argues. It sits just above the point where flesh forgets its own edges—cool enough that every motion is considered, cool enough that the plants grow dense and slow, as if thinking.

The plants are the ground.

They rise from a sediment that is more root than mud, a living thatch packed into every crack of the drowned terraces. What would be kelp in a salt sea is here a freshwater giant: blades the width of doors, stems like the columns of a hall that forgot it was supposed to be stone. They do not fill the basin. They occupy it the way a few large thoughts occupy a quiet mind. Between them the water is open, long sight-lines of empty blue-green, so that from any chamber you can see the next dwelling as a darker vertical, a tower of living tissue swaying half a degree out of true. The city is urban in its agreements—paths, thresholds, shared work, stacked rooms—and sparse in its body. You could count the habitations on two hands and have fingers left. Each is a grown thing: hollowed stems braced with interwoven laterals, floors of matted fiber packed until they take weight, windows that are simply places where the growers stopped asking the plant to close.

No metal. Bone awls, granite flakes, needles of fish-spine, cordage peeled from the inner face of the blades and left to cure in the cold until it sings when drawn taut. The work is old. Growth-rings in the load-bearing stems show decades of the same careful wounding and binding. Ancestors cut shallow stairs into the cirque wall where it shelves underwater; the treads are concave now, furred with a black moss that has the texture of wet felt. The lake level has not changed enough to matter in living memory. A dark mineral band on the granite coincides exactly with the highest roofs. The place is in its own weather, its own finished sentence.

Sound is the waterfall first and last. It enters the lake as a white concussion and becomes, below, a continuous thunder you feel in the teeth and the hinge of the jaw. The plants add their own noise: a vast, slow creaking as currents lean on the towers, a rubbery boom when two mature blades slap. Over that, life. This water is crowded with animals in a way the architecture is not. Schools of pale char turn as one thought in the open lanes. Freshwater shrimp hang in glittering weather under every eave. Newts the length of a forearm walk the fiber floors as if they paid rent. Diving beetles tick against the inner walls. At the margins, where a few crowns break the surface into air, frogs keep a wet iron chorus that carries down the stems like speech in a pipe. Otters—or something that has decided to be otters—use the lowest rooms as thoroughfares and leave fish-heads in neat, insulting piles. The basin is small enough that all of this is one conversation. There is no elsewhere for the noise to go.

People live here. They are not many. They move the way the temperature requires: economy of gesture, long stillnesses, work done in seasons rather than hours. They harvest the inner pith of the giants and dry it in air-bells—pockets the plants are trained to hold, little rooms of breath that ring, faintly, when a current crosses their skin. That ringing is the only clock besides the fall. Children learn to swim before they learn to name the walls. Burial is a matter of weighting a body and walking it to the talus at the deep end, where the light thins and the roots give way to bare stone and the char go still. No one surfaces except to tend the crowns and to watch, once a year, the thin plate of ice that forms in the hardest weeks and then, without drama, lets go.

The unusual thing is small and does not ask to be explained. Certain older stems, if struck in the right place with a knuckle, return a note that is not quite wood and not quite bone. The growers say it is the mountain answering through the plant. They do not decorate this fact. They use it to find fractures, and to call across the open water when the fall is too loud for voices.

Change happens the way the granite weathers: by forgetting to stop. A stem thickens. A stair hollows another hair’s breadth. The waterfall files its notch. In spring the melt thickens and the thunder steps closer; in late autumn the whole basin clarifies until you can see a dropped awl on the living floor from the surface, a dark comma in the gold. The city does not expand. It replaces itself in kind, the way a forest does, one bound joint at a time. To arrive is to understand almost immediately that you are inside a finished argument between water, plant, and height—and that the argument was settled a long time ago, and that the settlement is the city, and that the city is still growing, very slowly, in place.
