The basin occupies a high saddle no wider than a market square, a damp hollow pressed into the unfinished shoulder of the mountain. The ridges that cup it are still deciding their profiles: long, clean fracture planes catch what little light filters through the standing mist, and every few minutes a new plane shears into existence with a report like stone being torn. There is almost no soil. What ground there is consists of fresh, unweathered surfaces—glassy black in one patch, pale and granular in the next—already sheeting with water that seems to well from inside the rock itself.

The water is constant and shallow. It condenses on every face, beads, runs, gathers in temporary pans that last only until the next subtle lift or sag of the terrain, then vanishes into seams that close behind it. The air stays warm, heavier than the altitude should allow, carrying the smell of wet mineral and a faint ozone bite. Mist never quite becomes rain and never quite clears; it simply hangs, turning the already dim light into a low, directionless dusk. What illumination there is comes as much from below as above—an amber seepage along certain fissures, a dull glow inside the larger pools, as though the geology were still radiating the heat of its own assembly.

Plants have barely begun. Isolated cushions of liverwort fluoresce an almost painful green against the gray. A single fern, no taller than a hand, uncurls from a crack that opened yesterday and may seal tomorrow. That is the entire flora.

The animals, by contrast, treat the place as finished enough. Translucent salamanders the length of a forearm haul themselves over the wet stone in slow, purposeful migrations, their skins throwing faint rainbows; the mucus they leave behind smokes where it crosses warmer veins. Insect-analogues move in two modes at once: dense, coordinated rivers that graze the mineral films precipitating and dissolving by the hour, and sudden aerial swarms whose wings produce a continuous tearing-silk hiss. Above them, half-lost in the mist, membrane-and-frame flyers fold and unfold with loud, dry clicks. Their voices—whistles, wet slaps, overlapping clacks—never resolve into speech yet never stop sounding as if they might. Something heavier occupies the deepest, warmest basins; it is known mostly by sudden displacements of water and the brief, ridged silhouette that follows.

Sound is not background here. It is structural. The forming stone groans and then cracks. Water flashes to steam in hidden pockets and hisses out through new vents. Under everything runs an irregular, bone-deep thrum, the audible signature of whatever process is still laying the mountain down. In so small a space the noises fold back on themselves; a salamander’s slap can return from the opposite rim three times, each echo slightly lower in pitch, as if the basin were tasting the sound before releasing it.

Scattered across the raw floor are the only marks that look intentional. A partial arch of matte, unrusting metal stands alone; the missing half is implied by a curvature in the mist, a place where raindrops hesitate. Three low pillars, set in a loose triangle, extrude new material from their crowns at a visible crawl—the fresh layers indistinguishable from the old except for a brief, inner brightness. A shallow dish, half-full of the same water that weeps from the rock, sometimes throws a ghostly, inverted relief a few meters into the air: the basin as it will be after the next adjustment, or as it was before the last one. These objects do not announce themselves. They simply continue the work. Rock appears where the field indicates it should. Water is persuaded out of the air. Fractures open or heal according to a timetable that has nothing to do with ordinary tectonics.

The entire pocket feels walled, though no wall is visible. Step across the rim and the mist thins, the temperature drops, the thrumming fades; the mountain beyond is older, drier, already subject to the usual slow rules. Inside, everything remains provisional. A new terrace is rising now on the far side, water already finding the depression that did not exist at dawn. The salamanders have begun to move toward it in a single gleaming front. The flyers drop lower, their clicking denser. The thrum deepens by a note. The landscape is not eroding into shape. It is still arriving—wet, warm, loud with its own unfinished grammar, and occupied by creatures that appear to have been waiting for precisely this degree of incompletion.
