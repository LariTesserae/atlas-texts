The summit does not end in a point. It ends in a mouth.

You come to it after a long climb through thinning air and the kind of light that has already given up—pale, sideways, never quite arriving. The last few hundred meters of rock are old in the way that only high rock is old: not jagged, not young-broken, but worn into an agreement with wind. Edges have been talked down. Faces have been planed. The mountain has had enough time to decide its own shape and then keep it. What remains is a dark amphitheater no larger than a village square if you measured the floor, cathedral-tall if you measured the drop, the whole of it a single feature, a bowl with a slit of sky.

The slit is how you know there is still a world. A blade of not-quite-day lies across the upper lip and goes no farther. Below that line the stone takes over as the only climate. It is warm. Not the warmth of a lowland afternoon—there is no afternoon here—but a held warmth, the kind a body keeps after work, the kind rock keeps when something below it has never entirely gone cold. Breath does not frost. Sweat, when it comes, does not dry. The air has a weight it should not have at this height, a damp thickness, as if the mountain were exhaling through a cloth.

Water is not a river and not a weather. It is a behavior of surfaces. Condensation beads on every overhang and gathers itself into threads that find the same old paths they have found for longer than anything with a name has been counting. The threads tick. They tick into basins the stone has allowed: shallow dark bowls, lip-smooth, some no wider than a palm, some like baptismal fonts cut by patience alone. A few hold water that has been there long enough to grow a skin of mineral, a faint iridescence you only see if you stoop and wait for your eyes. When cloud snags on the peak—and it does, not often, not as storm, but as a wet thought the mountain briefly entertains—the drip becomes a rain that never quite decides to be rain. It is louder then. Everything here is louder then.

There is almost no green.

What grows does so as a rumor. Ash-colored lichens map the drier shoulders of the bowl in continents too slow to watch. In the wet seams, where drip has kept a promise for centuries, a few pale fronds insist on being ferns. They are the color of things grown under a fingernail. At the lip, just where the last true wind still knows the place, a single woody shrub has been trying to die for a very long time and has not managed it. Its branches are a grammar of failed seasons. Nothing else. The density is not plants.

The density is bodies.

The walls are occupied the way a hive is occupied, the way a drowned forest is occupied by fish: every ledge, every pock, every crack that erosion finished and then left alone has been claimed. You cannot see a clean face of stone larger than a door. What you see instead is layered life, near-touching, stacked, breathing in shifts. Wings fold and unfold in the dark like wet paper. Something with too many joints clicks along a vertical path and disappears into a hole that already has a tenant. The air itself is a traffic. It is not that the animals come and go so much as that they are the weather of the bowl. They occupy volume. They saturate it. If you stand still long enough you will be landed on, not from curiosity, not from hunger exactly, but from the simple overcrowding of a place that has only this much room and has had only this much room since the rock first opened its mouth.

They are loud in the way a market is loud, in the way a river in a gorge is loud: not one sound, a woven one. Clicks and churrs and a dry rustle that is thousands of membranes remembering they are membranes. When a whole terrace launches at once—rarely, slowly, as if the decision had to travel—the noise hits the far wall and comes back as a second flock. Drip keeps time underneath, a percussion section that has never learned another piece. There is a low register too, almost below hearing, the combined heat-voice of too much metabolism in too little dark. It is not a roar. It is the suggestion that a roar is being very carefully shared.

Light refuses the usual work. After a while you stop waiting for your eyes to finish adjusting. They do not. What you get is a grey that has opinions: the wet gleam of a basin, the paler suggestion of a spine or a wing-edge, the slit of sky which is less illumination than a wound that has not closed. Sometimes a body crosses that slit and for an instant you understand scale—how small the opening is, how complete the dark beneath it. There is no fire here, no worked stone, no path that was a path on purpose. If anything with hands ever came this far it left nothing, or the nothing was eaten, or the mountain took it back into the same long weathering that took the edges off the rock. The place is undeveloped in the strict sense: it has developed only itself.

The unusual thing is not theatrical. It is a matter of fit. These animals should not all be able to live here, not at this height, not in this closed heat, not in these numbers, not with so little green to underwrite them. And yet the bowl has been running this argument for so long that the argument has become a climate. Things eat the drip-film. Things eat the things that eat the drip-film. Things eat the fallen, and the fallen are many, and the fallen do not fall far; they are received by ledges already crowded, and what can be used is used before it cools. There is a smell like old coins and wet feathers and a sweetness that might be rot if rot were allowed to finish. It is not allowed to finish. Everything is mid-process. The warmth helps. The dark helps. The slowness helps.

Because the place is slow. Days, if they can be called that, do not so much pass as lean. The colony—if a single word can hold it—moves in gradients, not events. A terrace stirs. A basin overflows by a finger’s width and a new wet map is drawn down the wall, and over a season something will follow that map and nest where the map ends. Young are produced the way stone produces grit: continuously, without announcement. The mountain’s own changes are slower still. A flake of weathered face lets go once in a long while and the sound of it is a scandal; then the scandal is absorbed, the new edge is claimed, the dripping finds the flake and begins to make it familiar. Nothing here is rushing to become something else. The erosion finished its big work ages ago. What remains is maintenance. The fauna is the maintenance. The damp is the maintenance. The dark is the room in which maintenance can be loud without ever being sudden.

If you stay until you can no longer tell your own pulse from the tick of water, the bowl will teach you its one rule, which is not a law so much as a thickness: there is only this much world, and it is all in use, and it has been all in use since the rock learned to hold warmth and the sky learned to pass over without entering. You may leave by the same lip you entered. The shrub will still be failing to die. The slit will still be a slit. Behind you the sound will not diminish so much as become, again, a weather you are no longer inside.
