The sound arrives as a fact before the ground does. It is not weather in the ordinary sense; it is the country’s voice, a single unending rending, rain striking a surface that has had geological ages to become a drum. Every drop is a small percussion on stone so old and so thoroughly planed that it rings. Together they are a roar with a grain in it, a hiss inside a bellow, and under that another sound you feel in the hinge of the jaw: water striking standing water, a flatter, meatier report, millions of them, without interval.

The land is one worn table. Whatever ranges once stood here were spent long ago; their substance is this near-plain, this barely dished expanse the color of wet iron and old enamel. The slope is real but you learn it with the ankles, not the eyes. Elevation is a rumor the body keeps. In every microscopic valley left by the last possible erosion, water beads and crawls. The stone weeps from within as well. Condensation is a second weather, a film that never quite becomes a stream and never quite dries. There is no dry. Clothes become a second skin of cool weight and stay that way. Breath shows. Nothing freezes. The cold is the kind that settles in rather than bites.

Light is a bruise seen through milk. The cloud is not a passing cover; it is the sky’s occupation, a low, slow ceiling the same grey-green as the wet rock. Dawn is a thinning. Noon is the brightest the place will admit, a pewter hour when the standing water takes on a dull interior shine and you can see, for a little while, farther than a few hundred paces. Then the dim thickens again. Night is not dark so much as closer: the roar stays, the visible world contracts to the next pool, the next pale shape.

There is no green. Chlorophyll is a closed chapter, or a chapter that was never opened. What lives on the stone’s face is smear and stain, mineral bloom, a darkness in some of the hairline seams that might be bacterial and might only be the rock remembering water. You will walk a long time looking for a stem, a frond, a moss that commits to being moss. You will not find it. The world here is finished with that experiment. What remains is mineral, and the things that eat mineral, and the things that eat those.

And yet the plain crawls.

Life is abundant in the way gravel is abundant, in the way a shoreline is abundant after a wreck. The smallest are a living grit, thumbnail and smaller, the exact colour of the wet iron they rasp. They travel in sheets. When a sheet turns, the bedrock seems to flow, a sudden satin motion, and the sound of them is a dry seethe under the rain, millions of mouthparts and foot-hooks. They scrape the outer molecular skin of the stone, the leachate the rain keeps offering, the dust of one another. They are the base, if a place this old still has a base.

Above them, and among them, the mid-sized ones: armor that looks designed by someone who had only ever been shown mouths and joints. Low, many-legged, some flattened like limpets that learned to run, some vaulted like overturned bowls. They click. They stridulate until the air has a serrated edge. They open and close plates along their flanks and the plates are musical in a way that has nothing to do with comfort. At the pewter hour they call together, and the sparse distances between the large bodies do not matter; the plain becomes one instrument, a single stretched skin. Speech is a theory you abandon in the first hour. You think in taps, in the orientation of your shoulders, in the direction of the faint slope.

The large ones you mistake for outcrops. They are the size of boats left keel-up, or of the huts, or of both. They move rarely. An eye opens, slow as a change in weather. They feed on the mid-sized and on the pale swimmers that inhabit the sheet-water — frantic commas of flesh, more gape than body, flickering through water that is seldom more than ankle-deep and often less. When a large one shifts, the living grit pours off its back like weather. When it booms, it slaps the standing water with a paddle-limb and the report travels farther than sight. The boom is not communication in any sense you can use; it is a condition, like the rain.

Here and there the water remembers it was once allowed to cut. Old grooves, almost healed, hold a shin-deep commitment, a flow so slow it reads as stillness until a shed carapace turns end over end. Across some of these, someone has piled rock. Weirs. The work is primitive and exact: unshaped plates of the bedrock itself, stacked with a patience that matches the land’s. They catch the mid-swimmers when the sheet-flood comes. Beside them, catch-basins: rings of upright slabs, waist-high, funneling the perpetual seep into a slightly deeper dark. The water in the basins has a different voice, a contained throat-clearing.

The built things are scattered the way the large fauna are scattered — much emptiness between, then a fact. A hut of stacked plates, a collapsed deck of cards with a doorway that faces away from the prevailing roar (there is always a prevailing). Inside: flakes of the same iron stone, edges still sharp because this rock, for all its age, remembers how to part; hooks and scrapers made from the horny shed plates of the crawlers; a tally cut into the inner wall, or a map of slope, or a count of booms, or all three. Ash, sometimes, in a ring of smaller stones. The fire is a temporary disagreement with the wet. It smells of chitin and mineral. Someone lives this way, or lived recently enough that the tools have not yet been taken back into the polish of the plain. You will not meet them often. Density is low. When you do, they are the same colour as the weather, wrapped in the cured skins of the mid-sized ones, and they do not waste gesture. They point with their whole posture at the change in the boom-pattern, or at the taste that arrives in the rain an hour before the flood.

The sheet-flood is the day’s other fact. A thicker pulse comes from the barely-higher interior, a moving lens a handspan deep and miles across. Everything that can clamp, clamps. Everything that can run, runs for the cairns — crude stacks, just high enough. The swimmers become a different animal for an hour, gills flared, bodies aligned to the pull. The living grit lifts in a brown smoke under water and resettles when the lens has passed. The weirs work or they do not; the people (if they are still people, if that word still applies to a craft this reduced) know which basins will hold and which will be erased and rebuilt. Afterward the stone shines as if newly made, which is the opposite of the truth. It is only cleaner. The roar stutters, then resumes its old grain.

There are rules, though no one recites them.

The rain is not always obedient. Sometimes, for a few breaths, a column of it hangs and then climbs, drops reversing in a chimney of silver, and every crawler on the plain freezes and then turns to face the same unseeable point. The sound does not stop; it changes key, a hollow in the middle of the bellow. No one offers a cause. Afterward the small ones resume their rasping as if a gear had slipped and then caught.

In certain low places the stone is warm. Not hot — the world’s last interior heat, pooling where it can no longer afford mountains. The abundant life thickens there. The pitch of the stridulation rises. The people site their more permanent stacks near these bruises of warmth, and sleep against the rock, and do not explain the comfort.

Culture leaks both ways, or the land is suggestible. The mid-sized ones, in the pewter hour, will sometimes arrange themselves in rings that match the catch-basins, a living weir that holds nothing. The tallies inside the huts have cousins scratched on isolated slabs miles from any doorway, the same marks, the same count, as if the stone were practicing. Tools left overnight in a warm seep are sometimes a fraction larger in the morning, or a fraction more themselves, edges clarified. No one relies on this. No one is surprised.

You can walk for days. The same iron table, the same rain, the same distant pale geometries that resolve into hut or beast or neither. The horizon is a wet thread. Scale here is regional in the way a headache is regional: it occupies more than the immediate and will not be left behind by walking. Emptiness is the other inhabitant. Between the weirs, between the boat-sized backs, between the rare doorways, there is a great deal of polished nothing, loudly wet. The sparseness does not quiet the place; it gives the roar room to be complete.

This is the world’s last face, the shape it will keep. Nothing will ever be sharper than this sheen. The future is more of the same polishing, the fauna the last motion the planet can still afford, the stacked plates a grammar spoken very slowly by hands that have accepted the sentence. At the dimmest hour the booms come farther apart, like a heart deciding whether to continue. They do continue. The rain does not consult them. The stone weeps, and weeps, and the living grit turns the bedrock into a kind of weather, and somewhere a flake of iron is lifted, used, and set down again, a small bright disagreement that the polish will, in time, forgive.
