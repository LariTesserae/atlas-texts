The highland did not have time to learn roundness. It came up in plates and blades, a young lift of pale stone still showing the grain of whatever pressed it skyward, edges unblunted, gullies cut as if with a knife last century and left to glitter. From any ridge you can see the next three, and the next after that: a mid-country of repeating scarps and hanging valleys, open enough that weather has room to arrive and leave, dense enough that the valleys themselves are almost filled.

What fills them is not forest in the ordinary sense. It is saturation. Mosses stacked in slabs a person could sink to the thigh in. Ferns in storeys. Liverworts and film-thin succulents and things that are mostly surface, glossy, overlapping until there is no interval left between leaf and leaf. They do not reach for height so much as for occupancy. Every face of rock, every fresh slide of scree, every pipe and lintel has been claimed. The green is cool to the touch and always slightly wet. Water here is not a river’s argument; it is a habit of surfaces. At dusk and just after dawn the air surrenders what it has been carrying, and the whole highland beads. Condensation runs in sheets down the undersides of fronds, collects in the cups the plants have learned to make, threads into hairline rills that vanish into the moss before they can become streams. Rain comes, but as a guest, infrequent, almost apologetic. The lushness lives on the in-between: dew, mist that never quite becomes cloud, the breath of stone that is still exhaling from its own uplift.

The light is the other weather. At this elevation the sky is a hard, continuous brightness, not hot—never hot—but so clean it seems to have weight. Shadows are short and precise. Every droplet acts as a lens. By midmorning the valleys flare; you walk with your eyes narrowed not against glare from above so much as against the answering glitter from below, from every wetted cuticle and every plane of new rock. There is a strangeness in it that is not quite supernatural and not quite explainable: distances compress. A far chimney looks as near as a fern at your boot. Sound, when there is any, arrives late or not at all.

There is almost no sound. The wind, when it bothers, is combed to nothing by the density of the greenery. No birds. No insects worth counting. Once in a long while something small moves in the moss—a suggestion of a body, a displacement—and then the green closes and you are not sure you saw it. The fauna of this place is a rumor the place tells itself and then forgets. In that hush the industrial pieces become more themselves.

They are scattered the way erratics are scattered: a brick pump-house half-swallowed on a terrace, a rank of condensers along a fault, a single tall stack that has not smoked in anyone’s living memory and yet is warm if you set your palm to the brick. Pipes run for miles, thin, riveted, disappearing into cushions of vegetation and emerging again, beaded like everything else. Some still carry a slow charge; you feel it more than hear it, a patience in the metal. The machinery here was built for scaled work—extraction, perhaps, of the oils the plants store against dry weeks, or of the minerals the young rock has not yet learned to hide, or of the water itself, wrung from air that would otherwise only decorate leaves. Whatever the purpose, the tempo is geological. A piston advances the width of a hand in a season. A belt creeps. Valves open on schedules measured in years. Electrified, yes; mechanized, yes; loud, never. The silence is part of the design, or has become the design. Moss grows into the gear-teeth and is cut, patiently, by the next increment of motion, and grows again.

People left marks and then thinned themselves to the same scarcity as the animals. A hut of corrugated metal on a saddle, its roof a garden. A tramway trestle spanning a young gorge, the cables still taut, the cars gone or parked forever in a siding of ferns. Initials in a lintel already being overwritten by lichen that prefers geometry. The built world here is a set of occasional sentences in a language of engines, unfinished, not ruined so much as paused.

The strangeness sits in the joints between these things. The plants do not merely cover the machinery; they seem to anticipate it, thickening where a pipe will warm, thinning where a shadow will fall in a decade’s time, as if growth were a kind of forecast. The rock itself is still arriving. Fresh hairline cracks appear across a summer, pale as paper cuts, and by autumn they are lined with the first film of green. You can stand on a scarp and feel, or imagine you feel, the residual lift, a pressure too slow to be a tremor. Time in the highland is a thick medium. Cycles complete themselves without witnesses. The dew forms, the light bears down, the distant stack holds its heat, a single dark leaf turns once in a place where no animal passed.

Walk long enough and the repeating valleys begin to feel like one valley thinking itself over and over, each iteration a little sharper, a little greener, a little more thoroughly occupied. The air tastes of wet stone and something faintly metallic, as if the industrial and the botanical have agreed on a chemistry. Your own footsteps are the loudest event for miles, and even they are eaten quickly by moss. Behind you the glitter closes. Ahead, another bright scarp, another dense filling of the space between stones, another quiet house of engines doing work no one is late for.
