The cirque is old enough that its name has worn off the maps, if it ever had one. A hanging bowl, not large: from the lip you can see the whole of it, a white amphitheatre perhaps two hundred paces across, the back wall rising in one long, grooved sweep of pale granite that looks as if a giant thumb had been dragged down wet clay and then left to freeze for ten thousand years. Everything here has already happened once. The U of the valley is finished. The erratics have found their seats. The melt-channels in the bedrock are so deep and so smoothly fluted they might have been carved on purpose and then abandoned to weather. The place is in its own state. It does not argue with gravity anymore. It only weeps.

The weeping is constant. Not rain — the sky is a hard, enamel brightness without cloud, a light so complete it has no direction, only intensity. Snow on the rim and the hanging glaciers above throw it back until there is almost no shade, only grades of dazzle. That light, and a warmth that lives inside the mountain and nowhere in the air, keep a skin of water moving over everything. The granite sweats. Rime grows on the lee of every stone in feathery beards and then sloughs in silent sheets. Thin ice forms on puddles, milky and flexed, and shatters under a goat’s hoof or a child’s step and is already knitting again before the fragments have finished sliding. The tarn at the bowl’s lowest point is never fully liquid and never fully ice: a black, breathing lens, its surface crawling with plates that collide, override, drown. Steam lifts from the seeps along the back wall in threads that do not rise so much as wander, torn apart by the thin air before they can become mist. The air itself is wet. You feel it condensing on your lashes and in the wool of your collar, a cold film that never quite freezes and never quite dries.

There is almost no green. What lives rooted here has given up height and color. Black liverworts varnish the wettest faces of stone. In one terrace where a seep is faintly warm, a grass the color of old brass grows no taller than a finger-joint, each blade edged with rime at dawn — though dawn is a word that does not work here. A few dwarf willows, more root than branch, crouch in cracks like clenched hands. That is the flora. The rest is mineral, ice, and hide.

And yet the bowl is full.

The settlement occupies the cirque the way a hive occupies a hollow: not on the land so much as as the land’s next layer. Dry-stone rooms stacked three and four high, fitted without mortar, the joints packed with moss and the greasy felt the inhabitants beat from their animals’ undercoats. The oldest courses are the color of the mountain and have begun to grow back into it; lichen and a slow calcite weep have welded them. Newer rooms sit on older roofs. Ladders are notched logs, polished to a dark shine by palms and hooves. Covered ways duck under flying buttresses of stacked slate. There are no streets wide enough for two laden people to pass without turning sideways. What plazas exist are the size of rooms, irregular, their floors paved with the same weeping granite, always wet, always a little treacherous. Cisterns catch the wall’s runoff. A communal ice-house, more cave than building, holds what they cannot eat today. Everything is reused. A lintel is a glacial cobble. A threshold is a millstone from a valley that no longer has a mill. The whole hamlet could be walked in four minutes if you knew the turns and if nothing were coming the other way. Something is always coming the other way.

The density is not only stone. Life here is a saturation. Pale goats — smaller than valley goats, with translucent ears and hooves like frosted glass — pick their way along wall-tops and sleep in the lee of chimneys, so many that the roofs seem flocked. White foxes the size of large cats pour through the alley-cracks in silent ribbons at the hours when the light shifts from one kind of glare to another. The tarn and every standing film of water seethe with shrimp the color of skimmed milk; children scoop them with bone ladles and do not bother to cook them. Ravens, huge and almost soundless, occupy the highest stacks like a second architecture. There are moths whose wings are plates of frost; they lift in clouds when a door-hide is pulled aside and settle again on the nearest wet stone, where they melt and are replaced. In the deepest, warmest seeps live things the inhabitants do not name, colonial, slow, like breathing lichen with a pulse. You cannot put a hand on a wall without touching fur, or feather, or the cold suction of a shrimp that has climbed too high. The people themselves — short, layered in hides, faces darkened not by sun but by the bounce of light off ice — move through this the way blood moves through a crowded organ. They do not so much live in the hamlet as complete its packing.

All of it happens in a hush that feels like a rule. The air is too thin to carry much. Snow and rime eat what sound there is. The goats do not bleat. The foxes do not yap. The ravens’ wings make a noise like someone turning a page in another room. Water, which should tick and slap and roar, here only sighs: the collapse of an ice-curtain is a long exhalation, a glitter, a slump, and then the bright hole of its absence already filming over. Voices stay in the chest. Tools are stone and bone and the occasional beaten scrap of native copper; they click, they do not ring. The loudest recurring sound is the small, regular one of someone restacking a course of stones that the seep has undermined since the last time someone restacked it.

Because the bowl is finished, and also because it will not stay still. The light never really changes and so there is no night to rest the ice, only a ceaseless, silent weather of phase. Spikes grow in the shade of a lintel and are gone by the time you return with a bucket. A wall slumps. A channel that has run in the same groove for centuries suddenly leaps its bank and cuts a new one overnight, then forgets. The inhabitants repair without comment, the way one breathes. Children are set to catching shrimp and to packing moss into joints. Old people sit in the lee-plazas and flake scrapers, their breath a private weather. Animals reconfigure the traffic: a river of goats at one hour, a tide of foxes at another, the moths lifting and settling like a thought the place keeps having. Steam-devils walk the tarn. Ice-plates collide and override in patterns that look like writing if you are tired enough. The hamlet is a bazaar in which nothing is sold and everything is being moved. It has the energy of a storm and the manners of a library.

What is strange is not any one creature or custom but the agreement among them. The light has no source you can point to without also pointing at the snow, the ice, the wet stone, your own hands. Time of day is a habit imported from below and soon dropped. Water climbs as often as it falls: films creep up the inner faces of cisterns, beads travel the undersides of lintels and drop upward into the rime-beards, as if the mountain were inhaling. The oldest rooms, down against bedrock, have tool-marks no one living can match with their scrapers; the fitting is too fine, the curves too patient. The present people maintain. They do not claim to have built. They speak of the bowl the way one speaks of a relative who has always been old. The fauna use the same doors they do. A fox will wait, politely, for a woman with a hide-bucket to pass, and she will wait for the fox. The shrimp are in the drinking water and are the drinking water’s point. At the rim, if you look long enough into the glare, the hanging glaciers seem to breathe. Perhaps they do. Perhaps the whole high place is one long, established shudder, a storm that found its shape millennia ago and has been faithfully performing it ever since, too bright to see clearly, too quiet to interrupt, too full to enter without becoming another body in the press.
