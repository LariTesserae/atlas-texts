The plateau does not announce itself as a summit. You come up through a long, heat-softened approach of switchbacks cut into old ignimbrite, and then the land simply levels into more land, higher than it has any right to be, already warm underfoot before the sun has fully cleared the far rim. The air is thin enough to taste of iron and far enough from the sea that rain is a rumor with a schedule no one trusts. What moisture there is arrives as condensation on metal at first light, as a film on the inner walls of vents, as a brief vertical weather that darkens the stone for twenty minutes and is gone.

What occupies the height is not emptiness. The plateau is packed. Basalt columns, hoodoos, collapsed dikes, and the upright remnants of extraction stand so close that walking is a matter of choosing seams. Wind and a long, even weathering have sanded the distinction between the two kinds of upright thing. A smokestack forty meters high has the same fluting as the neighboring organ-pipe lava; a boiler housing has grown a rind of sinter the color of old bone; pipe runs disappear into the rock and reappear as if the plateau had simply decided to continue them. The industry is not running. It is also not quite dead. Geothermal pressure still finds the old valves. At irregular intervals a stack exhales a white breath that lasts the length of a held thought and then folds back into the heat. The sound of this is small. Almost everything here is small in the ear. The air is too thin to carry much, and what lives here has little use for announcing itself.

Light is ordinary and therefore severe. At this elevation the sun does not so much shine as arrive. Shadows are hard-edged and move like clock-hands across a floor of packed stone. Midday makes the air into a lens: a distant ridge will hang a handspan above itself, waver, and settle. The heat is not the dry knife of a low desert. It comes from below as much as above. The ground holds the night’s work of the vents and gives it back through the soles. Shade is cooler only by comparison; sit against a column and you will feel the day’s stored work in the rock, a slow pulse, as if the plateau were a single exhausted animal.

Vegetation keeps to the mistakes. A pale succulent occupies the lee of a rusted I-beam, its leaves the color of poured slag. In a few wind-shadows there are stands of a wiry grass that silver and then vanish when you look for them again. Once in a while a plant that is more mineral than leaf occupies a vent-mouth, growing in the wet breath, crusted with the same sinter as the pipes. Nothing here makes a canopy. Nothing here asks for a second story of green.

Water is a local event. There are basins no wider than a room where mineral crusts hold a skin of liquid the color of weak tea, shuddering when a vent nearby speaks. After the rare rain the packed stone becomes a brief river system: sheets, braids, sudden waterfalls off the lips of old slag terraces, all of it gone into cracks and throats before the heat can take a second interest. At dawn the metal sweats. People who used to work this place—whoever they were, however long ago the last shift ended—left cisterns under collapsed roofs, and some of those still catch the night’s thin gift. The cisterns are the only built things that still have a job. The rest is scatter: a stair to nowhere, a gear the size of a cartwheel half-swallowed by flowstone, a row of workers’ huts reduced to rectangles of paler dust. The huts are not ruins so much as the plateau’s way of remembering a geometry that was not its own.

Fauna fills every interval the stone leaves. This is how the density completes itself. Rock-lizards stacked in the flutes of columns, so many that a passing shadow makes a whole face of the hoodoo seem to slough and resettle. Insects with wings like mica, feeding on the vent-edge plants, their flight a dry glitter rather than a sound. Flocks of pale, almost silent birds nest in the upper courses of the dead stacks; they drop at dusk in a single motion, like a handful of ash thrown down, and feed on what the heat has stunned. Something larger moves in the deeper seams—you see the heat-shimmer of a flank, the wet print of a pad in the mineral mud, a scatter of bones arranged with the neatness of habit. Predator and prey keep the same hours as the vents: pulse, wait, pulse. The quiet is not emptiness. It is the sound of a great many lives conducting themselves without remainder.

The plateau has been this way long enough to have forgotten the difference between accident and intention. Erosion here is not a crisis. It is the local climate of form. Wind has rounded the industrial angles until they agree with the lava. Rain, when it comes, writes the same white mineral script across a girder and a dike. The old engines, where they still turn at all, turn at the pace of geology—one slow revolution while a cloud crosses, a valve lifting because the mountain lifted it. You can walk a full day’s worth of packed stone and feel you have not left the same room, and also that the room has no walls. Ridgelines continue the same crowded upright grammar to the limit of sight. Beyond the western drop the land falls away so hard the air below looks like another country, cooler, greener, irrelevant.

Toward late afternoon the heat does a last piece of unusual work. Sound, already scarce, thins further; you hear your own blood and the tick of cooling metal and, once, the precise click of a lizard’s eyelid. The birds return to the stacks without calling. A vent opens on the far side of a hoodoo-field and the steam stands for a long time, unmoving, like a column that has not yet decided to be stone. If you stay until the light goes ordinary and then ordinary-dark, the ground keeps radiating, a low red-black, and the packed bodies of the place—rock, rust, hide, chitin—adjust themselves for the shorter heat of night. Nothing here is waiting to become something else. The plateau has already arrived at the temperature and the density it can keep.
