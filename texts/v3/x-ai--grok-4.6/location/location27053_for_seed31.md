The plateau has not finished arriving. It stands high enough that the old lowlands are a rumor of haze far below the last escarpment, yet the stone itself still weeps as if it remembers being buried. Water issues from every fresh joint and bedding plane—not in torrents, but in a steady, almost soundless exudation. Sheets of it glide over unweathered planes of pale granite and darker basalt, so thin they look like heat-shimmer until they collect in the shallow, perfectly level basins that occupy every depression. The basins do not overflow; the water simply stands, clear as air, reflecting the even daylight. Where the rock is more fractured, the seepage becomes a slow vertical weeping that never quite becomes a fall. Droplets detach, hang, and are replaced so regularly that the cliff faces appear glazed, permanently wet, the color of the stone deepened by a film that never dries.

The air is warm and holds the moisture close. It does not rain; it condenses. A fine, unmoving mist occupies the middle distances, thickening only enough to silver the edges of things. Breathing it is like breathing the inside of a greenhouse that has never known night. The warmth is constant, without the pulse of day or season. Nothing here cools or heats; the equilibrium is complete.

Vegetation occupies the wet ground in isolated, almost ceremonial patches. Between the basins and along the barely perceptible drainage lines grow dense, low cushions of moss so saturated they are more water than plant, their surfaces a living chartreuse that never browns. Taller ferns rise from these cushions in single, perfect fronds, each unfurled to the same degree, each the same height as its neighbor. They do not crowd. Twenty paces of bare, glistening stone or shallow water separate one colony from the next. The effect is of a garden laid out by someone who understood both lushness and emptiness and refused to choose. No roots crack the young rock; the plants seem to rest upon it, as if they too have only recently decided to be here.

The settlement is scattered across this same open measure. It is urban in the sense that it is organized and extensive—enough dwellings, store-houses, and connecting ways to house a town—yet it never clusters. Each structure stands in its own interval of wet stone, linked to the others by raised paths of fitted slabs that stay just above the standing water. The buildings are of the same unweathered rock that forms the plateau, cut into blocks whose edges are still knife-sharp. There is no mortar, only the precise stacking of weight, the simplest corbelling over doorways, lintels that have never sagged. Openings are rectangles or low triangles. Roofs are either living moss or further slabs laid almost flat, so that water sheets across them and falls in the same slow, silent curtains that issue from the cliffs. No metal is visible. Tools, if any remain, would be stone or wood left in the same unchanging damp. The whole town looks recently assembled and also impossibly old, because nothing has been added and nothing has been taken away.

Sound is almost absent. The water makes no chatter; it has no drop high enough, no channel narrow enough. The mist absorbs what little there might be. One hears, at most, the faintest continuous whisper of seepage, like breath held just behind the ear, and the occasional, perfectly regular tap of a single droplet finding a hollow. There are no animals. No insects skate the basins, no birds cross the open air, no worms turn the saturated soil beneath the moss. The ecology is plant, water, and stone only, held in a closed loop that neither grows nor decays.

Light is ordinary daylight, yet it has no direction strong enough to cast a moving shadow. It arrives evenly, as if the sky itself were a single pale source. Distances remain readable: one can see the next ridge of the still-rising land, the next cluster of dwellings half a mile off, the far escarpment where the plateau simply stops and the weeping becomes a hanging curtain that never quite reaches the older world below. The view is regional, not intimate; the place extends, feature after repeated feature, without climax or close.

Nothing here is in the process of becoming something else. The water table does not fall. The moss does not spread. The stone does not soften. The town neither expands nor crumbles. Time has been admitted only as a constant, not as a current. The landscape is wet, high, newly made, and already finished. It simply continues, open and quiet, for as far as the even light will let the eye travel.
