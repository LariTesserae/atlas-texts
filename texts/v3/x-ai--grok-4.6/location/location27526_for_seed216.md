The first thing that is not empty is the heat.

It does not come from a sun. Whatever light reaches this place has already been used by something larger, wrung out, and passed down as a brown-gold dusk that never quite becomes day and never quite finishes becoming night. The warmth is metabolic. It rises from the walls, the decks, the impossible beams — not as a blast, but as the even, wet heat of a body that has been running for a geological age. You feel it in the soft of your throat. You feel it in the way mist does not know whether to fall or to hang.

There is no stone.

That is worth saying again, because the mind keeps trying to put stone underfoot. There is no bedrock, no scree, no mineral horizon. Every surface that can be stood on, leaned against, or sheltered beneath was grown, and then grown again over the growing, until the original intention — if there was one — has been buried under centuries of cambium and decision. The world is timber in the oldest sense: not lumber, but the living fact of wood continuing. Bark here is architecture. Heartwood is load-bearing. Where a human city would have poured a foundation, something once trained a root-mass the size of a watershed to knot itself into a plate, and then left it to lignify.

And yet the volume is almost nothing.

This is the contradiction the place refuses to resolve. The plant is total — it is floor, vault, weather-skin, aqueduct, and grave — but it is not a jungle in the ordinary meaning. It is a hollow. Galleries open into galleries that open into weather. Between the great members there are distances that develop their own clouds. You can stand on a living buttress and look out into a dim gulf so wide that the far wall is only a darker band of dusk, ribbed with pale veins, weeping. The structure fills every role that matter is supposed to fill, and still most of what exists here is air.

The air is thick with unfallen water. Not rain, exactly — rain would require a sky that agrees to be a sky. Condensation beads on every hanging organ, every aerial root as thick as a road, every curtain of leaf that has forgotten it was ever meant to be a leaf and has become a hall. The water travels more than it falls. It sheets along the underside of decks. It follows the old vessels of the wood, which are also corridors, which are also mistakes that became useful. In the low seams, where one grown plate overlaps another and the overlap has rotted into honesty, there are marshes. They are not large. They are frequent. Black water stands in living basins lined with moss so dense it has a nap, like cloth. Things move in that water without making a sound you can keep.

Sound is the other emptiness.

A place this wet, this inhabited, this structurally enormous ought to roar. It does not. The architecture is cork and sponge and a million years of soft tissue teaching itself to swallow vibration. Drips are absorbed. Wingbeats arrive as a change in the warmth on your face. When wind forms — and it does form; the hollows are large enough to invent weather — it does not howl. It shoulders. It moves the hanging gardens the way a sleeper moves a blanket. You hear, if you wait long enough, a creak so low it is almost a pressure in the knees: the structure adjusting, as it has always adjusted, to its own weight.

The structure has been adjusting for so long that erosion and growth have become the same process. Nothing here is new in the way a building is new. Edges are rounded. Galleries that were once square have slumped into ovals. Where water has run for a thousand seasons the wood has gone to a dark, sealed gloss, hard as any riverstone, still faintly warm. Galls the size of hills have been inhabited, abandoned, inhabited again; their interiors are stratified with old nests, old bindings, old soot from fires that were never allowed to become large. The whole vast thing is in its own weather of making and unmaking. It is not ruins. Ruins imply a finished object that failed. This is a finished object that refused to stop being an organism.

Who made it is the wrong question, and also the only one the smaller inhabitants still ask.

There are inhabitants. They are not the builders, or they do not remember being the builders. Their tools are edge and fiber: knives of fired shell and split bone, awls, grafting-cords of their own hair and of a vine that cinches as it dries. They prune. They brace. They walk the seep-lines with gourds and encourage water to go where the old chants say water should go. They do not cut the great members. To cut a great member is not forbidden so much as unthinkable, like deciding to drain a sea with a cup. What they make, they make from the shed — from bark-fall, from the annual slough of certain vault-skins, from the pale, light timber of plants that live as guests in the joints of the host. Their houses are pockets in the moss, stitched and propped, always damp, always smelling of tannin and sleep.

They live as if the world were a garden that forgot them.

Above and beside and somehow through their small economies, the fauna is abundant in the way stars are abundant: countless, and usually elsewhere. In the marsh-seams the water is crowded — larval cities, soft walkers with too many quiet feet, filter-ribbons that pulse and drink the brown light. In the mid-hollows, things fly that have leaves for tails and a patience that is not animal. Herds cross the upper decks at distances that make them into weather: a darkening, a faint thunder you feel in the wood rather than hear, a glitter of wet flanks. Predators exist. You know them by the sudden extra quiet, the way a hanging garden stops dripping for a few breaths. They do not announce. Nothing here announces.

The dimness is not uniform. There are places where the living walls thin to a membrane and the brown-gold thickens toward amber, and you can almost believe in an outside. There are other places — older, deeper in the weave, or higher; direction has begun to fail you — where the light is only the suggestion of light, a memory stored in pale fungi and in the milky sap that beads from fresh wounds. Eyes adjust. Then they adjust again. Color becomes a rumor: the red of a fruiting body, the brief green of a new graft taking, the colorless shine of water.

Warmth, height, and wetness should not sit together this comfortably. They do. The structure holds a climate the way a body holds a fever it has decided is normal. You are high. You feel it in the slight wrongness of your breath, in the way clouds form *below* certain decks and wash the undersides of roots like tide. You are also in a greenhouse the size of a country, and the greenhouse is the country, and the country does not end when you think it should. Walk a month along one beam and the beam will still be the same beam, only now it has weather systems of its own, and a dialect of moss, and a story the inhabitants tell about a person who tried to find the beam’s end and instead found a room that was the inside of a seed.

That is where the place stops agreeing to be a place.

The grown geometry is older than the rules you brought with you. Corridors fork into more space than the wall should contain. A staircase of living ribs ascends, and after a time the concept of ascent becomes decorative; you are still climbing and the water is now running the wrong way along the same grain, and the heat has a direction that is not up. Distances lie with a kind of courtesy, as if embarrassed. Two galleries that have never met will share a puddle. You look into the puddle and see the ceiling of a hall you will not reach for years, or saw yesterday. Graft-scars are doors if you do not insist they are scars. The inhabitants navigate by smell, by the grain under the hand, by songs that are maps of moisture rather than of length. A drawn map is considered a kind of boast, and also a kind of blasphemy. Paper pretends the world holds still.

Sometimes the structure dreams it is smaller, and a chamber will close around you with the slow intimacy of a fist, all moss and exhaled warmth, until you are in a space the size of a mouth — and then it remembers its scale, and the walls recede, and you are on the lip of a gulf again, and the same drip is falling into a marsh you could farm for a lifetime.

None of this feels like an event. The breakdown is the climate. It is as established as the rounded edges, as the metabolic heat, as the hush. The world has been impossible long enough to weather into a kind of sense. You stop asking the hollow how it can be a hollow and also a fullness. You put your palm on the wood. It is damp. It is working. Far off, so far that it might be in another weather, something large changes its weight from one root to another, and the entire continent — if that word still means anything — takes a breath you cannot hear.

In the marsh at your feet a line of bubbles rises and does not break. The water is warm. The dusk continues, as it has always continued, being used by the leaves.
