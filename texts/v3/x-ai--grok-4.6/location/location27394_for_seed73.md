The high country does not cool. That is the first wrongness, and it never becomes ordinary.

You come up through a last belt of cloud that is not cloud so much as a standing wall of warm rain, and the world opens into a country that should be ice and is instead a soaked, breathing heat. The plateau does not so much sit on the mountains as ride them: a chain of basins and hanging benches, some wide as a day’s walk, some pinched between black shoulders of stone, all of them tilted just enough that water is always deciding which way to go and never quite finishing the thought. The drop beyond the rims is extreme. Distances below resolve into a pale, unusable haze. Hawks from the middle slopes do not come this far. The air itself feels thicker up here, as if the atmosphere had been folded once and left to lie.

Everything weeps.

The cliffs sweat in sheets. The sweating is not metaphor. Pore-stone and old lava hold water the way lungs hold breath; heat drives it out in slow, continuous films that never quite become falls and never quite stop. Where the films meet ledges they thicken into warm runnels. Where the runnels find cracks they vanish and reappear a hundred meters on as sudden bright seams. The sound of this is everywhere and never single: a hissing, a ticking, a low intramural pouring, as if the range were a cistern with a hairline fracture the size of a nation. After true rain—which comes often, warm and heavy, more a change of intensity than of kind—the whole plateau goes temporarily deafening. Water hits broad leaves the size of doors. Water finds the hollow stems of the cane-groves and plays them. Water hits the old metal.

There is old metal.

Not much. You can walk half a morning and see only stone and steam and the piled green. Then a rib shows in a ridgeline, too straight, the color of tarnished mercury, and you realize the ridge has been wearing a spine for longer than the forest has been rehearsing how to bury it. The spines are not buildings. They are more like decisions left in the ground: needles and shallow rings and, once, in the long basin they call the Kettle, a tripod of openwork that stands in a hot marsh and does not rust. Insects treat it as a tree. Vines treat it as a cliff. At dusk the tripod gives off a note just below hearing, more a pressure in the teeth, and the marsh-lights—whatever they are, not fireflies, too slow, too willing to hold still—align themselves along its members as if remembering a diagram. No one maintains these things. Maintenance, here, is a weather. The plateau has been in its own state for a duration that has worn the technology into geology. The engineering, whatever it once did, still does a version of itself. The heat does not bleed off the heights. The water does not quite escape. Something in the stone keeps the country tropical at an altitude that ought to kill tropics. Whether that was the point or the side effect is a question the place no longer answers.

The green is lush the way a fever is lush. It is not delicate. Tree-ferns shoulder one another until their fronds interleave into a second, dripping sky. Between them stand the sweatwoods, pale-barked, always faintly damp, their roots knuckled over the rock in architectures that look considered. Epiphytes do not so much cling as queue. There are plants that drink from the air and plants that drink from the stone’s weeping and plants that send pale cords down into the warm peat and do not come back up as the same organism. In the wetter benches the forest thins into marsh, but the marsh is not a thinning so much as a change of strategy: hummocks of moss you could lose a calf in, black water that is hot to the wrist, stands of reed that rattle even when the wind is only the convection off the vents. The vents are modest. They do not erupt. They exhale. White breath stands over certain hollows all day and all night, smelling of minerals and a sweetness that is probably organic and probably unwise to name.

Light is ordinary and therefore slightly insulting. The sun is a sun. It crosses. It makes shadows that behave. Midday on the open benches is a glare off wet leaf and wet stone, a brightness you squint through, nothing holy and nothing wrong except the heat, which is wrong, which sits on the skin like another skin. In the understory the light arrives already used, green and patient. Evenings last. The high air holds the day’s warmth the way cloth holds smoke. Night is only a darker heat, full of louder animals.

The animals are many and they do not hurry.

This is a slow country. Change here is a settlement, not an event. The forests thicken on clocks that outlast maps. The marshes migrate a body’s width in a generation, eating a path, abandoning a path. The fauna has learned this tempo and made it flesh. There are grazers the color of wet slate, high at the shoulder, that move in groups of three or four through the cane with a deliberation so complete it registers as silence until you hear the sucking of their feet in the peat. They wear gardens. Mosses and small flowering things take on their flanks and withers; some of the older animals are indistinct at the edges, more copse than beast. They do not mind. Birds—if they are birds: hot-blooded, loud, iridescent in ways that suggest interference more than pigment—keep to the canopy and argue. The arguments are structural, hours long, phrases passed back and forth across a whole basin until the basin itself seems to be the one speaking. In the weeps and the black pools there are amphibians that never fully choose a skin. They hang in the films of water on the cliffs, translucent, pulse visible, and when they drop they do not fall so much as join a thicker water. Insects are a weather of their own. Some are ordinary in the way a hammer is ordinary. Some have wings that leave a brief afterimage, a second pair just out of true, as if the air were laminated. At certain hours the cane-groves fill with a metallic din that is not quite stridulation. It comes in slow waves. It has a key.

People have been here. The marks are faint and they do not accumulate. A stair cut into a sweating face of rock, the treads dished by weather more than feet, leading from a bench to a higher bench and then simply ending in a shelf of orchids. A low wall around a spring, the stones fitted without mortar, warm to the touch because everything is warm to the touch. Once, a house—or the idea of a house—three walls of some glassy pour that has taken on the color of the ferns behind it, roof long gone, floor a tray of rain and seedlings. Tools turn up in the peat when the marshes shift: not blades, not quite. Objects that fit the hand and do not explain themselves, edges that are sharp in a direction you cannot point to. The living who pass through—herders of the slate-grazers, gatherers of the sweet vent-lichen, the occasional surveyor who still believes a map can be finished—sleep in the lee of the spines or under the sweatwoods and do not stay. Staying would mean agreeing to the country’s clock. The country is not cruel about this. It is only very old, and hot, and wet, and finished with haste.

From a rim at the western drop you can see the shape of the whole mid-country: basin, ridge, basin, the Kettle’s pale tripod like a standing equation, steam in columns that do not wander, forest in layered blacks and violently living greens. The scale is regional, not infinite. You could, in a hard week, walk the habitable chain end to end. You would not be done knowing it. The knowing is one of the patient processes. Water finds the same ledges it found last century and the century before. Roots take a grip that will outlast a language. The spines hum their one remaining instruction into the stone. Something large and unseen moves in the deeper cane, not hunting, not fleeing, only going on with a life that has already accepted the heat, the height, the endless mild roaring of water that cannot decide whether it is falling or rising or simply remaining, against all sense, at the top of the world.
