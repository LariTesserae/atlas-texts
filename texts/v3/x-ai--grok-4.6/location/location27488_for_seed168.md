The high country does not begin with a climb so much as with a refusal. At some unmarked height the last suggestion of moisture simply ends, as if a line had been drawn through the air, and beyond it the world is kiln-dry and warm and unfinished. The warmth is not the heat of a desert noon. It is the even, residual heat of stone that was never allowed to cool all the way, a held breath in the rock, constant as a thermostat no one set.

What should be mountains are only the first intentions of mountains. Ridges lift a few hundred meters and stop, their crests still squared, their flanks unscarred by talus. Valleys exist as shallow troughs with no rivers to justify them and no alluvium to prove they were ever cut. The stone has the look of something poured and then abandoned mid-pour: flow-banding frozen in mid-ripple, columnar joints that never quite finished their hexagons, vesicles like gas-bubbles that never escaped. Erosion has barely introduced itself. There are no rounded shoulders, no weathered skins, no polite softening. The geology is embryonic and has been embryonic for as long as the light has fallen this way. Time passed. Form did not.

Between the unfinished uplifts the plateau opens—not a single surface but a succession of pale mineral flats, each one slightly different in hue, none of them soil. Feldspar grit. Crushed anorthosite. A salt that is not salt, a fine white flour of sulfates that never dissolved because nothing ever came to dissolve them. You can walk an hour and leave almost no track. The scale is the scale of a region: grove, flat, scarp, another grove, a farther scarp the color of cooled iron, and then the air going on, clear and unused, until the next range of half-made highlands hangs in the distance like a sentence no one completed.

There is almost no water. Not hidden water, not fossil water, not dew. The air has the dryness of a sealed archive. What lives here does not drink.

The vegetation arrives as islands. From a distance each grove is a dark, saturated mass on the pale ground, lush in the way a greenhouse is lush—too much leaf, too much surface, a density of green and copper and bruise-purple that seems to argue with the emptiness around it. Close to, the argument becomes stranger. The plants have the architecture of a wet forest and the materials of a foundry. Leaves are thick, succulent in outline, but they are not succulent; they have the cool, slightly greasy give of polymer, or of metal that has learned to be flexible. Veins catch the light like solder. Some fronds are the exact verdigris of copper left in weather that never came. Others are a waxy black that drinks the ordinary daylight and returns almost nothing. They stand in crowded, overlapping tiers, understory and midstory and a broken canopy, as if they remembered rainforests from a world that had weather. Nothing drips. Nothing wilts. Nothing has ever needed to.

The groves are internally lavish and spatially stingy. Between them the flats run empty for kilometers. Density here is a matter of intervals: almost nothing per volume, then a sudden excess, then almost nothing again. Walking from one stand to the next is like crossing a factory floor after the shift has ended and only the potted specimens remain, except there is no floor and no shift and the specimens planted themselves.

They did not plant themselves recently. Nothing here is recent. The groves are in equilibrium so complete it reads as stillness. No seedling advances on the mineral. No elder collapses. Flowers—if that is the word for the heat-radiating structures at some of the terminals, pale and ceramic, faintly warm to the unused air—stay open. The whole vegetable mass has the quality of a process that ran until it found a number it liked and then stopped, permanently, without shutting down. Lush and arrested. A jungle in stasis.

There are no animals. Not a bird-shape against the ordinary sky, not an insect tick in the leaf-litter that is not litter so much as a slow shed of metallic scale. The silence is not the silence of snow. It is the silence of a shop after the power has been cut but the metal is still warm. Occasionally, at long intervals, the stone itself makes a sound: a single deep tick of contraction, or a faint, almost sub-audible settling, as if a boiler the size of a ridge were cooling through one more degree it will never actually lose. Then the quiet closes again. Wind is a rumor that does not apply. The warmth does not travel as breeze. It simply is, an even field, the temperature of skin that has been working.

Light is normal and therefore slightly wrong for the place. A clear, high sun, or whatever this world uses for a sun, throws honest shadows, hard-edged on the pale grit, mottled and green-gold inside the groves. There is no special tint, no alien spectrum to excuse the rest. The strangeness has to live in the forms themselves, under ordinary illumination, which makes it harder to dismiss. You can see too well. You can see the way some of the larger trunks have the radial symmetry of extruded stock. You can see vesicles in the raw scarps that look like bolt-holes no one drilled. You can see, on certain flats, a faint regular dimpling, a grid too patient to be dunes, as if the ground had once been the bed of something that stamped and then forgot why.

Nothing here was built. There are no walls, no roads, no tailings piles that admit to being tailings, no rusted anything that was ever a machine. And yet the place has the character of industry in the way a caldera has the character of fire. Heat as a working condition. Metal as a native speech. Process implied in every surface—refinement, precipitation, the slow concentration of ores into the very bodies of the plants, which seem less to grow than to plate themselves outward, layer on layer, an electrochemistry mistaken for botany. If there were ever engines they were geological. If there were ever factories they were the act of the crust coming into being and then, for reasons the land does not disclose, declining to finish. The tech is in the grain of the world, not on it.

Stand long enough in one of the groves and the lushness starts to feel like insulation, a living cladding on a mechanism that no longer cycles. The air among the leaves is a degree warmer still, and carries a taste like hot pennies and ozone, faint enough to doubt. The canopy does not stir. Light comes through in the old tropical coins and remains where it falls. You could believe, for a while, that you had stepped into the interior of a single stalled thought: warmth, green-dark, the idea of production, the fact of drought, a forest that does not know it is a desert and a desert that does not know it has forests.

Farther on, the flats change color by slow degrees—cream to bone to a dull nickel—and the next unfinished range presents its unused cliffs. Same embryonic joints. Same absence of scree. Same isolated dark islands of leaf. The region does not develop as you cross it. It reiterates, with small variations, the same argument between emptiness and excess, between the just-made and the never-changing. High, warm, dry, quiet, open to the horizon and hollow in the intervals. No one has ever put a structure on it. Nothing with a pulse has ever crossed it. The plants, if they are plants, keep their lush counsel and do not spread. The stone keeps the shape of a world that was interrupted in the act of becoming, and has found, in that interruption, a kind of peace.

Evening, if evening comes, will be the same temperature. The shadows will only lengthen. The groves will hold their color a little longer than they should, the way slag holds heat, and then the ordinary light will go, and the silence will be the same silence, and the high country will continue not happening, beautifully, at scale.
