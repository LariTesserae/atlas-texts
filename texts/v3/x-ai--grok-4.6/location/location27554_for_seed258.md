The high basin holds itself open like a bowl that was never fired.

You come to it from below, through a long throat of living wall, and the first thing that is not visual is the sound: a single, unvarying roar, not of one waterfall but of a thousand thin ones, all the same height, all the same volume, as if the air itself had been tuned and then left. The roar does not swell. It does not recede. It is the same at dawn as it is at the hour when the light goes pewter. You stop hearing it as water after a while and start hearing it as a held note the place is singing to keep itself from finishing.

The floor of the basin is not soil. It is a grown pavement of interlocked cambium, cork, and flattened root, warm underfoot, slightly giving, always damp. Water stands in it the way water stands in a sponge that has been cut into shallow terraces and left to remember the knife. The pools are rarely more than shin-deep. They do not drain. They do not deepen. They simply occupy the depressions that were grown into the living floor and stay there, skinning over with a faint iridescence that might be pollen or might be the pavement sweating. Between the pools the pavement is mottled—old leaf-scar, old graft-line, pale ridges where two growths met and decided to become one surface. There is a great deal of it. The basin is valley-sized; you can see the far rim as a dark lip of the same material, hours of walking away, and the space between is mostly this wet, warm, almost empty plane.

What occupies the plane is sparse and enormous.

At irregular intervals, far enough apart that each one has weather of its own, stand the structures. They are not trees and they are not buildings. They are both and neither: single organisms (or colonies so fused they have forgotten they were many) that were directed, once, into architecture and then stopped. A tower of braided trunk the color of old tea, hollow, its interior a spiral of living galleries that never received floors. A vaulted nave of interlaced boughs that arched toward one another and ceased a man’s height short of meeting, so that a permanent slit of sky runs the length of the nave and rain—when it is not already raining—falls through it in a straight, unwavering curtain. A stepped ziggurat of succulent plates, each plate a leaf thickened into a terrace, each terrace holding a still pool that weeps over its lip into the terrace below, the whole thing dripping with the same patient volume as everything else. A colonnade of unopened buds the size of cottages, their outer bracts the texture of wet leather, forever on the verge of splitting. They do not split.

The growth has the look of something interrupted in the act of becoming stone. Bark is thickening toward a mineral dullness it will never reach. Vascular bundles run in the walls like veins of unprecipitated ore. Here and there a surface has begun to calcify—white crusts, weeping lime—but the lime is still soft, still being secreted, still embryonic. The geology of the place is being assembled out of plant and then held at the instant before it hardens. Nothing erodes because nothing has been given time to become erodible. The basin is under construction in a tense that has no future.

Water is everywhere that is not the roar. It condenses on the undersides of the great unopened buds and drops in slow, fat cadences. It wells from graft-scars. It runs in channels that are not cut but grown—grooved petioles the width of streets, their inner surfaces ciliated, carrying a sheet of warm water that never quite becomes a stream and never quite stops. The air is thick with it. At this elevation the warmth should have burned the moisture off; instead the moisture hangs, a second, heavier sky. You walk in a permanent post-rain, skin filmed, clothes never dry, the taste of green iron at the back of the throat. The light is ordinary daylight made slightly milky by the suspended water, so that shadows are soft and distances look nearer than they are. You can see three, four of the great structures at once, standing in their own weather, and the wet shining pavement between them going on and on.

There is almost no animal. Once in a long while a thing like a damselfly, long as a forearm, hangs in the roar and then is gone. Its wings do not beat the air so much as remember beating. Tracks appear in the soft cambium after a night and are gone by the next, filled by the pavement’s slow, impartial swelling. Whatever made them is not seen. The place does not seem to need them. Pollination, if it still occurs, is a rumor the structures tell themselves.

The strangeness is not an ornament. It is the rule the basin is keeping. The structures were grown without tool or hand that you can find—no cut, no lashing that is not itself a living graft, no device. The intelligence that aimed them (if intelligence is the word) is gone or has become the growth. What remains is a made world with no maker left in it, a total construction that is also a total organism, held in an equilibrium so complete it looks like the moment before the first moment. The buds will not open. The arches will not close. The lime will not become stone. The water will not find a sea. The note will not resolve.

You can walk for a day and the sound will be the same at the end as at the beginning. The temperature will not drop. The light will slide, but the quality of the light—this normal, humid, slightly too-even illumination—will not change its mind. The far rim will still be the far rim. The pavement will still give underfoot, warm, wet, unfinished. If you shout, the roar takes the shout and returns nothing. If you sleep against one of the great trunks, you feel it neither growing nor decaying, only continuing, a held breath the size of a cathedral.

This is the basin as it is and as it has been and as it will remain: a high, warm, weeping country of living rooms that were never entered, of a floor that is also a marsh that is also a single patient body, of architecture that stopped in the act of being born and found that stopping was a kind of permanence. The emptiness between the structures is not absence. It is the space the place requires in order to keep singing the same note, forever on the verge of a form it has already decided not to become.
