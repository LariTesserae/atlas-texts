The place is a hanging bowl no wider than a nave, cut into the cold side of a high ridge and left there long enough that the rock has forgotten the violence of its making. You come on it by following the wet, not the path—there is no path—until the slope simply stops being a slope and becomes a lip, and the lip gives onto a concavity the size of a single held breath of landscape. Beyond the lip the mountain continues; you cannot see it. The bowl occupies the whole of what can be known.

It is always the same hour. Not night, not quite: a darkness with weight, the kind that sits in a room after the last lamp is taken away and the walls still remember a little grey. Cloud does not pass through this hollow so much as inhabit it. The air is a cool broth, continuously condensing on every upright thing, so that stone, stem, fur, and wing all weep the same thin water. There is no rain and no dry. Moisture arrives as a decision the atmosphere made once and has not revisited.

The rock is old limestone gone soft at the edges, its bedding planes opened into shelves and throats. Every surface has been used. Water has found the same routes for so long that the routes are polished, not carved—slick dark paths no wider than a finger, ending in beads that hang and fall and hang again in the same places. The falls are inaudible unless you put your ear almost to the stone. Then you hear a ticking so regular it might be counted, except that counting would change nothing. The pool at the lowest point is no larger than a table and never rises, never sinks. What arrives leaves by seams you cannot see. The water is the temperature of a mouth that has been closed a long time.

Vegetation is not forest and not meadow. It is occupancy. Liverworts plate the rock in overlapping coins of olive and black. Mosses have the thickness of pelts; they hold so much water that a hand pressed in comes away cold and heavy, as if you had reached into an animal. Ferns grow in the vertical, their fronds so densely ranked that there is no gap large enough for light to remember the ground. Where a little pale comes through the cloud it is used immediately: a gleam on a wet midrib, a brief silver on the underside of a cap of fungus, then gone. Nothing here is trying to grow taller than the bowl. Height would be a kind of leaving. Everything is lateral, packed, finished.

The animals are the rest of the volume. There is no empty air. Midges hang in a stillness that is not rest. Moths cover the inner curve of the lip so completely that the rock’s colour is moth, and when one shifts another is already in its place. Salamanders occupy every seep: dark, wet-skinned, stacked along the weeping joints like a second masonry. Their sides move. That is the only motion you can be sure of. In the moss, the bodies of worms and beetles and small nameless things make a slow internal weather; the pelt ticks once, twice, as something rearranges itself into the identical arrangement. Bats are present as a density among the hanging ferns, not as flight. You become aware of them as extra warmth, extra breath, a velvet congestion in the already congested dark. Nothing calls. The quiet is not absence of life but life without surplus. Sound would be a gradient, and there are no gradients.

A person standing in the bowl—if a person could stand without displacing what has already claimed the standing-room—would feel the temperature first: cool that does not bite, cool that has been cool since the last ice left this height and has no intention of becoming anything else. Then the wet, which is everywhere including the lungs. Then the press of other bodies that are not yours. The scale is intimate and complete. You could throw a stone across and hit the far wall, except there is no room to throw and the wall is not a wall so much as a packed vertical field. The place has a boundary as clear as a room’s. Outside it, the ridge wind exists, and distance, and the ordinary succession of weather. Inside, succession has ended.

There are no structures. No stacked stones, no cut wood, no mark that was made on purpose. Once, perhaps, something primitive happened at the lip: a flake of chert the colour of the cloud, half-buried in liverwort, edges dulled by the same water that dulls everything. It might be a tool and it might be a coincidence the rock offered. No one has come back for it. The bowl does not record visits. It has the equilibrium of a process that finished and then, out of some courtesy of physics, continued to look like a process. Drip. Breath. The minute rearrangement that restores the previous minute.

What is unusual is only this: you cannot tell whether the lives here are many or one life using many surfaces. The salamanders do not leave the seeps. The moths do not lift into any larger dark. Birth and death, if they occur, occur as substitutions so exact that the packedness never thins and never spills. Time is present as duration without event. Stay long enough—though “long” has no local meaning—and you will begin to suspect that the bowl is not preserving a moment but refusing the idea that moments differ. The mountain around it goes on wearing itself into other shapes. This one shape has already been worn to its last useful form and has stopped.

If you leave, you leave by the lip you entered, dripping, your clothes heavier, your hearing readjusted to a world that still bothers to make weather. Behind you the ticking of water on the same stones continues, inaudible again at ten paces, and the dark holds its quantity of bodies without remainder.
