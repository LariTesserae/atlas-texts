The place is a shallow dish, hardly a valley, more a settling of the land that never quite decided to be a hole. You can feel the rim when you arrive: a low concrete lip, rounded by weather until it reads as bone, and beyond it the true marsh begins, though you cannot see the marsh from here. The air is too thick with water. The dish holds its own weather.

Nothing here is dry. This is not a landscape that received rain; it is a landscape that *is* rain, delayed, undecided, still arriving. Water stands in sheets no deeper than a calf, then deepens without warning into brick channels, then thins again over platforms of old slag. It weeps from every upright surface. The stone sweats. The metal sweats. The air itself condenses on your face as if you had walked into someone’s breath and the someone had been breathing for a century. When you stand still, droplets bead along your lashes and fall inward.

The light is the color of wet nickel. There is a sky, technically — a low, felted gray that never quite becomes cloud and never quite becomes clear — but most of what you see by is bounce-light off water, a dim circulating gleam that makes depths hard to judge. Edges soften. Distances collapse. You are never more than a dozen paces from something you could put a hand on, and often you are closer than that without meaning to be.

There are no plants. Not a stem, not a moss worth the name, not the greening you expect at water’s edge. The palette is iron, bone, soot, and the pale of old lime. What grows is simpler than growth usually allows. Orange floc hangs in the stiller pools like torn wool, lifting and settling with currents too slight to see. Black films skin the puddles and wrinkle when a drop hits them. White crusts bloom along every evaporation line and are dissolved again before they can become anything architectural. On the weeping faces of brick and cut stone, mineral fans have built themselves in slow overlapping plates — ochre, rust, a gray that looks like ash until you touch it and it is slick, almost fatty. These are the forests here. They do not move when the air moves. They thicken by the season and slough by the season and the water takes what they shed.

The dish is small enough to be a room if rooms were allowed to have weather. A long nave of a room, then, or a courtyard that forgot its building. You could cross it in minutes if the footing were honest. It is not. The floor is layered: silt over tile over older silt, pipe-runs underfoot like buried ribs, sudden drops where a sump still remembers its job. Everything occupies the same volume. Hanging sinter and hanging cable and hanging sheets of condensed water occupy the air. Uprights occupy the rest — posts, pipe-stems, the remaining jambs of a pump-house whose roof is only a suggestion of beams, black and soft. You move as if through a crowd that does not step aside.

The built things are few and they have been here long enough to have opinions. A brick house, half a house, sunk to the sills: one doorway, no door, interior a single chamber of standing water and a ladder that climbs to a vanished floor. A cylindrical tank on its side, mouth open to the dim, becoming a cave; something nested in it once, or still does. A gantry that spans nothing useful now, its rails beaded, its walkway a memory of grating. Three steps of a stair that end in air, then water. Cable drums like sleeping animals, their windings fused into a single mineral mass. All of it stained the same, rounded the same, no longer distinct from the geology so much as a denser clause in it. The erosion here is finished with drama. Nothing is falling. Everything has already fallen into the shape it will keep. Water has found its paths and keeps them. Rust has become a kind of stone. The place is in its long middle — not ruin as event, ruin as climate.

Somewhere a machine still works, or works at working. You do not see it first. You feel a pulse in the standing water, a rise of a finger’s width that arrives and recedes on a cycle longer than breathing and shorter than weather. Later you find the source, or one source: a valve-house no larger than a cupboard, its door gone, inside a wheel that turns so slowly you must watch it against a fixed point to be sure. It is not powered in any way you can name. Pressure from below, perhaps; the land still trying to dewater itself through habits the works taught it. The wheel clicks at the top of each rotation, a sound like a nail tapped once on porcelain, and then there is a long quiet in which the click is remembered more than heard.

Sound stays near the ground. Drip is the grammar. Different pitches from different lips — brick, iron, sinter, the lip of the tank — so that the dish has a chord, unresolved, which changes when the fine rain thickens and changes again when it thins. Water moving inside pipes is a swallow heard through a wall. Once in a while a wing, close, the wet slap of something leaving a girder. Once in a while the valve. Voices would carry strangely here and so nothing uses them. The quiet is not empty; it is occupied, the way a held note is occupied.

The animals are the other density. They are everywhere you look once you have learned how to look, which takes a while because they are the color of the place. Pale amphipods stitch the shallows, so many that a step sends a visible shiver outward, a moving grain. Flatworms ribbon the undersides of every overhang. In the darker channels, longer bodies — eel-shaped, or not, the distinction hard in this light — hang in the water with their heads upstream, mouths working on whatever the floc and the films provide. Filter-colonies beard the mouths of pipes, beige and faintly pulsatile, not quite anemone, not quite rust. They close when a shadow crosses them, even your shadow, even the shadow of a drip.

Higher, on the gantry and the remaining lintels, stand the still ones. Birds if you need the word, though the word is a reach: the size of grebes, the color of wet concrete, one-legged for hours, heads tucked not against cold exactly but against the labor of being visible. They feed in a brief fury at dusk, which here is only a further dimming, stabbing the shallows and the colonies, then returning to stillness as if stillness were the point and feeding an interruption. Between the posts, something crabbed and many-jointed works the silt with a patience that looks like industry. It does not flee. Nothing here flees the way prey flees in open country. There is nowhere to flee to that is not already occupied. Instead there are pauses, redistributions, a closing of gaps.

They eat films and each other and the slow rain of mineral slough. There is no green to underwrite them. The chemistry is enough. The water arrives already worked — cool, metallic, faintly sweet at the back of the throat, with a taste that stays on the teeth. Whatever lives here lives on gradients: iron, sulfur, the last warmth of whatever still turns underground, the protein of their neighbors. It is an old arrangement. You can feel the age of it in the way the paths are worn, not by feet so much as by repetition: the same bodies using the same edges of the same bricks for so long that the bricks have developed a polish, a dark wet sheen like the inside of a shell.

The strangeness is not an event. It is a property of the air, like the wet. Stay long enough — and the place asks you to stay, not hospitably, just by being difficult to leave in a straight line — and the drips begin to phrase. Not melody. Grouping. A run of three from the tank-lip, a rest, two from the pump-house eave, a rest that is too exact. The amphipods still their shivering in the same rests. The concrete birds tuck and untuck as if keeping time. You can tell yourself this is physics, coupled oscillators, water finding a lowest energy, animals cuing on vibration. You can also notice that the pulse from the buried wheel and the phrasing of the drips are not independent, and that when you move, both hesitate, as if the dish were including you in a count.

Reflections in the blacker water do not quite match. An extra upright. A span that is not there when you look up. Once, a figure in the pump-house doorway that is only a curtain of drips until it is not only that, and then is only that again. The light is too poor to prosecute the difference. You stop trying. The place is allowed its extras. They may be how it stores what has left.

Weather here is a change in degree, not in kind. The fine rain thickens until walking is like passing through a beaded curtain that never ends; then it thins until the condensation on your skin is the only precipitation and you cannot say when the rain stopped. Coolth holds. Breath shows if you work. Hands go clumsy, then merely wet, then clumsy again. There is no sun to wait for. Day is a rumor that arrives as a slightly less dim hour and spends itself without warmth.

If there are rules, they are hydrological and old. Water comes from above and from below and from the sides, and the dish does not drain so much as circulate, a slow gut. The remaining machines are not separate from this; they are constrictions in it, habits the water has not unlearned. The animals keep the films from sealing the pools. The films keep the metal from going entirely to stone, or hurry it, depending on the week. Nothing is in charge. Everything is in the way of everything else, and this has been true long enough to be a kind of peace.

At the far rim — far being a word that means *a short walk if you knew the channels* — the concrete lip dips, and the water there is a shade less mineral, a shade more alive with things that have drifted in from the marsh you cannot see. The concrete birds do not hunt that edge. The amphipods thin. It is as if the dish ends not by wall but by preference, a density that will not dilute. You can stand at that lip and look out into a flatter gray and know that green exists somewhere in it, and know also that turning back into the nave of pipes and weeping brick will feel like going indoors, though there is no door, and never was a roof that lasted, and the rain is the same rain.

You turn back. The wheel clicks. The drips take up their count. Something many-jointed occupies the print you left in the silt, already, as if the print had been a resource. The air condenses on you with the patience of a place that has been wet since the works were new and will be wet when the last brick is only a softer stripe in the floor. The scale of it never grows. That is part of the holding. A whole weather, a whole quiet industry of mouths and valves and falling water, fitted into a dish you could shout across if shouting belonged here.

It does not. You go carefully, one hand out, and the place closes its small distances around you as if that were the same thing as welcome.
