The chamber is not old enough to have a proper name. The miners who broke into it last season still call it the soak, or the new room, or simply *down*, as if the rest of the workings were merely a corridor leading here. It sits two hundred feet under a ridge that never knew this cavity existed. The rock overhead is a pale, unfinished limestone, still flaking along the blast-lines, still sweating. Wherever a charge was set, the wall looks chewed rather than cut. Fresh joints glitter with a film of water that has not yet decided whether it is a seep or a stream.

Heat comes up through the floor. Not fire-heat — there is no visible magma — but a patient, wet heat, the kind that lives in deep aquifers and rises when someone opens a door. The air holds it like a clenched fist. You feel it first in the lungs, then in the cloth against your back, then as a continuous film on every metal surface. Pipes sweat. Bolts weep rust in long ochre tears. The thermometer nailed to the pump-house lintel has stuck at a number no one bothers to read anymore.

Water is everywhere and nowhere in quantity. It does not pool so much as occupy. It beads on the ceiling and falls in irregular stitches. It sheets down the raw eastern face in a glaze so thin it looks like polish until you put a hand to it and come away dripping. Under the iron walkways it collects in shallow, steaming trays the workers call pans. The pans are not designed; they are what happens when the floor is not quite level and the rock is still deciding its own drainage. A man can walk the whole chamber in three minutes and never be more than a few yards from the sound of water, and never step in more than an inch of it unless he wants to.

The settlement occupies the chamber the way furniture occupies a room that is slightly too large for it. There are eight structures, if you count the pump-house and the lean-to that holds the dynamo. Brick, most of them, the bricks brought down in slings and still pale where the kiln-smoke hasn’t stained them. They sit on pads of poured slag, not quite square to one another, with wide intervals of bare stone between. No streets. Just the walkways — riveted plates that ring when you cross them — and the gaps, which are the true floor of the place: wet limestone, tool-scored, already beginning to take the first shallow grooves of runoff. At the far end a single chimney, more pipe than architecture, punches up through a bored hole in the roof and is lost. You never see what it vents into. Smoke, when there is smoke, hangs and then is eaten by the moisture.

The machines are few and large. A beam engine in the pump-house lifts water that would otherwise drown the pans by midweek; its walking-beam nods with the slow arrogance of something that will outlast the men who oil it. A smaller engine turns the dynamo. Belt-drives slap. Somewhere a compressor cycles on a long, irregular breath. None of this is loud in the way a surface mill is loud. The chamber swallows and then returns sound as a single compounded pressure — engine, drip, the tick of cooling iron, and under it all a living noise that is not machinery.

The fauna is the reason no one sleeps easily, and the reason the place functions at all.

They are beetles, mostly. A dark, heat-loving species that was already in the limestone when the first round went off, living in the hairline joints, feeding on whatever film the seeps left. The opening of the chamber was, for them, a sudden continent. They cover the wetter walls in moving sheets that look, in the dimness, like the rock itself deciding to walk. They are in the pans, skating the meniscus. They are inside the brickwork. At shift-change they lift in brief, soft storms when a lantern passes, and the air fills with a sound like dry rice poured on tin. No one has counted them. Counting would be a kind of surrender. They do not bite. They do not, as far as anyone has determined, carry anything worse than themselves. They eat the algal smear that tries to grow on the pans, they eat the spilled grain from the store-hut, they eat each other when the grain runs short. Their castings make a fine black silt that the water carries into the lowest corner, where it is already becoming a kind of soil.

There are other things. Pale crickets the length of a thumb, whose song is a continuous high wire behind the engines. A population of small, transparent frogs that appeared in the third month, no one knows from where, and now sit in the warmest seeps with their throats pulsing. Something larger moves in the dark throat of the unfinished adit at the north end — a sound of claws, or of stone settling, no one has gone far enough to see. Bats come down the chimney at dusk-above, though dusk is a rumor here, and hang in a trembling pelt from the highest unshored span. Their droppings hiss faintly when they hit the hot floor.

Vegetation is an afterthought and knows it. In the cracks where mortar has not yet been pointed, a few stems of something fern-like, almost white, uncurl and then burn at the tips from the heat. A lichen the color of old brass maps the cooler western wall in continents no larger than a palm. That is the forest. No one tends it. No one could.

Light is what the dynamo can spare after the pumps. Four cages of incandescent bulbs, their glass already frosted on the inside from the perpetual condensation, hang on long cords and make four overlapping circles of a color that is not quite yellow. Between the circles the chamber is the color of a held breath. Shadows have weight. You see the beetles as texture until they move. You see a man’s face clearly only when he steps under a cage, and then it is glazed, dripping, the eyes a little too wide from the heat.

The work is slow because everything here is slow. The rock is still finding its joints. The water is still cutting its first true channels; in a year the pans will be a creek, and someone will have to decide whether to fight it or pave it. Rust moves at a visible pace on anything ferrous left unpainted. The beetles have generations in a fortnight. The engines, by contrast, are geological: they were designed for a different mine, a drier one, and they have not yet been convinced this is their home. Men move between these tempos. A shift is six hours because a longer one cooks you. In those six hours a man might grease a bearing, clear a screen of beetle silt, carry brick for the ninth structure that has been “going up” for two months, and stand for a long time watching water find a new way down a wall that was dry yesterday.

There are rules, though no one has written them. You do not leave meat unwrapped. You do not sleep on the stone; the heat will take the water out of you and the beetles will find the salt. You do not go into the north adit alone, and you do not go far. You listen to the beam engine the way you would listen to a sleeping animal — a change in its nod is the first warning that the seep is winning. When the dynamo stutters the whole chamber inhales, and in that half-dark the frogs start up as if they had been waiting for permission.

Above, rain is falling on the ridge, or it is not. It makes no difference for days. Then, slowly, the seeps thicken, the pans brim, the engine’s work deepens by a note you feel in your teeth, and the beetles lift in a darker cloud than usual. That is weather, here. That is the only season: a little more water, a little less, the rock sweating harder or holding back, the unfinished walls taking one more grain of themselves into solution and laying it down somewhere else as a skin of calcite no thicker than breath.

The place is still being born. You can see the birth in the unpointed brick, in the adit that stops in a face of raw drill-holes, in the way the walkways do not yet meet, in the chimney that has no hood, in the soil that is only beetle-cast and rock-flour and has never held a root worth the name. In twenty years it may be a town, or a flooded hole, or a room someone sealed and forgot. Tonight it is a hot, dim, loud pocket of organized iron and accidental life, just under the world’s skin, learning how to be a place at all.
