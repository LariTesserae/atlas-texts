The plateau has no edges you can reach in a lifetime of walking. That is the first thing it teaches, and it teaches it without speaking. Whatever mountains once stood here were finished so long ago that even their absence has been worn smooth. What remains is a single continuous high country, continental, then more than continental: a table of old stone lifted into thin cold, so level in its final patience that weather forms on it the way weather forms on a world. Fronts are born out of nothing but distance. They travel for weeks and die of old age before they find a coast.

The stone is the color of dried tea, of bone left in shade, of a bruise that has forgotten why it was made. It does not glitter. It has been ground past glitter. In places the wind has taken centuries to make a shallow dish; in other places a harder seam remains as a long, barely perceptible ridge, like the memory of a spine under skin. You can walk a day and believe the ground is perfectly flat, then look back and see that you have been descending the idea of a mountain.

It is cold the way deep time is cold. Not dramatic. Not a storm’s opinion. A settled fact, older than the names for seasons. Breath hangs and does not quite decide to fall. Fingers stiffen around tools. The light is ordinary and complete — a pale, high, honest daylight that has nothing to hide and nowhere to put a shadow that lasts — and still the cold lives inside the light, as if illumination itself has been stored in a cellar.

Moisture comes as a guest, never as a resident. There are no lakes with names. There are films. There is the damp that lives under stones. There is the rain that arrives already tired, a fine cold stitching that darkens the plateau for an hour and is gone, leaving the rock darker only in its pores. In the long shallow valleys — if a tilt of one degree can be called a valley — mist collects at dusk as if the air were remembering how to be a river. It does not run. It condenses on the lichens and on the inner faces of walls and on the undersides of leaves, and by morning it has become frost so thin it has no weight, only a sound, and the sound is almost nothing.

The quiet is structural. At this height the air is too thin to carry a shout any useful distance. Snow, when it comes, is a dry flour that drinks noise. The cities do not clang. There is no iron industry to speak of, no mills, no wheels that matter. What metal exists is scarce and small: a knife, a needle, a thin saw, treasured, resharpened, passed down until it is more story than edge. Work is stone on stone, wood on wood, fiber twisted against the thigh. The loudest ordinary sound is a mallet on a chisel in a courtyard, and even that arrives at the next street as a rumor.

And yet the place is not still. It is finished, which is not the same as stopped.

The vegetation should not be here. That is one of the agreements the plateau has decided not to keep. Between the cities the land is sparse to the point of honesty: crusts of black and olive lichen, a few prostrate shrubs with leaves like hammered copper, long intervals of bare lithic skin. Then, without warning that any map can use, a stand of growth so thick it has its own interior night. Not a forest in the lowland sense. A grove the size of a district, or a single tree whose habit is civic. The plants are cold-made and water-thrifty and somehow lush anyway — leaves packed, cuticles waxed, undersides silvered to hoard what little radiation and dew the day offers. Mosses run in pelts down the shady sides of everything. In the damper seams, ferns of a kind that have never learned summer unroll with a slowness you can watch if you are willing to ruin an afternoon. The green is dark, almost black-green, a stored summer that does not trust the sky.

There are no animals. This is not a recently emptied country. There are no tracks that are not a person’s. No birds stitch the huge weather. No insects tick in the moss. The lushness has no mouths. Pollination, if it occurs, is a weather of its own: pollen lifting on the thin wind in sheets that catch the normal light and make it briefly gold, then settling on stigmas that have waited with a patience indistinguishable from geology. Seeds go where frost heave and rare rain take them. The ecosystem moves, but it moves like a clock with half its gears removed — processes without appetites. Growth. Freeze. Thaw. Spore. The lichen advances a thumb’s width in a human childhood. Something in the groves respires so slowly that the air among the trunks is a different climate, damper, almost kind, and if you sleep there you wake with your hair beaded and your dreams condensed into a single image you cannot keep.

The cities sit far apart, the way thoughts sit in a tired mind. You can leave one at the start of a cold spring and reach the next as the next year’s frost begins, if the weather of the plateau does not invent a reason to turn you. Each city is stone, stacked and fitted, the blocks taken from the plateau itself so that from a distance a settlement looks like a place where the ground has simply decided to become vertical. No one remembers a first city. The masonry is too competent for a beginning and too humble for an empire. Walls are thick against the cold. Roofs are slab or turf or living pelt-moss. Streets are wide not from grandeur but from the old habit of leaving room for weather to pass through without taking the houses with it.

Inside, the urban is real and limited. Workshops. Cisterns that catch the intermittent rain and the night’s condensation, their inner walls furred with a cold algae that is harvested and dried into cakes. Stairways worn into troughs. Terraces where the lushness has been invited in and then failed to remain merely invited — courtyards drowned in green, walls that have become trellises for a vine that flowers once a decade in a color that is not quite a color, more a temperature. The technology is hands. Adzes. Fire in careful pits. Felted fibers from the copper-leaved shrubs. Ropes. Levers. The knowledge of which stones will split true. People — there are people, few, spaced as the cities are spaced — move through this with the unhurried competence of those whose tools will not improve and whose world will not surprise them in any ordinary way.

The surprises are not ordinary.

Reality here is an old garment. It still holds, mostly, but the seams have been let out and taken in so many times that you can see other weaves underneath. Distance is the first thing that frays. A tower you have been walking toward for three days is suddenly a cairn at your feet, and the three days remain in your legs as a debt. Or the opposite: a doorway across a square takes from noon until the light fails, and when you arrive the square has filled with a mist that is raining upward into a sky that has not changed. The plateau is vast enough to have weather of its own; it is also, in places, vast in a direction that is not on any compass. Some streets in the older quarters loop without turning. You can follow the mason’s marks — they are consistent, even elegant, a language of notches — and find that you have been reading the same mark for an hour, and the mark is slightly different each time, as if the stone were still deciding.

The erosion did not stop at rock. It took edges off events. Causes arrive late, or early, or wearing each other’s coats. A chisel left on a wall will be gone when you turn back, and in its place a small fern will already have the shape of a tool-mark in the arrangement of its fronds. Builders say they do not always know whether they are adding a course of stone or uncovering one that the plateau had put aside. The cities grow the way talus grows: by a logic that includes intention and does not require it. A new house will appear at the margin of a settlement over a winter, fitted, roofed, cold, empty, the mortar as old as the rest. No one claims the work. No one is surprised for long. They sweep it, light a fire, hang moss to dry. Occupation is a kind of weather.

In the groves, the breakdown is greener. Leaves stack in ways that imply a third side to a plane. You put your hand into a thicket and feel wet bark, and also the idea of wet bark, and also a dryness that is the memory of a drought this place has not had in ten thousand years. Some trunks are hollow and contain weather: a private squall no larger than a closet, snowing onto moss that is flowering out of season. The pollen-sheets, on certain still afternoons, do not fall. They hang, and in their hanging you can see the plateau as it was when it still had mountains — a brief, gold, inaccurate history that blows apart if you breathe.

Sound, already scarce, sometimes forgets its order. A mallet-blow from a city you left last month will arrive in a grove as a single soft report, like a fruit dropping, though nothing here fruits in any hungry sense. Conversely, a whole morning of work will vanish into the stone and never have been audible, and the workers will look at their progress as if it had been accomplished by the cold.

At night the light goes in the normal way. The dark is ordinary dark, high and clean, with whatever stars this world is allowed. Frost writes on every horizontal. The cisterns tick as a skin of ice finds itself. In that quiet the active things continue: the lichen’s unthinkable advance, the mist assembling in the one-degree valleys, the slow respiration of the groves, the cities settling another fraction into the only shape they will ever finally have. Sometimes a district will be a little more mineral in the morning, the carvings softer, the doorways more like caves. Sometimes a stair that was stone is sheathed in living green that was not there at last light, lush and beaded and already old.

No one hunts. There is nothing to hunt. No one farms in rows; the damp is too intermittent, the cold too sure. They gather what the groves and the wall-mosses and the cistern-skins offer. They cut blocks. They repair roofs against a wind that has been practicing the same phrase since before speech. They walk the long gaps. Between cities the plateau is so empty that a single figure is a punctuation mark the weather can erase. People leave cairns, then find the cairns rearranged into the ground plan of a room, then into nothing, then into a small grove that should have taken a century.

It is possible, standing on a roof at the edge of a settlement, to understand the whole arrangement as a last draft. The world wore itself down until only this high, cold, nearly level sentence remained. Water was reduced to a dampness that comes and goes. Animals were edited out as a kind of excess motion. What stayed was green where green could insist, and stone arranged into shelter by hands that may or may not still be the original hands, and a quiet in which a great many processes continue because stopping was never the same thing as ending.

Then the understanding slips, which is also native here. The roof is a terrace is a ridge of the old mountain. The city is a stand of rock that learned right angles. The grove is a city that learned leaves. Far out on the plateau a weather system is assembling that will take a month to cross the distance and will arrive as rain, or as a brief incorrect season, or as a day in which all the doors open onto the same courtyard, damp, moss-thick, echoing with a mallet that has not been lifted yet.

You can live a life in one of these places. The cold will teach your habits. The lush courtyards will feed you if you are patient with their calendar. The distances will keep you from believing you own more than a street. And on some ordinary afternoon, in honest light, you will put your palm on a wall and feel it sweating the way living things sweat, and also the way stone sweats in a thaw, and also the way a finished world sweats when it dreams it is not quite done — a thin, intermittent moisture, almost not there, enough.
