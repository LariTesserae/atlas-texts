The place is a hanging basin no larger than a cloister garden, cantilevered from a spine of unfinished superstructure that has no name yet and may never need one. You come to it along a grated catwalk that sweats. Below the grating the world falls away into a pale, unfocused depth—cloud or distance, it is hard to tell—and the air that rises past your ankles is already warm and sweet with chlorophyll and wet concrete.

Everything here was poured, sintered, bolted, or grown on a substrate that itself was poured. There is no native stone. The “cliffs” that ring the basin are retaining walls still wearing the ghost-ridges of their formwork; they weep in vertical sheets because the membranes have not been sealed and because the designers wanted them to weep. Water is the first occupant. It stands in shallow, perfectly circular sumps that have not yet been given overflow lips. It beads on every horizontal. It condenses on the undersides of the leaf-plates and drops with a sound like slow applause. The humidity is so complete that the light itself feels rinsed.

Vegetation occupies the basin the way a thought occupies a mind that has just woken: thoroughly, without apology, still arranging itself. Broad-leaved things the color of wet jade overflow their troughs and root into the cooling fins of silent pumps. Mosses have found the hairline joints between panels and turned them into velvet seams. There are ferns whose fronds are longer than a person is tall; they hang in layered curtains so dense that walking among them is like walking through a series of damp rooms whose walls breathe. Epiphytes have been clipped to the vertical pipes and have already forgotten they were clipped; their aerial roots braid themselves into the cable runs. Nothing here is old. The oldest plant is perhaps a season. Growth is visibly faster than it should be—warmth, water, and whatever is in the circulating nutrient film conspire—so that a stem you brushed this morning has already leaned a new angle by late afternoon.

The machinery is industrial but hushed, as if embarrassed to be present. Pumps the size of steamer trunks sit half-buried in moss, their casings warm to the touch, exhaling a low periodic sigh that you feel in your molars more than hear. A lattice of irrigation booms tracks slowly overhead on greased rails, misting in programmed bursts that look like weather. Somewhere under the floor a generator turns; its vibration is the place’s pulse, so regular you stop noticing it until it hiccups and the water in the nearest sump develops a momentary skin of concentric rings. Cables and conduits are everywhere, yet they have been routed with a kind of courtesy, given channels that the plants are already treating as trellises. The technology is not hidden. It is simply being colonized at the same rate it is being installed.

Because the structure is still coming into being, the geometry is embryonic. Edges have not been dressed. Corners remain sharp enough to catch cloth. Piles of unused sinter-pellet “scree” occupy one quadrant, each pellet the size of a thumbnail, waiting to be vacuum-formed into the next terrace wall; rain (or the irrigation that pretends to be rain) has already begun to sort them by size, the first faint suggestion of alluvial fans that have never known a mountain. Scaffolding still stands in places, its uprights becoming trellises. A single robotic mason, no larger than a goat, works the far rim on quiet hydraulics, laying courses of a living concrete that will later be seeded. It pauses when you approach, as if considering whether you are a change-order.

Light is ordinary daylight, but ordinary daylight at this elevation and in this much vapor has a particular quality: it arrives already scattered, already kind. There are no hard shadows. Everything is lit as if from inside a cloud that has decided to stay. In the late afternoon the western wall of the basin, which is glass-ceramic and still stamped with the manufacturer’s grid, turns the color of weak tea and the whole interior briefly looks underwater.

Sound is mostly the water’s various occupations: drip, sheet, gurgle, the tiny percussive slap of a droplet hitting a leaf the size of a platter. Once in a while a valve opens with a soft pneumatics-kiss. The plants themselves make almost no noise except when a heavy frond, saturated past holding, lets go a stored cupful and the cupful finds a metal surface. You can hear your own clothes wick moisture. Conversation, if anyone spoke, would feel like a violation.

Fauna is nearly absent. A single damselfly, metallic and implausible, patrols the largest sump on a repeating circuit, as if hired. Sometimes a moth the color of wet cardboard rests on a warm pipe and does not startle. That is all. The niche-space is still being negotiated; the plants have arrived first and in overwhelming numbers, and whatever is meant to eat them or pollinate them or hide among them has not yet been scheduled, or has declined the invitation.

The strangeness is quiet and structural. Water here does not always obey the gradient you expect. Capillary channels have been printed into certain walls so that a film climbs a few centimeters against gravity before deciding to fall; the effect is of the basin continually inhaling its own runoff. Some of the larger leaves are slightly warm on their undersides, as if photosynthesizing with a private surplus. The air tastes faintly of ozone and crushed stem, a combination that should not be pleasant and is. Time of day is difficult to keep; the mist and the even light erase the usual cues, and the irrigation cycles have their own circadian suggestion. You begin to measure duration by how far a particular vine has advanced across a conduit since you last looked.

The whole assemblage is active without being busy. Water is lifted, misted, collected, filtered through root-masses, returned. The living concrete continues its slow exothermic cure, raising the local temperature another fraction of a degree. Leaves open and reorient. Condensation forms, gathers, releases. The robotic mason lays another course. Cloud, when it comes, does not so much arrive as thicken out of the air that is already here, wrapping the basin until the catwalk you entered by disappears and the place becomes briefly, perfectly, a room with no exterior. Then the cloud thins again, and the pale gulf reasserts itself below the grating, and the hanging garden—wet, warm, unfinished, densely itself—resumes the work of becoming a landscape that was never found, only made.
