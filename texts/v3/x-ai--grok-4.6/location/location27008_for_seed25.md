The first thing that is not a thought is pressure in the ears.

It arrives before any picture, before the cool, before the wet grit that will later be understood as air. A sound with weight. Not one sound. A thickness of sounds stacked so close they have fused into a single continuous body, a weather of noise that has weather’s indifference and weather’s variations — cells of roar, troughs of clatter, sudden walls of shriek that travel through the rest like squalls. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in the hinges of your jaw. If you open your mouth the sound occupies it.

Light does almost nothing here. What little there is does not fall so much as leak: a greasy, exhausted gray that never decides to be dawn and never finishes becoming night. It lives low, at shin-height and below, caught in films of moisture on stone and on hide. Above that the air is simply dark, a cool dark, a cellar dark, the dark of a place that has been roofed by its own weather for so long the idea of a sun has worn smooth and meaningless. Sometimes a pale smear moves across the upper dark — not a moon, not cloud. A body. Many bodies. The smear has wings or the memory of wings and it is gone into the noise again.

This is a low country. Not a hole exactly, not a canyon. A settling. Long ago — long enough that the verb has become a noun — the ground accepted a concavity and kept it. The basin is regional. You can walk a day and the character does not change so much as rephrase itself: a broader flat, a residual spine of older rock, a cluster of swallow-holes where the floor remembers it was once many floors. Everything is worn to the shape it intends to keep. The stone is not dramatic. It is finished. Scalloped, fluted, pitted with the patient signatures of water that used to be more ambitious than the water that remains. Now the moisture is a habit rather than a river. It beads. It films. It gathers in lenticular pockets along the seams, black and still and no deeper than a palm, and the air drinks from them and puts them back as condensation on every surface that can hold a skin of wet.

There are no plants.

That fact has a texture. The eye, trained on other places, keeps offering green and being refused. What covers the stone is not growth in any photosynthetic sense. It is occupancy. The basin is dense the way a clenched hand is dense. Bodies against bodies against the residue of bodies. If there is a ground, it is intermittent — a boss of polished limestone, a rib of something harder that the traffic has not yet lowered, a slough of grit that was once shell or tooth or the mineralized afterthought of a thousand molted skins. You do not stand on earth. You stand on a negotiation.

They are not one kind.

Closest to the stone, in the film of damp, things like fists work the microscopic pastures that are not grass and not moss: bacterial sleeks, mineral blooms, the sour velvet of chemistries that do not require a sky. These grazers are pale, armored in plates that click when they shoulder one another, which is always. Their mouths are rasps. A hectare of them sounds like sand being poured onto more sand, except the pour never ends, and every grain is alive and hungry in a small way. They do not look up. Looking is not a sense they budget for. When something larger takes one, the gap closes as if a pore of the world had simply decided to seal.

Above that layer the walkers. Four legs, six, a dozen, arrangements that are not interested in the bilateral courtesy of other landscapes. Some are the size of dogs if dogs had been designed by a committee that had only ever heard dogs described. Some are low and wide as tables, their backs used as roads by everything smaller. They are cool-blooded in a cool air and they make up the difference with crowding, with the metabolisms of neighbors, with a communal heat that never quite becomes warmth — only a lessening of chill in the places where flanks press. Their eyes, when they have them, are cloudy coins. Many have none. They navigate by the deafening itself, by the way sound piles and thins, by the wet slap of contact, by smells that in this moisture do not dissipate so much as marinade the dark.

They eat one another with the unembarrassed circularity of a system that has had a very long time to stop pretending it needs an outside. A taker is taken. A grazer is a meal is a grazer again after enough conversions. Nothing here is building a store against winter because this is the climate the place chose and kept. Cool. Damp. Dark. Established. The hunger is not desperate. It is infrastructural.

And the air is full.

This is the part the ears already knew. The flying things do not migrate through. They live in the volume, a second ground, a third. Their density is the reason the darkness has texture. Wings — some membranous and wet-sounding, some stiff as scraped hide, some not wings at all but vanes or sacs that row the thick air — beat in a frequency that has become the basin’s fundamental tone. Under it, the voices. They are not songs. They are placements. Each cry a stake, a coordinate, a warning, a claim, a recognition, a lie. In a country without light, announcement is architecture. The sound is so total that silence, on the rare occasion a pocket of it forms (a predator’s passing, a sudden communal flinch), feels like a physical hole, a dropped stitch, and the hole is instantly reknit by a thousand throats that cannot bear the vacancy.

Some of the fliers never land. Their young are born in the air and learn the basin as a column of noise and collision. Their dead do not fall so much as filter down, becoming the rain the walkers understand, a slow precipitate of meat and cartilage that the damp keeps from dusting. The grazers do not distinguish this rain from the mineral films. Food is a direction, not a category.

There is strangeness that is not spectacle so much as a wrongness of fit, the sense that the rules were written for a different physics and then sanded until they seated here. Certain walkers share a skin. Not metaphorically — a continuous hide roofing two, three, five nervous systems, the legs cooperating with the slightly delayed courtesy of people carrying a table through a door. They argue in twitches. They eat with whichever mouth is nearest the opportunity. Other animals are hollow in ways that seem structural: you can see, when the weak light catches them, that their bodies are galleries, and that smaller fauna live in those galleries the way the whole basin lives in its stone, a density nested inside a density, sound inside sound. A few of the largest shapes are not animals in any patient sense. They are traffic jams that learned how to metabolize. A mound that moves. A dune of shoulders. If you watch long enough (and watching is mostly listening) the dune changes heading by a consensus that has no spokesman.

Water remains a specialty. The pockets — those shallow black lenses in the old runnels — are not still for long. Tongues, sponges, folded membranes, whole faces that are only mouth: the drinking is constant and unceremonious. Condensation is harvested off backs. Some fliers skim the films and leave a momentary script of ripples that the dark erases. In the cooler hours, which are distinguished from the other hours only by a slight increase in the weight of the air, a fine rain of drip falls from the upper bodies onto the lower ones, and for a little while the basin has weather that is only itself raining on itself. The stone darkens. The clicking of the grazers goes wetter, more intimate. Then it passes, and the damp returns to being a condition rather than an event.

No one has built anything. There is no need and no leftover impulse. The worn hollows where the largest walkers rest are not architecture; they are erosion of a newer kind, animal abrasion completing the work old water began. Bones, where they persist long enough, lose their identity and become another polished knob in the floor, slightly warmer to the tongue of a grazer than the limestone around them. There are no tools. A dropped stick would be eaten for its bacteria, or used as a brief rasping-post, or ignored. The world does not contain the category.

If there is a law it is this: occupancy is the only permanence, and occupancy is never still. The basin has looked like this — not these individuals, this *like* — for long enough that the forms have the authority of geology. Predator and prey are less a drama than a circulation, the way damp circulates, the way sound circulates, the way cool air sinks into the concavity and cannot be persuaded to leave. Things are taken. Things take. The gaps close. The noise does not dip for grief.

Walk far enough and a residual ridge lifts you half a body higher, and for a moment the density thins by a fraction you would not notice anywhere else. You can almost see a distance: not a horizon, nothing so grand, only a more distant darkness in which the pale smears of the upper fliers scribble and unscrawl. The smell from here is a whole climate — ammonia, wet mineral, a sweetness that is decay and a sourness that is life, indistinguishable. Below, the living pavement flexes. A shared-skin walker founders, is opened, is distributed. The fliers tighten. The grazers close. The sound, which cannot get louder, changes key, a vast animal chord resolving into the same chord.

Then the ridge lowers you again, and the bodies come back up to the knee, the hip, the chest, the near-touching press of a country that has no empty in it, and the dark takes back even the idea of a view, and there is only the cool wet air and the work of remaining upright in a place that has never heard of standing apart.

At the swallow-holes the density changes flavor. The stone funnels down into throats the old water made, and the pockets there are deeper, almost pools, and the things that specialize in edges congregate until the rims are a single writhing gasket of life. Something calls from below the waterline, a voice felt more than heard, a subtone under the deafening, and the gasket stills in a way that is not fear exactly — more like attention with teeth. Then the call ends, or is answered, or was never a call, and the gasket resumes, and a flier misjudges the downdraft of the hole and is simply gone, added to whatever account is kept in the throat of the rock.

Night does not fall. It was already the material. What changes, over the long grind of hours, is only which majority is feeding and which is announcing and which is briefly, privately, digesting in the press. The basin does not sleep. Sleep would be a silence, and silence here is a kind of death the living will not permit. Even the grazers, rasping their invisible pastures, contribute their sand-pour to the law.

You could leave by the way the land eventually, reluctantly, climbs — a barely-there rise toward countries that might have light, or plants, or the scandal of open space. Behind you the sound would continue without any awareness that it had been witnessed. It has been doing so since the stone took its finished shape. It will be doing so when the last residual ridge is walked down into grit and the basin is only a slightly smoother dark, still cool, still damp, still without a single leaf, still so full of animals that the idea of a vacant world would be, to anything born here, a story too thin to believe.
