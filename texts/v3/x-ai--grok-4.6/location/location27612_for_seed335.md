The hollow opened last season, or the one before—no one marked it—when a lens of ice and peat simply let go. What remains is a pocket no larger than a room that forgot its walls, set flush with the low country so that you do not see it until you are already at the lip. The lip is still raw. Black peat shears away in clean planes. A rind of pale mineral, thin as enamel, clings to the cut and weeps. Everything weeps.

Cold lives here as a resident, not a weather. It pools in the basin and does not climb out. Breath becomes a second mist. The water is everywhere and never still in the same way twice: a skin of melt across the floor that never quite decides to be ice or pond; threads working down the peat faces; a constant bead along every root-hair and stone; a condensing atmosphere that beads on your lashes and on the undersides of leaves and on the sudden, new rock that has not yet learned to be dull. When the air shifts, the whole pocket rains upward for a few seconds, a fine lift of droplets that hang, hesitate, and fall back as if remembering gravity late.

There is almost nothing in the volume. That is the first honest fact. You could throw a stone across it and hear the stone think about the journey. Space is the main material—cold, wet, slightly metallic space—and the living things keep to the edges and the skin of the water as if the center were a rule. Mosses have taken the walls in plush, dripping shelves: sphagnum the color of old brass, darker cushions that hold so much water they slump under their own weight, liverworts like wet leather glued to the youngest fractures. Horsetails stand in loose companies, jointed and sparse, each stem a green pipe with a drop trembling at its tip. A few willows have started, no taller than a crouched person, their catkins still tight, their roots already combing the peat for whatever heat the ground still owes them. It is lush in the way a wound can be lush. Nothing fills the air. The plants do not crowd. They occupy, and then they leave room.

Light arrives already spent. It is the color of a bruise under ice. On the brightest days the sky is only a paler gray rectangle far above, and even that is often occluded by the breath of the place, a low working cloud that forms and unforms without wind. You see by glints: a bead, a wet leaf, the sudden white of a quartz chip in the new cut, the oily dark of water when something moves under it.

The sound is the other occupant, and it is industrial without a single made thing in sight. It comes up through the floor and in through the peat and down from the unseen country beyond the lip. A regular, deep knocking, as if a great valve were seating itself somewhere under the marsh. A hiss that is not quite steam and not quite rain. Periods of hammering—water, almost certainly water, slammed through a throat of ice or stone at a pressure the young ground has not finished making sense of. The knocking has a tempo. You start to wait for it. Between the knocks there is the nearer noise: drip-lines finding different pitches on stone, moss, open water; the papery tear of a peat block calving from the wall; a high insectile fizz that should not belong to a place this cold and does. When the discharge comes, the whole pocket fills with a roar that has edges, a shaped loudness, like a millrace or a blowdown, and the standing water is shoved into a brief, circular panic. Then the level drops a finger’s width, the roar becomes a gutturals of pipes, and the dripping starts over, slightly rearranged.

No artifact. No timber, no brick, no cable, no bootprint that lasts. The industrial is a climate and a geology, not a construction. Something out of sight—or under—is processing water and heat and gas on a schedule, and this hollow is a condensate trap, a listening room, a fault that opened onto the works. The water tastes faintly of iron and something sweeter, almost solvent. In two or three places it wells a degree or two warmer than the air, and those wells never freeze. A skin of ochre and a skin of white precipitate take turns forming on the pebbles there, then slough off in flakes when the next surge arrives. The flakes drift like shed scales and settle into the peat, where the moss grows over them as if they were always part of the ground.

Life uses the rhythm. The fauna is abundant the way a tide pool is abundant: not large, not continuous, but everywhere you look once you have accepted the scale. Dark amphipods stitch the warmer wells. Midges lift in weak columns from the horsetails whenever the knocking pauses, a brief, hopeless summer lasting twenty seconds. Small frogs the color of wet bark sit so still they are only eyes, then move in a single tasteless blurt into the shallows. Something mole-like works the peat walls from inside; you see the wall dimple, then a snout, then nothing, and a new bead-line of water finds the hole and makes a new pitch. Birds come and do not stay—low, fast, dark, skimming the lip as if the hollow were a mouth they refused to enter. At dusk, which is most of the time, a larger shape uses the far wall: a mustelid, long as a thought, dripping, pausing to listen to the valve-knock the way you do. Predator and prey keep the same clock. When the roar comes they all freeze or all vanish. When it fades the eating starts again, quick and unsentimental.

The ground is still deciding its shape. Overnight a new shelf of peat may lean out and hold. By afternoon it has sagged and become a ramp for water. Needles of ice grow in the shade of the lip, smash under their own weight, grow again with a sound like sugar under a boot. Gas lifts through the pool in slow coins that smell of struck flint. The mineral rind on the cut thickens, crazes, peels. You can watch a crack travel if you are patient; it does not travel like old rock. It travels like something that was never supposed to be air-facing yet.

Rules, such as they are: heat is local and contractual, granted at the wells and revoked everywhere else. Sound is a tide. The center of the pocket is kept empty—not by threat exactly, but by a kind of weather, a pressure or a smell or a memory in the animals’ bodies that says the middle is for water and noise, not for standing. Nothing made by hands lasts a night; even a fallen twig you might have brought in is wrapped in film and sunk or lifted into a moss cushion and made anonymous. The place is young enough to be literal. It has not learned metaphor. A collapse is a collapse. A drip is a drip. The factory-sound is whatever it is, unashamed of having no factory you can point to.

Stay long enough and the pocket becomes a lung. Inhale: iron, wet peat, the sweet solvent note, cold so clean it hurts the teeth. Exhale: your own heat, immediately claimed, beading on the nearest leaf. The knocking returns. The midges lift and fail. A frog accepts the shallows. Somewhere under the low country a throat of stone takes another swallow of the world and the hollow answers, loud and exact, as if it were built for this and never built at all.
