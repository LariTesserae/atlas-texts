The cleft sits where the mountain’s last shoulder shears into emptiness, a vertical slot no wider than a man’s reach and perhaps three times as deep. From any distance it is invisible: a hairline of shadow in a face of pale, wind-honed mineral that has forgotten how to erode further. The stone itself is neither granite nor salt but something that once flowed and then set, now so thoroughly settled that its surfaces hold only the memory of motion—soft concavities, fluted pillars, a single overhanging lintel worn to the exact curve of an absent river. There is no water and has never been. The air inside the cleft is cool, still, and so dry it feels metallic on the tongue.

Every cubic handspan of that hidden volume is occupied. Fronds the colour of tarnished copper and old verdigris interlock in a density that leaves no path, no pocket of empty air large enough for a fist. They are not succulents; they have no sap, no turgor. Each blade is a thin, rigid lamina of silica-cellulose, dry as parchment yet endlessly layered, so that the whole mass has the look of a drowned rainforest frozen at the moment of greatest leaf. The plants do not grow. They do not shed. They simply persist in the same interpenetrating architecture they have held since the engine first turned.

That engine occupies the deepest third of the cleft, half-swallowed by the fronds. It is a single cast-iron block the size of a small altar, pistons still cycling in a rhythm that has not varied by a fraction of a second in whatever span of time the mountain has stood. Two pipes of flaking black enamel disappear into the stone; one returns. There is no fuel line anyone can see, no cooling jacket, no operator. The machine simply works, producing a low, continuous thunder that the packed vegetation both muffles and amplifies until the entire pocket vibrates like the inside of a struck bell. The sound is loud enough to feel in the teeth, unchanging, without attack or decay.

Light enters only through the slot, a dim, colourless wash that never strengthens or fades. It is enough to show the faint iridescence along the edges of the fronds and the slow, oil-slick gleam on the engine’s moving parts. Nothing else lives here. There are no insects, no mites, no bacterial film. The ecology is a closed equation of mineral leaf and perpetual mechanism, each apparently feeding the other in a transaction too old to remember its terms.

A few scattered artifacts remain: a brass gauge whose needle has been pinned at the same mark since before the stone finished weathering; a single iron staple driven into the wall, now sheathed in the same dry foliage; a length of chain fused by time into a decorative loop. These are not ruins so much as punctuation, faint marks left by whoever first set the engine running and then stepped away forever.

The place does not change. Temperature, humidity, light, sound, the precise angle of every frond—all are locked in equilibrium. Time here is not a river but a standing wave. Whatever process once carved the cleft, planted the first lamina, or ignited the engine has long since reached its final, unalterable state. The mountain continues its geological stillness around this one loud, green, waterless room that will still be exactly itself when the last light fails.
