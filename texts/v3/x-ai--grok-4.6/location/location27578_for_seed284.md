The place is a cleft.

Not a valley, not a pass. A single vertical wound in a summit that did not exist a generation ago. The mountain came up fast and unfinished: a spear of pale, sugary mineral driven through older strata until it stood higher than weather, higher than the last useful air. Its flanks are still bright with unweathered edges. Nothing has had time to round them. If you put a hand on the rock it comes away dusted with tiny, perfect blades.

Wedged into that wound, no wider than a long hall and no taller than a ship’s hold laid on end, is the settlement. It has no name that has stuck. People call it the Hold when they call it anything. From outside you would miss it. The entrance is a hinged plate the color of the stone, and the plate is usually shut against the thinness.

Inside, the air is cooled and dried until it tastes of metal filings and old current. There is almost no water. What moisture exists is a leak, a fault, a thing to be mopged with cloths and wrung into sealed tins. Throats stay dusty. Joints click. The chemistry of the place runs on oils, on salts that never dissolve, on electrostatic cling. Sweat is an embarrassment and a resource; it is scraped from skin and fed to the condensers, which complain and produce a few cloudy drops that no one drinks if they can help it.

Light comes from below and from the machines. High slits in the cleft admit a dim, directionless gray that might be day. It does not reach the floor. The rest is furnace-mouth orange, the small stubborn whites of filament bulbs, the occasional green of a telltale that has not yet burned out. Shadows are thick and layered. You walk through other people’s silhouettes.

The construction is industrial and intimate at once. Catwalks cross at shin height and at the height of a raised arm. Pipes of three different eras share the same brackets. A press the size of a coffin stands beside a winding stair of stamped plate that leads to a mezzanine no wider than a cot, where someone has bolted a bunk and a locker and a shrine of greasy bolts. Everything is near-touching. You turn sideways in the aisles. Heat exchangers breathe against your shoulder. The density is not only of objects but of work: three shifts occupy the same cubic volume by sleeping in relays, by hanging tools in midair on spring-hooks, by knowing whose elbow belongs where.

The machines do not rest. This is the turbulence you feel before you hear it. Belts reverse without announcement. A hopper that was empty at first bell is packed with pale slag by third. Gantry arms unfold along tracks that were not there yesterday; the tracks themselves are temporary, dogged into the young rock, and the rock sheds them when it settles another finger-width in the night. Whole work-bays migrate. A lathe will be here in the morning and two levels down by dusk, still warm, still attended by the same pair of hands. The Hold is always being born a little more, and a little more of it is always coming apart. People move through this the way people move through a bazaar in a high wind: heads down, possessions close, ready to step over a sudden gap.

Sound, for all that, stays low. The air is too thin to carry a shout far, and the settlement has grown baffles the way a body grows callus—felt pads, hanging curtains of woven wire, cork where cork can be afforded. What you hear is a continuous, almost shy vibration in the soles and the teeth: motors turning in vacuum-rated housings, the tick of cooling metal, the dry rustle of whatever lives in the walls. Voices stay close to the mouth. A laugh is a private thing.

The fauna are the rustle.

They are abundant and they are not plants’ children. Nothing green has ever held here. In the coolest, stillest seams a film sometimes appears, more mineral bloom than fungus, a dull lilac crust that the night shift scrapes for the trace metals it concentrates. The animals ignore it. They eat dust, they eat shed insulation, they eat each other, they eat the microscopic grease that condenses on cold housings. There are the flake-mites, flat as paper, that travel in silent carpets across vertical plate and only become visible when a bulb angle changes. There are the hinge-crabs, fist-sized, carapaces the same dull alloy as the older pipes, which nest in any right angle and extend a single pale feeler to taste vibration; if the vibration is a machine they leave it; if it is a person sleeping they may take a button, a loose screw, a flake of skin. There are the loft-rats, which are not rats—longer in the spine, ears like folded foil, hind feet that splay into dry fans—so they can drop from a catwalk and land without a sound on a pile of swarf. They run the vertical city the way fish would run a reef if the reef were made of rivets. At shift-change they pour, a soft, continuous motion along every edge, and for a few minutes the Hold seems twice as inhabited.

Children (there are children; the Hold is a town, however pocketed) learn not to startle them. A loft-rat in panic makes no cry but it will carry a spark. The extinguishers are always within reach and always half-empty.

The people themselves have the look of a high, dry place. Lips chap. Eyes sit deep. Clothing is layered felt and canvas, oil-stained, cinched at wrist and ankle against the mites. They keep small personal machines—hand-cranks, pocket dynamo-lights, listening trumpets pressed to bulkheads to hear what the next bay is doing. Industry here is not distant; it is the walls and the floor and the reason the cleft does not freeze entirely. A foundry no larger than a kitchen pours a dull, low-melting alloy into molds the shape of replacement teeth for the bigger gears. Next to it a woman in a leather apron draws wire through successive dies, the wire singing so faintly only she can hear it. Further along, behind a hanging of chain mail, someone is assembling relays by the light of a single orange coil, fingers moving in the small, furious hush of piecework.

The rules are few and they are geological.

Do not open the outer plate without the second bar set. The thinness outside is not an atmosphere so much as a rumor of one; it will take the wet from your eyes and the heat from your blood and leave you light-headed among the knife-edges of the new mountain, where a slipped boot travels a very long way down.

Do not introduce water if you can help it. A spilled cup becomes a spreading dark that lifts old salts out of the rock and leaves a crust that eats insulation. The night shift will fine you in labor.

When the mountain shrugs—and it still shrugs, young and unfinished—brace on the yellow-painted members. They are the ones socketed into the older stone beneath the spear. Everything else may walk.

If a bay vanishes overnight, it has not been stolen. It has been reassigned by the work, or by the settling, or by a foreman who does not sleep. Follow the heat. Follow the loft-rats. The work is still there.

At the very top of the cleft, above the last catwalk, there is a little platform with a grated floor. People go there when a shift ends badly or well. You can look up through the grate at the slits, at that dim non-color that might be sky. Sometimes a grain of the summit mineral works loose and falls, a single bright needle turning, and ticks against the grate and lies there, sharp enough to draw blood, pretty enough that children pocket them until they cut through the cloth. The air moves hardly at all. You hear your own pulse. Below you the Hold continues its silent, crowded, unceasing rearrangement—arms unfolding, belts reversing, fauna pouring along the lips of things—while the new mountain, which has not yet learned to be old, leans another fraction toward whatever it is becoming.

That is the place. A dry wound full of industry and uninvited life, too high for rain, too young for rest, too small to leave and too densely occupied to ever be still, except in the way a held breath is still.
