The place is not large. You could walk its long axis in the time it takes a kettle to come to a hard boil, if the paths ran straight, which they do not. It sits in a warm low saddle of land that never quite decided whether it was meadow or lake, and after a long time of indecision the ground simply became both: a black, saturated peat under a skin of water that is never deeper than a boot and never shallower than a film. Everything that was built here was built *into* that decision rather than against it.

What stands is a single industrial hamlet, long finished with the idea of growing. Brick and iron and tarred timber packed so tightly that courtyards have the proportions of rooms and rooms open, without ceremony, into sluices. The buildings lean into one another the way old teeth lean. Their mortar has gone green. Their lower courses have been rewritten by the waterline so many times that the original plinths are a matter of archaeology: a darker stain, a band of mussels that are not mussels but mineral, a course of brick that weeps continuously as if the wall itself had been taught to perspire. Gutters do not empty so much as redistribute. Every downpipe ends in another trough, every trough in a wheel-pit or a settling pan or a slack canal no wider than a coffin. The canals themselves are roofed in places by later floors, so that you walk over water you can hear but not see, and the sound of it travels the joists like a second set of plumbing.

It is always after rain here, or during. The air is a warm condensing thing. Breath shows. Iron sweats. Window glass, where it survives, runs with a continuous slow sheet that has nothing to do with weather and everything to do with the temperature of the brick behind it. Light arrives already used. It comes down through a low lid of vapor and through the upper stories of vegetation and through the smoke that is not quite smoke—more a pale industrial weather that the stacks have been making for so long the marsh has learned to wear it. Inside the sheds the light is the color of weak tea strained through felt. Outside it is the same tea, only thinner. There are few true shadows and no hard ones. Edges dissolve at a pace the eye can almost watch.

The plants are the other architecture. They are not ornamental and they are not reclaiming; they are *employed*, or they have employed the works, it is no longer easy to say. Reed and sweet-flag stand in the open canals in ranks as regular as fencing. On every horizontal—copings, window ledges, the flat tops of dead boilers—there is a turf of moss so thick it has a nap, and through the moss come ferns whose roots have found the lime in the mortar and will not leave it. Vines use the external pipework as trellis and have, in several bays, become the pipework: hollow stems carrying a trickle that was once condensate and is now something the plants have altered, slightly sweeter, faintly warm. In the old dye-house the vats have gone to waterlily and a kind of submerged grass that moves even when the paddles are still. On the north faces, where the brick never dries, there is a liverwort the color of old bronze that has mapped the entire elevation in a single contiguous skin. It shines when the dimness lessens, which is not often.

There are no animals. Not a midge, not a worm visible in the peat if you lift a brick, not a bird on the ridge tiles, not a spider in the angles of the trusses. The lushness is a vegetal solitude. Seeds arrive from elsewhere—you find them sometimes, a dry sycamore key lodged in a gutter, a burr on your cuff after you have been nowhere—and they either fail or they become more of this same green occupation. The absence is not dramatic. It is a quality of the air, like the warmth, like the wet: a finished fact. Sound has no small lives in it to catch on. What you hear is therefore larger than it should be, and closer.

The works are still running. They have been running, by the look of the wear, since before the present roofs were the present roofs. The engines are not hurried. They keep a tide of their own, a long industrial respiration: intake, compression, a release that shakes water out of every joint and sends a fresh skin of vibration across the nearest canal. You feel it in your knees before you sort it with your ears. Belt-drives slap with a wet sound. A millstone, or something that was a millstone and is now a slower circular argument with grit, turns in a basement you reach by a stair that is more ladder and always beaded with seepage. Steam does not jet; it eases. It finds the same paths the vines found. In the pump-house—lowest point, naturally—three beam-engines lift the marsh a few inches and set it down again a few yards over, forever, a local correction to a gradient the land forgot it had. Their cycle is almost tidal. Between strokes there is a pause in which you can hear the building drip, and the drip is not separate from the machinery; it has been timed, accidentally, over decades, into the same patience.

The loudness is not a single note. It is a saturation. Water on corrugated iron, water in downpipes, water over the lip of a launder that has been overflowing since someone’s grandfather. The engines. The slap of leather. A valve that has sung the same cracked syllable since it was last packed, which was not recently. When the wind bothers to move, it moves the upper canes and they knock like light scaffolding. None of this peaks. None of it stops. It has the quality of a room in which many clocks were started at different hours and then left, and the room is the whole hamlet, and the hamlet has no outside that quite counts. Stand in the central court—a well of wet brick, twelve paces by nine, open to the dim—and the sounds stack until they have weight. You shout only if you have forgotten where you are. The place answers by continuing.

Materials have reached their settled quarrel. Iron is a brown lace wherever it was thin; where it was thick it is still thick, only softer at the corners, as if the metal were being slowly persuaded back toward ore. Brick has lost its arrises. Timber that stayed above the waterline is silver and checked; timber that did not is a black paste held in shape by habit and by the plants that use it as a medium. Glass remains in the high lights of the engine-house, each pane a different green, each running. There is a great deal of ceramic: conduits, insulators, the throats of old retorts set as coping on a wall that needed none. Electricity is present as a brown hum in porcelain and as a few surviving bulbs that burn a dull orange, always, whether anyone is in the room or not. No one seems to replace them. When one goes it stays gone, and the dimness steps inward by that much.

The people, if that is still the word, are few and move like the machinery: on long errands, without haste, along routes that have been worn to a shine even on brick. They keep the engines oiled and the sluices clear enough and they harvest, at intervals measured in months, whatever the works were built to make. It is hard to tell, from the product stacked in the dry loft—bundles, cakes, reels of something fibrous and mineral at once—whether the harvest is industrial or agricultural or a third category the place invented for itself. They do not keep animals. They do not speak of animals. Their clothes wick. Their hands are the color of the brick.

Time here is a thick medium. Change happens, but it happens the way peat accumulates, the way a vine takes a girder, the way a stain climbs a wall. A new leak is not an event; it is the next sentence in a paragraph that began before the present roofs. The weather has only one real mood and several intensities of it. Dawn is a rumor in the vapor. Evening is the same rumor withdrawing. If there are seasons they announce themselves as a slight alteration in the smell of the water—more tannin, less—and as a decision, among the reeds, to be taller. The engines do not know the difference, or they know it as load.

There is a strangeness that never quite becomes a rule and never quite becomes an accident. The plants grow as if they had been briefed on the floorplan. Pipes that have not carried steam in a generation still warm a little at the hour the boilers used to be fired, and moss yellows there, obediently. In the deepest channel the water moves against the pumps for a few minutes at the bottom of every night, a small rebellion or a memory of a larger drainage that failed, and during those minutes the whole hamlet lists, not enough to see, enough to feel in the inner ear. Reflections in the canals sometimes show a story more than they ought: a chimney that is no longer standing, a window lit in a wall that has no room behind it. These are not reliable. They may be only the dimness arranging itself. No one locally treats them as news.

The boundary is wet and vague and also absolute. Ten paces into the open marsh the engines become a single muscle in the distance and the vegetation changes its mind—wilder, less employed, still without animals—and the ground remembers that it could drown you if it chose. Ten paces back and the brick takes you under its eaves again and the sound closes like a door that was never a door. The place has no need of a gate. It is too finished, too dense, too much itself. It has been coming to this equilibrium for longer than the iron has been rusting, and it will go on coming to it, the same few acres of warm dark water and leaf and labor, for as long as the pumps can lift a little and set it down again, which, from the look of the beams, is a patience with no particular end.
