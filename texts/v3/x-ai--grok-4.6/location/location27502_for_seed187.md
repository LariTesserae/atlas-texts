The hollow sits in a slight depression, no higher than a man’s chest above the surrounding peneplain, as if the land itself long ago decided it had finished rising and falling and would now simply hold this one concavity. Everything that can be seen, touched, or stood upon is plant. There is no mineral soil visible, no stone, no bare earth. The floor is a compressed, living weave of rootlets, liverworts, and the flattened bases of ancient stems that have grown into one another until they form a springy, slightly yielding pavement. It gives a little underfoot, like wet cork, and never quite dries.

The walls rise in a single, irregular vault. They are not trees standing side by side; they are one continuous organism, or the long-fused remains of many, their cambium layers welded across millennia into a single bark-and-wood continuum. The inner surface is smooth in places, almost polished by the passage of time and the slow, ceaseless drip of condensate. In other places it is deeply fissured, the cracks packed with smaller plants that have become part of the wall itself: filmy ferns, luminous mosses the colour of old bronze, threads of something that might once have been a vine and is now only another texture. The whole chamber is perhaps forty paces across at its widest, thirty high at the centre. Cathedral scale, but intimate; you can see every surface from any point inside it. The space between those surfaces is almost empty.

Light enters as ordinary daylight through a high, irregular oculus where the living vault thins into a lattice of branches and translucent leaves. It is neither dim nor bright, simply the light of a cool, overcast afternoon that never quite arrives or leaves. The air holds a constant dampness that never becomes rain and never quite evaporates. Beads of water form on the cooler patches of bark, gather, and fall at long, irregular intervals. Each drop lands with a sound out of all proportion to its size, because the chamber is an instrument. The acoustics are perfect and unchanging.

The loudness is not occasional. It is the condition of the place. From every fissure and hollow in the living walls comes a continuous, overlapping roar of animal life: the dry rattle of countless wing-cases, the wet pulse of amphibian throats in the small standing pools that collect in root-cups, the high electric whine of insects that never seem to land. The sounds do not come and go. They have reached some final, stable density, a single compound note that the hollow has been sounding for longer than anyone could measure. It has the relentless, uninflected quality of machinery running at a constant load; one almost expects to see belts and flywheels, but there is nothing of the kind. Only the plants, and the creatures that live inside their mass.

Those creatures are abundant yet occupy almost no volume. They cling to surfaces, tunnel through the living wood, hang in the thin films of water. The air itself stays clear. You can walk the entire floor and meet nothing larger than a thumb-sized beetle or a damp, motionless frog the colour of wet bark. The emptiness is not absence of life; it is life pressed into the walls, leaving the interior a resonating void.

Nothing here is built. There are no tools, no cut surfaces, no geometry that did not grow. And yet the interlocking of the great stems has a repetitive, almost industrial logic: parallel channels where sap once moved in quantity, now dry and sounding like pipes when the air shifts; regular chambers in the wood that might have been the work of some forgotten boring insect and now serve as echo-boxes. The whole structure has the settled look of something that was once productive on a vast scale and has long since finished its work. It does not grow. It does not decay in any visible way. It has reached the form it will keep.

The temperature never changes. Cool air moves in slow, circular currents that have no source and no destination, merely circulating the same moisture, the same green smell of crushed stems and old tannin. Outside, beyond the low lip of the depression, the peneplain continues: an endless, ankle-deep carpet of the same fused vegetation, worn so flat and ancient that it has the sheen of something planed by time itself. But that is another country. This hollow is its own climate, its own weather, its own unending afternoon. The drops fall. The chorus holds its note. The living walls stand exactly as they have stood, and will stand.
