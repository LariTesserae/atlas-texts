The pocket opened last season. No one above quite registered it as an event—just a slump in the service alley between the old condenser hall and the river-facing wall, a rectangle of pavement that settled a man’s height and then kept settling, taking with it a run of rusted handrail and a coil of abandoned cable. From the alley you still step over it without looking: a grate someone dragged across, half-covered in blown paper. Down here it is a room the weather made.

The walls are not walls. They are the fresh break of what used to be made ground—fill and clinker and poured slab, sheared along a plane that is still sharp enough to cut a palm. You can see the layers like a badly stacked sandwich: black ash, a band of ochre clay that never dried, a seam of crushed brick, then the older river silt the factory was built on. The cut faces glisten. Moisture does not fall so much as arrive. It beads on the metal remnants, threads down the clay, collects in the lowest corner as a film rather than a pool. When the plant’s remaining heat exchangers cycle—twice a day, still, though the rest of the line is dead—the concrete above sweats and the pocket fills with a warm fog that does not rise. It hangs. It condenses on everything at once.

There is almost no soil that would count as soil. What grows does so on the seams. A few mats of something like liverwort, the color of old tea, cling to the clay band. Two or three wiry stems of a plant that might once have been a dock have found a crack and gone nowhere: they are six inches high and already woody, as if they understood there would never be more light or more room. That is the vegetation entire. The rest of the occupancy is not green.

The density is the first thing that refuses the eye. Nothing here has a margin. Pipe stubs jut from the broken slab at the angles they were left in—feed lines, a steam return, a nameless elbow painted the industrial green that outlasts paint. Between them the space is taken by what the pocket has made of itself. Mineral crusts have grown in weeks, not years: pale, blade-thin plates of something sulfate, stacked like cards, occupying every idle surface. They ring when a drop hits them. Rust has not powdered; it has organized. It stands in thin vertical vanes, almost feathered, almost deliberate, occupying the same volumes the pipes already occupy, so that iron and oxide occupy each other. There is no empty air at standing height. You move by turning sideways. Your shoulder finds warm metal, then a crust that yields with a sound like sugar, then the damp flank of something living.

The fauna is what uses the leftover millimeters. They are abundant in the way a packed drawer is abundant. Small. Most of them are the color of the place—rust, wet ash, the faint yellow of sulfur bloom. Beetles with elytra that look rolled from the same green paint as the pipes. A kind of isopod that has learned to graze the liverwort and the condensate film equally, leaving faint silver tracks that last only until the next fog. Things with too many joints in the crevices of the cable coil, which still carries a residual hum when the exchangers kick, a vibration you feel in your teeth more than hear. At the wet corner a congregation of pale, flattened things—neither quite slug nor quite fish—pulses in the film, opening and closing mouths that seem to be tasting the temperature rather than the water. They brighten when the fog comes. They dim when it thins. Predation here is a matter of timing and of who is occupying the same cubic inch. A beetle takes an isopod not by chase but by simply being where the isopod has to pass. There is no room to flee in. The quiet is almost total: the drip, the distant two-cycle thump of the heat plant, the minute click of crusts adjusting as they grow. When something is eaten the sound is a soft, wet paper tear.

Light comes as it would in a basement that still has a window. Noon makes a bright, dusty rectangle on the grate and a softer, moving lace on the broken faces. Late day the light goes amber and the rust vanes throw thin shadows that look like writing no one taught. Nothing here is dark enough to hide in; the strangeness is not gloom. The strangeness is that the pocket is still becoming. The break is young. Edges have not rounded. A slab lip still holds a perfect right angle from the pour, now undercut by seepage so that it hangs like a cornice, dripping on a schedule of its own. New crusts appear between one visit and the next, occupying spaces you would have sworn were already full. The animals rearrange. A pipe that was empty is threaded with a chain of the pale pulse-things, using its interior as a river. The liverwort has advanced a finger’s width along the clay and already looks ancient.

What was built remains only as punctuation. A valve wheel, seized open. A stenciled number on a fragment of wall—47—half sheared away. Someone’s glove, stiffened into a shape that is no longer a hand, occupying a niche the crusts have decided not to take. The industrial is not a ruin so much as a parent the pocket has not finished leaving. Heat still arrives on a timetable. Current still ghosts the cable. The fog still tastes faintly of glycol and warm iron. The place is low, almost river-level; when the real river rises in the wet weeks the film in the corner becomes a skin of actual water for a day, and the pulse-things become frantic, and the crusts hiss as they dissolve and then, when the water drops, grow back sharper than before, as if rehearsing an older geology at speed.

You can stand in it only by becoming part of the packing. Your breath adds to the fog. Your heat is noticed. The isopods correct their paths around your boots without hurry. After a while the quiet is not absence; it is the sound of a great many small processes agreeing not to waste space on noise. The pocket does not want to be larger. It wants to be finished, and it is not, and that unfinishedness is what it is made of: a warm, damp, overfull room the ground invented, still cutting its own walls, still filling every vacancy the moment a vacancy appears.
