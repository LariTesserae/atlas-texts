The saddle was not meant to hold a sea.

It is a young saddle, still angular, the kind of high place that looks unfinished: two ridges of pale basalt lifting into a sky you never see from below, their edges unblunted, their scree still locked in the positions of first fall. Nothing here has had time to round. The rock remembers the uplift as a single gesture. Then the water begins, and the water is the whole of the rest of the world.

You do not come to a shore. There is a lip of stone, knife-clean, and then a vertical dark that has no surface glitter because the surface itself has been told not to move. The lake—if it is a lake—fills the cleft from wall to wall and from that impossible lip down through depths a highland basin should not possess. It is cool the way a cellar is cool, a held temperature with no weather in it. It does not stratify. It does not turn. It does not remember wind. Whatever engine or covenant keeps it this way sits outside ordinary description; you feel it as a pressure that is the same at every depth, a refusal of gradient.

Below, the city occupies a volume no larger than a closed valley or a very large nave. You could swim its long axis in a time that still feels like a walk. That is the scale of the place: enough room for streets, for a civic idea, for more than a household and less than a capital. The structures stand far apart. Between them the water is a black commons, unoccupied, so that each building is an island of intent in a medium that does not care.

They are not ruins. They are not growing. They were finished in one campaign of making and then the making stopped, as if a clock had been closed. Towers of something that behaves like glass if glass could be poured at the temperature of thought: optically thick, faintly stratified, edges still sharp enough to cut if you were foolish with your hands. Walkways that do not rest on pilings so much as occupy agreed-upon densities of the water itself, paths you know by the way your body suddenly weighs differently. Habitations stacked in three and five, their openings unshuttered because nothing arrives. A hall whose interior is a slower water, a room you enter by becoming slightly more viscous. Infrastructure without apology: conduits of white ceramic that carry nothing now, or carry a current that is not electricity and not heat, only a standing instruction. At the center, if the word still applies, a spindle of dark metal taller than the rest, fluted, its surface inscribed with a script that rearranges when you are not quite looking. No one claims to have read it twice the same way.

There is almost no light. What little there is does not come from above. The over-water day, if day still happens up there among the new ridges, does not penetrate; the held lake has been optically finished against it. Instead there is a residual, a kind of tired phosphorescence trapped in the basalt walls and in certain seams of the glass, the color of old bruises and of instruments left on in another century. It is enough to know a corner from a drop, not enough to cast a shadow that means anything. People here—when there are people—navigate by other senses: the grain of pressure against the inner ear, the way certain alleys taste of iron, a private cartography of coolness. Eyes are a courtesy.

Sound is a rumor. Water should be a cathedral for noise. This water is not. It drinks motion. A hand moved slowly produces no swirl you can hear. Once in a long while something ticks inside the spindle, a single metallic acknowledgment, and then the quiet closes over it as if embarrassed. You learn to hear your own blood. That is most of the music.

There are no plants. There have never been plants in this volume. The stone is sterile in the old sense, not dead so much as never invited. No film on the glass. No hair of algae in a joint. The city’s materials do not weather because weathering is a kind of conversation and the covenant here is silence. What you might mistake for moss in the lowest angle of a stair is only a precipitate, a mineral frost that formed when the water was first emplaced and then ceased, each crystal still showing its original face. You can cut your palm on a dendrite that has been sharp since the morning the lake was switched on.

Fauna is a maybe. There is a thing, pale and segmented, that is seen the way a word is seen when you are falling asleep: against the faint wall-glow, moving or not moving, the size of a forearm, with too many points of articulation. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It occupies a route between two towers that has no name and completes that route on a schedule that is not a schedule so much as a proof. Some say there is only one. Some say the one is a maintenance organ of the city, a roaming continuity check. If you put a hand near it the water between you becomes slightly more itself, a local increase in the idea of water, and you withdraw.

The strangeness is not decorative. It is load-bearing.

Time does not pass here in the way the ridges above would understand. The young stone stays young. The unweathered edges stay unweathered. A tool left on a lintel is there when you return, not because no one took it but because taking has no gradient to follow. People who stay long enough report that memory begins to stack instead of sequence: you do not recall yesterday, you recall the city, a single thick present with rooms in it. Children are theoretically possible and practically mythical. The urban plan assumes inhabitants the way a theorem assumes a reader. Whether the original builders remain, or whether the city is now a habit worn by whoever can tolerate equilibrium, is a question the water does not forward.

You can leave. The lip is still there, the knife of basalt, the sudden end of pressure and the return of ordinary cold air and the ordinary stars, if it is night, or the hard alpine sun if it is not. Behind you the surface will not show a ripple. The city will not have changed. The pale thing will be between its towers or not, which is the same fact. The spindle will have ticked or not. The glass will still be sharp enough to mean that nothing has been allowed to happen.

This is the whole of it: a small, dark, quiet polity held in a motionless highland sea, built once with tools that treat physics as a medium rather than a law, then locked. No green. Almost no blood. A mineral citizenship. The ridges around it will someday learn erosion. The water will not permit the lesson to enter.
