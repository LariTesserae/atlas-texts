The crack is easy to miss: a seam in the ground no longer than a forearm, edged with a rind of pale mineral that has not decided whether it is salt or bone. You would step over it. Most things do. The seam is the only honest entrance, and even that honesty is provisional—sometimes the crack is a little to the left of itself, as if the earth were still choosing where to split.

Drop through and the world below is not dark.

It is a chamber the size of a held breath, a pocket pressed into unfinished country. The ceiling is close enough that standing feels like an argument; the floor is a shallow dish of stone that still remembers being mud. Everything here is slightly too bright. The light has no source you can point to. It sits in the air the way condensation sits on a glass, a cool glare that makes every surface look newly washed and slightly overexposed. Shadows exist, but they are late, and they arrive in the wrong places, as if they had to be invented after the objects they belong to.

The temperature is the temperature of a cellar that has never stored anything. Cool enough that your breath wants to show, and sometimes does, and sometimes the little cloud hangs there after you have already moved on, a ghost of respiration that the room has not agreed to dismiss.

Damp, not wet. Moisture lives in films. It beads on the undersides of ledges that are still growing. It gathers in thumbprints of water at the lowest points of the dish-floor, each puddle no larger than a palm, each one so clear it looks like a hole punched through to a brighter room beneath this one. The puddles do not connect. Between them the stone is only faintly darkened, like skin after a fever. When a drop falls from the ceiling it makes almost no sound—more a decision than an impact—and the film of water rearranges itself with the patience of something that has not yet learned splash.

The walls are embryonic geology. You can see the grain still assembling: faint laminations that fade in and out, crystals that have sprouted half a face and then stopped, as if waiting for instructions. In places the rock is matte and in places it is wet-glass, and if you press a thumb to the softer seams it yields a fraction, cold and grainy, then slowly forgets the dent. This is not decay. It is the opposite. The chamber is being constructed by processes that have not agreed on a schedule. Ribs of mineral push a little farther out each time you look away. A vein of something white, thinner than thread, crawls a finger’s width along the ceiling and then lies still for a long while, shining as if it contained its own weather.

Vegetation is an afterthought, and knows it. There are patches of a lichen so pale it is nearly the same color as the glare, clinging to the more decided stone. A few stalks of something like hair-grass, no taller than a knuckle, grow at the rim of one puddle and nowhere else. Their roots are visible through the thin mineral skin, white and indecisive. Nothing here is trying to be a forest. The plants seem less like inhabitants than like stains that acquired chlorophyll by mistake.

And yet the place is crowded with animals.

They do not fill the space so much as they keep arriving into the emptiness. The density of bodies is high; the density of occupation is not. You will look at a stretch of bare floor and then realize it is being crossed by many small lives that do not displace the brightness. They are quiet. Almost everything here is quiet. The activity is ceaseless and nearly soundless, a silk-work of motion.

There are the pale millipedes first, if millipede is even the right generosity: long, segmented suggestions that travel the walls in braided processions, their legs so fine they read as a shimmer. They drink from the condensation. When two columns meet they pass through one another without collision, or perhaps they occupy slightly different versions of the same few inches. Behind them come the moths that are not quite moths, flat as pressed flowers, wings the color of overexposed paper. They do not fly so much as they are handed from one current of air to another. The air itself is barely moving. You feel it more as a change in the quality of the light, a coolness that slides.

On the floor, around the isolated puddles, things like isopods gather in numbers that should click and rattle and do not. They feed on the film. They feed on each other with the same unhurried courtesy. Their shed skins accumulate in drifts no thicker than ash and are already being mineralized, becoming part of the dish, so that the next generation walks on the ghosts of the last. Above them, in the brightest band of air, hang midges that do not seem to beat their wings. They simply are, a motionless blizzard, and then they are a little to the left, and the space they left behind takes a moment to notice it is empty.

Larger animals exist at the edge of the pocket’s logic. Something with too many shoulder-blades—white, damp, the size of a closed fist—clings to a forming cornice and watches with eyes that have not opened so much as they have been omitted. It breathes. You can see the flanks move. You cannot hear it. Once in a while it extends a tongue that is longer than the chamber should allow and drinks from a puddle without crossing the distance, and the puddle’s surface dimples as if from beneath.

There are traces of making that are not animal.

Against the far curve of the wall, if far is a word that still works, a few stones have been stacked. Three, perhaps four; the count changes. They are the same unfinished rock as everything else, but someone or something has asked them to be a cairn, or an altar, or a mistake. The stack leans in a direction that is not quite down. Beside it, faint marks: parallel scratches in a patch of stone that is slightly more committed than its neighbors, as if a nail or a tooth had been trying to invent writing and had gotten as far as the idea of lines. The marks are bright. Everything that is cut here becomes brighter, as though the chamber’s light were leaking out through wounds.

No tools remain. Nothing metal, nothing knapped, nothing that would survive as an object. The built things are only decisions, lightly held. A ring of dampness on the floor that is too regular to be a puddle’s cousin, the ghost of a vessel that was never brought. A single hairline groove that runs from the cairn toward the entrance-seam and then forgets itself, fading into the ordinary grain.

Time in the pocket does not pass so much as it eddies. The dynamic is real: the millipedes keep coming, the films of water thicken and thin, a stalk of hair-grass leans and rights itself, the white vein on the ceiling advances by a grain. Predation happens in silence—the fist-sized watcher takes a moth out of the air without the moth appearing to arrive in its mouth; the moth is simply no longer among the handed currents. New animals assemble at the wetter seams, first as concentrations of glare, then as legs, then as the habit of walking. The geology answers. Where many feet have passed, the floor hardens a little, chooses a texture. Where nothing has walked, it remains almost clay, cold, bright, willing.

The weirdness is not an overlay. It is the weather.

Look too long at any one puddle and you will see the chamber reflected, and in the reflection the chamber is larger, or older, or already gone, a finished cave with real shadows and a sound like distant water that this room has not earned. Blink and the puddle is only water again, a palm of damp in a dish of new stone. Distances stretch when you are not measuring them and snap back when you are. The entrance-seam above you is sometimes a crack and sometimes a pale oval of the overworld, grass-roots hanging into the light like a fringe, and sometimes it is not there, and the brightness has no outside.

Once, for a stretch of time that might have been a minute, the animals all faced the same way. Not toward the cairn, not toward the seam. Toward a point in the air that had become slightly more real than the rest of the air, a thickening, a place where the glare condensed into a shape that almost had edges. Nothing came through. The thickening thinned. The millipedes resumed their braids. The watcher on the cornice closed the idea of its eyes.

Sound stays at the threshold of hearing: the almost of a drop, the silk of a hundred thousand legs that refuse to rustle, your own blood, which seems loud here and a little embarrassed. If there is wind it is only the coolness rearranging the light. If there is a voice it is the mineral vein, advancing.

You could stay. The pocket would not mind. It is still being born and has room in its schedule for a visitor who does not take up much space. The damp would find you. The lichen would consider your shoes. The stacked stones might gain a fifth, or lose one. And the light—the sourceless, cellar-bright, slightly wrong light—would go on sitting in the air, washing every unfinished surface, while beneath the films of water the next version of the room practiced being true.
