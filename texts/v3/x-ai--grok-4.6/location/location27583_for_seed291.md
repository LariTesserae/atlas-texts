The basin has no proper name that anyone still uses. It sits where a valley should have finished forming centuries ago and simply didn’t: a wide, shallow dish of land still deciding its own contours, rock arriving in slow, irregular pulses from below. You feel the newness in the ground before you see it. Pavement that was level at dawn has a faint crown by afternoon. A factory wall leans a degree more than it did yesterday. Nothing dramatic, nothing loud—just the continuous, embryonic shrug of geology that has not yet learned stillness.

The city occupies nearly all of it. From any high gantry or cooling tower you look out across a packed, low-rise industrial tissue that stretches to the haze-softened rim of the basin, building pressed against building, alley against siding, no vacant lots, no parks that aren’t already being used as staging yards or moss farms. Streets exist, but they are more like intervals between machines than thoroughfares. Everything is close. Breath, exhaust, condensation, the damp exhalation of stone that is still cooling from its birth—there is no unused air.

It is cold here in a damp, unhurried way. Not the dry bite of high country, but a clinging chill that lives in the mist and in the metal. Moisture does not fall so much as assemble: fog that thickens in the long hours before the weak sun, condensation beading on every pipe and leaf, thin rains that arrive without announcement and leave the world smelling of wet iron and crushed fern. Water collects in unexpected pockets—old foundation pits that have become tarns, the troughs between newly risen stone blisters, the gutters of roofs that no longer quite drain because the pitch has changed. A sluggish canal system still threads the city, but its channels are frequently recut by the shifting ground; yesterday’s towpath is today’s gravel bar, tomorrow’s new loading dock.

Vegetation thrives in the damp and the cold with a dark, insistent lushness. Mosses of several greys and one startling copper colonize brick, concrete, the cooling fins of idle engines. Ferns uncurl from every crack that holds a teaspoon of grit. There are trees, but they are low and densely needled, more like living scaffolding than forest; they grow from courtyards, from the roofs of foundries, from the crowns of half-collapsed smokestacks that the earth has begun to swallow. In the mist they look older than the city, which they are not. The city is older than the ground it stands on. That reversal is one of the quieter strangenesses.

The industry never quite stops and never quite announces itself. Great presses, rolling mills, extrusion lines that draw the still-warm, slightly plastic stone up from new vents and shape it into beams, pavers, replacement wall sections—the city rebuilds itself from its own unfinished geology. The machines are massive, close-set, and oddly hushed. Sound dies quickly in the saturated air; what remains is a low, felt vibration more than a noise, the occasional drip of condensate from a high pipe, the soft tear of moss being sheared by a moving gantry. People move through the packed streets with the same economy. Conversations happen at doorways, in the steam of tea-stalls tucked under conveyor belts, in the brief intervals when two neighbors’ buildings have not yet leaned quite far enough to close the gap. There is a market that reassembles every few days in whatever plaza has remained level longest; it is busy in the way of ants, not of shouting.

Fauna is abundant in the interstices. Dark, short-legged birds that never call nest in the lee of heat exchangers. Pale insects with metallic wing-cases live on the algae that coats every permanently wet surface. Something like a fox, but longer in the spine and quieter, threads the alleys at the hours when the mist is thickest; it eats rust flakes and the small pale frogs that breed in the temporary ponds. There are cats, of course—there are always cats—but they have learned to walk the vibrating pipes and to sleep in the warm pockets behind transformer housings. Nothing here is wild in the old sense. Everything has made an arrangement with the density.

The light is ordinary daylight, thinned by vapor, sometimes stained a faint mineral green when the newest stone is still outgassing. Shadows are soft. At night the city does not blaze; it glows in isolated, practical patches—work lamps, the dull red of cooling metal, the bioluminescent edge of certain mosses that the night-shift workers have learned to read like signage. The turbulence is visual and structural, not acoustic. A street that was straight becomes a shallow S over the course of a week. A row of workers’ tenements slowly rotates a few degrees as a new anticline shoulders up beneath them; the residents jack the floors level again, replumb, and go on. Entire factory halls are occasionally abandoned in place when the floor rises too fast, then reoccupied years later when the stone has cooled and the moss has made them almost pastoral. Creation and unmaking happen at the same unhurried, crowded pace.

The rules are simple and local. Do not build higher than the prevailing mist will hide; the ground does not like tall things. Leave the newest stone alone for three days before you cut it—it is still remembering how to be solid. If your neighbor’s wall begins to occupy the air your window needs, you speak to them, or you wait, or you grow ferns across the closing gap until the geometry changes again. The city has no single center. It has many temporary ones: the current market, the foundry whose current vent is richest, the stretch of canal that happens to be navigable this month. Everything is mid-sentence. The basin is still being written, and the people and machines and mosses are writing it from the inside, densely, quietly, without quite knowing the shape of the finished page.
