The highland does not begin. It has already been going. You understand this first as pressure in the ears and a warmth that has no source, a wet heat that lives in the stone itself and does not wait for a sun. There is no sun. There has not been one long enough for the land to remember wanting one. What stands in its place is a darkness so complete it has weather, so vast it has coasts of its own, so old the mountains have worn themselves into a single continuous argument with water.

This is a plateau the size of a climate. You could walk a season and still be on the same worn table of the world, the rock underfoot a dull basalt and older metamorphic stuff, rounded, glazed, pitted where water has been deciding the shapes for longer than any machine still running. Elevation sits in the body as a thinness of air that never quite starves you and never quite lets the lungs forget. Warmth comes up through the soles. The planet’s interior is close here, or the plateau is a lid, or both: vents breathe in the dark like sleeping animals, and the mist they make does not rise so much as stay, a second ground, a hanging marsh.

Water is the other gravity. It does not fall in weather so much as occupy. The air is always on the verge of becoming lake. Condensation beads on every upright thing and runs. The rocks weep as a habit. Cliffs that would be dry faces elsewhere are curtains. You hear them before you map them: a thousand thin threads, a roar when they gather, a ticking when they only drip. Every hollow holds a black pool. The pools are not still. Things move in them, and the water is warm as blood left in a bowl. Between the pools the ground is sponge — peat and mineral fluff and the collapsed bodies of plants that grew without light and died without seasons, layered until the plateau is as much organic mattress as stone.

The plants are not green in any useful sense. Color is a rumor here. What grows is a lushness of texture: rubbery sheets that drink vapor through their whole surface, ribbed towers like organ-pipes of fungus, mats of something between moss and hide that pulse slowly when stepped on, fern-analogues whose fronds are sound-catchers, broad and veined and always slightly moving though there is no wind you can name. They grow in storeys. The lowest storey is underwater more often than not, a drowned prairie of stems. Above that a tangle you walk through with your hands, leaves slapping wet against wrists and throat. Higher still, if you climb the old ridges, the vegetation thins into isolated clubs and hanging bells that knock together when the air pressure shifts. It is lush the way a lung is lush: crowded, wet, dedicated to exchange.

Nothing here photosynthesizes. The chemistry is older and ruder. Heat, sulfur, the slow bleed of metals from the weeping rock, the effluent of the machines. The plants taste of iron and sweet rot. Their roots go down into warmth and sideways into one another until whole valleys are a single organism pretending to be a forest. When a ridge slumps — and ridges do slump; the erosion is not violent, it is accomplished, a long settled conversation between water and time — the forest goes with it in a sigh you can hear for miles, a vegetal avalanche that smells like a greenhouse left shut.

The machines are part of that conversation now. They are not a city. There is no city. Built things occur the way erratics occur: a pumping house half-eaten by the sponge-ground, its roof a garden of the sound-ferns, its interior a single enormous piston that has not stopped in anyone’s count of years. A line of pylons crossing a gorge, cables still singing when the air is right, carrying current to somewhere that may no longer exist. A factory terrace cut into a mountainside, one wall gone, the vats inside still cycling a liquor that glows with no light — you know the glow as heat on the face, a prickle, a smell like hot copper and lily. Engines the size of halls, buried to the waist, exhaling steam that becomes the local weather. Rails that run a little way and then dive into a lake and do not come up. A chimney alone on a plain of pools, still working, its rhythm the nearest thing this country has to a clock.

Whoever built them built at industrial scale and then left the scale running. Or did not leave. There are small structures that suggest attendance: a hut of stamped metal on a spur of rock, a chair facing nothing, a rack of tools whose purpose has been revised by rust into sculpture. Marks on stone that might be numbers. A staircase climbing a wet cliff to a door that opens on more cliff. The built world is scattered the way seeds are scattered after a long wind — enough to prove intention, not enough to constitute a people you can find. The industry continues without needing to be believed in. It takes water in and puts water out hotter. It takes mineral seep and concentrates it. What it is for has become a property of the landscape, like the vents, like the weeping.

You navigate by sound because there is nothing else that reaches as far. The darkness is not a night. Night implies a day that will negotiate. This is a medium. Sound lives in it the way fish live in a reef. Water, always, in every register: drip, run, fall, seiche, the subsonic shove of a whole lake deciding to go over a lip. The machines underneath that, a layered industrial ostinato, never quite in tempo with itself because the distances are too large; one factory’s downstroke arrives as another’s echo. And through both, the animals.

They are abundant the way the plants are lush — not decorative, structural. You hear grazing in the sponge, a thousand small mouths. You hear something heavy and patient moving along a ridge, pausing, moving again, the pause long enough to be thought. Wings that are not wings, membranes snapping in the wet air. Choruses that start in one gorge and are answered three valleys over, a conversation in frequencies that make the teeth ache. Predator and prey are a weather system. Things hunt by heat and by the shape of displaced water. Things hide by becoming the temperature of the stone. There are herds that migrate on a circuit so wide it takes a generation of shorter-lived creatures to complete, following warmth, following the taste of certain seeps, following the sound of a particular engine that has run so long it is a landmark. There are swarms that live in the hanging mist and only come down when the pressure drops. There are solitary lives in the deep pools that have never surfaced, that know the plateau only as a ceiling of roots and iron.

Some of the fauna are difficult to keep in the category. A ridgeline that breathes. A flock that is also a single decision. Pale cables in the lakes that are animals or are the machines’ leftover nerves or are both, grown together the way the forest’s roots have. The weirdness here is not a rupture. It is the long habit of a world that never had to agree with eyes. Shapes persist that would have been corrected by light. Processes run that would have been ashamed in a brighter physics. Time puddles. You can stand in a narrow defile and hear your own footfalls arriving from ahead. You can put a hand on a weeping wall and feel a pulse that matches no engine and no heart you can name, only the plateau’s own slow cardiac, water and heat and stone in a rhythm older than the factories.

Weather happens anyway. Not as light and dark, not as seasons of green. As pressure. As the mist thickening until walking is swimming. As a sudden thinning when a vent-field opens and the air goes sharp and mineral and the sound-ferns clap all at once. As rains that are indistinguishable from the ordinary condition except by volume, a vertical lake, the pools joining, the sponge becoming continuous water, the animals climbing or diving according to their kind, the machines taking on a higher note as they work against the flood. Then the draining, days of it, water finding the old grooves it carved when the mountains were still ambitious, going down and down toward edges of the highland you will never reach, carrying silt and spores and the shed skins of things, and the smell after is the smell of a mouth opened: warm, metallic, faintly sweet.

At the true edges — if the word means anything at this scale — the plateau does not stop so much as forget to continue. The land crumples into gorges whose far walls you will never confirm. Water goes over in falls that are only a sound and a wind of droplets. Sometimes a machine-light, not light, a radiation the skin reads as a faint direction, pulses from very far below, as if the industry had cellars in the planet. Sometimes a migration pours off and does not come back. The rest of the world, if there is a rest, does not send anything up.

What remains is the interior, which is essentially endless. Warm dark. Wet dark. A darkness with lushness packed into it until the dark itself feels crowded, bodies and leaves and pistons and rain all nearly touching. Established. In its own state. The erosion finished its big work ages ago and now only maintains. The factories maintain. The forest maintains. The animals eat and are eaten and the pools refill. You could set a clock by none of it and by all of it. The loudness never quite becomes noise; it is too structured, too full of overlapping purposes. If you stay long enough you stop listening for a sun and start listening for the next change in the water, the next engine that is slightly out of true, the next call that means the herd has turned.

There are places, a few, where someone once tried to make a mark that would outlast the weeping. A plaque bolted to a chimney. A cairn of cut stone on a rise, already softened, already hosting the moss-hide. A trench that might have been a road and is now a linear pond, full of the pale cables, full of the ticking of small lives. The marks are faint because faintness is what the plateau permits. Everything here is being translated into its wet equivalent. The industrial and the biological have been in the same weather so long they have started to use each other’s grammar. A pipe puts out flowers of mineral. A root system has learned to conduct. An animal nests in a gear-housing and the gear, turning, is part of the nest’s season.

Stand still. The warmth comes up. Water finds your skin as if it had been looking. Something large changes its mind on a ridge you will never see. A factory two days’ walk west misses a beat and catches again. The sound-ferns lift. The hanging marsh drifts against your mouth, tasting of iron and lily and exhaust. This is the country. It does not brighten. It does not empty. It continues, vast enough to keep its own nights inside the night, dense enough that solitude is only a way of arranging the crowd, old enough that every shape you meet has already been the answer to a long problem of water and heat and time.
