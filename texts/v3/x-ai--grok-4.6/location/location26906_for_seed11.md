The plateau has already finished becoming what it is.

Whatever mountains once stood here finished dying so long ago that even their graves have been worn level. What remains is a single surface, slightly bowed, slightly pitted, the color of old bone and iron tea, sitting so high the rest of the world is a rumor beneath the rim. You do not climb onto it so much as arrive already exhausted by the altitude and discover there is still nowhere to rest. The stone underfoot is warm. It has been warm for geological ages. Heat lives in it the way a story lives in a people who have forgotten the first telling.

There is almost nothing here, and what is here is either screaming or green.

The wind is the first fact and the last. It has no opposing architecture left to break against, so it simply occupies the entire volume of the air, continuously, as if air were a solid being planed. It arrives from several directions at once and cannot decide. Cells of weather form, collapse, and reform across distances you can watch happening. A wall of torn cloud will walk toward you for an hour, arrive as a brief, furious rain that never quite becomes a river, and be gone before the stone has finished darkening. Then condensation blooms on every surface like a second, temporary flora — beads, sheets, a fine milk on the inside of every hollow — and the plants drink as if they have been waiting their whole lives for that exact minute. Then the sun again. Then another cell. The land does not change. The sky cannot stop.

Because the rock is so old it has been chewed into a kind of geology of flutes. The last stubborn knobs and hogbacks are riddled with holes the wind bored when it still had patience. Each cavity has a pitch. Together they make an instrument the size of a region. On a merely violent day the chord is a single continuous roar with harmonics you feel in your teeth. When a true storm crosses the table, the plateau becomes a throat. You do not hear individual sounds. You are inside one sound. Speech is a local custom that does not travel. People who live here — and a few still do — talk with their hands, or by setting small marked stones in sequences in the lee of a wall, or by touching forehead to forehead in the rare pockets of still air that form for minutes at a time behind a hump of rock. Children are taught early that listening is for weather, not for words. A change in the chord means a change in the sky. That is the useful information.

Vegetation should not be this lush and is. It refuses the open pavement, mostly. Out on the scoured flats there is only a low, aerodynamic carpet — not grass, something thicker, waxed, pressed flat by centuries of the same argument with air, a pelt of living matter you can walk on for a day and never see a stem rise higher than your ankle. Kneel and it is a jungle viewed from above: layered, wet in its understory, busy with the tiny work of holding water that arrived as mist and will leave as mist. Then, wherever the stone offers even a suggestion of shelter — the downhill lip of a shallow bowl, the interior of a flute-cave, the leeward face of one of the last remaining swells — the plants lose all shame. They hang. They stack. They drip. Curtains of leaf thicker than a body, mosses that have become furniture, air-drinkers with no interest in soil, pale roots feeling along the rock like blind fingers. In those pockets the air is a different country: warm still, but syrupy, green-smelling, loud with the drip of condensed water and the small ticking of growth. Step out of the pocket and the volume drops away into the roar and the emptiness again. Lushness here is not a blanket. It is a series of arguments won in secret.

Water has no career on the surface. There are no proper rivers. Anything that tried to be a river would be torn into spray or drunk by the honeycombed stone within a morning. Moisture is a visitor. It comes as those sudden cell-rains, as the great nocturnal sweats of condensation when the warm stone breathes against a colder scrape of air, as fog that arrives already shredded and still manages to soak you to the skin. In the flute-caves, people string gut and fiber and beaten bark into hanging gardens that do nothing but intercept fog. A good cave sings and drips at once. A family can live on what a single well-chosen hollow collects, if they are patient and if the weather does not change its mind about that hollow for too many seasons in a row.

The emptiness is horizontal and vertical both. You can walk from one green bowl to the next and spend half a day in a country of nobody and nothing standing, just the pelt and the stone and the enormous, ordinary daylight, which at this height is a clean, slightly merciless version of itself — shadows knife-edged, colors a little too themselves, the sky a thinner blue than lowland memory allows. Distance does not behave. A cairn you thought was an hour away is four, because there is no middle ground to help you count. The rim, on a clear stretch, is a line so far off it looks like a mistake in the seeing. Beyond it the world falls through stacked weathers you will never visit. Birds do not cross that edge often. The ones that live here are rare and mostly do not land: pale kites with bodies like slivers, riding the standing waves of air above the flutes, visible as glints and then not. On the ground there are rumors of animals that come out only in the sudden calms, which are themselves a kind of violence — the roar cutting off, the whole instrument stopping mid-note, your ears ringing in a silence so complete it feels like a new kind of noise. Whatever lives in those minutes is gone when the wind remembers itself. You find tracks that begin and end in the middle of bare stone. You find bones in the lee of things, scoured light as paper.

There are marks of people, faintly. Not towns. The wind would not permit a town the shape of a town. What persists are the things that agreed in advance to be worn: rings of fitted stone no higher than a kneeling adult, their outer faces already returning to the grammar of the plateau. Wind-houses dug into the larger flutes, their mouths baffled with hanging mats that must be replaced after every bad week. Lines of standing slabs, each with a hole bored through, so that on certain days the wind plays a row of notes in an order someone once cared about. Offerings in the smaller cavities — fiber, a tool, a tooth, a twist of dried leaf — put there either as thanks or as a way of asking the instrument to keep its present pitch a little longer. The tools are bone, stone, fire-hardened wood, the occasional piece of hammered native metal found in a vein the erosion finally confessed. Nothing that assumes a future in which the wind will be reasonable. Repair is the main technology. Listening is the main science.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is the slow kind, the kind you notice only after your own thoughts have started to take the plateau’s shape. The mist, in the brief minutes when it is thick enough to be a room, sometimes holds the impression of the last thing that walked through it, a few paces behind, fading. The plants in the deep hanging gardens move when the wind cannot reach them. Not much. A rearrangement. A turning of faces. The stone under your palm is warm and, if you wait, not entirely still; there is a pulse that is probably only your own blood learning the chord, and probably that. Time of day becomes a matter of opinion. Storms rewrite the light so often that noon can arrive three times, or not at all. People who stay too long begin to speak of the wind as a who, and then stop speaking of it, because speech is expensive and the who is obvious.

To cross the table is to accept that you are a small upright mistake in a place that solved uprightness a long time ago. You travel in the early warmth, when the stone has not yet fully woken and the first storms are still assembling themselves out along the rim. You sleep in whatever hollow has a pitch you can tolerate, mats over the mouth, the drip of harvested fog ticking into a bowl. You eat what the carpet yields if you know how to peel it, and what the hanging gardens give if someone before you was generous, and almost never meat. You learn the difference between the roar that means go and the roar that means get in the hole now. You learn that silence is not safety; it is the plateau inhaling.

And always the same finished land under you — rounded, pitted, finally itself — and always the unfinished sky tearing itself apart and putting itself back together as if the work had only just been invented. The oldest surface. The youngest weather. Between them, a few green secrets, a few holes full of song, and the long, warm, deafening vacancy in which a person is optional.
