The place is called the Upper Vat only by the plates still screwed to its outer doors. Inside it has no name, because nothing that lives here uses one.

It sits at the high end of a made mountain: a stacked mass of tanks, stair-cores, heat-houses and ballast halls, all of it fabricated, none of it bedrock. The mountain is young. You can still see the lifting lugs on the lowest courses, the bright unweathered lips of the flanges, the numbered match-marks stamped into every plate. Nothing has had time to soften. Edges stay edges. Welds keep their peacock tarnish. The whole summit was bolted together and then, as a last act of commissioning, filled.

Filled completely.

There is no airspace. The nave — if a nave is what you call a single volume the size of a stone church, ribbed with I-beams and glazed on three sides — is a warm solid of water. Not seawater. Process water: slightly sweet, faintly metallic, held at a patient blood-heat by exchanger banks that tick through their valves once or twice an hour. Light comes in as ordinary daylight through the high panels, then thickens, going green-gold, then green, then the colour of old bottle-glass in the deepest racks. Outside those same panels the weather is thin and bright. You are underwater at the altitude of raptors, looking out at empty air. Clouds pass *below* the sills.

Everything in here was built to be occupied. There is no substrate that was not first a drawing. The “floor” is a grating of punched stainless, now a false bottom three metres down, and under that another, and under that the true hull. Between the gratings the growers packed the crop.

The crop is what the work-orders still call vegetation, though the word is a courtesy. It is lush in the way a filled warehouse is lush. Stems as thick as wrists rise from clamp-collars. Leaves the size of hands and the size of doors and the size of sails occupy every interval the pipes left them. They are not wild. Each cultivar sits in its registered interval: oil-kelps on the mid racks, lacquer-weeds against the warm bulkheads, a purple bladder-moss that secretes a slow varnish used, elsewhere, to seal turbine housings. Tendrils have been trained through eye-bolts. Flowers — if they are flowers — open on a calendar, not a season, and they open underwater, white and waxed, releasing no scent the water will carry. There are no insects to come for them. There is no fish-shadow, no snail-track, no larval drift. The vat was sterilised at fill and has been kept that way. Life here is only the planted life, and the planted life has been given every cubic centimetre.

You cannot pass between things. You can only choose which friction to accept. A shoulder of leaf. A rib of conduit. The cold of a still-bright bolt-head. Density is the first law. The second is that the density was designed: a production density, a yield. Nothing here is decorative except by accident of closeness.

The machines do not hurry. Industrial, yes — you can read their makers on the castings, the same foundries that built the mountain — but they were specified for a long shift. Circulators turn so slowly the vortices they make are almost geological. Nutrient injectors pulse on a fortnightly tide. A harvest gantry hangs in its overhead rail like a dark rib, unmoved since the first flush was declared complete. Somewhere in the west transept a transformer hums at a pitch the water eats. What sound remains is small: the private click of a thermostat, a single bubble detaching from a weld and taking half a minute to decide which way is up, the almost-inaudible rasp of a leaf-edge against a cable when the whole volume flexes in the wind that exists only outside.

It is strange in the way a finished sentence is strange if no one is left to read it. The vat was built for attendants who never arrived, or who were designed and then cancelled. Along the nave hang empty work-frames, articulated, too many-limbed, sized for a body that would have breathed this water as easily as the plants do. Their harnesses still drip. No one has put them on. The plants have begun, in the months since seal, to use them as trellis. A lacquer-weed has filled a helmet. This is not decay. It is occupation according to the only remaining instruction, which is *grow until stopped*.

The light is ordinary and therefore wrong. Midday stands in the water as a pale column and does not sparkle; there is not enough free motion for sparkle. Toward the glazed wall the column flattens into sheets. You can hang in that sheet and see, through your own hanging, the real weather: a hard high sky, the shadow of the Vat’s own superstructure thrown down the made cliffs, a distant service road like a scratch. No bird crosses it. The emptiness outside rhymes with the emptiness inside. Only the plants, and the iron, and the warm green dark.

Change here is a patient profession. A leaf adds a millimetre. A bladder fills by a teaspoon over a week. The varnish-moss will, in a year, have to be scraped — the specs say so — and the scrapings sent down-slope to the works that have not yet been staffed. The mountain is too new for rust. The water is too young for stain. Given enough slow time the plants will close the last gaps and the Vat will become what it already almost is: a single packed organism with a steel skeleton, a forest that is also a machine, a room that is also a sea, raised up where seas do not belong, finished, waiting, holding its breath in a medium that has no breath in it.

If you stay long enough you stop listening for animals. The silence is not absence then. It is the sound of a process that was started cleanly and has not yet been given a reason to end.
