The highland does not remember being mountains. Whatever range once stood here finished its work so long ago that height itself has become a habit rather than a drama: a long, cold table of the world, open enough that weather arrives as a country, not a local event. You can walk for a day and the horizon will still be the same dull pewter line, except the line is never quite where you left it.

Underfoot the stone is gone. Not buried—metabolized. What remains is a living rind, thin in most places, a cold vegetal leather stretched over whatever still refuses to be eaten. It weeps. Everything weeps. The rind exudes a clear, slightly viscous water that never quite decides whether to freeze. Films of it hang in the dim air and drop without wind. Isolated bosses of older tissue—call them trees only if you have never seen a tree—stand kilometers apart, each one a slow factory of itself: ribbed columns, valve-clusters the size of halls, peristaltic ridges that tighten and slacken with a wet industrial sound, not loud close by, but carrying, so that the whole plateau ticks and sighs like a plant that learned engines and then forgot who taught it. There are no buildings. There were never buildings. The production is endogenous. Sap runs under pressure. Occasional sheets of bioluminescence gutter and fail in the dimness, more like a short in wet insulation than like light meant to see by.

Between the bosses the rind is sparse in the way a sea is sparse: present everywhere, occupying almost nothing. You can go an hour seeing only the sheen of water on living ground, a few pale uprights like broken masts, and the sky, which is not sky so much as a low, condensing lid. Sound gathers in that emptiness. Drip becomes percussion. The factories converse across distance. Something abundant lives here and you hear it more than you meet it—chitinous rustles in the water-film, flocks that are not birds crossing the dimness in tight, flickering sheets, lowing from herds that graze the rind and leave it already healing. The fauna is not scarce; the scale simply refuses to let any gathering look like a crowd. When a bloom comes, it comes as weather: a sudden darkening of the air with wings or spores or soft bodies, a roar that is half water and half mouths, and then the openness again, as if the plateau had inhaled them.

Cold is the grammar. Breath hangs and falls as another kind of dew. The water should be ice and is not, or is ice that flexes. The living rind stays pliant the way fat stays pliant in a butcher’s cold room. Heat, when it occurs, is local and mechanical—friction in a valve-cluster, a brief fever in a boss as it digests a seam of leftover mineral, a herd packing together until their own steam becomes a standing cloud. The atmosphere condenses on every surface and on the idea of surfaces. You walk in a permanent post-rain that has no rain.

The turbulence is older than the wearing-down. This is the settled form: not stillness, but a chaos that has had so long to practice it has become reliable. A stretch of rind will slough overnight into black water, then knit, then push up a new boss that was not there and will not be there next season. Storms are metabolic. The air thickens with uncompleted shapes—half-leaves, membranes, things with too many joints—that the plateau assembles and then thinks better of. Distances misfire. You approach a factory-organism for an hour and it is the same size; you look away and it is beside you, dripping, smelling of crushed chlorophyll and hot copper. Sounds arrive before their sources, or without them. A corridor of warmer air will open, industrial and wrong, full of the clatter of processes that have no machines, and then the cold will seal it as if it had been a rumor.

Light stays dim even at what should be noon. The condensing lid never burns off. What illumination there is seems generated as much by the water as by any sun—an even, gray-green wetness in which shadows are optional. In that light the breakdowns are easier to notice and harder to trust. A pool will show a sky that is not the sky above you. A boss will stand in two places. The fauna sometimes occupy the afterimages of themselves, grazing yesterday’s rind, leaving prints that fill with water that is already older than the prints. The place is not haunted. It is simply finished with the courtesy of single locations and single times. It has been this way long enough that instability is the bedrock.

No path persists. The living ground takes impressions and then eats them, or weeps them full, or grows a film across them that is already ancient. You are not meant to be here in the sense that nothing is meant; the plateau does not have purposes, only throughputs. Water in, tissue out, sound across the open, fauna rising and vanishing like a tide that forgot the moon. The factories pulse. The rind shines. Somewhere very far away another boss answers, a deep vegetal clang, and the whole high country, worn almost flat and still somehow above everything, continues the work it has always been doing: remaining, barely, while changing too fast to be mapped.

At the marsh-edges of itself—though the whole of it is marsh-edge—the water stands deeper and the rind thins to a meniscus. Things come up through it that are neither plant nor animal nor weather, hold a shape for the length of a breath, and go back under. The sound there is louder, a constant pouring that has no fall. You can believe, for a little while, that this is the oldest possible world, the one that outlasted its own geology and then its own physics, and settled at last into this cold, dripping, half-empty manufacture of itself, open as a rumor, sparse as a lung, and never, even for a second, still.
