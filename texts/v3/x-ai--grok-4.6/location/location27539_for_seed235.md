The plateau does not end. It only thins, then thickens again, a series of lips and hanging shelves that recede into heat-haze the color of old brass. You are already inside the city when you notice you have been walking on a roof. The roof is also a street, also a canal, also the crown of a single living wall that has been depositing itself, layer on wet layer, since before the idea of streets.

Hot water comes out of everything. It beads on the undersides of fronds the size of barge sails, runs in braided sheets down faces of travertine that were never carved, only allowed. The stone is warm as blood. Every surface weeps. The air itself condenses a handspan in front of your face and falls as a fine, continuous rain that has no beginning and will have no end. There is no weather here, only this: saturation held at the exact point of overflow, forever.

The structures are not built. They are precipitated. Mineral-rich water, rising from somewhere far below the extreme elevation, has been laying down terraces, colonnades, amphitheaters, and nested courtyards for so long that the forms have found an equilibrium with the plants that occupy them. Aerial roots have become load-bearing. Moss has become mortar, then masonry, then moss again. A tower is a single cycad that calcified while it grew, its crown still putting out new fronds that instantly begin to stone. Another “building” is a hollow waterfall, the water having deposited a shell around its own descent until the fall became a spiral staircase you can walk, though the water continues down the inside, hot, deafening, unvarying.

There are no animals. Not a gnat, not a scale, not the memory of a wing. The lushness is total and silent of life that moves. What fills the volume instead is plant body pressed against plant body, wet stone against wet stone, steam against the next terrace over. Density without crowding, because nothing here has ever needed to make room for anything that breathes or flees.

The sound is the water, all of it, at once. It is not a roar that rises and falls. It is a single, finished chord of white pressure, so complete it has no texture left to change. It occupies the skull the way the heat occupies the lungs. After a time you stop hearing it as sound and begin to feel it as the medium you are standing in, the thing the city is actually made of. It has always been this loud. It will not get louder. The equilibrium is perfect; the falls do not pulse, the steam vents do not gutter, the sheets of water do not thicken or thin. Motion without variation. A world that learned how to pour and then forgot how to stop, or how to start differently.

Light is ordinary in color and yet wrong in behavior. It arrives as if from a sun that has been nailed in place just off zenith, a flat gold-green that never lengthens a shadow. The light obeys the terraces more than it obeys distance. It pools in courtyards that should be dark and refuses the open shelves that face the void. Sometimes it stands in the air as a column, independently of any surface, a still shaft of illumination the plants have grown around as if it were another trunk. You can walk through it. It does not flicker.

Geometry here is a settled argument, not an ongoing one. Streets return you to themselves after the correct number of turns, except the “correct” number is not the number you counted. A plaza is larger inside than the terrace that contains it, and this has always been true; the discrepancy is as weathered as the stone. Staircases descend into the same landing they left. Avenues fork and both forks are the original avenue, equally wet, equally loud, equally lined with the same sequence of weeping arches. The breakdowns do not shift. They are as established as the erosion that rounded every corner a million identical years ago. Reality here is not coming apart. It came apart once, thoroughly, and then fossilized in the new arrangement.

From any high lip you can see the rest of it: district after district of the same logic, hanging gardens that are also aqueducts that are also cliffs, receding until the plateau’s own curvature (or the failure of curvature) takes them. The scale is civic, regional, more than a city and less than a country, a single continuous settlement that never needed a name because nothing here has ever arrived or departed. No tools marked it. No one stacked these blocks. The mountain simply continued its old habit of making terraces, and the habit, at this elevation, in this heat, with this much water, produced rooms, processional ways, cisterns that are also thrones, windows that look into other windows that look back.

The plants do not grow. They occupy. A fern unfurls in the same position it has always unfurled, droplets held at the exact same points along each pinnule. Vines have found every joint and remain in those joints, flowering with blossoms that are already browning, already falling, already replaced by the identical blossom an inch to the left that is also this blossom. Time is not passing; it is displayed, like the water, like the light. The whole plateau is a completed sentence that the world has not stopped pronouncing.

You can walk for days of subjective effort and the heat will not change, the roar will not change, the distance to the next hanging shelf will not change in quality even when the numbers say it must. The city does not remember being otherwise. It does not anticipate. It is the shape that water, stone, leaf, and a broken rule of space made when they were finally left alone long enough to finish.
