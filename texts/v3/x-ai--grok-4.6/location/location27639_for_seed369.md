The bowl is no larger than a nave. You could shout across it and be heard twice—once from your mouth and once from the wet stone answering back—but you would not shout. The place already speaks.

It sits high, not in cold, but in the warm shoulder of a mountain that never quite learned how to be alpine. The air arriving from below is thick and sweet; it cools just enough, here, to wring itself out. There is no storm required. Water is simply the habit of the rock. It beads on every face, gathers in hairline channels cut so long ago they have forgotten whether weather or a hand first opened them, and then it falls. Not in one cataract. In a patient congregation of falls: threads, sheets no thicker than cloth, fat slow drops that strike the same dark pits they have been striking since the stone was still deciding its shape.

The rock itself is pale, almost flesh-colored when dry, which it never is. Where water has run for centuries the color goes to iron and old tea. Lichen maps the drier shoulders in dull silver. Everything else shines. The floor of the bowl is not soil so much as a shallow, trembling skin of peat and powdered stone, always wet to the ankle, never quite a marsh, never quite a floor. Step and the ground accepts you with a sound like a held breath released. Step again and the first print is already filling.

Vegetation here is lush the way a few candles can be lush in an empty chapel. There is space between everything. Four, perhaps five, great lives hold the place.

A tree-fern the height of a house, its trunk a column of old leaf-bases and dripping moss, its crown a single horizontal thought. It does not compete. Nothing stands near enough to compete. Ten paces off, a plant that is mostly leaf—leaves the size of doors, ribbed, slightly serrated, each one a catchment, each one loudly and continuously draining into the next. Between them, emptiness you could lie down in. Farther, against the back wall where the weep is strongest, a hanging garden of liverwort and filmy fern so dense it looks like one organism wearing the cliff. And near the lip of the bowl, where the light is least interrupted, a single flowering tree whose bark is the color of wet ash. Its flowers are small, white, almost ashamed of themselves, and they do not come all at once. One opens. Days later, another. The air around it is always faintly sweet and always a little too still.

Roots do not so much seek water as wait for it. Everything here has learned that water will come to the surface; there is no need to go down. The peat is a sponge of pale root-hair and something that is not quite mycelium, a slow silver webbing you only see when you lift a fallen frond. Signals, if they travel at all, travel as wetness, as a change in the pitch of drip, as one leaf lifting a fraction because another, across the bowl, has begun to flag. This takes time. Time is what the place has.

Light is ordinary daylight made extraordinary only by what it has to pass through: a standing gauze of mist that never quite becomes fog, never quite burns off. Midday is bright enough to throw the falling water into brief glass. Late afternoon the whole bowl goes gold-green and the wet rock looks edible. Night would be its own country, but the description does not stay for night; even so you can feel how the stone would hold the day’s warmth and how the dripping would become the only clock.

Sound is the argument against the sparseness. There is almost no one here and almost nothing that walks, and still the bowl is loud. Water on stone, water on leaf, water on water. A dozen pitches at once, none of them quite a note, all of them together a chord that never resolves. Then the wind—daily, late, coming up the mountain’s warm flank as if late for something—finds the hollows. The rock is fluted. Some of the fluting is honest weather. Some of it, if you put your hand in and feel the regularity, is not. A few notches too clean, a few lips too even, cut once with something harder than fingernails and softer than steel, then left for a thousand wet seasons to forget the difference. When the wind hits those mouths the bowl becomes an instrument. Not music. A long, changing roar with holes in it, like breath through a shell the size of a room. It lasts as long as the wind lasts, which is not long, and then the dripping is deafening again by comparison.

Animals are a rumor the place tells badly. Once, a pale crab the size of a thumbnail, the same color as the wet limestone, lifting itself over a rim of moss and vanishing as if the rock had taken it back. Once, something larger in the hanging garden that was probably only the garden rearranging its own weight. Birds cross the open sky above the rim and do not come down. There are no insects in any number that would make sense. The lushness has an audience of almost no one. This is part of the strangeness: a wet, warm, green room that should tick and whirr and instead only pours.

What is strange is not theatrical. It will not startle you. It is the slowness with which your own breathing begins to match the interval between the heavier drops. It is the way sound lingers a half-second too long, as if the mist were reluctant to let go of it. It is the faint geometric stubbornness of the great plants—each one holding a territory of empty air with a completeness that feels decided rather than grown. It is the suspicion, never confirmed, that the water in the deepest pit at the bowl’s center is not only falling in but circulating, a slow invisible gyre, the same molecules coming past again after a time too long to measure by standing there.

No one lives here. No one has built. What remains of use is indistinguishable from wear: those few tuned lips of stone; a place near the fern where the peat is firmer, as if knees had found it often and then not at all for a long time; a twist of fiber, plant, not hair, bleaching in a cleft, which might be an offering or might be a root that lost its way. Primitive, if it is anything—hands, edges, patience. The mountain has accepted the marks the way it accepts everything, by weeping over them until they belong.

Change here is a profession, not an event. The channels deepen by a width you will not live to see. A leaf of the great catchment plant will yellow over a week and take another week to let go. The flowering tree will open seven blossoms between the first warm night of the year and the last. Mist will thicken and thin on a cycle older than the notches in the rock. If you stayed a season you would learn the bowl’s one haste: the afternoon wind, which arrives, plays the stone, and leaves, like a visitor who has always done exactly this and will not be persuaded to do more.

Stand in the center. The falls stitch the air to the ground. The few green presences hold their distances. Warmth comes up through the wet and down from the ordinary sky. There is room enough to be alone without being small, and noise enough to be accompanied without being joined. The place is finished with becoming and has not yet begun to end. It weeps, and it waits, and the waiting is loud.
