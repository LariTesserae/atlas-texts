The mountain does not peak so much as open.

You come along a ridge no wider than a goat’s decision and the ground simply ceases to be a back and becomes a lip. What lies inside is not a valley and not a crater in the volcanic sense; it is a throat the ice finished carving a long time ago and then abandoned, a single hollow of weather-smoothed stone about the size of a great nave and its side-chapels, perhaps two hundred paces across the floor, a little more from rim to rim if you count the inward-leaning walls. That is the whole of it. There is no hinterland. The world beyond the rim is wind and distance and other summits. This place has a boundary you can walk in an hour if you keep to the ledges, and a volume that is almost entirely unused air.

The emptiness is the first fact. The cirque is a bowl of cool vacancy. Light—ordinary mountain daylight, neither milked nor gilded—pours in over the eastern notch in the morning and climbs the opposite wall in a slow pale sheet. Clouds use the same notch later, and often. They do not fill the hollow so much as test it, pouring over the lip like a slow white animal and then tearing themselves to rags on the inner cliffs. Between clouds the space is clear enough to see a hawk cross from one rim to the other without a wingbeat of effort, and the hawk looks small, which tells you the truth of the scale: cathedral, not country. A shout from the floor reaches the rim intact and comes back slightly rearranged.

Water is not a lake. Water is the habit of the rock. The walls weep. They have wept so long the streaks have become architecture—dark vertical scripts of mineral, black where iron, pale where lime, green-black where the seep never stops and the moss has taken hold in felted ropes. After the daily cloud the weeping quickens; you can hear it as a thousand separate ticks becoming a single sheet-sound, not quite a fall, more like a building exhaling. On the floor the water does not gather into anything you would call a tarn so much as a skin. The ground is a sponge of sedge and sphagnum and fine black grit, saturated a hand’s depth down, cool as a cellar. Step and the print fills behind you. There are brighter seams where the seeps braid into rills no wider than a wrist, and these rills find the one low place—the western notch, a broken tooth in the rim—and leave, dropping into a gorge you cannot see from here. The leaving is audible. The arriving is audible. The staying is a glisten.

It is cool. Not the knife of true alpine night, not the torpor of snowline. A held cool, like a room that has never known a fire and does not miss one. Breath shows in the early hours and again when cloud occupies the bowl. Hands go stiff if you work stone too long. The plants have accepted this: nothing deciduous in the lowland sense, nothing that bets on a long summer. What grows is lush in the way a wet wall is lush, not in the way a jungle is. The floor is a tight, living carpet—cushion plants, dwarf shrubs no higher than the knee, mosses thick enough to hide a fist, small flowering things that come in flushes after the wetter weeks, white and sulfur and a blue so dense it looks like a hole. On the walls the hanging gardens begin wherever a ledge holds a palm of grit. Ferns. Liverworts. Pale orchids no larger than a fingernail, in hundreds, in thousands, so that certain seams look frosted. Higher, in the lee of overhangs, there are woody things that have learned to grow down: stems like rope, leaves like small leather coins, always wet on the underside.

The form is old and finished. You can feel the settledness. Talus has found its angle of rest in three long aprons; nothing large has moved in a long time except grain by grain. The rock itself is banded—old sediment stood on end, then planed by ice, then fretted by freeze and seep until the faces look less quarried than worn by thought. Edges are rounded. Niches are deep. The cirque is not falling apart and not being born. It is in its own state, the way a worn stair is in its own state. Weather works it, but weather has been working it since before anyone stacked a stone here, and the stones that have been stacked know it.

Those stones are few. On a shelf above the wettest wall someone built a windbreak of unmortared blocks, low, the height of a kneeling person, its back to the prevailing inflow of cloud. The blocks are the same rock as the walls; they have begun to weep in sympathy. Inside: a floor of packed grit, a ring of ash so old it is only a stain, a niche with a lip of clay where a lamp or a bowl once sat. Scattered elsewhere—on the approach ridge, on two of the more trustworthy ledges, at the outflow notch—are cairns no taller than a child, and one line of steps pecked into a rib of harder stone, each tread scooped by use and by the same water that scoops everything. There is a post of weathered wood at the eastern notch, gray as bone, lashed with something that was once cordage and is now a memory of twists. From it hang three things that still move: a blade of hammered copper gone green, a fan of dark feathers, a small hanging board of unknown purpose that knocks when the wind finds it. That is the built world. Hand-tools made it. Hands still maintain the cairns, or some hands do, not often. You will not find metal beyond that one green blade. You will not find a roof that keeps.

The emptiness of the volume and the abundance of life are not a contradiction once you understand where life is allowed to be. Nothing claims the air for long. The air is a transit. But the skin of the place—every ledge, every seep, every cushion of moss, every crack that holds heat for an hour at noon—is occupied.

Birds first, and most. A colony of swifts uses the highest overhangs on the north wall, a dark stippling that resolves, when they erupt, into a scream of wings. They do not erupt once. They erupt whenever a shadow crosses, whenever cloud pours, whenever evening cools the bowl two degrees. Their sound is a blade drawn along the inside of the skull; in this hollow it becomes architecture. Choughs keep the rims, red-legged, insolent, riding the updraft that the sun pulls off the eastern face. Smaller things work the carpet: a pale pipit, a wren that has no business at this height and lives here anyway in the wettest ferns. At dusk the swifts come back in such numbers the air seems briefly solid, and then they are holes in the rock again, and the loudness they leave behind is only water and wind—which is still loud.

The wind is almost never still. It does not blow through so much as circle, finding the notches, making the bowl an instrument. There is a note, a true one, when the inflow is steady from the east and the outflow is clear: a low, continuous vowel you feel in the floor. Gusts add consonants. The hanging board knocks. The copper blade ticks against the post. Meltwater, even in this cool, has a voice in the rills—not roar, but a close, busy speaking, like many people just on the other side of a wall. After cloud, the weeping becomes a applause that does not end, only thins. In the spongy floor there are frogs. You would not expect frogs. They are small, the color of wet stone, and they sing at a pitch that should be delicate and is not, because there are so many, and because the bowl takes their song and stacks it. The first time you hear the frogs start you will look for a mechanism, some hidden water-engine. There is none. It is only throats, and moss, and an amphitheater that has been waiting.

Other animals keep to the margins and the hours. A hoofed thing the size of a large dog—short, dense, the face too straight—uses the ledges at dawn, leaving berry-dark droppings on the steps. Something otter-shaped works the outflow at night; you find the clean hinges of frogs and the occasional feather. Insects are a weather of their own when the light is good and the cloud has lifted: midges in columns over the wettest shine, beetles like drops of tar on the cushion plants, a moth so pale it reads as a petal until it moves. None of this fills the space. All of it keeps the space from being dead. The density is still near zero if you measure volume. If you measure attention, the place is crowded.

The unusual thing—the thing that is only a little wrong—is how the hollow keeps what enters it. Scent hangs. A word spoken on the floor can be met again, thinner, on a ledge you reach a quarter-hour later, as if the rock were still deciding whether to give it back. Cloud that has poured in will sometimes refuse the notches and rotate, a slow room of white, condensing on every cold face, feeding the weeping until the walls look varnished. On certain still twilights the frogs and the last swifts and the rills and the hanging board find a common tempo, not a rhythm you could count, more like a shared nervous system. It passes. The place is not enchanted so much as acoustically and hydrologically sincere. It has one job, which is to be a bowl, and it does that job without remainder.

Days have a plot. Morning is a clean pan of light and the hoofed animals and the first loud declaration of the swifts. Midday often brings the cloud-animal over the eastern notch; the temperature drops a notch with it; the weeping thickens; the birds go in and out of visibility like thoughts. If the cloud stays, the world shrinks to drips and the green of near moss and the sound of your own jacket. If it tears, the far wall reappears wetter than it left, shining, and the choughs complain as if someone had rearranged their furniture. Late day the outflow speaks louder—more melt, more seep arriving at once—and the frogs rehearse. Night is not silent. Night is the vowel of the wind and the frogs at full stacked chorus and, sometimes, a sound like cloth torn slowly, which is ice you cannot see releasing from a high seam. Things move on the ledges you will not see. The ash-stain in the windbreak, if you are in it, seems to remember fire.

There are rules, if you stay long enough to need them. Do not trust the carpet near the brightest seeps; the sponge is deeper there and the grit has no bottom you will like. The pecked steps are safer wet than the clean-looking ribs, which polish themselves into lies. The swifts will tell you when weather is about to occupy the bowl—their eruption has a particular dense quality, a decision. Offerings left at the post do not stay; the choughs are theologians of a sort. If you add a stone to a cairn, use a stone that is already loose. The walls have opinions about being quarried. Water you can drink from the higher seeps, where it first leaves the rock; by the time it is a rill on the floor it has walked through too much life. Fire, if you make one in the windbreak, will be seen from rims you cannot see, and will be commented on by every throat in the colony. The hollow takes sides with neither visitor nor resident. It takes the side of being a hollow.

Stay a week and the place becomes less a view than a climate you wear. Your hearing changes; you start to know which notch the wind is using by the pitch of the vowel. You learn the three mosses that mean a ledge will hold, and the one that means it is only a stain of green on a slant you will not walk twice. You find, in a niche no larger than a bread oven, a cache of small carved things—bone, soapstone, a seed-pod fitted with a stopper—left by someone who understood primitive the way this mountain understands water: as a complete technology. You do not take them. You add nothing that cannot weather into the same sentence as the copper blade and the feathers.

The cirque does not care. It weeps. It shouts with borrowed throats. It holds a cathedral of unused air over a floor that is almost marsh, under rims that are almost sky, at a height where the world has already become simple, and it does this as it has done it since the ice put down its tools and left the work finished. On the ridge you can look back and see nothing but a notch and a suggestion of depth. Inside, every surface is busy and the space between surfaces is a ringing vacancy, loud as a market, empty as a lung at the top of its breath, lush only where a finger could lie, wet without being a sea, cool without kindness, old without ruin. That is the whole of it. That is enough.
