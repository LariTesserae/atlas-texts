The plateau does not end so much as it thins. You come up out of the switchbacks into a country already half water, already half work, and the first thing that takes you is not the view but the noise—a single, unrelieved pressure that has been here longer than any living memory of silence. It is not a city shouting. It is a city that has become a fall.

From the old survey cairn on the western lip you can see most of it at once, which is the point of the height and also its cruelty. The highland runs twenty miles end to end, a worn table of pale, weeping stone, and the settlement is laid across it the way rust is laid across a blade: thoroughly, without hurry, leaving large honest gaps. Between the works there are acres of wet flag and shallow meres the color of weak tea. The buildings do not huddle. They stand off from one another as if each were a fact that needed room to be believed. Chimneys, cooling towers, the long low sheds of the pump halls, the stepped weirs, the black iron galleries—each has weather in it, each has been standing in the same rain for so long that the rain has learned their shapes.

The water is not dramatic in the tourist sense. There is no single cataract to point at. The plateau is saturated. Groundwater stands a handspan beneath the paving almost everywhere, and in the low courts it stands above. The stone itself perspires. Cut faces of the older quarries shine as if freshly washed, which they always are. Gutters are not accessories; they are the streets’ first citizens, cut deep, lined with glazed brick the color of old bone, and they run full in every season because the seasons here are only different intensities of the same wet. Warmth keeps the water moving. This is not a cold height. The air is heavy, bath-warm, and when the wind drops the mist does not lift so much as reorganize itself around the next obstacle. You taste metal and peat and the faint sweetness of heated pipe-oil.

What grows, grows as an afterthought. Liverwort in the joints. A grass that looks like wire and holds no color. On the leeward sides of the cooling towers, in the constant steam-shadow, a black moss has taken whole elevations and made them look furred, like the flanks of something sleeping standing up. No trees of any conviction. A few willow-things have been planted in the civic squares and they lean, always, as if listening for a drier instruction that does not come. Their leaves are small and they drop early and they make no sound worth mentioning under the rest of the sound.

The sound. It is necessary to say this plainly. The plateau is a sounding board, and the city has spent two hundred years teaching it one note. Water over the stepped weirs. Water through the Archimedean screws that never stop because stopping them would mean admitting the water table as an enemy instead of a partner. Steam through relief valves set to a pressure that was calculated by a dead engineer and has not been recalculated because the calculation still holds. The great fans in the drying halls. The belt-roads, those slow iron rivers that carry ore-slurry and pressed brick and sacks of precipitated salt from one end of the works to the other, clicking at a pace you could walk beside if walking beside them did not mean going deaf faster. All of it together is not music and is not chaos. It is a climate. People who are born here do not flinch. Their speech, when they use it, is short and aimed at the teeth; anything finer is wasted. Visitors last three days before they start dreaming in drone. After a week the quiet of the lower valleys feels like a theft.

The works themselves are industrial in the old, settled sense—no glitter of new process, no clean rooms. Brick the color of dried blood. Iron painted and repainted until the paint is a geology. Glass in the high clerestories that has gone milky from a century of mineral spray. Inside the pump halls the machines are the size of cottages and they have names, not numbers, and the names are of women who have been dead long enough that the names have come around again in the living. The pistons rise and fall with a patience that is almost agricultural. You can set a watch by some of them and a calendar by others. Change happens, but it happens the way a river changes its bed: by insisting, for years, in the same direction. A new gantry goes up and looks immediately older than it is because the air ages everything on contact. A chimney is decommissioned and left standing because taking it down would be louder, in the long accounting, than leaving it to drip.

There are people. Not many, for the size of what they tend. You see them at shift-change on the high catwalks, oilskins black and shining, faces turned down against the perpetual fine rain that is sometimes rain and sometimes only the city condensing on itself. They move between the widely spaced halls on the belt-road’s pedestrian ledges or in the low electric drays that hiss along the wet stone like resigned animals. Windows are lit in the long barracks-terraces that back against the northern scarp, a modest constellation, never crowded. Children play in the shallow flood-courts with boats made of tin and cork; their voices do not carry. At the far eastern verge, where the plateau finally consents to drop, the old civic quarter sits a little higher on its own worn knoll—council house, a covered market that smells of vinegar and warm grain, a temple whose bell was taken down forty years ago because it could not be heard and the empty bellcote has since filled with the same black moss. The temple keeps hours anyway. The hours are a courtesy the noise does not return.

Animals are rumor. A pale dog sometimes at the slurry gates. Water-rats in the deeper culverts, said to be blind, said to be the size of cats, said to sing when the big screws change pitch, which they do twice a year by design. Birds cross, they do not stay; the air is too thick and the ledges too loud. Once in a generation someone swears to a heron standing in the Mere of Three Chimneys as if it had mistaken the place for a proper marsh, and then the heron is gone and the story is added to the others and no one bothers to decide if it is true.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a quality of duration. Live here long enough and you begin to feel that the city is not built on the water table so much as precipitated out of it, the way scale precipitates inside a kettle—the brick and iron an inevitable mineral of this particular wetness, this particular heat, this height. The older walls have a bloom on them that is not quite salt and not quite life. If you put your palm to the stone in the deep pump levels you can feel a tick that is not the nearest piston. Some of the weirs have worn not into the profiles the engineers drew but into profiles that look, in certain slants of the ordinary gray daylight, like mouths considering speech. No one makes a religion of this. It is only that the place has been itself for so long that itself has started to include a few extra rooms.

Light is the light of a day that cannot quite commit to being a day. The sky is usually a close, luminous pewter. Shadows are soft and multiple. When the sun does take the place fully—midafternoon, two or three times a month—the wet stone throws up a glare that makes the whole plateau look briefly like a single instrument left out in a field, and the noise seems, impossibly, to brighten with it. Then the vapor closes again and the glare becomes a memory of glare and the work continues at the speed it has always continued, which is the speed of water finding the next lower place, and the next, and the next, forever willing, never spent.

At night the towers are marked by red obstruction lamps that bleed in the mist. The weirs go on. Someone walks a long gallery with a lantern because the electric has failed in that wing the way it fails, slowly, predictably, in that wing. The mere waters hold the lamps and double them, and if you stand on the western lip again you can no longer tell the city from its reflection, only that both of them are loud, and warm, and in no hurry to become anything else.
