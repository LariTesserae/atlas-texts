The highland does not begin so much as it is already everywhere once you are in it. There is no approach that feels like arrival. The air thins first, then the light, then the sense that solid ground ought to be mineral. What remains is cold, a damp that never quite becomes rain, and a darkness so complete it has weather of its own.

This is a young place. The living mass that constitutes the entire substrate—every ridge, every floor, every impossible vault—has not yet learned the patience of erosion. Edges stay knife-sharp. Fresh splits run for tens of kilometers, still weeping a slow, viscous sap that freezes into dark glass before it can pool. The plant-body (one organism, or a conspiracy of them so intergrown the distinction is meaningless) has risen recently, shoved upward by whatever internal logic replaces tectonics here. It formed the way a bruise forms, or a fever: rapidly, completely, without asking permission of the older stone it buried or consumed. The result is a continent of vegetation that is also a continent of voids.

From any vantage the density reads as emptiness. The tissue itself is total. There is no bare rock, no soil that is not already root and compressed pith and the packed ghosts of earlier leaves. Plant is the architecture and the ground and the weather-maker. Yet the volumes it encloses are so vast that entire storm systems are born, mature, and die without ever touching a wall. Chambers open into chambers the way lungs open, except the alveoli are the size of drowned valleys and the bronchi are canyons whose ceilings have their own climates. A traveler could walk for days across a single floor of interlocked cambium and never see the far side, only feel the slow concavity of it, the way the living surface rises almost imperceptibly toward walls that remain rumors. Between these hollows the connecting membranes are thin as geography allows: curtains of living wood a hundred meters thick that nevertheless register as gossamer at this scale. Most of the highland is air. Cold air. Dark air. Air that tastes faintly of tannin and iron and the metallic sweetness of condensation.

Light is a hypothesis. What little arrives from whatever distant or occluded sun this world possesses is swallowed by the upper growths long before it can descend. The interiors keep a darkness that is not merely absence but a positive quality, a pressure. Occasional bioluminescent films—unusual, patchy, never reliable—stain a wall the color of old bruises or give a hanging root the faint outline of a drowned cathedral. They do not illuminate so much as they confess that illumination once existed as an idea. Eyes, if you have them, become ornamental. Navigation happens by sound, by the changing texture of the air on skin, by the way the living walls tick and flex.

The damp is intermittent and therefore precious. Moisture collects on the colder faces of the plant-mass as rime, as sudden fogs that uncoil from hidden stomata the size of caves, as brief rains that are more like the highland sweating. These rains last minutes or hours and then the water is gone—drunk by the walls, frozen into veils that later slump and shatter with a sound like distant applause, or gathered into meres so still and black they seem like holes in the world rather than surfaces. There are no true rivers, only threads that appear after a condensation event, run for a while along grooves the plant itself seems to have grown for them, and then vanish back into the body. In the lowest folds the air stays wet enough that every surface weeps. Elsewhere the cold keeps the water disciplined, a guest that never quite unpacks.

Sound occupies the emptiness the way the plant-mass occupies the idea of land. The highland is loud. Wind is the oldest musician. It finds every aperture, every fresh split, every hollow obelisk of living wood that spikes upward still sharp with new growth, and it plays them. There are drones that last from one false dusk to the next, notes so low they are felt in the joints and in the sap. When a new fissure opens—and they open often, the mass still deciding its shape—the report travels for leagues, a tearing that becomes a roar that becomes a silence that is itself a kind of chord. Drips, in the wetter chambers, turn the dark into percussion. The plant-body groans as it grows, a sound like ships straining in a slow sea. And then there is the fauna.

Life here is abundant in the concentrated way of oases or of the spaces between ribs. It does not fill the volumes; it ornaments the membranes, the damp pockets, the meres, the hanging gardens of specialized leaves that never see light and have stopped pretending they need to. Things cling. Things scrape the walls for the sluggish sugars. Things with resonant chambers in their bodies sing to one another across distances that would beggar ordinary lungs; their choruses rise and fall like weather fronts. Swarms move through the greater hollows as coherent darknesses, clicking or humming, harvesting the slow snow of shed scales and pollen that the highland continually sloughs. Predators are mostly stillness followed by geometry: a sudden rearrangement of limbs, a drop from a ceiling that was not, a moment ago, a ceiling. Everything ultimately eats the plant-mass or eats something that does. There is no other currency. The food web is a single, vast, cold conversation with the highland itself.

Here and there, rarely enough to feel like punctuation rather than text, are the built things. A platform of lashed, still-living stems jutting from a wall, its lashings long since half-healed into the supporting tissue so that the structure is now more gall than artifact. A low ring of hardened exudate, shaped by hands or something like hands, its purpose no longer obvious—perhaps a windbreak, perhaps a calendar that tracked the rare rains, perhaps nothing. Simple blades of split wood left in niches, the plant already trying to close over the cuts, incorporating the tool back into itself. Fiber ropes gone slack and furred with frost. The technology is the technology of the first thought after hunger: cut, bind, endure. Whoever made these marks is not in evidence, or they are among the singing things, or they passed through and the highland, being young and busy with its own becoming, filed them under weather.

Nothing here is still, even when the cold and the dark conspire to suggest stillness. The plant-mass breathes on a scale of years, walls advancing or receding by meters, new vaults swelling into existence like bruises in reverse. Weather cells nucleate in the atriums, spin up their own brief winters, collapse. The fauna is always mid-migration, mid-chorus, mid-ambush. Condensation events sweep through like itinerant gods, leaving the meres refilled and the air briefly, impossibly, loud with the sound of water remembering it can fall. The highland is an ecosystem that has decided to be a world, and it is still in the early, impolite stages of that decision: sharp, hollow, damp in pockets, dark almost everywhere, and so alive that the emptiness itself feels like another organ.

You do not so much inhabit this place as you are permitted, briefly, to move along its grain. The air remembers every sound you make and returns it changed. The walls are never quite where the last visit left them. Time is told by the thickening of rings you can walk inside, by the shifting routes of the singing swarms, by the rare and local rains. There is no outside that matters. The living mass goes on, high and cold and mostly void, forever in the act of finishing itself and never finishing.
