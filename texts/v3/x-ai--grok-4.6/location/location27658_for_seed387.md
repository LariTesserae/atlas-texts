You would miss the flue if you did not already know the count of rivets. It is a vertical scar in basalt, then in poured industrial mix the color of old tea, no wider than a man’s shoulders, drop after drop of dark until the shaft itself is full. There is no meniscus worth naming at first, only a thickening, a warmth climbing the wrists, and then you are in it: water as the only climate.

It is fever-warm. Not the clean heat of a spring. The heat of something that has been working too long in a closed room. It soaks the suit, then the idea of skin. Below, the flue ends the way a throat ends—an abrupt, circular opening into a volume that does not feel like a cave and does not feel like a hall. It feels like a held breath that learned masonry.

The vault is a single room. That is the whole country. Ceiling lost in brown twilight, floor farther still, and between them a quantity of water so large that structures become rumors of themselves. What was built here clings. Gantries stitch the walls at unhappy intervals. Tension cables cross the emptiness and go slack, then taut, then slack again as the current changes its mind. From the cables hang the town: stacked barracks of riveted plate, a market deck that never needed weatherproofing, a clock that is also a pressure stack, streetlamps crusted into dull coins, a row of lock-houses like teeth. It is urban in the way a ship is urban, or a mine: every surface assigned, every corridor named in stencil that the water has half-erased, infrastructure layered on infrastructure until habitation and machine are the same grammar. There are streets. They are suggestions now—pipes you can follow with a hand, old rail that once guided diving sleds, a plaza whose flagstones you find only by kicking.

Most of the volume is nothing. You can swim until the lamps behind you are a rumor and the next barrack is still a darker rectangle in the dark. Density here is a moral condition: almost nothing per cubic meter, vast patient gaps, a hollowness that the builders either planned for or learned to live inside. The town is a settlement-scale fact occupying a pocket of almost-void. It does not sprawl. It perches.

There are no plants. Nothing green has ever been argued into this water. What grows is mineral and simpler: iron-oxide antlers from every bolt, calcite veils on the warmest exchangers, bacterial leathers the color of old ivory where the pipes run hottest. In the slack behind a generator housing a white branching thing holds still. It does not photosynthesize. It may not be alive in any ledger you were taught. It is not common. You will not see another.

Light is a failure that persists. Sodium ghosts behind mineral scum. The dull cherry of overworked metal seen through meters of tea-brown water. Sometimes a sealed transformer room, itself drowned, leaks a sick green through a porthole. Your own beam dies at twenty meters and becomes a cone of rust flour. There has never been a sun in this room. The idea of day is a maintenance schedule.

Sound is the architecture. The vault is a bell and every valve is a mouth. Turbine throats the size of chapels tear the water continuously; cavitation screams in frequencies that remove the middle of hearing; when two currents collide they clap like steel doors in a storm. Metal fatigue has a choir. Pressure-relief speaks in white thunder that has no interval. After a short time you do not hear so much as occupy a single, total tone. Conversation is a theory. The place has one weather and the weather is this noise. It is old noise. It has the settled, balanced quality of a river that has worn its own bed for a century and does not intend to stop.

Because the water is not still. This is the first and last law. Inflow from fractures that smell of boiled stone. Outflow through the turbines because something above—something you will never see—still wants the power, or because no one with authority has died in the correct office. Waste heat dumps from exchangers that have not been asked a question in living memory. Convection turns the chamber over on itself. Standing waves climb the barracks and leave their silt line higher each year. Whirlpools organize over the plaza, forget themselves, organize again. Fronts of ground metal and rust-snow blow through like weather, sudden and brown-black, scouring the stenciled names off the lock-houses and depositing them, illegible, on the next gantry. The forms of the town are established—softened corners, concrete given the patience of long immersion, every industrial angle rounded by the same hammer—but the medium is storm. The room is in balance with its own chaos. The storm is not an event. The storm is how the room remains a room.

Rarely, a shape at an intake screen: pale, jointed, forearm-length, a problem the old manuals filed under pipe-eel and then stopped filing. It is not there when the current turns. The filters were not designed for it. Nothing else moves that means to.

Pressure is a citizen. There is a surface if you insist: a greasy meniscus pinned against the vault’s highest rib, a pocket of old air that tastes of oil and wet limestone, trembling when the turbines change pitch. You do not live there. You live by gauge and lock. The machines are not optional. If the exchangers quiet, the warmth becomes a different sentence—the chamber cooks, or the inflows remember they are also capable of other temperatures. If the turbines stop, the water has nowhere to go and the flue that admitted you becomes a jet. So the empty town works. Automatic. Electrified in a brown, stubborn, failing way. Sparks walk the wet. Relays close in rooms no one enters. Production continues at a scale that no longer has workers, only the ghost of scaled production: the industrial idea, still punching its card.

Sometimes the turbulence organizes for a minute that is not a minute. Whirlpools pick out the old street grid. The rust-snow falls up. The clock-stack keeps a time that does not divide into hours. You will not stay long enough to decide if this is a fault or a habit. The pocket does not care. It is embedded in a larger plumbing you are not invited to map. It has been itself longer than the maps. It will be itself after the beam dies and the last pale shape slips the screen and the noise, which is the true city, goes on speaking to no one.
