The highland does not begin or end in any place a traveler could mark. It is a single worn surface the size of a world, or of the better part of one, lifted so long ago that the original upthrust has been forgotten by the stone itself. Whatever ranges once stood here have been planed into long, shallow swells and broad concavities, their flanks now so gentle they scarcely register as slopes. Wind has done most of the work. For ages there has been almost nothing else to do it. The rock is pale, often a dull bone or ash color, sometimes banded with older mineral stains the color of rust or dried blood. Surfaces carry a thin desert varnish, a microscopic glaze left by time and the merest films of night frost. In the lowest bowls the finest dust collects in still, pale sheets that look from a distance like water and are not.

Light arrives without mercy or moisture. The sun is a hard white disk in a sky that stays a deep, ungenerous indigo even at noon; the air is too thin and too dry to scatter much. Shadows are inked in at their edges. Every grain of gravel, every fluted trunk, every distant grove throws a clean black silhouette. Yet the air itself never warms. Heat strikes the ground and is radiated away almost as quickly as it comes. The temperature sits in a perpetual cool, the kind that makes breath visible at dawn and keeps the fingers slightly stiff at midday. There is no humidity to hold the day’s warmth. Night drops hard and clear.

Water is a rumor the land no longer bothers to tell. No channels, no playas with their ghost-white rims, no seeps. What little frost forms in the deepest shade after the long radiational night is gone by the time the light has fully climbed. The plants, when you reach them, do not contradict this. They simply refuse the usual bargain.

They appear first as dark interruptions on the enormous brightness, stains that refuse to resolve for hours of walking. The barren intervals between them are measured in tens or hundreds of kilometers. A person could walk for days across nothing but gravel, wind-cut yardangs, and the slow crawl of dust, and see only one such stain grow larger. Then the grove arrives all at once: a sudden, crowded architecture of foliage so dense it seems imported from another climate. Leaves the size of small shields overlap in layers. Some are a dusty, almost metallic green, others nearly black, others silvered on the underside so that when the wind turns them the whole mass flashes. There are hanging structures like lianas, an understory of cushion plants and fern-like fronds, all of it packed so that the interior ground is a twilight of filtered brightness. It looks lush. It is lush. It is also dry to the touch, the tissues thick and waxy, the surfaces bloomed with a reflective powder that turns much of the brutal light away.

The trunks are pale, fluted, more like fired ceramic than ordinary wood. Where roots show at the grove’s edge they do not spread in search of surface moisture; they appear fused to the bedrock, following hairline fractures downward. Whatever chemistry they run is thrifty beyond ordinary xerophytes. Transpiration is almost nonexistent. Moisture, such as it is, is recaptured, recircled, perhaps even generated in trace amounts by photochemical side-reactions the intense illumination makes possible. The grove holds a faint microclimate—still air, a degree or two less chill, a green dimness—but it does not leak wetness into the surrounding mineral. Each stand is an island that has learned to be sufficient.

Inside, sound dies. The thin air already carries little; the foliage swallows the rest. You hear your own pulse, the almost inaudible tick of a leaf reorienting, sometimes the dry rasp of something moving a long way off. The rare animals treat the groves as way-stations rather than homes. A large, low-slung grazer the exact color of the dust may spend weeks crossing the empty leagues, arriving to feed with slow, methodical patience on fallen fronds and the lower cushions, then departing again. Smaller lives—pale, moth-like fliers that appear only in the brief dim hours, burrowers that surface after the frost has vanished—exist at densities so low they feel like afterthoughts. There is no constant background of insects, no chorus. Predation, if it occurs, is an event measured in seasons.

The highland generates its own weather because it is large enough to do so. Winds begin as nothing and, given a fetch of uncounted kilometers of open, dry surface, become steady aerial rivers. They lift the pale dust into long, lazy veils or, when stronger, carve the gravel into aligned ridges. High, fibrous clouds form and dissolve without ever committing to rain; their shadows race across the bright land like living things, briefly softening the light to pearl or pale gold. At the edges of vision the airborne mineral sometimes prisms, a faint, fleeting iridescence that is the closest the place comes to spectacle. Night is absolute and star-crammed. The cold deepens. In the coldest hollows a glitter of frost appears, the only water most of the surface will ever see, and by mid-morning it is gone again.

Everything here has had time to reach its own equilibrium. A fallen trunk may lie for centuries, slowly becoming a grey sculpture before the dry, cool air and the rare decomposers return it to dust. The groves themselves expand or contract on timescales of lifetimes, perhaps following slow pulses in the deep mineral chemistry or in the planet’s faint, irregular deliveries of whatever trace volatiles they require. Nothing is hurried. The land does not remember catastrophe; it remembers only the long, even pressure of wind, radiation, and the patient metabolism of things that learned to live on almost nothing and still appear abundant.

There are no paths that were made. No cairns, no cut stone, no lingering geometry that is not grown. The alignments some groves show—certain trunks leaning in the same direction, certain openings facing the same compass point—are simply the result of wind and light acting for a very long time. The place is finished with being made. It is only being itself, brightly, coolly, and almost silently, on a scale that renders any single life a brief interruption in the glare.
