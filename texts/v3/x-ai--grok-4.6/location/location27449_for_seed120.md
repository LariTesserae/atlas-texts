The place is smaller than the height it occupies.

You do not arrive by climbing so much as by being reduced. The last true slope is a stack of unweathered blocks still wet at the joints, each one set by hands that have not learned metal except as a rumor in a single smeared tool—an awl of something pale and stubborn, left driven into a seam as if the wall might forget itself without a reminder. Above that stack there is no summit in the ordinary sense. There is a lip of packed clay, thumb-ridged, warm, and then the interior, which is all the landscape there is.

It is a basin. Room-scale if you are willing to call a room something that has no corners it will keep. The floor is not floor: it is a constructed marsh, laid course by course the way a pot is coiled. Mats of living fiber have been pressed into fresh mud that has never been rained on by an old sky. Water stands a finger deep and will not decide whether it is pool or sweat. It weeps from the walls. It condenses on the underside of the lid—there is a lid, a low woven vault of withies and steaming moss, dim as the inside of a closed mouth—and falls back without having been weather yet. The air is thin with altitude and thick with wet heat. Breathing is like inhaling a cloth that has been boiled.

Everything here was made, and recently, and is still being made. There is no parent rock. What looks like stone has the temperature of a working body and the grain of something pinched into shape a few hours ago, or a few centuries that have not been allowed to pass. Erosion has not been invented for this place. Edges stay sharp until a thumb rounds them on purpose. Dust does not exist; there is only slip, a fine warm slurry that seals each new addition and then becomes, reluctantly, a surface. The mountain underneath—if underneath is the right word—is being assembled upward into this hidden cup, as if the world were being finished from the roof down and this pocket were the first true room.

Vegetation has been given no unused volume. Reeds are threaded through the wattle until the walls are half plant. Broad hot-climate leaves, thick as tongues, have been folded into every socket and lashed with bark cordage that is itself sprouting. Sphagnum is stuffed behind the stone the way insulation is stuffed, except the moss is drinking the weep and exhaling a sour green dusk. Where two stems might have left a gap, a third has been driven in. The density is such that motion is a negotiation: a leaf against your mouth, a rootlet finding the heat of your ankle and deciding you are another course of construction. The plants are lush in the way of a wound packing itself. They grow at a biological pace you can hear—unfurling as a wet click, a series of small tears, the sound of something taking a space that was promised to something else.

It is loud in here. Water argues with itself in every joint. The thin-air boil of it, when the heat pulses up from whatever embryonic geology is still deciding to be magma or clay, makes a continuous pot-simmer that fills the basin the way a bell fills a tower. Cordage ticks as it dries and is wetted again. The lid groans. Somewhere—above, or in the idea of above—wind shears across an unfinished sky and arrives in this pocket already shredded into a roar that has no fetch and no horizon, only the shape of the bowl to rattle in. If there are builders they work by slapping mud, and the slaps carry. If there are no builders the place works on itself with the same vocabulary.

Light is leftover. It comes through the weave of the lid in the color of tea and bruises, and through the water as a dull internal shine, as if the marsh remembered a brighter instruction and is trying to comply. Shadows do not fall correctly. They pool in the heat and then climb, because down has not been fully ratified. You notice this when a drip detaches from the vault and hesitates, considering two directions, and sometimes chooses the one that returns it to the moss it came from. Rain, when it happens, can fall up. The plants are built for this: they drink at both ends. Roots hang into air that is wetter than the mud. Leaves press to the floor and take from the standing water as if it were sky.

Fauna is almost an error. Once, something like a frog—only the suggestion of a frog, the wet clench of a throat that has not been assigned a species—moves under a pad and then is gone, or was never more than a pocket of marsh contracting. The rarity feels enforced. There is not room, and there is not permission. The ecology is vegetal and hydrological and constructive. Predator and prey, if they exist, are processes: heat eating water into steam, steam eating the dimness, growth eating the last unoccupied cubic inch, the basin eating the mountain by requiring more mountain beneath it so that the elevation can remain extreme while the interior remains a secret the size of a held breath.

The rules are local and under revision. This is the part that will not stay still.

Because the place is embryonic, law arrives as craft. A line scored in wet clay with a fingernail becomes, for a while, a ban on crossing. A knot in the withies becomes a limit on how tall a stem may go, until the stem learns to be the knot. Names stick and then slough the way slip sloughs. You can watch a stone forget it is stone and become a concentration of heat with the memory of a handprint. You can watch water remember it was not yet separate from air. The weirdness is not decoration; it is the condition of being first. Causality here is a primitive tool. It works if you bear down. It slips if you look away. Sequences happen in stacks, like the walls: this, then this, then a third thing that was already true and is only now being mortared in.

Scale misbehaves because the basin is hidden inside an elevation that should not be able to contain a climate. Stand in the marsh and the heat is equatorial, a packedhouse wetness, sweat that cannot evaporate because the air is already a sluice. Look for the rim and you may find that the rim has been carried farther off by the last course of work, or that you are standing on the rim and the marsh is above you, a ceiling of roots and warm puddles, gravity having been lashed into a new orientation with the same bark cord. The pocket is intimate—you could map it with your palms—and also it is the entire available world, because beyond the work there is not landscape yet, only the raw lift of something that will be geology when it has been handled enough.

Time is one of the active weathers. It comes in cycles you can feel in the soles: a pulse of making, a pulse of soaking, a pulse in which the plants take what was stacked and thread it through themselves until architecture and thicket are a single saturated body. The lid darkens and lightens without a sun you can swear to. Steam rises and is packed back down by the altitude, by the unfinished sky pressing close, by the sheer occupancy of the air. Nothing here is allowed to be background. Every presence is a full presence. The clay has opinions. The water has a voice that is too large for the vessel and so it shouts. The heat is a crowding.

If you stay, you will be incorporated or you will be used as a tool. Fingers have already been here; the walls are a library of pressure. A bone awl waits in the seam. Cordage wants another twist. The marsh wants another body to wick through. The elevation wants to go on being impossible, and the only way it knows how is to keep this secret room at the top of the work, wet and dim and roaring, a handmade climate sealed inside a mountain that has not agreed, yet, to be old.
