The basin has not finished arriving. That is the first thing the water tells you, and the water is nearly everything.

It is not a flood in the ordinary sense, nor a sea with a surface you could name. The air itself has given way to a warm, slow, overwhelming medium that occupies every interval — a column of liquid that is sometimes transparent to ten meters and sometimes the color of rusted brass, sometimes thin enough to walk through if you lean, sometimes so thick it holds your limbs the way clay would. It rains upward as often as down. Sheets of it detach from the dimness overhead and hang, trembling, like curtains that have forgotten which way gravity points. When they collapse they do not splash so much as invert: the basin’s floor becomes the ceiling for a few seconds and the drowned chimneys stand on their heads, still exhaling.

Those chimneys are few. The built world here is a scattering of industrial afterthoughts — a single turbine house half-swallowed by a new basalt spur, a lattice of pipes that emerge from one wall of the rift and vanish into another as if the rock grew around them last week (it did), a row of cooling towers whose tops have never been seen because the water-column above them is always rearranging its own height. Nothing looks maintained. Nothing looks abandoned either. The machines run. They run because the heat of the young stone and the chemical generosity of the water have become a kind of unasked-for power station. Dynamos spin in housings of precipitated mineral. Conveyor belts of living metal carry nothing, or carry the idea of ore, or carry the same broken gear in an endless loop that sometimes forgets to be circular and becomes a helix climbing into the murk.

The light is the color of a bruise held underwater. What illumination there is comes from three quarreling sources: a distant, smothered day that never quite reaches the floor; the dull orange pulse of the still-cooling lava dikes that stripe the basin like fresh scars; and the vegetation, which has decided that photosynthesis is optional. The plants are lush in the way a fever is lush. Forests of ribbon-kelp thicker than a man’s torso rise from every crack, their blades the width of doors, their color a black-green that drinks the remaining light and gives back a faint, wrong phosphorescence — not glow so much as the memory of glow. They grow through the machinery. They grow *as* machinery. A stand of them will suddenly lignify into a cooling stack, venting steam that is also spores. Another will dissolve back into soft tissue mid-cycle, leaving only the afterimage of pistons still pumping in the water. There are no animals. The absence is not peaceful. It is a held breath that has lasted too long. The plants occupy every ecological niche that would have been filled by fish, by worms, by anything that moves of its own will. They pulse. They strangle one another with ceremonial slowness and then share the same vascular system. They flower in shapes that hurt to look at directly — not because they are ugly, but because the petals keep counting themselves and arriving at different numbers.

Sound is the true weather. It is deafening in the specific way that a factory floor inside a waterfall inside a collapsing cathedral is deafening. The turbines contribute a basso continuo that you feel in your teeth. The water contributes everything else: the roar of currents that reverse without warning, the shriek of pressure differentials as new vents tear open in the young rock, the wet tearing of plant-mass the size of buildings shearing off and tumbling through the column. Over it all, or under it, or somehow beside it, is the sound of the place failing to decide what it is. Reality here is a work in progress that has been left on the shop floor. Distances breathe. A pipe you could touch a moment ago is now a kilometer of perspective, then a necklace of droplets, then the same pipe again but older, encrusted with a century of scale that formed in the last half-minute. Sometimes the water remembers it is supposed to have a surface and a bright, impossible sky appears fifty meters “up,” complete with the silhouette of a cooling tower that does not exist in the world you are standing in. Then the sky folds, becomes another layer of kelp, and the sound of that folding is like every rivet in a hull letting go at once.

The terrain itself is juvenile and proud of it. Basalt and something greener, glassier, something that has not finished being stone, rise in knife-edged ridges and sudden terraces. There has not been time for rounding. Every edge is a decision still being made. Fresh scarps weep mineral-rich warmth. In the lee of one such scarp a whole secondary basin has begun to form, already filling with the same impossible water, already sprouting its own miniature forest of industrial vegetation, already generating its own slightly different version of the roar. You can see three or four of these nested, arguing topographies from any vantage that lasts longer than a breath. The scale is valley-sized, town-sized, the size of a thought that will not stay still — large enough that the far wall of the rift is a rumor of darker water and the suggestion of more chimneys, more forests, more of the same furious becoming.

Nothing here is finished. The factories are growing the plants that are dismantling the factories that are becoming the next set of ridges. The water is manufacturing new kinds of wetness. The dimness thickens and thins like a living membrane. If there were a shore it would be receding in every direction at once, including inward. The only constant is the overwhelm — of medium, of noise, of change so rapid it has become a kind of stillness, the way a propeller becomes a disc. You do not visit this place. You are processed by it, briefly, while it tries on the idea of having a visitor, and then the idea is composted into another stand of black-green blades, another length of singing pipe, another unfinished mountain of glass and heat, all of it roaring, all of it still arriving.
