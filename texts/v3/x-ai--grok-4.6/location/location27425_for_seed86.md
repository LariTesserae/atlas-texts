The hollow is not a cave in the ordinary sense. It is a country that failed downward.

What sits above it still pretends to be ordinary ground for long stretches: a plateau of pale limestone, thin soil, a few wind-stunted shrubs. Then the rock simply is not there anymore. The roof came down in sheets and blocks, not long ago as stone measures time, and what remains is a sunken province of broken ceilings and unfinished walls, a valley-system that never quite finished becoming a valley. Daylight arrives as a rumor. It leaks through rents and chimney-holes and the long, raw slots where the overburden tore, and it dies on the way down. The light that reaches the floor is the color of old pewter. You can walk an hour and never lose the sense of being indoors, and you can walk another hour and never lose the sense of weather.

It is cool the way cellars are cool, a held temperature that does not argue. Breath shows. Stone sweats. The air is damp without being wet in any generous way: condensation beads on every fresh fracture, gathers, ticks off. There are pockets of standing water in the low bowls of collapsed rooms, black and still, no wider than a house, sometimes only a basin you could step across. A thin seep may run along a bedding plane for a few hundred paces and then vanish into rubble. Rain, when it finds a way in, does not fall so much as arrive—sudden vertical weather in a shaft of dimness—and then the drips persist for days, a second, smaller rain that belongs only to the hollow.

The rock is young in the way a wound is young. Faces are sharp. Corners have not yet been argued into curves. Where slabs sheared, the break is clean enough to cut a palm. Talus lies in heaps that have not settled into slopes; you can still see the original bedding in the fallen pieces, still match a block to the vacancy it left in the ceiling. Nothing here has had time to become scenery. The hollow looks as if it happened, and then stopped.

There is almost no green. In the wetter seams, a fur of moss; in a few of the better-lit collapses, a scatter of ferns no taller than a hand, and pale grasses that never quite commit to being plants. Fungi take the rest of the work: shelves, threads, a bloom of something gray-white along a damp joint. Between these, emptiness. Floor after floor of bare limestone, grit, and the particular dust that fresh rock makes when it breaks.

And yet the place is full.

The animals are what the hollow is for. They are not crowded. That is the first wrongness you feel, if you come from a forest or a river-mouth: there is space between every body, sometimes a great deal of space, so that a single creature occupies a room the size of a market and does not seem to mind. But they are everywhere you look, and they are large enough, and numerous enough across the whole sunken country, that the emptiness never wins. You see them as you would see standing stones—each one discrete, each one inevitable.

They are ordinary animals, or as ordinary as animals become when they have been given a climate that does not change and a roof that does not quite close. Heavy-bodied ungulates the color of the stone, with eyes adapted to the pewter light. Low, compact carnivores that follow the seeps. Birds that do not so much fly as row through the dimness between hanging blocks, their wings making a sound like wet canvas. Colonies of something smaller—bats, yes, but also things that are only bats if you do not look carefully—hang in the higher fractures, and when they shift they do so as a single decision, a rustle that becomes a roar and then is a rustle again. None of this is rare. None of it is arriving or leaving. The hollow is not a corridor. It is a holding.

They call. That is the other fact, the one that arrives before the eyes have finished adjusting. The hollow is loud the way a shore is loud: not always at the pitch of crisis, but without pause and without apology. Hooves on fresh talus. A cough from a throat built for distance. The slap of a heavy body entering one of the black pools. Wings. The long, tiled echo of a bellow that was meant for open air and has found, instead, a country of hard rooms. Drip-water ticks underneath it all like a second clock. Because the stone is young and unsoftened, every sound is edged. Because the hollow is large—larger than a town, larger than most valleys you could name from the surface—the sounds do not cancel; they stack. You hear animals you will not see for half a day. You hear the same animals again later and understand that they have not moved, or that they have moved only along the paths they have always used, which are not yet worn into the rock and somehow already permanent.

Nothing here is building. There is no wall, no cairn, no ash-ring that has been allowed to remain. Once, in a side chamber where the light is a shade less dim, you might find a flake of worked stone, the kind of thing a hand makes when it is still learning what a hand can do, and then the dust again, and the animals again. The hollow does not keep company with making. It keeps company with occupancy.

Time, in the ordinary sense, has stepped out. The drips find the same stones. The herds stand in the same relations. A carnivore crosses the same empty floor at the same interval, or at an interval so regular it might as well be the same. The light does not improve or fail; the rents in the roof are the rents in the roof. If the plateau above knows seasons, the hollow knows only this: cool, damp, dim, loud, and finished. The collapse already occurred. The animals already arrived, or were already here, and found the new rooms acceptable. What you walk through is the equilibrium after the event—an underworld that has no intention of becoming anything else, a recently torn country that has decided, with complete seriousness, to stay exactly as torn, and full of living bodies that have no intention of leaving.
