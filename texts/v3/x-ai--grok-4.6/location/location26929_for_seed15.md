The chamber is smaller than the sound it makes. You know this only after your eyes adjust, because the first thing that arrives is not space but glare: a column of daylight driven straight down through a circular well-mouth, so dense it looks solid until the surface of the water breaks it. Below the break the light does not fade. It thickens. The whole volume of the room is a single bright body, warm as blood just under the skin, and every dressed face of stone is lit from inside the water as if the masonry itself were a lantern.

There is no unworked rock. The floor, the four walls, the shallow barrel of the ceiling, the lip of the overflow, the square mouth of the inlet—every plane was cut and set. The blocks are still new. Chisel-tracks run in even, slightly overlapping strokes; arrises have not yet been kissed round by current; the mortar in the joints is pale and slightly proud, like icing not yet scraped. Whoever built this finished recently enough that the room still smells of wet lime and the iron of tools. No cave, no sprung vault of geology. A box. A made pocket, sunk a storey beneath whatever courtyard or ridge sits above the oculus, and then filled almost to the throat.

The water is the room’s true architecture. It stands at a height a swimmer would call chest-deep if they could stand, but the floor is a little deeper toward the inlet, and the whole mass is in motion. A ceramic channel—unglazed, thumb-smoothed, joined with bitumen—spills a continuous sheet from a dark rectangular mouth high on the north wall. The sheet hits the surface with a flat, constant roar that the stone throws back on itself until the chamber seems louder than a river of this size has any right to be. Under that roar are smaller languages: the ticking of trapped air along the joints, the hollow gulp when a pulse of the inflow swallows itself, the thin bright hiss where overflow slurs across a weir into a lower throat you cannot see. The weir is a single slab, still sharp, its upper edge a ruler-line of white water. Whatever this cistern was cut to do—hold, measure, baptize, cool, forget—it is doing it now, actively, without pause.

Heat lives in the water more than in the air. The shaft of sun is tropical, or nearly; the inflow has traveled some distance of sun-struck conduit. Your skin, if you entered, would not flinch from cold. It would accept the warmth as already belonging to it. Ripples from the pour travel the short width of the room and climb the opposite wall a hand’s breadth before falling back. Light goes with them. The submerged blocks flash and dull in a rhythm that is almost breathing. Because the chamber is so small—you could, swimming, touch two facing walls in a single stretch—the flashes feel intimate, like someone moving a lamp just behind your shoulder.

Green has barely begun. In the upper corners, where splash and vapor meet the newest mortar, a dust of algae has taken, more stain than plant. One niche, cut as if for a lamp or an offering, holds a single clump of something deliberately placed: a few stems of a reed or a water-lily start, roots bound in a rag of fiber, not yet decided whether to live. The rest of the stone is clean. No roots have found a crack. No film of life has had time to make the surfaces soft. The room is too young for a ecology and too sealed for accident. What grows here will have to be invited, and so far almost nothing has been.

There are no animals. No fish, no larvae in the sunlit column, no spider in the dry ring of stone above the waterline, no bat. The loudness is only water talking to stone. That absence makes the space feel held, or waiting. A pocket that has not yet been given its inhabitants, or that was never meant to have them.

Above the waterline a narrow dry gallery runs around three sides, a ledge wide enough for a person to crouch with a bucket or a lamp. The fourth side is the inlet wall. Tool-marks there are fresher still, as if the last dressing happened after the first filling, someone leaning out to correct a joint while the roar was already going. A wooden sluice-gate, simple, pegged, darkened only by use and not by years, can be dropped across the ceramic mouth. It is raised now. The system is open. Somewhere upslope, in heat and light you cannot see, a catchment or a diverted rill is being allowed to run.

The oculus is the only sky. It is a perfect circle of such brightness that the stone rim around it goes black by contrast. When wind crosses the mouth it adds a low flute-note to the pour, a vowel the room did not have a moment before. Cloud-shadow, if any, would slam the underwater world into sudden dusk and then restore it; on a clear day the column simply turns with the sun, walking its white ellipse across the floor from morning corner to afternoon corner, and the water follows, restlessly glittering.

You could miss the entire place. From above it is a hole the width of a well, set in paving or turf, easy to step around. The chamber is a secret the size of a room, fully invented, still sharp at every edge, filled with a brightness that has no business existing this far below the air, and loud with the work of staying full. It is not old enough to be a ruin and not natural enough to be a spring. It is a decision in stone, recently kept, and the warm water is still learning the shape it was given.
