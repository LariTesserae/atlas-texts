The plateau is what is left when a mountain range has finished being mountains.

It sits so high the sky has thinned to a pale, permanent bruise, and so long has it sat there that every peak, every ridge, every last dramatic gesture of stone has been ground down to a single almost-level. What remains is a table the size of a modest country, faintly dished, faintly ribbed with the ghosts of old watersheds, its edges falling away in cliffs so ancient they no longer look like cliffs so much as the worn lips of a bowl. Wind has had geologic time to argue with it. Rain has had geologic time. Ice, when there was still enough ice. The argument is over. The land has settled into its last shape and intends to keep it.

You feel the height in the cool, which is not the clean cold of snowfields but a damp, close cool, the temperature of stone that has never quite dried. Cloud does not pass over this place so much as live on it. Banks of it crawl the surface like slow livestock, snagging on anything that still has the pride to stand up—a factory chimney, a terrace wall, a stand of the dark, low trees. When the cloud lifts, it does not go far. It hangs just overhead, a ceiling of wool, and the light that filters through is the color of old pewter. Midday is a rumor. Evening arrives early and stays.

Water here is not a river and not a lake. Water is a behavior. It condenses on every cooled surface, beads on iron, threads down brick, collects in the shallow saucers the plateau forgot to drain. There are cistern-hollows no deeper than a man’s thigh, dark and still, ringed with liverwort. There are gutters older than the city that still remember how to carry a night’s worth of drip toward the next hollow. After a true rain—rare, sudden, almost embarrassed—the whole table steams and shines for an hour, and then the shine is gone and only the damp remains, soaking back into moss and mortar. People here speak of “wet” the way lowlanders speak of weather: as a visit, not a resident.

And yet the green is everywhere.

It should not be. The soil is thin, the light is stingy, the water intermittent. The green does not care. It has had the same forever the stone has had, and it has learned to live on film and breath. Mosses thick as felt coat the horizontal world—roofs, roads, the tops of walls, the long backs of idle machines. Lichens map the vertical in oranges and sour yellows and a gray so pale it looks like frost that learned to grow. In the slightly deeper soils of the old river-ghosts, there are forests, if you will accept the word: trees no taller than a house, trunks twisted, bark plated with more moss, crowns knitted so densely that walking beneath them is like walking in a green room. Their leaves are small and dark and hold water the way a miser holds coin. Between the trees, ferns, and between the ferns a crowding of lesser things, all of it damp to the touch, all of it quietly eating the last of the mountain.

The city is not on the plateau. The city is another layer of the plateau’s long digestion.

It began, the story goes, as a camp for people who came to cut the last standing stone, or to catch the cloud, or to hide. That was so long ago the story has worn as smooth as the land. What stands now is a town that thickened into a city without ever quite deciding to—kilns and yards and tenements and counting-houses packed into the shallow folds, then stacked when the folds ran out. Streets are narrow because the green and the damp make wide streets feel wasteful, like leaving a table unset. Buildings lean. They share walls. They grow extra rooms the way the trees grow extra moss, opportunistically, into any air that isn’t already claimed. Brick is the common speech: dark, pitted, sweating. Iron is the accent—pipes, brackets, the ribs of sheds, the endless quiet plumbing of a place that lives by condensation.

Industry here is not fire so much as patience. The factories do not roar. They tick, they sigh, they turn. Great condensers stand on the higher ground like organ-pipes, their flanks running with captured cloud; the water they wring from the air is piped down to dye-works and paper-mills and the small foundries that still, stubbornly, smelt a little metal from the plateau’s exhausted bones. Belt-drives slap in the dim. Pistons move as if thinking it over. The power is steam more often than not, raised in boilers fed with the compressed peat of a thousand years of moss, and the steam itself is only water remembering it was cloud. On still days you can stand at a crossing and hear three factories at once and still hear your own coat drip.

It is crowded without being loud. People live close—rooms over workshops, workshops over cisterns, cisterns under streets that are themselves only roofs for older streets. You smell peat-smoke, wet wool, tannin, the sweet rot of leaves in the gutters. You smell people. Voices stay indoors. Outside, the sound-world is drip, distant belt, the soft tear of someone pulling moss off a lintel, and under it all a hush that belongs to the altitude, to the thin air, to the knowledge that there is nothing for a long way in any direction but more of the same worn table and then the drop.

Animals are few and they keep their counsel. A kind of hare the color of wet mortar lives in the moss-forest and is seen at dusk, if dusk can be told from the rest. There are moths the size of a hand that drink from the condensers and leave a dust like rust on the iron. In the deepest cisterns, something pale threads the water and is gone. Birds pass through on longer journeys and do not stay; the plateau offers little that a bird would call a horizon. Children are taught not to follow the hares into the thickest green. Not because the hares are dangerous, but because the green is a kind of weather, and weather here has rooms.

The strangeness is not theatrical. It is the strangeness of a place that has outlasted its own reasons. Clocks run a little slow, or people say they do; it may only be that days have no edges. In the oldest cellars the brick is not brick but a fused grit of brick and root and bone, as if the city had begun to remember it was geology. Some streets end in walls that are not walls but the original plateau, dressed so long ago the dressing has grown a forest of its own. At certain hours the cloud comes down so completely that the factories become isolated islands of lamp and tick, and people navigate by the feel of pipes along the walls, hand over hand, as if the town had gone underwater without asking. There are maps in the municipal house that show earlier versions of the same streets, stacked, and the archivists will tell you—quietly, as everyone here tells you anything—that they have stopped trying to decide which layer is current.

Nothing here is finished working. The moss advances a finger’s width after every wet. The condensers riming and clearing. The cloud dragging its belly across the roofs, tearing, reforming. Men and women shift shifts in the dye-works; vats turn from one brown to another; cloth is hung in the dim to dry and does not, quite. On the western lip, where the table finally admits it must fall, a slow collapse has been underway since before the first kiln—an acre at a time, a century at a time—and the city has simply built a little back from the edge, and then a little back again, leaving a fringe of empty windows that look out into white air. The plateau erodes. The city adjusts. The green occupies whatever is left. It is not a violent activity. It is the activity of a world that has already decided what it is and is only, continually, becoming more so: cooler, damper, greener, dimmer, closer, quieter, and still, in its unshowy way, relentlessly busy with the work of lasting.
