The hollow sits just off the old flood-line, a shallow dish of land that never quite became a valley. What water remains does not run so much as linger: a seam of damp in the packed root-mat, a shine on iron in the mornings, rain that arrives in brief, decisive hours and is gone before the light changes. Condensation is the reliable river here. It beads on leaf-scale and on the cold faces of the machines, runs the gutters of bark, collects in the saucers the buildings have grown for it. You can walk the whole place in a few minutes and never hear a current, only drip.

The ground itself is alive and has been for longer than the settlement has a name. A continuous substrate of cork-moss, compressed root, and something like lichen-thatch, pale and springy, worn into paths where feet and carts have always gone. It is not a forest floor. There is almost no understory. The living mass is the floor and the walls and the few roofs; it is not a crowding of stems. Between the structures the substrate opens into courtyards of packed tan fibre, as empty and considered as rooms. You feel the sparseness first as air, then as interval: six, perhaps eight, great hollows standing far enough apart that each keeps its own weather.

They are the town.

Each building is a single organism, or a graft so old the distinction has weathered out. Boles the width of cottages, fluted, shored with their own buttress-roots, windows cut and callused over generations until the rims are smooth as worked stone. The wood is cool to the touch and faintly damp, as if the walls sweat a little. Inside, the rooms are irregular in the way of long habitation, not of wilderness: floors levelled with resin and old brick, stair-spirals that follow the grain, hearths lined with firebrick set into living timber that has learned, somehow, not to burn. The industry is in them the way the grain is in them. Copper lines have been let into the cambium and sealed; they carry sap to the boilers in the mill-house, a low structure at the downhill edge where two trunks have been trained together into a nave. The boilers are small, old, scrupulously maintained. They do not roar. They tick, and once or twice an hour a valve eases with a sound like a person setting down a cup.

This is a working place, and has been for so long that work and weathering are the same process. Belts run through slots grown to their width. A dynamo in the mill turns on plant-oil and on the slow pressure of the main bole, which is tapped the way a hillside is terraced—never enough to kill, always enough. Wires, insulated in rubber and in a bark the colour of old tea, stitch from roof to roof at human height, dipping in the long gaps. At dusk they take a modest current; the lamps are ordinary, yellowish, the colour of indoor light anywhere. Nothing here is luminous by itself. The light is the sky’s light, a cool even grey or a thin sun, falling into the courtyards without drama, broken only by the silhouettes of the buildings and by the occasional rack of pipes.

You notice the quiet as a texture. Footsteps on the fibre. The far, regular knock of a single piston. Leaves—there are leaves, high and few, a canopy that is more lid than forest—turning when the air moves. Rain, when it comes, is a full sound that the place seems to have been shaped to receive: channels, lips, cistern-hollows in the roots, a brief shining of every surface, then the long private work of drinking. After rain the condensation lasts into the afternoon. You can see your breath some mornings. The temperature never argues; it is the temperature of cellars and of shade.

There is almost no one else living here that is not plant. A moth-dust of midges over the dampest court after rain. Once, perhaps, a bird in the high leaves, a short unmusical note, and then the interval again. The rarity feels established, not tragic—as if the ecology finished its negotiations a long time ago and kept only what the mill and the moss required. The activity is not animal. It is the buildings’ own: the almost-imperceptible swell of the boles, the creep of a graft along a lintel, the scheduled opening of a tap, the weather arriving and being used. Sap rises. Steam lifts and is recaptured. The dynamo’s note changes by a fraction as cloud covers the sun and the leaves close a degree. Everything is in motion at the pace of habit.

The forms are finished. Whatever first decision made a factory of a grove, or a grove of a factory, has been worn down to a single fact. Brick shows in places, iron in places, both crusted with the same pale lichen that coats the windward bark. No edge is new. The paths have the camber of a century of the same feet. Even the unusual thing—the fact that the town is grown, that the engines are nested in wood that listens to them—has the unstartling quality of a local custom. You stop finding it strange about the time you notice that the door-sills are warm from the slow inner life of the walls, and that the quiet is not emptiness but a great number of small, agreed-upon processes keeping out of one another’s way.

At the lowest point of the hollow there is a trough, stone-lined, holding a dark water no deeper than a hand. It is not a spring. It is overflow, condensate, the settlement’s only open pocket of wet. The substrate leans toward it without hurry. A pipe enters it and leaves it. On still days the surface shows the undersides of leaves and a disc of ordinary sky. That is the whole of the place: a handful of living halls, the spaces they have left between them, the cool work that goes on inside the wood, and the damp that is never quite a river and never quite gone.
