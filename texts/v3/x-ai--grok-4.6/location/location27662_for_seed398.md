The needle was not here a generation ago. It came up in a single season of nights, a glass-and-sinter fluke punched out of a plateau that had already forgotten how to make mountains. From the flats you would never find it: the shaft is thinner than a rumor, black against a blacker sky, and the plateau’s own heat-haze eats the silhouette before the eye can keep it. You enter, if you enter, through a seam no wider than a doorway that only opens when the internal pressure dips—an hour, maybe two, in any given cycle. Inside, the world is a vertical room.

It is not a cavern. A cavern implies rock that waited. This is a chimney still remembering it was magma. The inner wall is a single continuous surface of volcanic glass shot through with filaments of something that is not metal and is not quite crystal: sintered, yes, but sintered by a process that leaves the lattice warm to a depth of meters and faintly conductive in ways that make compasses weep. The chamber is perhaps four hundred meters across at its widest, which is near the middle. Above and below it tapers. You cannot see the floor. You cannot see the roof. Both are lost in the same brown dimness, a light that seems to come from the walls themselves and then immediately regret it. What illumination there is sits in the deep red, almost infrared, the color of a closed eyelid facing a furnace. Faces are readable only at conversational distance. Beyond that, structures become suggestions.

The city occupies the inner wall the way lichen occupies a boulder, except there is no lichen, and the occupation is deliberate. Platforms. Galleries. Thin towers that grow inward and stop, their tips never quite meeting across the void. Walkways of the same sintered stuff, some of them no wider than a plank, spanning emptiness that would kill you three times before you finished falling—heat, impact, and a long interval of nothing. The architecture is urban in the old sense: there are districts, though they are stacked rather than spread; there are junctions, cisterns, a kind of forum that is really just a wider ledge with three more ledges above it and a drop you do not look at. Infrastructure is everywhere and almost invisible. Heat-channels run behind the glass like veins. Pressure gates irise open and shut without sound. Somewhere in the wall, machines whose operating principle is not in any handbook drink the gradient between the needle’s molten root and its high, thinning tip, and turn that difference into work. The work is not locomotion. The work is keeping the needle from finishing what it started: finishing the rise, finishing the cooling, finishing the ordinary death of a volcano. The city is a thermostat with streets.

There are no plants. There have never been plants here. The walls bloom, sometimes, with mineral dendrites—white, feathery, brittle as frost—where a vapor film has flashed and left its salts. People who live here (and there are people, fewer than a town, more than a household, distributed so thinly that you can walk a whole circuit of a level and meet no one) do not call these flowers. They call them receipts. Proof that water happened.

Water happens like this. Deep in the root, far below any gallery, superheated fluid is forced up through hairline conduits. Most of it never arrives as liquid. It arrives as a breath, a sudden humidity that makes the dim air feel thick for ten minutes, twenty, and then the walls sweat. A sheet of condensation, almost too hot to be called water, runs the entire inner circumference in a single coordinated glaze. It hisses where it hits a cooler spar. It vanishes into intakes. On rare cycles the pressure is wrong and the film becomes a rain: not drops but long, heavy threads of mineral-charged steam that fall the height of the chamber and never reach a floor you could stand on. They are gone before the sound of them finishes. Afterward the air is dry again, and the dendrites have a new generation, and the cisterns—sealed, humming, set into the wall like organs—have taken on another few liters of something that will be drinkable once it has been argued with.

The heat is not weather. The heat is the medium. It sits at a pitch that makes metal tools uncomfortable to hold after a minute and makes the idea of cloth feel like a joke. Skin learns a new vocabulary of almost-burns. The inhabitants move slowly, not from languor but from thermodynamics; haste is a way of cooking yourself from the inside. They wear very little, and what they wear is more aperture than garment, vented, reflective on the inner face. Their technology handles the rest. Small devices, worn at the throat or the wrist, bleed heat into a sink that is not here—into a direction that is not a direction, a dump that physics, as taught, does not budget for. You can stand next to someone and feel the air around them cool by a few degrees, a private winter the size of a body. The devices tick, very softly, like cooling iron. That is most of the sound in the needle: ticks, the rare hiss of a condensation event, and a subsonic pressure that is the city’s heat-engines thinking. Voices do not carry. The volume of empty air eats them. People lean in to speak. Conversations look like conspiracies even when they are about cistern levels.

There are no animals. There is, at the edge of the dimness, sometimes a motion that is not a person. It has no good name. It is the size of a closed fist or the size of a room, depending on how you fail to look at it. It prefers the unlit upper taper, where the glass is newer and the sintering incomplete. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It is present the way a reflection is present, and then it is not. The inhabitants leave it offerings of cooled slag in niches, not from religion exactly, more from a wish to remain uninteresting. The offerings are always gone later. Whether they are taken or simply unmade is not discussed.

The needle is still growing. This is the activity that replaces weather, replaces seasons, replaces the whole slow grammar of a living landscape. Every few days the walls shiver—a single, low, complete shudder—and a new ring of glass is added at the high end, centimeters of fresh, razor lip against a sky that is not the sky you came from. The upper apertures, when they iris, show a darkness pricked with lights that do not match any catalogued sky. The elevation is extreme in two ways at once: the plateau already sat above the last useful air, and the needle has added its own arrogance on top of that, and then the interior has its own verticality, a private altitude measured in ledges. Stand on a mid-level gallery and you are in a hidden room. Look up and you are on the edge of something that has not decided whether it is a mountain or a vehicle.

Erosion has barely started. The glass is still sharp. Edges cut. New spars have not yet learned to be kind. Dust is rare; what falls is frit, tiny curved shards that tinkle once and then are gone into the intakes, recycled into the next sintering. The city is young in the way a scab is young. Everything is too clean, too intent, too recently decided.

And yet it is a city. There are names for the ledges. There are arguments about who maintains the eastern heat-channel. There is a place where three walkways meet and people leave small objects—worked slag, a polished dendrite, a device that no longer ticks—and other people take them, or don’t. There is a schedule, of sorts, keyed to the condensation cycles and the growth-shudgers, which is as close as this place comes to a clock. Children (there are a few) learn not to run and not to look down and not to put their hands on a wall that has just finished raining. They learn the sound of a pressure-gate thinking about opening. They learn that the empty middle of the chamber is not empty in the way a field is empty. It is empty in the way a held breath is empty. Something is using the space, even if the something is only the city’s own unfinished purpose.

At the very top, where the newest glass is still cooling from a color you can almost see into a color you can’t, there is a platform with no rail. It is not a lookout. It is a calibration point. Someone stands there, not often, with a tool that does not resemble a tool, and does a thing to the gradient, and the needle’s private physics settle another fraction. The air up there is thinner and, paradoxically, a degree less brutal, as if the heat itself were tiring. Condensation never reaches this high as liquid. It arrives only as a ghost of wetness on the lips, a taste of minerals and old lightning, and then the dryness takes it back.

Below, in the brown dim, a walkway ticks as it sheds a day’s heat. A cistern seals. Far away—distance here is mostly vertical—a film of water decides to exist, runs the circuit of the world in under a minute, and is gone. The dendrites brighten by a generation. No one speaks. The emptiness between the towers holds its temperature like a secret. The needle, which should not be a city and should not still be rising and should not open onto that particular sky, continues the only work it knows.
