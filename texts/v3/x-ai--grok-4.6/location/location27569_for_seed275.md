The descent is not through rock. After the last ordinary tunnel pinches out, the floor underfoot changes texture: a fibrous, cork-dry give, slightly warm from within, that never quite feels like walking on something dead. Light arrives all at once, ordinary and even, the color of a high overcast afternoon, though there is no sky and never has been. It comes from the tissue itself—every surface faintly photoluminescent, a stored daylight the plants have never seen.

This is a single organism still deciding its own architecture. Kilometers of open sightlines under a vault so high it holds its own slow weather of falling spores. The “ground” is living substrate, a continental mat of compressed, arid parenchyma that has only recently begun to differentiate. In places it is still smooth and undecided, the color of old ivory. In others it has already thrown up the first load-bearing ribs: root-buttresses thick as boulevards, hanging mid-span, not yet joined. The air is cool and almost without moisture; it tastes of cellulose dust and faint ozone. Nothing here drinks.

Scattered across the half-born plain stand the works. They are cities and they are organs. Towers of iron-hard lignin and metallic xylem rise in deliberate, unfinished grids, their walls already respiring in long, dry cycles. Between them lie vast intervals of emptiness—cathedral voids where the substrate remains uncommitted, so that a district of grown-foundries can feel as sparse as an archipelago. Elevated root-roads, still thin as scaffolding, connect some clusters and simply stop in mid-air at others, waiting for the next decision of growth. The scale is regional: you can walk a day between processing groves and still be inside the same body.

Inside the towers the industry is quiet and continuous. Pistons of living wood drive belts of woven bast fiber. Presses crush nodules of mineral the deeper roots have drawn up from strata that have never known water; the solvent that glints in the veins is milky, silica-rich, non-aqueous. Chimneys are hollow stems exhaling not smoke but fertile gray dust that settles like slow snow. The products are strange and immediately useful: sheets of photosynthetic foil, seeds that unfold into calibrated tools, oils that never wet. Everything is grown to spec and then finished by the same tissue that made it. There are no separate machines, only differentiated organs that happen to stamp, extrude, and assemble.

The geology itself is embryonic and being rewritten in real time. What looks like erosion is appetite: root-tips dissolve and reassemble stone into architecture at a pace just slow enough to watch if you stand still. A wall thickens while you look at it. A new gallery begins as a dimple, then a hollow, then a vault already lined with the same even light. The whole basin is under construction by itself, raw edges everywhere, some towers listing as their foundations continue to pack cells into new load-bearing patterns.

Fauna moves through the sparse intervals as if the emptiness were pasture. Pale, long-legged browsers crop only the fallen dust, their hooves clicking once and then absorbed. Glass-winged insects pollinate the factory-flowers, which open not with pigment but with the scent of warm copper and ozone. Higher up, in the unfinished galleries, things like living calipers wait with mechanical patience. None of them drink either. Their metabolisms are closed, arid, running on the same geothermal gradient the plants conduct through metallic xylem.

Sound is mostly the absence of it. The great presses murmur at the edge of hearing, a vegetative thrum like distant digestion. A dry crack now and then as a branch elects to exist. The rest is swallowed by the living floor. Yet the place is active on every timescale that matters: growth, manufacture, slow weather of spores, the deliberate opening of new chambers, the unhurried traffic of browsers and the sudden precise strike of a caliper. The rules are simple and still being written. Growth follows an encoded urban logic no one remembers implanting. Waste heat becomes light. Carbon is pulled from stone. The entire ecology remembers a solvent that is no longer present, or never was.

It feels unfinished because it is. Rails end in air. Districts stand isolated in their own light. The living substrate between them is still smooth, waiting to sprout the next neighborhood or the next foundry or simply another empty kilometer of cool, dry, faintly glowing plain. This is how a city comes into being when the city is also the ground, the factories, the light, and the only weather: slowly, quietly, and all at once, in the dry dark that has decided to illuminate itself.
