The chamber sits under a lid of unweathered stone, a single room the size of a nave, and it has always been exactly this full.

You feel the heat before you understand the dark. It is the dry heat of a kiln left on overnight, of metal that has forgotten what cooling means. There is no moisture in it. The air does not drink; it abrades. Salt and fine mineral dust hang in a standing haze that never settles and never thins, as if the atmosphere itself were a packed solid that only pretends to be gas. Breathing it is like breathing warm cloth.

Light comes from below and from the machines. Amber bulbs, caged in dirty glass, burn at a constant wattage along the undersides of catwalks that go nowhere. They do not flicker. They do not dim. Their glow is the color of old tea and it dies within a few body-lengths, so that the upper vault is only a rumor of facets. The facets are new. The walls are a shatter-pattern of salt and gypsum and something harder, paler, that rings if struck: a mineral that formed in a hurry and has not had time, or permission, to dull. Every edge is still a knife. Nothing here has been rounded. Water never arrived to do the rounding, and time, in this room, does not file things down.

The floor is not a floor so much as a packed congregation of surfaces. Pillars of the same young crystal rise almost to the vault, leaving only shoulders of space between them, and in those shoulders the industrial things have been fitted with the patience of someone filling a puzzle that will never be taken apart. A single great engine occupies the center, a horizontal cylinder the length of a cart, jacketed in riveted iron, its flywheel a dark moon that turns at a speed you stop noticing. It does not accelerate. It does not slow. The belt that leaves it disappears into a slot in the nearest pillar and does not come back. Smaller housings crowd the engine’s flanks: gearboxes, a dry compressor whose intake is a grill of salt-eaten mesh, a dynamo that hums a note just under the engine’s note. Cables, cloth-wrapped, run in shallow channels cut into the crystal. Some of the channels still show the tool marks. The tools are not here.

Scattered among this, as if left by a shift that ended and never restarted: a stool with one missing rung. A tin cup welded by dust to a valve-wheel. A short flight of stairs that climbs three steps and ends in air against a pillar’s flank. A stencil on the engine’s housing, letters in a language of block capitals, half-eaten by the same dust that coats everything and yet has not, somehow, accumulated another millimeter. These are not ruins in the usual sense. They are not decaying. They are parked.

The sound is the room’s other architecture. It is loud the way a foundry is loud, a layered, unceasing pressure: the engine’s basso, the high turbine whine of something smaller, a clack-clack of a pawl that has been catching the same tooth since the room began. Over that, and through it, the fauna.

They are everywhere the machines are not, and often where the machines are. The density is the first wrongness you can name. Bodies occupy every interval. They do not mill. They hold positions the way the flywheel holds its rpm.

Along the crystal seams, packed shoulder to shoulder, live the clickers: fist-sized, carapaced things the color of tarnished brass, each one locked to its neighbor by the touching of wing-cases. They rasp. The rasp is a single chord, a dry stridulation that has no attack and no decay. It is possible they are eating the salt. It is equally possible they are only sounding it. Their legs do not scuttle. If you watch one for a long time you will see a mandible close, open, close, on the same grain.

Higher, in the dim, the vault is furred with hangers. They look at first like bats and then like leather straps and then like tongues of smoked meat, and they are none of these. Each is a flattened, warm-bodied ribbon hooked to a crystal point by a hook that grew there. They pulse. The pulse is shared. A slow peristalsis travels the colony as a wave that never quite becomes a movement of place. From their undersides falls a grit that is not droppings so much as shed mineral, and the grit joins the haze and is breathed and is, perhaps, breathed back out as more hangers, somewhere, in the same numbers.

On the engine’s warm jacket, packed in the seams between rivets, a third people: pale, sessile, like barnacles made of paper and wax. They open and shut small mouths in time with the dynamo. When they open, a smell comes — not wet, never wet — a smell of hot copper and crushed pepper and something floral that has no flower. Sparse vegetation, if it can be called that, threads among them: hair-thin stalks of a dry white plant that has no chlorophyll and no root-crown, only a holdfast of glassy fibers sunk into iron. The stalks do not grow. They occupy. Here and there a stalk has put out a single flag of tissue, stiff as parchment, and the flag does not tremble because there is no current that is not already accounted for.

The chemistry of the place is not water’s chemistry. What little fluid exists is an oil, a seep of amber lubricant that beads on the engine and is sipped by the barnacles and returned, somehow, to the sump, so that the sight-glass on the housing always shows the same meniscus. The clickers metabolize salt and current. The hangers live on heat and on the constant noise, which they strain, perhaps, the way other lives strain light. Nothing drinks. Nothing sweats. The warmth is a closed account.

You could walk the whole room in eighty paces if the bodies allowed it. They do not, quite. There are paths — the suggestion of aisles between pillar and housing, the ghost of a maintenance route — but the aisles are occupied to the width of a turned shoulder. To pass is to be touched: carapace, ribbon-skin, the papery mouths, the hot iron. Nothing startles. Nothing gives more than the give of a thing that has been leaned on forever.

The weirdness is not an event. It is the rule that nothing here is an event. The fracture-faces of the walls are as sharp as the hour they split, and they split in a century that has no weather and no successor. The bulbs do not burn out. The belt does not fray. The fauna do not die in a way that leaves a gap, and they do not breed in a way that makes a crowd. Population is a shape, like the room’s shape. The engine’s note is a law. If there is a world above this lid of stone, it has seasons, winds, the long insult of water; none of that is admitted. The chamber is a sealed sentence, complete, spoken once, still being spoken.

Stand long enough and the loudness becomes a kind of silence you can think in. The heat becomes a climate you were always going to have. You notice smaller fittings: a brass plaque screwed to a pillar, its inscription a set of numbers that might be a date or a pressure or a prayer. A single glove, stiff, hung from a nail that was driven into crystal and has not rusted. The stencil on the engine, looked at until the letters stop being letters and become only a texture that belongs here as much as the clickers belong.

There is a door. It is a hatch of the same iron as the engine, set into the wall at the height of a chest, reached by those three stairs that end in air and then, if you step off them, by a little gap the hangers have left, perhaps out of courtesy, perhaps because the gap is part of the packing. The hatch has a wheel. The wheel is warm. It will turn, you suspect, and it will not have been turned, and on the other side there will be either more of this — another nave, identically dense, identically loud — or the unthinkable: a corridor that leads up, toward a surface where things still change, where edges dull, where water, somewhere, is doing its old work.

You do not have to open it. The room does not require you. It has its equilibrium, and you are, for as long as you stay, only another presence per volume, near-touching, layered in. The flywheel turns. The clickers rasp. The white stalks hold their one flag each. The dust hangs. The young walls keep their knives. Warmth without weather. Industry without product. A grove of bodies and iron under the earth, finished, and remaining finished, as loud as a secret that has never been told and never will be.
