The bowl is new. You feel it in the way the rock still remembers being elsewhere. A hanging cirque, perhaps half a kilometer across, bitten into the shoulder of a high ridge that itself looks unfinished: knife-edged, unweathered, flaking in thin plates that ring almost musically when they finally let go and slide. The break that made this place cannot be old. The cliffs are faceted like freshly split flint. Scree fans are still white at their heads, not yet grayed or rounded. Occasional flakes detach in the cool air and whisper down, adding to the fans with a sound so slight it is more a change in pressure than a noise.

Light arrives already used. A roof of cloud sits on the rim most hours, not storm-dark but the color of wet slate, and what filters through is the green-gray of late dusk even at what must be midday. Breath shows. The temperature never quite decides to become cold, only to stay just below comfort, a persistent damp cool that lives in the joints and the back of the neck. Moisture does not fall so much as appear. It condenses on every underside: the lee of a boulder, the hanging fronds, the eaves of the houses. Sometimes a fine drizzle walks through for twenty minutes and is gone. There are no standing rivers, only seeps that darken the young basalt in vertical stains and collect in shallow, rain-cut basins no larger than a table. Those basins are everywhere and never last; they fill, they are used, they evaporate or sink into the still-settling ground.

Vegetation should not be this lush here, and yet it is, in the way a single note can be lush. The plants do not carpet. They stand as isolated, extravagant individuals with wide empty ground between them, each one a hanging garden unto itself. Tree-ferns the height of a two-story house, their trunks furred in living moss that holds whole cups of dew. Broad-leaved things that look like they belong in a wetter, lower world, except their leaves are thick and slightly succulent, adapted to drink from the air. Trailing lichens like pale hair. Where a plant has found a pocket of better soil or a persistent seep, it becomes almost indecently leafy, dripping, layered, a small vertical forest. Then ten or twenty paces of nearly bare, sharp-stoned ground, then another. The emptiness is part of the lushness; your eye is forced to take each plant whole.

On the gentler eastern curve of the bowl a settlement has been fitted into the young slope. It is organized, unmistakably a town rather than a scatter of huts, yet everything about it is handmade and close to the ground. Dry-stacked walls of the same pale, angular stone that the cliffs keep shedding. Roofs of layered fern-thatch weighted with more stone, already growing their own moss. Narrow paved ways that follow the contour, a central open place with a ring of larger basins cut to catch condensation, a few covered workshops where people work wood, bone, and the occasional piece of beaten copper. No wheels that you can see. Levers, wedges, fiber rope, fire. The buildings have space between them the same way the giant ferns do; the town is urban in intent and layout but never crowded. Smoke from a few hearths rises and is immediately lost in the low cloud. People move quietly, as if the air itself discourages raised voices. They collect dew, they tend the planted terraces that step down from the houses (more of those same extravagant greens, now in rows), they watch the pass.

The fauna is the thing that makes the scale feel wrong, that turns the small bowl into something larger than itself. There is always movement. Not a roar of wings or a crashing of hooves; almost no sound at all. Pale, long-legged browsers the size of small deer but built more lightly, their coats the exact gray-green of the cloud-light, move through the open ground in a continuous, unhurried flow. They do not bunch. They maintain the same sparse interval as the plants and the houses, yet there are always more coming over the western lip, always more disappearing over the eastern. It is a migration that has nowhere else to go, or that has chosen this new, raw cut as its only road. They crop the lower fronds without ever stripping a plant bare; the vegetation grows back almost as you watch, unfurling in the cool dimness with a visible, vegetal haste. Smaller things accompany them: moths the size of hands that do not flutter so much as glide, silent; ground-crawlers whose shells are the same flaking stone as the cliffs, so that they seem like pieces of the mountain deciding to walk. Occasionally something larger and slower, moss-grown across the shoulders, pauses at a seep and drinks. The air itself sometimes thickens with translucent, pulsing shapes that might be insects or might be something the mist is doing. You are never alone. Even when the nearest animal is thirty paces off, the sense of surrounding life is total, a pressure behind the eyes. They do not call. They do not startle. They simply continue, as if the bowl were a throat they must keep passing through.

Everything here is in process. The cliffs keep shedding. The dew keeps forming and being drunk. The plants keep expanding and being pruned by mouths that never quite take too much. The animals keep arriving and departing. Small weather moves through on its own schedule: a veil of drizzle, a sudden lifting of the cloud-lid that lets a colder, brighter light in for a few minutes before the roof closes again. The ground itself sometimes ticks or sighs as it settles; this place is still becoming. The people have learned the rhythms well enough to live inside them. They have cut their channels to follow the seeps. They have left wide lanes for the browsers. They do not try to wall the valley off; the valley is too new and too much in motion to be owned that way.

There is a rule, or something that behaves like one. Sound does not travel far, and what sound there is arrives softened, already half-forgotten. A flake of rock striking another flake. The faint rub of a browser’s hoof on wet stone. Water ticking from a frond into a basin. A human voice, when it is used, stays close to the speaker. The quiet is not empty. It is full of all the things that have chosen not to make noise, or that the air has chosen not to carry. After a while you find yourself listening with your skin instead, feeling the passage of the herds as a change in the coolness, the approach of drizzle as a shift in the way the moss smells.

At the lowest point of the bowl, where three or four seeps almost become a stream before sinking again, the stone is darker and the plants lean in closer. The browsers pause there longest. The people have set their largest catchment beside it, a series of shallow, interlinked basins that look almost ceremonial. No one has explained what is being caught besides water. The light is dimmest there, greenest. If you stand still long enough the motion of the place begins to feel like breathing: in with the arriving animals and the condensing mist, out with the departing ones and the slow drip of overflow into the thirsty young rock. The bowl is small enough that you can see every edge of it from almost any point, cathedral-like, a single room with living walls. And yet it will not hold still long enough to be only that. It is still arriving, the same way the herds are still arriving, the same way the dew is still deciding to become water.
