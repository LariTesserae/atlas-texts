The chamber sits just under the last competent layer of stone, closer to the weather than a true cave has any right to be and farther from it than any cellar. You would miss the seam if you did not already know the ground here remembers an older incision. The space itself is smaller than a good room and larger than a closet: a single enclosed volume, irregularly ovate, its long axis perhaps four body-lengths, its ceiling low enough that a raised hand finds damp rock before the elbow straightens. It is a pocket, sealed more by habit and by time than by any remaining door.

Almost no light reaches it. What little does is not light so much as the rumor of light: a pressure behind the eyes after a long stillness, a greyness that never resolves into edge. The stone keeps its own black. The plants keep another, slightly softer, as if they have learned to be visible to one another by means that do not involve photons. A visitor standing still long enough begins to navigate by cold and by the faint elastic resistance of fronds against the shin, by the way sound dies differently against leaf than against wall.

It is cold the way a well is cold, the kind that has forgotten the idea of summer. The temperature does not fluctuate enough to notice. Breath, if anyone still breathed here, would hang and then freeze as a thin rime on the nearest pale surfaces. The rock itself is a reservoir of that cold. Touch it and the damp film on your skin goes stiff. The damp is not water in any generous sense. It is intermittent, mean, exact. Condensation beads on the ceiling in slow constellations and lets go only when a bead has grown heavy enough; the interval between drops can be counted in many breaths. The drops strike leaves more often than stone. They do not pool. They are taken.

The floor is a compact of old grit, fine root-felt, and something that was once a poured surface and is now only a hardness under the organic layer, cracked along lines that have been stable for longer than the cracks are deep. Nothing here is raw. The chamber has the settled geometry of a thing that finished happening a long time ago and has since only weathered inside its own finished shape. The walls bulge and recede in worn lobes. Mineral skins have crept and stopped. Where water has repeatedly filmed and withdrawn, the stone is satin. Where it has not, the stone is still faintly tooled—chisel memory, or the ghost of a different kind of working, edges softened until they read as geology.

Vegetation occupies nearly all the volume that is not stone. It is lush in the particular way of a closed, patient system: not riotous, not seeking, but packed, layered, complete. There is no waste space. Fronds overlap fronds until the air between them is a second, slower architecture. Pale structures predominate—blades the color of old paper, of bone left in shade, of frost just before it becomes transparent. Some rise from a moss that has thickened into cushions the size of folded coats, their surfaces beaded with the same reluctant moisture that hangs from the roof. Others climb. They do not climb toward light. They climb because the volume rewards occupation, because an older stem makes a trellis and a younger one uses it, because density is the local law.

Close to the floor the growth is a nap, then a tangle, then a series of overlapping canopies no higher than the knee, the waist, the shoulder. In the last hand-span beneath the ceiling the plants thin and flatten, pressing themselves into the irregularities of the rock as if the rock were another kind of soil. Leaves here are reduced, thickened, almost succulent, their undersides lined with a felt that holds water against gravity for a long time. Nothing flowers in any way a surface season would recognize. Occasionally a structure that might once have been a flower persists as a dry, geometric cup, holding a single bead of melt. The bead stays for days. When it is finally absorbed or falls, the cup waits.

There are no animals. Not a springtail, not a moth, not the rumor of a worm in the root-felt. The silence of that absence is part of the chamber’s temperature. Decomposition, when it happens, is microbial and unhurried; fallen tissue blackens at the edges and is taken back into the cushions without drama. The air smells of wet mineral, of crushed pale leaf, of a sweetness so faint it might be imagined—the long residual of whatever chemistry keeps this biomass from simply freezing into a museum.

Sound is a short list. The drop. The almost inaudible resettling of a frond after the drop. A creak, very rare, when ice films somewhere in a hairline and then yields. If you stand long enough you hear your own blood and then you stop hearing even that. The chamber does not echo. The vegetation drinks the noise and returns a padded dark.

Scattered through this are the built things, which are few enough to count and old enough to have been claimed. In the north curve of the wall, a panel of material that is not stone sits flush, its face slightly warmer than the rock around it—warmer only in the sense that ice does not form there. Water that touches it does not behave quite as water should: it sheets, hesitates, and is drawn in a film so even it looks plated. No vents, no seams you can get a nail into. The panel has no lights and does no work that can be watched. It has been doing whatever it does since before the moss reached it. A second artifact lies half-swallowed by the floor-cushions near the chamber’s narrow end: a ring, or the remains of a ring, of a dull alloy that refuses both rust and ice. Rootlets have braided through its open center and treated it as a trellis. A third is only a set of regular depressions in the ceiling, too even for dissolution, now occupied by the flattened upper leaves.

These remnants do not announce themselves as machines. They have the tact of geology. And yet the pocket’s entire continuance leans on them in ways current description cannot close. The energy budget of a lush, cold, lightless garden this small does not add. The plants should not be this many, this structured, this stably damp. Something in the scattered works still mediates—between the residual heat of the planet a few meters up, between phases of water, between a darkness that ought to be sterility and a biomass that refuses it. The mediation is slow. It does not cycle on any clock a person could wait out. Moisture is metered across weeks. Growth is a secular trend. A new frond, when it comes, takes a season of the hidden sort, the kind measured by how many times the ceiling has fully beaded and let go.

The rules of the place are few and local. Volume is to be filled. Water is not to be wasted. Cold is the given, not the enemy. Nothing with a pulse is admitted; whether that is a leftover interdiction or simply the settled fact of the climate no longer matters. Change happens, but only as a patient redistribution: a cushion thickening by a finger’s width over years, a stem dying back and becoming substrate, a film of ice advancing one winter (if winter still has meaning a meter down) and then being persuaded, grain by grain, back into the common damp. The chamber is in balance with itself. It has been in balance long enough that balance has become a kind of weather, a climate the size of a room.

Stay and the darkness stops being a lack and becomes the medium everything here was designed, or evolved, or simply decided, to inhabit. The plants do not wait for you to see them. They occupy. They layer. They take the drop when it comes and they hold it. The old works in the wall and under the moss continue their untranslatable maintenance. Above, somewhere, there is a surface with a different set of seasons. It does not reach. This is the whole of it: a hidden, finished, densely living cold, kept by remnants that outlasted their makers’ physics, and by a garden that no longer requires an explanation to go on.
