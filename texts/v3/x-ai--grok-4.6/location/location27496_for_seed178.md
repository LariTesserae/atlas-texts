The basin has no proper name yet. It is too new for that. What the maps still call the Inner Shelf is already something else: a drowned, half-risen lowland perhaps forty kilometres across, still settling, still deciding where the water wants to live. The ground came up only a few seasons ago—whether by a last convulsive lift of the old seafloor or by the sudden emptying of an ice-dammed lake farther inland, no one has stayed long enough to agree. The result is the same. Everything is sharp. Fresh scarps of blue-grey clay stand two and three metres high, their faces still vertical, still weeping. Gravel bars have edges like broken glass. Channels cut overnight and are abandoned by morning. The whole place feels like a wound that has not decided whether to clot.

It is always almost dark. Not the clean dark of a polar night but a heavy, wet, particulate dark, the colour of iron filings held in suspension. Cloud sits on the land as if the sky itself had subsided. What little light exists arrives already used: a greasy pewter that never quite becomes day, broken at irregular intervals by sheet lightning that does not thunder so much as tear. The lightning is the only clock. Between flashes the world is felt more than seen.

Water is everywhere and nowhere reliable. It stands in black sheets an inch deep across the flats, then suddenly runs in braided torrents that rearrange themselves while you watch. It hangs in the air as a fine, continuous sleet that never quite chooses between rain and ice. It sweats from the clay banks in slow, persistent beads. It condenses on every surface the moment the temperature flickers half a degree. The air itself is a kind of thin soup; you breathe wetness. Underfoot the ground is never merely wet. It is saturated, plastic, always on the verge of becoming fluid again. Step and the print fills before your weight has left it.

Yet the place is lush to the point of claustrophobia. Vegetation arrived as if it had been waiting just offshore. In the few seasons since emergence it has packed every available volume. Feather-mosses a metre thick swallow the new gravel. Stands of black alder and dwarf willow grow so tightly their trunks touch; their crowns interlock into a single, ice-laden roof that groans and detonates as limbs fail. Between them rise dense thickets of horsetail and a cold-adapted rush whose hollow stems ring like glass when the wind finds them. Everything is coated. Every needle, every frond, every last catkin wears a sheath of clear ice that thickens by the hour, then shears away in sudden, ringing collapses. The plants do not merely endure the cold; they seem to metabolise it. Growth is frantic, almost audible—new shoots pushing through the ice crust even as older stems snap under the same weight. The density is layered: moss over mud, sedge over moss, shrub over sedge, a second and third storey of ice-glazed canopy, and above that the storm itself, which is another kind of vegetation, endlessly torn and re-knit.

The storm does not pass. It is the climate. Wind arrives in discrete, physical masses, each one a wall that leans on the trees until they scream and then, with a sound like a great sheet of canvas ripping, lets go. Behind the wind comes the water—freezing rain that falls so hard it is a continuous impact, a white roar that erases conversation at two metres. Ice pellets bounce and accumulate, then liquefy under their own weight and run in sudden sheets. Thunder is almost continuous, but it is not the thunder of a ordinary cell; it is a low, structural grinding, as if the cloud-base itself were being torn along a fault. Superimposed on all of this is the vegetation’s own voice: the high, glassy chime of ice-coated rushes striking one another, the deep wooden reports of trunks splitting, the wet slap of entire canopies giving way. The fauna adds its own strata. There are too many of them for the dark. Pale, thick-furred rodents the size of cats move in nervous rivers through the moss, their teeth audible as they strip bark. Flightless waterfowl with oily, almost metallic plumage crowd every open lead, their calls a hoarse, overlapping clamour that never quite resolves into individual voices. In the deeper channels something larger turns—sleek, cold-blooded shapes that surface only as a slick disruption of the sleet. Insects, somehow, persist: clouds of midges so dense they register as a second rain, their wings a thin, maddening hiss beneath the greater roar. Everything is trying to eat or not be eaten in the few metres of visibility the storm allows. The sound is not background. It is the medium. After an hour you stop hearing it as noise and begin to feel it as pressure against the bones of the face.

Scattered through this are the artefacts. They are few, and they do not belong to any architecture you can name. A single upright pylon of something that looks like frosted glass but is warm to the touch even in the sleet, its surface crawling with slow, nested geometries that never quite repeat. A low ring of what might be stone or might be cooled slag, half-swallowed by moss, inside which the rain falls upward for a radius of three metres and the air is suddenly, impossibly still. A cluster of fist-sized nodes half-buried in a gravel bar; they emit no light, yet the ice will not form on them, and the water that touches them briefly forgets how to be wet. Whatever made them is gone, or was never present in a way that occupancy would recognise. They do not explain themselves. They simply continue to operate according to rules the rest of the basin has not been told. The plants grow around them without quite touching. The animals avoid the still air inside the ring. The storm, for reasons of its own, leaves them unburied.

The land itself is still being born and is already dying in the same gesture. Banks calve into the channels with the sound of wet masonry collapsing. A stand of alder that was there at dusk is a raft of ice and splintered wood by the next lightning flash. New gravel fans appear overnight, sharp as if cut with a wire. The moss colonises them before the ice can fully seal them. There is no equilibrium, only a furious, local negotiation between freeze and thaw, growth and breakage, emergence and drowning. You can walk for hours and feel the basin as a single open system—its far scarps occasionally visible as a darker smear when the sleet thins—yet every ten metres is a different country of mud, ice, and thrashing green. The rules are ordinary ones taken to a pitch that feels like another physics: water seeks the lowest point and never finds it; cold preserves and shatters in the same instant; life, given even a few seasons and enough melt, will pack itself to the point of self-destruction. The advanced objects do not contradict these rules so much as sit beside them, politely, as if waiting for the basin to finish becoming whatever it is going to be.

Stand still long enough and the ice begins to armour you. The sound does not diminish. The dark does not lift. The next bank is already leaning.
