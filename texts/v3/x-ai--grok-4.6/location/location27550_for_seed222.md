The light here does not so much fall as occupy. It arrives early, a dry gold that seems to assemble itself out of the air a little before the sun clears the eastern break, and once it is present it stays—hard, even, almost without direction. Shadows are short and polite. They hug the bases of things and do not travel far. At this elevation the sky is a thin, unsparing white-blue, and the warmth is the kind that lives in stone: it does not smother; it simply never quite leaves.

What you stand on is old in the settled way. Not ruin, not wilderness first-growth. A highland basin, maybe forty kilometers on its long axis, held between two worn escarpments that have forgotten how to be mountains. The rock is pale—chalks, tuffs, a limestone that has been rained on for so long the rain has become a rumor. Wind has done most of the remaining work. Edges are rounded. Gullies have found their final shapes and kept them. The whole place looks as if it reached an agreement with weather some time ago and has been honoring it since.

Water is a local event. There is no river you can follow for more than a morning. Instead: seeps. A darkening on the underside of a ledge where breath collects overnight. A shallow lens in a hollow of darker stone that will be gone by midafternoon, drunk by air and by whatever comes to it. After the rare, brief rains—warm, almost embarrassed showers that last twenty minutes and then apologize—the karst drinks everything. You hear the drinking if you stand still: a faint interior ticking, like gravel settling in a throat. By evening the only wet left is condensation filming the leeward faces of certain uprights, a skin so thin it is more suggestion than liquid. Things know where those films will be. They always have.

Vegetation keeps to the grammar of cracks. Silver-leaved shrubs no higher than the knee, their leaves rolled against the light. A wiry grass that grows in isolated tufts, each tuft a small republic. In the deeper gullies, where a seam of moisture persists, a few trees lean the same direction they have leaned for decades—short, thick-barked, their canopies cropped by wind into the shape of held breath. Lichen does more of the covering than anything with roots. Some of it is ordinary. Some of it is not quite. In certain bands, especially on the south-facing rubble, the lichen holds a faint geometry, a hexagonal hint, as if it were growing along a preference the rock still remembers.

The animals are the surprise, given how little green there is. They are everywhere and they are quiet. Herds of a low, long-bodied grazer—coat the color of the stone, pads instead of hooves—move in loose constellations across the open floor. You see them more than you hear them. Their passage is a change in the texture of distance. Above, raptors hold in the bright air with almost no wingbeat, as if the thermals here were a kind of furniture. At the seeps: a fidget of small things, rodent-shaped, bird-shaped, and a few that are neither, drinking from the film with tongues or with the undersides of their feet. Insects concentrate wherever moisture does. They do not hum so much as shiver the light. In the hottest hour they vanish into the same cracks the plants use. Toward dusk they return in soft, localized storms that make no sound you would call a sound.

There is a reason the silence holds. The air is thin, yes, and the distances are open, yes—but something in the ground also takes noise and files it away. Voices do not carry. A dislodged stone clicks once and is done. Even the wind, when it comes, is a dry push more than a voice. You become aware of your own blood. You become aware of the small wet noises your mouth makes. The fauna seem built for this: they signal with posture, with the angle of an ear, with a sudden stilling that travels through a herd like a thought.

Scattered through all of this are the marks.

They are not settlements. They are not wreckage in the usual sense. A ring of uprights, knee-high, their inner faces cooler than the day should allow, each upright a little out of true in the same direction, as if leaning toward a center that is no longer occupied. A path that is only a slight compaction, a change in how dust prefers to lie, running in a line too straight for game and too old for anyone you could ask. Once, in a fold of the northern escarpment, a single room with three walls and a roof that is not attached to the walls in any way you can see—hovering a hand’s breadth above the stone, motionless, its underside beaded with the same condensation the ledges wear. The gap has been that width for as long as the weathering around it has been that weathering. Lichen has grown up to the gap and stopped, neatly.

Farther out, at the scale where the basin starts to become a region, there are larger instances of the same grammar. A rib of material that is not quite metal and not quite glass, half-buried, its exposed length warm at dawn and cool at noon, reversing the stone’s habit. Animals graze along it as if it were a fence they have always known. In one place the rib disappears into a hillside and the hillside is very slightly the wrong shape, a convexity too smooth, too patient. Light pools there after the sun has gone, a residual brightness the color of weak tea, lasting perhaps twenty minutes, enough for the last drinkers to finish. Then it goes out the way a thought goes out.

No one living built these things—or if they did, they built them so long ago that building and geology have since sat down together and become one process. The engineering, whatever it was, does not announce itself as machinery. It has no seams you can open, no script you can read. Distances along certain alignments do not add quite the way they should; a walk that ought to take an hour takes fifty minutes, or seventy, and the discrepancy is consistent, as if the ground were folded a little in a direction you do not have a name for. Heat moves wrong in the uprights. Water beads on surfaces that should drink it. The silence may be part of the same habit: a damping, a preference the field still keeps.

None of this feels like a threat. It feels like a climate. The place is in motion the way a healthy high desert is in motion—weather cycling through its thin repertoire, herds translating themselves from seep to seep, the brief rains arriving and being absorbed, the condensation films assembling in the dark and burning off after first light. Predators work the edges of the herds with a patience that matches the stone. A cub, or whatever the analog is, learns the quiet the way it learns the heat. The old structures participate without commenting. Birds nest in the hovering roof’s lee. The grazer-things scratch against the rib. Lichen continues its slow, hexagonal argument.

At the basin’s western lip the land drops—not a drama, an established step—into a lower series of benches, more of the same grammar repeated at a slightly kinder elevation, a slightly thicker air. You can see, on a clear day, the suggestion of another ring, another rib, another place where light will linger after it should have left. The scale is regional, not infinite. You could walk it in a week if you knew the seeps. You would not walk it in a straight line.

Toward late afternoon the gold hardens into something almost white and the warmth comes up out of the rock into your shins. The herds begin their unhurried fold toward whichever hollow will hold water after dark. A raptor slides down a gradient of brightness you cannot feel and vanishes behind an upright. If you put your palm on the stone of the nearest ring, it is cooler than the day, and under the cool there is a faint, regular pulse, too slow to be a machine as you understand machines, too even to be a heart. It has been doing that, you suspect, since before the escarpments learned their present faces. It will be doing that after the next rain, and the one after that.

The place does not ask to be solved. It has already arrived at itself. You are only walking through the version that happens to be lit.
