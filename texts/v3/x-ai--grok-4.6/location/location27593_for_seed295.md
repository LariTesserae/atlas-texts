The highland does not announce itself as a summit. You come up through warmth that does not thin, through air that stays thick and faintly sweet, and the ground underfoot never becomes rock. What you walk on has the give of old packed fiber and the grit of something mineral that was never quarried. It is dark enough that your eyes keep waiting for a dawn that the place does not owe you. Not cave-dark. Canopy-dark, fold-dark: a brown-green dusk held in place by layer on layer of living roof, and by the land’s own habit of closing over itself.

This is a made country. Not recently, and not with tools. The entire mid-elevation of the range—valley floor, benches, the long shoulders that would elsewhere be ridgelines—is accumulated work. Generations of nest, lodge, mound, and midden have been weather-welded into terrain. Surfaces look at first like laterite or compacted ash; they are castings and chewed wood and salivary brick and leaf that has been forced, over centuries, into load-bearing planes. Rain has rounded every angle. Roots have stitched the joints. What once had the logic of a chamber now has the logic of a hillside. You can still, if you put your palm flat, feel the faint hexagonal memory of cells, or the radial press of a long-abandoned lodge, but the memory is only texture. The place has been in its own shape long enough to be mistaken for geology.

Height here is a contradiction you feel in the ears and not in the cold. The air is warm as a closed mouth. Moisture does not run in rivers so much as it occupies. Mist moves in slow sheets along the galleries. Condensation beads on every hanging thing and drops with a sound that would be delicate if it were not multiplied past counting. After a rain—which comes without much theater, a warm vertical soaking that the canopy takes personally—the whole highland ticks and weeps for a day. Water finds the old interior ways: seep-channels that were once corridors, cistern-hollows that were once brood rooms, dark pockets where a palm-width of standing water lasts a week and then is drunk or exhaled. There is never quite drought and never quite a flood. The country holds a damp the way cloth holds a stain.

Vegetation is not a covering; it is a second masonry. Because there is no unused substrate, every plant is an epiphyte of the made world or a root-intruder of it. Ferns stack in wet staircases down the faces of old walls. Broad leaves plate the platforms. Mosses do the fine pointing between courses of forgotten brick. Vines do not climb so much as they renegotiate load, throwing hawsers from one mass to another until whole benches hang together like a single slow decision. In the slightly more open folds—places that function as valleys within the valley—the green becomes almost excessive, a plush that drinks the leftover light and turns it into more surface. Flowers exist, but they are not for you. They open in the permanent dusk as pale throats and are gone into mouths before you have finished noticing them.

The light never quite arrives. What there is comes sideways, filtered, already used: a tarnish on wet leaf, a rumor of day at some unseeable rim. You navigate by paler bark, by the gleam of water, by the way a mass of wings can briefly make a ceiling. At what must be noon the darkness only thins to a tea-colored gloom. Night, when it comes, is a change in the quality of the roar rather than a change in visibility.

The roar is the true weather.

Fauna is not a population here; it is the medium. The highland is occupied past any ordinary sense of carrying capacity, and has been for so long that occupation and structure are the same fact. Insects own the audible air—cicada-analogues in overlapping sheets, beetles whose wingcases clatter like thrown gravel, night-moths so dense that a sweep of the arm writes a brief tunnel. Frogs hold every seep and every hanging cup of water; their calling is not chorus so much as architecture, a pressure you feel in the sinuses. Bats unspool from slits you had taken for erosion. Something heavy and warm moves in the mid-layers of the canopy with a sound of wet rope. Smaller mammals—rat-shaped, possum-shaped, things with hands—use the old interior as street. They do not flee so much as rearrange. You are a local thickening, briefly inconvenient.

The sound does not peak. It has already peaked, always. It is a single fat note made of ten thousand notes, and it occupies the skull the way the mist occupies the galleries. Speech, if anyone tried it, would be a pantomime. Even the drips are only visible; you cannot hear them except in the rare pockets where a mass of earth-brick has slumped and left a momentary acoustic shadow. Those pockets close. The country does not keep silences. It has no use for them.

And yet the motion of the place is slow. Individuals blur, die, replace; the highland itself is patient. A new course of nest-material appears along a bench over a decade and is vegetated in another. A gallery roof sags, is shored by roots, becomes a ramp. The great masses settle a finger’s width in a wet year. Seasons are a change in which throats dominate the roar, a slight warming of the already warm, a week when the mist hangs lower and the condensation runs more continuously down the old faces. Nothing is abandoned. Nothing is finished. The built world is maintained by the same bodies that inhabit it, without plan and without remainder. There is no unused ground because unused ground would be a failure of occupation, and occupation here is the only law that has ever applied.

If you stay long enough to let your eyes accept the dusk, the scale of it opens—not to a vista, which the darkness and the density refuse, but to a sense of adjacency without end. Fold after fold of made hillside, each one roofed, each one leaking, each one loud. A region, not a room. Town-sized if a town were allowed to forget streets and remember only walls and wet gardens and the press of neighbors. You could walk a day and change elevation more than you change condition. The same warmth. The same dark. The same unrelenting occupancy. Far off, perhaps, a paler seam where the highland finally sheds itself toward some lower dryness; you do not see it. You hear only a minute thinning in the roar, or imagine you do.

Materials declare themselves by smell and by the way they fail. Old saliva-brick smells faintly sour when wet, like risen dough left too long. Compacted leaf and dropping have a stable, library darkness, almost sweet. Where bone has been incorporated—and it has, everywhere, in small anonymous quantities—it has gone the color of the rest and takes a polish from passing feet and passing flanks. Nothing shines that is not water. Nothing is squared that has not been rounded back by weather. The country is in balance with its own making: erosion and deposition have reached the long truce of a thing that rebuilds itself from its inhabitants as fast as it wears.

There are no devices. There have never been any that lasted. What looks like a lintel is a root that learned a gap. What looks like a stair is traffic, repeated until the made ground accepted a grade. The animals do not use tools; they use time and bodies and the adhesiveness of their own products. The plants do not wait to be planted; they take the offered surfaces. The rule of the world is only this: that matter which can be organized will be organized, and that once organized it will be lived in past the point of distinction, and that the living-in will be loud, and dark, and warm, and in no particular hurry to become anything else.

Toward what you guess is evening the frogs take a larger share of the noise, and the mist drops until you walk in a warm internal cloud. Droplets find your neck. Something with too many legs uses your shoulder as a brief ridge and continues. The ground, which is not ground, breathes a little under the collective weight. You are inside a valley that built itself out of its own occupancy, high up where the air should be thin and is not, green where the light should fail and has, wet in pockets, dry enough on the old crowns that dust can still lift when a thousand wings decide at once. It does not feel strange. It feels like a place that has had a very long time to stop arguing with what it is.
