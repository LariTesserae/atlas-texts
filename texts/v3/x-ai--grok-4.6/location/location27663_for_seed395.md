The ground is younger than memory. It has the look of something poured and not yet set: broad sheets of pale evaporite, uncracked in places, elsewhere already hairline-fissured by heat, interlayered with fresh siltstones the color of old bone and low, unweathered basalt that still holds a faint residual warmth after dark. Nothing here has been rounded. Edges remain knife-bright. The land sits almost uniformly low, a single continental-scale basin whose farthest rims are only rumor; stand anywhere and the horizon is a flat mineral line under ordinary daylight. There is no standing water. Dew is a rumor that sometimes visits the undersides of leaves an hour before sunrise and is gone by the time shadows shorten. The air itself is dry enough to taste of salt and stone dust.

Yet the basin is occupied to saturation. Life arrived before the geology finished deciding what it was, and it has not left room. A continuous, low, drought-sculpted vegetation covers every surface that is not already claimed by construction: thickets of waxy-leaved shrubs, columnar succulents packed so tightly their ribs interlock, mats of gray-green groundcover that look almost lawn-like until you step and feel the stored water shift underfoot like a slow animal. The plants are not tall. They form a living pavement two or three meters high in most places, denser than any forest understory, so that movement is always a negotiation of stems and spines. In the warm hours they give off a faint resinous smell that never quite becomes perfume.

Through this vegetation, and often grown into it, run the structures. They are not machines and they contain none. Walls of sun-dried basin clay, roofs of living succulent lashed with fiber, rooms whose partitions are still-rooted shrubs trained and interwoven over generations so brief the plants themselves have not yet learned the habit of wood. The settlement has no edge. It is the landscape. Alleys exist only as slightly less-impassable channels between dwellings; courtyards are simply places where the vegetation was persuaded to leave a little more sky. Density is such that every surface is spoken for: sleeping platforms occupy the warmer southern faces of clay masses, storage hollows are carved into the evaporite itself, and the spaces between are filled with the quiet traffic of bodies. There are no streets in the old sense, only gradients of occupancy.

The inhabitants move as if sound itself were rationed. Footfalls on the mineral crust produce a dry, papery whisper. Voices, when they occur, stay close to the breath. Children learn early the difference between necessary speech and the rest. The abundant animals observe the same economy. Lizards the color of the siltstone flow along walls without a scrape. Small herds of long-eared, dust-colored browsers pick their way through the thickets, their hooves wrapped by some instinct or simple wear so that they click only on the rare patches of exposed basalt. Insects are everywhere—on skin, in the seams of clay, mining the succulents—but their wings are soft and their stridulations have never been loud here. Even the heat has a muted quality: it arrives as a steady pressure rather than a shimmer that sings.

Activity never ceases; it simply refuses volume. New walls appear overnight, raised from the same raw clay the land is still exuding in places, patted into form by many hands and left to bake. Plants are guided, not cut; a corridor that was impassable last season now admits a person sideways because someone bent the stems while they were still flexible and tied them with their own fibers. Animals follow forage cycles that ripple across hundreds of kilometers: a slow, almost tidal drift of browsers, then the smaller things that clean what they leave, then the still-smaller things. The geology itself continues its unfinished work. Fresh mineral skins form on yesterday’s surfaces. Hairline cracks widen by a finger’s width in a week and are immediately colonized by the gray-green mats. In the largest sense the entire basin is still emerging, a single organism of clay, salt, and lived-in vegetation that has not yet decided where city ends and ground begins because the distinction has not had time to matter.

Weather is local and almost silent. Dry convection cells the size of former nations rise in the late morning, lifting pale dust that never quite becomes a storm; the light merely thickens, turns the color of weak tea, then thins again by afternoon. Night brings a rapid radiative cool that is still warm by any highland standard, and the succulents release a little of their hoarded moisture as a brief, invisible breath that the next dawn will reclaim. There are no rivers to name, no lakes to reflect the ordinary sun. Moisture lives entirely inside bodies—plant, animal, human—and is spent with the same quiet frugality as speech.

Stand long enough in any of the countless half-courtyards and the place reveals its single governing fact: everything is both new and already full. The rocks have not learned erosion. The plants have not learned to leave gaps. The people have not learned to build higher than they can reach with living material and bare hands. The animals have not learned to be loud. And because the basin is large enough to make its own climate, these habits have become planetary. You can walk for a season and the texture never changes: packed, warm, dry, quietly busy, raw at the edges, green in the only way this place knows how to be green. The horizon stays a mineral line. The light stays ordinary. The silence is not emptiness; it is the sound of a world that formed too recently to have invented echo.
