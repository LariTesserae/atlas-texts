The highland does not begin. It is already underfoot, already past the horizon, already stacked above itself in pale receding shelves. What you first take for sky is only more of the same mineral, thinned by distance until it forgets to be solid. The light is the color of a bruise an hour before dawn and it never finishes arriving. Coolness lives in the stone the way a secret lives in a closed mouth.

There is no water and there has been none long enough that the word has become ornamental. The ground is a sintered cake of salts, gypsum ghosts, and something finer than ash that was never fire. It takes a print and then, over a span of hours you will not notice passing, gives the print back. Established weather has rounded every angle that once pretended to be architecture. Corners have the patience of old teeth.

And yet it is a city.

Not a metaphor for a city. A city: avenues that know they are avenues, plazas whose emptiness is civic, stepped masses with the exact arrogance of administrative blocks, colonnades that march until they forget why, towers whose windows are darker rectangles of the same material as their walls. The plan is urban at a scale that makes your body an error of typesetting. Between one façade and the next a person could walk for a day and meet nothing but the faint herringbone of old drainage—channels that never carried liquid, only the idea of downhill. Density here is a rumor. The buildings stand like chess pieces left out after the players died, each piece visible from the last as a pale interruption on a field of paler interruption.

No toolmarks. No fittings. No metal that remembers a forge. The masonry is continuous with the bedrock in a way that is not construction so much as a change of mind in the mineral. You can put your hand on a wall and feel, very faintly, that the wall is still deciding whether to be a cliff. Erosion has had centuries to finish the sentence and has chosen a grammar of slumps, wind-scoops, and honeycombed undercuts that look like galleries, like balconies, like the negative of a crowd.

Sound is almost a theory. Once in a long while the stone contracts in the cool and produces a tick so small it might be happening in your neck. Those ticks are the city’s only clock. After enough of them a doorway will have moved a finger’s width to the left, or a flight of stairs will have gained a tread, or a plaza that was circular will admit, if you walk its rim, that it has always been a slightly larger circle. The processes are active. They are simply uninterested in being witnessed at human speed. Dry weather comes as a front of dimness slightly dimmer than the standing dimness; dust lifts in sheets that have no hiss; the sheets settle into new streets that were always implied by the old ones. Geometry is the weather here.

Elevation is not a number. It is the continuous fact that everything is above everything else. You stand on a terrace and the next terrace is a cliff below you and also, if you turn your head at the wrong calm speed, a cliff above. The highland repeats itself vertically without stacking. Sometimes a “street” is a vertical face you can walk as if it were floor; the inner ear files a complaint and then, finding no moisture to argue with, goes quiet. Far down—or far up—other districts hang in the same lilac grain, their towers aligned to a grid that does not survive binocular vision. Count them. There are nine. Look away. There were never nine.

This is the part that will not stay still in the mind.

Perspectives refuse to close. A colonnade recedes correctly until the tenth column, which is also the first column seen from its own far side. Interiors are not contained. You pass through a portal the height of a person and find yourself on a plaza the size of a valley, which is the same plaza you left, which is a salt-pan with the memory of paving. Shadows lean in two directions at once and neither is a lie the light is willing to correct. Occasionally a building is only the idea of enclosure drawn on the air in a thinner grade of mineral, a standing vacancy with cornices. Walk into it and you are still outside, only more so. The city keeps its municipal functions in these absences: markets where nothing is exchanged but distance, baths that remember a liquid they never held, archives of blank tablets that have been weathered into perfect unreadability.

Vegetation is a rumor with better manners than most rumors. In the lee of certain walls a crust forms, neither lichen nor rust, a slow mineral bloom that copies the branching of plants the way a stain copies a map. It has no juice and no ambition. It records the last weather and then becomes indistinguishable from the wall. That is the entire flora.

Fauna is rarer and less polite. Once in a crossing you may see a walker. At a kilometer it has the silhouette of a tower that learned to pace. At ten meters it is the size of a seated child and is made of the same pale cake as the ground, joints marked only by hairline cooler-shadows. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It adjusts the city by standing in a place until the place agrees to have been designed around it, then it is gone, or it was a trick of the stacked elevations, or it is still there and you have become the thing that moved. Smaller lives exist at the edge of attention: flocks of something like shed window-glare, paper-thin and dry, that lift when a tick sounds and resettle as a different arrangement of absences. They have no mouths. They eat alignment.

The rules, insofar as the highland permits rules:

Distance is a courtesy the place extends and withdraws. Do not rely on it for rescue or for grief.

Water is not forbidden; it is ungrammatical. If you carry any, it leaves you in the first hour as a white ghost on your clothes and then as nothing. Thirst becomes a kind of hearing.

The city is finished and has always been unfinished. You may add nothing. You may subtract only by walking, and walking here is a form of erosion so slight the stone will not admit it until you are gone.

Light does not indicate time. It indicates permission. When it thins further, the upper districts become theoretical and the lower ones crowd closer, or the reverse. Do not sleep in a plaza that has begun to believe it is a room.

Sound is scarce because most events here decline to announce themselves. The ticks are not warnings. They are the sound of a decision finishing.

If you stay long enough the highland will offer you a district of your own: a widening of the mineral, a set of steps that fit your stride, a tower with one window that looks onto the same window. This is not hospitality. It is how the place metabolizes the error of a visitor. The rare walkers may pause there, as if to confirm the amendment.

Beyond the last countable towers the city does not end. It thins into a grid so open it is only a suggestion of civic intent drawn across a salt-field the size of a climate. The elevation continues. The cool continues. The dimness does not break. Somewhere in that further openness the processes are faster—or slower—and the geometry is less willing to pretend it ever tiled the plane. You can see that country from any high terrace if you do not focus. It looks like more city. It looks like the first city. It looks like the mineral thinking about names it will never need.

Stand still. Let a tick happen. The street you came down is still there, and is also a cliff, and is also a colonnade seen from a year you have not spent. The air tastes of nothing, which is the local word for purity. Above you, or below you, another district completes a weather that has no moisture in it and calls that completion a day.

You may leave by walking until the buildings forget to be near. You will not hear yourself go. The highland will keep your prints until the next dry front, then return them to the general pale, which is the city’s only census.
