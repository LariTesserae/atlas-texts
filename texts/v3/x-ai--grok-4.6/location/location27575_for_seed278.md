The high country does not begin. It has always been underfoot, a single continuous elevation worn so long that “range” and “plain” have forgotten they were different words. You can walk for a month and the horizon will still be the same worn lip of the world, only the quality of the dimness changing as ice-haze thickens or thins. The sun, when it can be guessed at, stays low and exhausted; light arrives already scattered, a gray-blue milk that never quite casts a hard shadow. Everything is the color of wet slate, old antler, the inside of a mussel shell. Cold is not an event here. It is the medium. It lives in the stone the way moisture lives in felt.

Water is a rumor that sometimes keeps its word. A fine rain may fall for three days and never wet the ground, sublimating into a drifting meal of crystals that vanish into the rock’s ancient porosity. Frost feathers every lee. In the deepest joints a dark seepage appears for a season that lasts years, then forgets itself. There are rivers, but they are more memory than current: black threads of ice that creep a meter in a decade, sculpted into forms so patient they look designed. The most reliable moisture is breath and condensation. It beads, it rimes, it is gone.

The landforms have finished their argument with time. Ridges slump. Buttes have the softened shoulders of things that have stood in the same wind since before names. You read an entire glacial grammar in a single weathered face, then walk until the grammar repeats at a larger scale. Inverted valleys, residual spines, shallow saucers that hold nothing but sky-colored ice for centuries. Erosion here is not a verb in the present tense. It is the settled condition of the world, the way the place holds itself.

Vegetation is a kind of writing, sparse and dark. Black lichen maps the available film of water, never more than a stain. In a few hollows that remember south there are patches of something like grass, metallic, never taller than a finger, more mineral than plant. They do not grow so much as refuse to finish dying. Between these marks the rock goes on, and the rock is most of what there is.

And yet the fauna is the fact that organizes everything else.

They are not many in any local accounting. Density is the density of islands, or of the last stars at morning. You may travel ten days and see only stone and the idea of frost. Then one of them occupies the middle distance and the middle distance is suddenly full. They stand as high as the leftover buttes and are easily mistaken for them until the long, sub-audible sigh of their breath arrives in your sinuses and the soles of your feet. A step takes the better part of a day. Their hides are the same weathered continuum as the ground; perhaps the ground is shed hide, the distinction long since planed away. They are the color of the plateau. They are the plateau, walking.

Inside them, work happens. Not the wet work of ordinary viscera. Chambers. Slow reciprocating masses of cartilage and something denser. Heat moved from place to place. They take in the thin damp air, the grit of lichen, the mineral flour of the old rock, and they refine it. What leaves them, now and then, is a brief warm mist that freezes in the downwind air into a glittering rime, and a fine black slag they deposit in trails like the droppings of a factory that learned, over deep time, to walk. There are no buildings. There have never been buildings, or if there were they have been ground into the same flour as the rest of the world. The industrial is not an imposition. It is how these bodies persist: scaled production without a roof, engines without a hall, the plateau itself the only floor.

At gatherings that last years they become, from a high place, a kind of archipelago. Six, seven, rarely more in any one view, each a dark island in the pale waste. Between them the emptiness is almost a substance, a positive thing. The wind, when it moves at all, does so with great courtesy.

Smaller lives farm their weather. Crab-like grazers, silent, live in the lee of the great ones and harvest the rime that forms on cooling flanks at the long dusk. Aerial forms exist as well: pale, kite-like things that ride the weakest thermals the walking engines produce, never flapping, never calling. Burrowers stitch the slag-trails, turning last year’s black residue into the next century’s soil, such as it is. In aggregate, across the continental reach of the upland, the fauna is overwhelming, a thin but total film of appetite and conversion. In any single gaze it is sparse, almost absent. The ecology is stretched to the scale of the place and therefore is the place.

Sound does not live here in the ordinary way. The quiet has weight and a temperature. It is produced, you come to believe, as a byproduct of the great ones’ internal workings, a damping, a swallowing of vibration. Your own footsteps arrive late and already muffled. There are no birds. There is no insect drone. Even the ice, when it shifts, does so below hearing. The only true sound is the change in pressure that means one of the walkers has breathed, and that is less a sound than a weather.

Time is a solvent with no urgency. A storm may take a month to cross from one worn horizon to the other. Birth among the large ones is a geological occasion; the smaller fauna arrive over weeks, leaving their own faint industrial traces. Seasons are not seasons. They are multi-year fadings and returnings of the frost-line, or the slow congregation and dispersal of the walkers. Light never quite commits to day. The cold never quite becomes an emergency; it is simply the clause the world is written in.

You could walk until the memory of lower country wore away like everything else. The land would still be performing its few long operations: holding the idea of water, turning rock to dust, dust to the slow refined bodies that walk the dust, those bodies back to rock and rime. Established. In its own balance. Vast enough that its physics are slightly private—sounds decline to carry, water prefers to be ice or exhaled metal-tasting breath, light arrives already tired. Nothing here was constructed. The only engines are the ones that eat stone and exhale winter, and they have been doing so long enough that the distinction between creature, factory, and landscape has eroded into a single continuous, quiet fact.
