The highland is not a range. Ranges finished here so long ago that even their roots have been planed to a single decision: a table of the world, lifted and then worn until lift and wear canceled, and what remains is a floor hung in the upper air. You feel the height in the thinness of everything that is not stone. There is almost no water to make weather with, so the air does not stack or turn. It simply occupies. Warmth comes up through the soles and out of the rock itself, a residual, even heat, the temperature of a palm held too long against a sleeping animal. It does not come from any sun.

There is no sun to come from. What occupies the sky is a darkness so complete it has weight, a darkness that has had time to settle into seams and door-jambs and the mouths of stacks. It is not the darkness of night waiting for morning. It is a finished dark, the dark of a room after the last lamp has been sold for scrap. Occasionally, at the far edge of a district, a brick will remember that it was fired, and give off a dull red the color of a held breath. That is the local idea of dawn. It lasts a few minutes every few years and no one adjusts their work for it.

The city is laid across this table like a sparse sentence. You can walk a long time between clauses. Foundries stand a kilometer apart, each one a black hull of brick and plate, each one still warm, each one murmuring at a frequency you feel in the teeth more than hear. Between them the plateau is nearly featureless: a pavement of old metamorphic dust, sintered by age into something that rings, faintly, if you strike it. The dust does not blow. The wind that could have moved it finished its work in a previous geology. Now the air is a still warm envelope, and the only motion is the slow expansion of metal as the plateau’s heat breathes through it—so slow it counts as stillness.

They built with what the highland offered after everything else had been taken. Slag, yes, but slag so ancient it has a grain, like wood. Basalt cut in blocks the size of rooms. Sheets of a dull alloy that drinks what little infrared the kilns leak, so that even heat-sight would find the streets almost empty. The architecture is industrial the way a cliff is geological: not a style but a leftover of process. Hoppers the size of hills. Conveyor galleries that run ruler-straight for an hour’s walk and then simply stop, their belts gone to a dark flour that no one sweeps. Cooling towers that have not cooled anything in a span of time that would embarrass calendars; they stand because standing is what is left to them, and because the plateau does not shake.

There is a logic to the spacing. Each works was planted on a vent of the deep heat, a place where the tableland is a lid on a slower fire. The vents are not dramatic. They are lukewarm exhalations through gratings in the floor of the casting halls. The industry here never needed rivers. It never needed forests. It runs—insofar as “runs” is still the verb—on dry circuits: powdered metals walking themselves through electrostatic galleries, salts that melt at the temperature of blood, presses that close once and stay closed, having achieved the density they were asked for. Production and geology have traded clothes. A finished ingot left on a pallet for long enough becomes a low hill. A hill, cored, sometimes yields an ingot.

Water is a rumor with a filing number. In the sub-basement of the Assay there is a sealed ampoule, thumb-sized, said to hold a quantity of the old ocean, or of sweat, or of a mistake. People (the word is generous) do not visit it. The air is so dry that lips crack in a single shift, except there are no shifts, not really. Labor here is a posture. A few figures still stand at the consoles. Their hands rest on levers that no longer travel. If you watch long enough you will see a knuckle whiten, then unwhiten. That is the day’s news.

Sound is a rationed thing. Once in a great while a stack will tick as a plate contracts by the width of a thought. Once in a longer while something far off—another works, or the plateau remembering a river—will send a single low note along the sintered dust, a note you hear with your knees. Conversations, when they happen, happen in the same register: few words, long intervals, as if speech itself were a mineral that had to be quarried. The quiet is not peaceful. It is the quiet of a held tool.

What lives here besides the standing workers is scarce and uninterested in being seen. There are the ash-foxes, if they are foxes: low, heat-seeking, their pelts the same matte alloy as the gallery sheets. They do not hunt so much as attend. They lie against the warm gratings and become, for hours, indistinguishable from the architecture. There are the pane-moths, rare even by the local standard, flat as a stain, that feed by sitting on residual magnetism until they are charged enough to make one slow flight across a street and die on the far curb, where they are eventually pressed into the pavement by nothing in particular. And there are, people say, the Listeners—though no one claims to have counted more than one—who are not animals so much as a habit the dark has of pooling in a doorway and then moving on, leaving the doorway slightly warmer.

The strangeness is not an event. It is the medium. Time on the plateau does not pass so much as occupy, the way the dark occupies, the way the heat occupies. Clocks were converted long ago into counterweights. The works do not start up or shut down; they persist at the exact output required to remain themselves. If a part fails, a neighboring part has already been failing in the complementary direction, and the average is undisturbed. This is not maintenance. It is the end-state of maintenance, the way the highland is the end-state of mountains. Equilibrium was achieved and then, having nowhere else to go, became a kind of faith.

You can walk the open kilometers and feel the city as a constellation of warmths rather than a skyline. To your left, the dull body of Number Four Sinter. Ahead, so far it is almost an idea, the long roof of the Wire Expanse, where filaments were drawn until there was nothing left to draw from, and the dies were left in place, each hole a perfect dark. Behind you the Assay, and the sealed ampoule, and the last measurement of wetness in the world. Above you, nothing that answers to above.

The rules are simple because they have had so long to simplify. Nothing arrives. Nothing leaves. What is dry stays dry; the air will take the film from your eye if you linger unblinking. What is warm stays warm. What is standing may lean but will not fall, not because it is shored but because falling was a fashion of an earlier erosion. If you bring a light—and some visitors, in the stories, still do—the dark does not flee. It waits just outside the beam and, when you tire, takes the lamp’s heat as a gift and adds it to the plateau’s general account. The city does not punish illumination. It metabolizes it.

There are streets with names that have worn down to initials, then to the memory of initials. There are yards where molds wait in ranks, each mold the negative of a part that was cast so often the idea of the part outlived the need. In one yard the molds have begun, very slowly, to cast the ground: the dust that sifts into them takes their shape and hardens in the even heat, so that the yard is growing a second, softer city underfoot, a city of absences made solid. No one interrupts this. Interruption would be a change, and change is the one resource the highland has spent down to nothing.

At the edge of the mapped districts the tableland continues, same height, same dark, same warmth, the works thinning until a last chimney stands like a period, and then not even that. The sparse urban fabric frays into the mineral sentence of the plateau, and you understand that the city was never imposed on the highland. The highland, having finished with mountains and rivers and forests and mornings, grew a city the way a cooling slag grows crystals: because that was the shape left to grow. The engines, the galleries, the standing figures, the moths, the sealed drop of extinct water—all of it is the plateau thinking, very slowly, in brick and heat and quiet, about how to remain.

If you stay, you will learn to navigate by warmth the way others navigate by stars. You will learn which gratings are a fraction less lukewarm, which walls remember the last red blink of a brick. You will learn that the silence has grain, and that if you put your ear to the sintered dust you can hear, not a river, not a wind, but the old erosion still finishing, a sound like a file drawn once across a century. You will not see a plant. You will not see a dawn. You will feel, always, the mild pressure of a world that has already become what it is, and is spending the rest of duration simply being that, without remainder, without apology, in the high dark.
