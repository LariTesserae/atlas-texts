The hollow is not a cave. Caves remember stone. This place remembers only what grew after the stone forgot itself.

You come down through a throat of intergrown boles, the descent already warm, already loud, already dim in the way a closed eyelid is dim. Condensation films every surface. It does not rain here so much as the air continually fails to hold what it has taken; beads swell on the undersides of leaves the size of shutters, hesitate, and fall with a sound like someone testing a drum from very far away. The drops never quite make a stream. They soak into the living floor, vanish into a capillary dark, and rise again as breath. The whole volume smells of wet lignin, warm oil, and something sweet that has begun to turn.

The chamber itself is cathedral-scaled and hamlet-bounded: you can walk its long axis in a few minutes if nothing stops you, which something always does. The architecture is plant. Not plants growing on architecture—plant as the only remaining substrate. Trunks have braided into piers. Aerial roots have thickened into vaulting. Leaves have stacked and lignified into clerestory screens that no longer photosynthesize so much as remember photosynthesis, holding a dull chlorophyll dusk even though the true sun has not reached this far in longer than anyone bothers to count. The wood is established in the way a coastline is established: weathered, in its own state, no longer arguing with the space it occupies. Bark has the polished, handled look of banisters. Where growth once surged it has been sanded by centuries of passage into something almost courteous.

And yet the vegetation is total while the settlement is sparse. That is the first of the place’s agreements with itself. The living mass fills every volume it is allowed; the made things occupy only the hollows the mass has, over generations, consented to keep open. There are perhaps a dozen industrial nodes, scattered the way furniture is scattered in a vast unused hall. Each is a compact of iron, ceramic insulator, and living timber that has been trained, grafted, and bolted until the distinction is administrative. Boilers squat in the sockets of old root-crowns. Drive belts run through tunnels of woven liana that tighten or slacken with the plants’ slow circadian flex. Dynamos turn. The electricity is real, industrial, unapologetic: it snaps in the damp, it lights a few caged filaments to a jaundiced moth-color, it feeds machines whose purpose is the reduction of still more plant into oil, fiber, solvent, and a resin that sets harder than the wood it came from. Pipes sweat. Pressure-valves chatter. The sound of the works is a middle register—clank, hiss, the long groan of a flywheel that has never been allowed to stop—and it is only one layer.

The fauna is the rest of the noise, and the fauna is overwhelming.

Nothing here is empty of animals. The air is a weather of bodies. Moths the size of open hands beat against the dim lamps until their dust becomes a second condensation. Beetles commute in sheets along the undersides of the great leaves, their collective tread a dry rain. Things with too many joints hang from the vaulting and unspool themselves when the boilers change pitch, as if the machines were a tide they had learned. There are birds that have never seen weather, only this indoor climate of warm damp and industrial heat; they nest in the cooling fins of transformers and scream in frequencies that make the leaves shiver. Colonial organisms—half membrane, half habit—slide across the living floors and leave behind a gloss that other things eat. You do not look for wildlife. You wade in it. It parts and closes. It gets in your mouth if you speak too long.

The strangeness is not decorative. It is load-bearing. The plants here do not merely grow; they keep accounts. Cut a gallery and within a season the walls have budded small, regular chambers the exact size of the tools that were left behind, as if the wood were trying to remember the shapes of absence. The fauna includes species that are, by any honest taxonomy, the industrial process itself given legs: beetles that mill cellulose into a paste the factories later press; moths whose cocoons are spun around spent wire until the wire is insulated better than any human wrapping. There are hours when the whole hollow seems to inhale—the leaves aligning, the animals stilling, the machines dropping half a note—and then exhale a warmer, wetter air that tastes faintly of copper. No one has successfully explained the pulse. People who work the nodes stop trying after the first year. They learn instead the local manners: do not leave metal unattended near a growing tip; do not sleep in a newly opened hollow; if a corridor narrows while you are in it, do not push, wait, the wood is deciding.

Light stays dim because the plants prefer it and the lamps are few. What illumination there is arrives as spill from the factory cages, as the dull internal glow of certain fungi that colonize only the oldest heartwood, as the occasional sheet-lightning of a short in the wet. Shadows are thick and occupied. You navigate by heat and by the changing texture of sound. Near a working engine the animal roar lifts half an octave and the condensation on the nearest leaves begins to jitter. In the sparse intervals between nodes the noise drops to a constant, structured din—stridulation, drip, the deep vegetal creak of a pier adjusting its weight—and you can hear, underneath, the old hollow thinking in its slow hydraulic way.

Water remains a guest. There are no rivers. There are seams. A dark, mineral dampness weeps from a particular graft-union on the north vault and is caught in a trough of living bark that someone, long ago, trained into a cistern. Insects drink there in shifts so dense the surface looks armored. Elsewhere the moisture is only ever intermittent: a night-cycle of heavier condensation when the boilers ease, a morning of louder dripping, then the warm recapture. Pockets. Breath. The suggestion of a climate that once knew rain and has since learned to perform a memory of it.

The people—there are people, though the fauna makes them feel like a minority opinion—live in the interstices. Sleeping niches carved (or grown) into the thickness of the greater trunks. A short run of catwalks that have become half root. A canteen whose tables are sections of a single fallen limb that has not finished dying and still puts out pale, inedible shoots between the plates. Their work is industrial and circular: they harvest what the hollow overproduces, they feed the machines, they keep the dynamos from being engulfed, they in turn are fed and housed and slightly altered by the same system. Children learn early which insects may be touched and which are, in a legal sense, machinery. The settlement has the density of a village that forgot to stay small and the organization of a shop floor that forgot it was underground. There are rules posted in three scripts, one of them a bite-pattern the wood itself will eventually overwrite.

Time here is active in several tempos at once. The machines cycle in hours. The animals in minutes, in seconds, in the frantic present of things that live only a week. The plants in seasons that do not match any calendar used above: a sudden flush of translucent new tissue across a whole wall, a decade-long thickening of a single load-bearing root, an afternoon when every flower in the hollow—there are flowers, nocturnal, meat-scented, the size of fists—opens at once and the air becomes briefly unbreathable with sweetness and wings. Weather is internal. A “front” is a change in the boilers’ output or a migration of the colonial gloss from one side of the chamber to the other. You can stand in one place and feel the ecosystem working on you like a weather you are inside of rather than under.

If you stay long enough the hollow finishes arriving. It is not a backdrop. It is a single, weathered, loudly inhabited organism that happens to contain factories, a sparse urbanity of catwalks and nodes, an overwhelming animal nation, and a damp warm dark in which all of that has reached a kind of grinding, photosynthetic equilibrium. The plants are the ground and the sky. The machines are a metabolism someone once installed and can no longer fully uninstall. The animals are the weather and the citizenry and the noise floor. Nothing here is waiting to become a more ordinary place. It has already become itself, thoroughly, and it is still moving.
