The heat arrives before the place does. It is not the heat of a sun. There is no sun here to argue with. What you feel is a stored heat, a basin-heat, something the ground has been keeping for a very long time and is now quietly giving back through the soles and through the wet air that sits against the skin like a second, heavier skin. The darkness is nearly complete. Not the darkness of a room. The darkness of a volume that has decided light is a guest it no longer receives. What little there is comes from the water itself—not glow so much as a reluctant pale, a milkiness that gathers where vapor thickens, enough to guess at edges and then lose them.

You are low. You have been low for a while. The land does not climb; it sags, a wide shallow dish whose rim you cannot find. The scale is regional without ever becoming a view. Things recede, yes, but they recede into more of the same humidity, more of the same dark, so that distance is a rumor rather than a picture.

Water is the primary architecture. It does not wait in lakes. It occupies. It beads on every surface and refuses to finish falling. The air is so near saturation that breathing is a kind of drinking. Condensation runs in sheets down faces of stone and then, at some height you cannot measure, decides to lift again as steam, so that the same water is always in the act of arriving and leaving. There are channels that might once have been streets. They hold a few inches of warm, slow water the color of weak tea if tea could be seen. The water is mineral, faintly metallic on the tongue if you were foolish enough to taste it, and it is always moving—not current so much as a long, patient rearrangement. Puddles migrate. They empty themselves uphill. They appear in the middle of what you thought was dry stone and then are gone when you look back, leaving a darker stain that itself begins to weep.

The city is here. It has always been here. That is the only way to say it that does not immediately become a lie.

It is not a city of tools. Nothing here was cut with a blade or lifted with a machine. The forms are organized the way a river organizes a floodplain, the way salt organizes a pan: inevitable, patient, without intention you can name and yet unmistakably arranged. Walls rise out of the marsh-edge the way teeth rise from a jaw. They are stone, or they are something that has forgotten it was not stone. The material is pale where the water has not stained it, dark where it has, and everywhere pitted, fluted, honeycombed by a weathering so old it has become a style. Corners are gone. Ornament, if there was ever ornament, has been read back into the grain. What remains are masses: blocky, shouldered, set far apart across the wet ground so that each structure stands in a clearing of its own silence. Between them, a great deal of nothing that is not empty—only unused, only waiting, only wet.

The spaces between are the larger part of the city. You could walk a long time from one mass to the next and the walking would be through warm shallows and over crusts of mineral that break with a sound like wet biscuit. Sparse. The word is almost too small. Presence here is a rationed thing. A wall. Then a hundred quiet meters of seeping ground. Then a colonnade that is only the idea of a colonnade, shafts of the same worn stone standing in a row that may or may not be straight, depending on which end you start from. Then more ground. Then something like a plaza, a slightly higher shelf of rock that the water has not quite claimed, pocked with basins that fill and unfill according to a schedule no one kept.

Vegetation is a rumor with occasional evidence. Here and there a pale, heat-drunk growth—something between lichen and a tired succulent—clings to the lee of a wall, living on the drip and the dark. It does not green. It does not flourish. It persists in the way a stain persists. No canopy. No understory. No root-mat thick enough to call a soil. The ground is more water than earth, more mineral crust than loam. What little grows does so as an afterthought of the wet, and the heat keeps it thin.

There are no animals. This is not a hush that animals are keeping. It is a hush that never learned animals. No insects tick against the stone. No frogs, though the water would seem to invite them. No night-birds, because there is no night distinct from day; there is only this. The quiet is therefore almost clean. What you hear is water: the long pour of condensation from a high lip, the smaller ticking of drips into a basin, the rare soft collapse of a mineral crust giving way. Steam makes a sound if you stand close enough to a vent—a low continuous unvoiced syllable. Sometimes the stone itself ticks as it expands in the heat. That is nearly all. The quiet is not peace. It is the sound of a place that has already said what it needed to say.

And yet the place is not still. Dynamism here is slow and thorough and does not require witnesses. The water cycle never completes; it only changes phase. Heat wells up from below—this basin sits on something that has not cooled, a buried remainder of older fires—and the heat drives the water into vapor, and the vapor finds the cooler dark of the upper air (if “upper” is a word that still works) and returns as a rain that is more like a decision than a weather event. Sheets of it walk across the city. You hear them coming as a denser quiet, a thickening, and then you are inside the rain and the rain is body-warm and it does not cool you. Afterward the stone weeps for a long time, and the weeping feeds the channels, and the channels find new paths because the old paths have silted with the pale flour of dissolved masonry. The city is being rewritten by its own dissolution, and the rewriting has been going on long enough that dissolution and form have reached an agreement. The walls are thinner than they were. They are also more themselves. Erosion here is not a violence. It is a tenure.

The rules begin to slip the longer you stay, which may not be long in any clock you brought with you.

Distance is unreliable. A structure that sat on the near side of a channel is, after you blink the sweat from your eyes, farther than it should be, or closer, or slightly to the left of where the ground says it must stand. You learn not to point. Pointing makes the thing you point at consider other options. Reflections in the standing water do not always match what stands above them. Sometimes the reflection shows a complete facade where the stone itself is only a stump. Sometimes the reflection is of a sky that is not present, a pale, featureless brightness that hurts to look at even as an image, and you understand in your body why this place keeps the dark: the dark is a kindness, or a lid.

There are doors that are only darker rectangles in darker walls, and they do not open onto interiors so much as onto more of the same city, folded. You may step through and find yourself looking back at the door from a modest and incorrect angle. You may not. The city does not punish curiosity. It simply fails to share your geometry.

Time is a weather. Periods of slightly less dark, which are not dawn, during which the milkiness in the vapor increases and you can see perhaps a hundred meters of wet stone and the suggestion of further masses beyond. Then the dark tightens again, not like nightfall, like a fist remembering it has fingers. In the tighter dark the heat feels closer. The water sounds louder. The walls, you would swear, lean. Not falling. Attending.

What the city was for is a question the stone has stopped answering. There are spaces that feel like they were meant to hold gatherings, and they hold water instead. There are long alignments that feel like processional ways, and they process only the slow traffic of condensate and heat. If there were inhabitants they left no bones, no tools, no charcoal. Tech was never the grammar here. The grammar was arrangement, and arrangement was accomplished by whatever force teaches rivers to meander and crystals to lock. The city is a habit the landscape got into and has not seen fit to break.

You can walk it for a day that is not a day and not come to an edge. The scale is open in the way a fever is open: there is always more of the same condition ahead. Isolated monuments. Wide wet intervals. The faint pale of vapor where a channel steam-breathes. The rare patch of that tired growth, clinging. The heat, unvarying, coming up from the dish of the world. The dark, nearly entire. The quiet, made of small wet sounds.

Sometimes, at the limit of what can be called hearing, there is another sound: not fauna, not weather. A low, structural complaint, as of masses shifting their weight by a finger’s width. The city settling further into its own long agreement with water and heat and time. Or the city, very slowly, taking a step.

If you stay long enough the step might include you. The ground is already more water than you accounted for. The air is already more body than air. The dark has a temperature. The temperature has a direction, and the direction is inward, toward the oldest, lowest, wettest courts, where the stone is so thoroughly worked by centuries of weeping that it has become a kind of script no one is left to read, and the script is still being written, drip by drip, in a language of absence and mineral and heat.

That is the place. It does not end. It only thins, the way a thought thins when you have held it too long in a room with no light.
