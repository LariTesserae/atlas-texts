The ground is never dry. It is a continuous, interlocking pavement of ferrocrete slabs, riveted iron plates, and aged brick whose mortar has long since been replaced by compacted silt and root-mass. Water stands in every depression an hour after rain and weeps steadily from every joint even in the lulls. It beads on the undersides of overhead ducts, runs in thin sheets down the faces of cooling towers now half-swallowed by moss, and collects in the shallow, geometrically perfect basins that were once catchments or settling ponds and are now dark, reed-choked meres. The air itself is a cool, condensing medium; breathe and you taste iron and wet leaf.

This is not a city built upon a highland. The highland itself was poured and bolted into being: an elevated platform of stacked decks, retaining walls, and service viaducts that rise in long, weathered terraces. Whatever original rock once stood here has been jacketed, undercut, and finally forgotten. Elevation is felt as a constant damp wind that threads the corridors and as a thinness in the light. The light itself is a low, gray illumination that never quite becomes day. It arrives already filtered through three layers of gantry, through the perpetual mist that clings to the upper works, and through the dense canopy that has knitted itself across every open span. Shadows have edges of green rather than black.

Everything occupies. There are no voids large enough to be called streets or courtyards; those spaces have been colonised by later machines, by stair-towers added in different decades, by the lateral growth of pipe-runs, and finally by the plants that treat every remaining crevice as soil. You move through a saturated volume of rusted catwalk, hanging cable, fern, and dripping metal. A factory wall is also a cliff face for ivy the thickness of a man’s wrist. A smokestack is also a trellis. A collapsed loading bay has become a hanging wetland whose floor is a mat of sphagnum over submerged conveyor belts. The density is not merely architectural; it is botanical and mechanical at once. Roots pry plates apart and are themselves clamped by later brackets. Vines follow the same routes as steam lines and sometimes share the same insulation.

The vegetation is lush in the precise, opportunistic way of a place that has been wet and cool for a very long time. Broad-leaved trees whose trunks are half-encased in older brick force their way through former roofs. Their branches interlock with neighbouring walkways so that the distinction between structure and forest canopy disappears. Beneath them the understory is a dripping architecture of mosses, liverworts, and pale ferns that unfurl from every bolt-hole and weep-hole. In the lower, permanently flooded decks the plants become more aquatic: horsetails, irises, and thick rafts of water-cress that hide the fact the “pond” is a flooded turbine hall. The colour is not bright. It is a deep, shadowed green that drinks the already-dim light and returns only a dull sheen when the mist thickens.

Industry continues, but not as a single rhythm. Engines still turn in sealed halls whose outer walls have become hanging gardens. Conveyors still clank for an hour and then seize when a root has grown through a bearing. Automated cranes swing through spaces now half-blocked by trees, their operators long gone, their programming looping through the same three tasks with decreasing success. New construction appears as sudden, bright interruptions: a fresh pour of concrete that will be green within a season, a replacement truss already streaked with runoff. The whole assemblage is in a state of continuous, local failure and repair. A section of deck sags under the accumulated weight of soil and water, then is shored with new steel that itself begins to rust and sprout. Steam vents unpredictably, condenses, and feeds another flush of growth. The turbulence is not loud. It is a slow, grinding rearrangement felt more than heard: the creak of stressed metal, the sudden slump of a moss-laden parapet, the almost silent advance of roots through mortar.

Sound overall remains low. Water is the constant: drip, sheet, gurgle through hidden channels that have become streams running inside the walls. Wind moves through the packed foliage and the lattice of iron with a sound like distant breathing. Machinery, when it runs, is muffled by vegetation and by the sheer mass of surrounding structure; you hear it as a subsonic pressure more than a noise. Birdsong is rare enough to startle. A single call, thin and echoing, may travel a surprising distance through the canyons of green metal and then stop. Insects exist in pockets but do not fill the air. Larger animals are almost absent; the habitat is too uniformly constructed, too continuously wet and shifting, for anything that needs a stable den or a clear run.

From any of the higher gantries the scale asserts itself. The packed industrial-vegetal mass extends in every direction, terrace after terrace, until it dissolves into the same gray-green haze that supplies the light. You can pick out other cooling towers, other collapsed sheds now supporting their own small woods, other streaks of brighter rust where something has recently torn or been replaced. It is a single, continuous artefact the size of a valley, long-settled into its hybrid form yet never still. The weathering is complete: every original edge has been rounded by decades of drip and freeze, every painted surface reduced to a mineral stain beneath lichen. The place has arrived at a kind of equilibrium that is itself a storm, a balance made of constant, quiet upheaval. Water, plant, rust, and remaining machine have become a single weather system, cool and dim and densely occupied, repeating its small catastrophes across kilometres of made ground that no longer remembers it was ever anything else.
