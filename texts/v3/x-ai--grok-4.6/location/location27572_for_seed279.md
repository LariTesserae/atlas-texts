The basin sits lower than the maps expect. You come down off a last ridge of older stone and the ground simply gives up its height, not as a cliff but as a long, wet unclenching, and then you are in it: a breadth of newly broken mineral country that runs farther than the dimness will let you count. The air is cool enough that breath hangs. Everything is wet in the patient way of a place that has not finished arriving.

What fills the basin is not vegetation and is not quite ruin. It is stone that has only recently learned how to be surface. Plates and blades and interlocked wedges of dark rock stand packed so densely that you cannot take a true stride without choosing a seam. They lean on one another like a crowd that has stopped mid-collision. Their edges are still sharp. Rain—or what functions as rain here—has not had the centuries it would need to dull them. Water is everywhere that stone is not. It films the faces. It wells from hairline joints. It hangs in the air as a fine, continuous condensation that never quite becomes fog and never quite becomes weather. The rock weeps. If you put a palm to a slab it comes away colder than the air and slick, as if the interior of the land is still deciding whether to be aquifer or ground.

Light arrives already used. There is a sky, technically; it is a low, even gray that seems less like cloud than like the basin’s own moisture lifted and thinned. What illumination reaches the stone is pewter, wet-metal, the color of a coin found in a stream. Shadows do not fall so much as pool. In the tighter corridors between plates the dimness thickens until your own hands look like someone else’s.

The sound is the thing that will not let the place be only visual. It is loud in the way a millrace is loud, or a hall of clocks, or a forest in a night rain—many small continuous events adding to a pressure. Water finding paths. Water dropping from lip to lip of stone. Water running in sheets down faces still too new to have channels, then detaching, then striking, then running again. The density of the standing rock turns every fall into an instrument. Tones stack. There are places where the dripping locks into a pulse you feel in the teeth, and places—deeper in, where three or four slabs lean into a crooked chimney—where the sound becomes a single held note, as if the basin were trying to remember a word. It does not stop. It only shifts register as the slow weather of the place redistributes itself. Hours change the chord. Days barely do. Seasons, if this country has them, would be a matter of which frequencies dominate.

Nothing green holds. There is a mineral smear in some of the wetter seams that might be mistaken for life if you wanted it badly enough: a graphite bloom, a faint rust, a translucent film that wrinkles when the water sheets over it and then lies flat again. It does not photosynthesize in any way you could prove. It does not grow toward the dim light. It may be a chemistry that has not yet decided to become biology, or a biology that has forgotten the argument. The stone itself sometimes shows a false grain, like wood, like muscle, but if you break a fresh edge—and the edges break clean, young, almost eager—the interior is only more of the same dark mineral, wet as if it had been stored underwater.

The openness is real even so. Lift your eyes from the immediate crowding and the basin continues, plate after plate, ridgelets of shatter, long low swales where the standing stone thins enough to make a kind of street. You can see a kilometer, perhaps more, before the moisture eats the detail. The scale is regional: not a grotto, not a single field, but a country of this one idea, repeating with small variations the way a language repeats. In the farther west the plates trend taller and thinner, like a standing storm frozen in rock. To the east they slump into broader tables with water sheeted across them so completely that the tables become dark mirrors, broken only where a newer fracture has lifted a lip. South, the basin floor drops another increment and the water stands rather than runs, a black shallows dense with drowned edges, the sound there more gulp than strike.

A few things move.

Once in a long while a pale shape works the seams—something the length of a forearm, jointed more times than seems useful, the color of wet bone. It does not like to be seen. It travels by deciding a crack is a road. If you stay still it may pass close enough that you hear its particular sound inside the greater sound: a soft ticking, like a fingernail on porcelain, which is its way of testing whether a surface will take its weight. It eats, if it eats, whatever the films are. There are not many. You can walk half a day and see none, and then see three in a single wet alley, as if they had been queued.

Birds, or the idea of birds, cross very high and do not come down. Their cries arrive late and wrong, bent by the moisture, so that a single call may reach you as two.

The built things are easy to miss because the stone already looks arranged. Then you find a run of identical blocks too regular for fracture, mortared with something that has mostly dissolved, a short wall that begins in a slab and ends in water. Or a post of worked metal, oxidized to the same dark as the rock, still standing because the plates have closed around it like a thought. There is, in one of the wider swales, a floor: not large, perhaps the footprint of a single room, tiles the color of old tooth, each tile slightly cupped as if someone had expected rain to be gathered and used. The tiles are slick. Water treats them as it treats everything else. No tools remain. No writing that has survived the weeping. The impression is not of a people who failed so much as of a people who passed through, set a few marks to prove the visit, and understood that the basin would not keep them. The marks are already being learned by the stone. Edges of tile have begun to grow a rind of the same mineral as the plates, a slow overgrowth, patient as lichen and not lichen.

Time here is a weak gradient. The plates settle. A hairline becomes a seep becomes, in some future you will not see, a new corridor. Water rewrites the chord by millimeters. If anything is still emerging—if the basin is still being born—it is doing so at a pace that makes weather look hasty. You can camp between two leaning faces and wake convinced nothing has changed, and only later notice that a drip which struck the left side of a stone is now striking the right, and that this is the whole of the news.

The strangeness is not a spectacle. It is a consistency that has gone slightly off true. Sound that behaves as if the air were thicker than air. Water that seems, in certain chimneys, to hesitate before it falls, as if consulting. A feeling, when you have been among the plates long enough, that the crowding is not only spatial: that the basin is densely occupied by its own attention, a mineral noticing, slow and loud and wet, with no eyes and no need of them. The rare animals move like visitors. The old floor waits to be stone again. The light does not improve.

If you leave, you leave by climbing back into older country, where the rock has had time to forget how sharp it was, and the sound falls away behind you in layers, the way a crowd’s voice falls away when a door is shut, except there is no door, only the ridge, and then the ordinary gray of a cooler world that grows things, and then, if you look back, a last glimpse of the basin still standing packed and dripping in its dimness, working through the long first century of being a place.
