The first thing that reaches you is not a view but a pressure-change in the ears, a coolness that has been waiting. You have already descended. The rim is somewhere above and behind, a pale lip of country you no longer occupy. What opens instead is a basin the size of a small nation of fields, so long-collapsed that its origin as cavern has been forgotten by everyone except the stone. The floor is nearly a plane. Whatever mountains or vaults once stood here finished their dying so long ago that dying became a kind of weather, then a kind of law, then nothing anyone bothers to name.

Light arrives as a solid. It does not diffuse so much as strike and stay. The pavement — gypsum, recemented grit, the ground-down faces of blocks older than any living syntax — throws it back until the air itself seems overexposed, a white that has weight. Shadows exist, but they are short, blue, and indecisive. They pool in the joints between stones and in the leeward sides of walls no higher than a person’s shoulder. You squint without meaning to. The brightness is not hot. The basin holds a cool that belongs to cellars and to the undersides of stones. Breath shows if you look for it.

Water is a rumor that keeps its appointments. At dawn the entire floor sweats: a film, then beads, then a brief glaze that the light drinks by midmorning. In a few low troughs the film thickens into actual puddles the color of weak milk, and these last longer, fed by seeps that have no visible source. People set out shallow pans and cracked basins. They do not speak of rain as a season. Rain is an incident. When it comes it is a fine, undecided wetting that darkens the salt for an hour and leaves the air smelling of metal and old rooms. The channels cut through the city are too wide for what they carry. They remember a wetter argument.

There are no plants. This is not a recent absence. The chemistry of the place will not have them; whatever green once tried here has been edited out so completely that the idea of a leaf feels like a mistranslation. In the damp joints a colorless smear persists — not moss, not quite a film of life anyone would claim — and the animals lick it. That is the whole of the verdure.

The city is everywhere and also thin. It has the scale of a town that decided to become a region without ever consenting to density. Low walls of dry-fitted stone run and stop and resume. Courtyards open onto other courtyards with nothing in them but glare and a single post. Workshops stand apart from workshops: a roofline of hide and salvaged plank, a floor of swept mineral, a man or a woman or two at work with hammerstone, antler pick, copper if they are lucky and the copper has not yet been recast into something that needed to be a knife more than an awl. The blocks themselves are not original to these hands. They have been recut so many times their faces are soft. Corners remember other corners. You can walk the better part of an hour between knots of occupation and meet only a wall that someone began and then abandoned because the ground had other plans, or a threshold leading into a room that is only sky.

Under the pavement the salt still moves. This is the oldest fact and the least discussed. It heaves by the thickness of a fingernail in a year and by a handsbreadth in a generation; walls lean; a street that ran true in a grandmother’s time now has a kink that everyone walks around without comment. The city’s final form is this: a continual, unhurried failure of alignment, and the labor of putting it back. That labor is the weather. People restack. They chock. They lever a tilted lintel with a beam of imported wood so precious it has a name. They do it quietly, because the basin takes sound into itself and does not give it back. Voices carry a little and then stop, as if they had walked into a hanging. Stone on stone makes a dull, swallowed click. The loudest ordinary noise is the faint continuous grit of soles.

What fills the space between the walls is animals.

They are abundant without being crowded, which is a different thing. Pale, low, built for glare. Some are the size of goats and move in loose constellations, licking the morning film, shouldering through ungated lanes as if the city were a kind of weather they had agreed to tolerate. Some are smaller and come in sheets — a rustle you feel in the ankle more than hear — turning a plaza into a single changing hide for the time it takes them to cross. There are flyers, but they keep to the rim-shadows and to the rare hours when the light stands down; their wings make no announcement. None of them cry in a way that would count as crying. The whole ecology has settled, over whatever span this place requires to settle anything, on a pact of near-silence. You become aware of them as pressure, as a change in the available floor, as a smell of clean dust and protein. People take milk, take hair, take the dry dung that is the only reliable fuel. They do not so much herd as stand in the rivers and dip.

The turbulence is this standing. Paths change. A neighborhood that was a thoroughfare at first light is a still courtyard by noon because a different animal-weather has chosen a different grain of the city to pour through. Children learn the new grain the way other children learn tide. Work pauses, resumes, relocates to the next empty room. Scaffolding of lashed reed and bone goes up against a wall that will not be there in that configuration next year. Someone is always carrying a stone. The bazaar, when it condenses, is not a place so much as a density that happens — mats unrolled in a glare-struck intersection, copper bits and salt-cakes and the dried meat of whatever last crossed in sufficient number, hands talking more than mouths. Then it thins again. The city inhales.

At the edges, where the basin begins its long rise toward the broken rim, the pavement gives way to a scree of older work: steps that climb toward nothing, the stumps of piers, a scattering of spheres someone carved for a reason no one now advances. These are not ruins in the ordinary sense. They are the basin’s own bones, worn to the same final softness as everything else, and the present city uses them as quarry and as furniture. A sphere makes a good seat. A pier-stump is a landmark. The light on them is the same light, which is to say it refuses nostalgia. It bleaches equally.

Night, when it comes, is not a true contrary. The mineral holds the day for a long time, a slow interior white, and the animals keep moving through it. Condensation begins again. You hear, if you have been here long enough to hear anything, the first ticks of water starting their work in the joints. By then the rebuilders have gone in under hide and stone, and the city is only its plan and its herds and the cool that has been waiting the whole time, older than both.
