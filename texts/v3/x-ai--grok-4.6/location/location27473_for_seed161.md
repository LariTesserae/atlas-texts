The Last Terrace sits where the world forgot how to fall any farther. After ages of wind that outlasted mountains, after every slope had been planed into dust and every range reduced to rumor, one small table of stone remains: a hamlet-sized disc of primordial basalt and fused grit, its edges scalloped into soft cliffs that drop away into a haze so deep the lowlands are only a rumor of ochre. The Terrace itself is nearly level, worn to a final, patient horizontality, yet the drop around it is so abrupt that standing at the rim feels like standing on the lip of the first day. Nothing below can be seen clearly; the air there is thicker with the powdered remains of everything that used to be higher.

No rain has fallen here in living memory, nor in the memory of the stones. Moisture exists only as a faint mineral film that the oldest plants wick from the rock itself, a chemistry older than sap. The vegetation is nevertheless dense, a low, interlocking thicket of grey-green succulents, wiry bunchgrasses gone to seed-heads like rusted wire, and cushion plants that have grown into one another until they form a single living carpet broken only by the paths and walls. Their leaves are thick, dusty, almost mineral in texture; they do not gleam. They simply occupy. Between them rise the buildings, and the two have long since begun to share an outline.

The settlement is packed to the point of interpenetration. Houses of the same ancient stone as the Terrace itself stand shoulder to shoulder, their walls rounded by the same wind that flattened the mountain. Doorways are low, lintels worn concave by centuries of hands. Roofs are stepped and overlapping so that one family’s terrace is another’s floor; narrow lanes tunnel between them, roofed in places by living mats of the same drought-plants that grow on the ground. There is no timber. Everything is cut, stacked, or simply left standing where the erosion first suggested a room. Tools are stone, bone, and the occasional salvaged sliver of meteoritic iron worked cold. People move slowly among these rooms, their gestures economical, their voices carrying in the thin, warm air like pebbles dropped into a cistern.

The light is always late. A high, ochre veil of dust—lifted from the vanished ranges and never quite settled—turns the sun into a dull coin. Shadows are long even at midday, and the color of everything is the color of old bronze left in a drawer. Heat is constant but never fierce; it radiates from the rock itself, a residual warmth stored across geological afternoons.

Life is loud here precisely because it has so little space. Insects rasp in the succulents with a sound like dry seeds shaken in a gourd. Small, dust-colored lizards rattle across walls in numbers that make the masonry seem to crawl. Birds the size of fists nest in every crevice, their calls overlapping into a continuous, metallic chatter that the wind then takes up and throws against the cliffs. The wind itself is the loudest citizen: it funnels through the lanes, moans under the overlapping roofs, and rises at the rim into a long, unending note that has been the same note for longer than language. People speak over it without raising their voices; they have learned the exact pitch that will carry.

Nothing here is in a hurry. Plants add a ring of tissue once a decade. Walls slump another finger’s width in a generation. The animals follow circuits so old they have worn grooves in the stone. Even the dust falls slowly, circling the Terrace in patient gyres before it is taken by the wind again. The people keep the same hearths their great-grandparents kept; they repair rather than rebuild. The Terrace has reached the shape it will keep until the last grain is carried off, and everyone who lives on it has settled into that same finality. They tend the plants, they listen to the wind, they count the lizards, and they wait with the rock.
