The water has no surface you can remember reaching. It simply is—the medium, the weather, the gravity, the time. Cold enough that movement through it feels delayed, as if the body must negotiate with a thicker physics. Not frozen. The cold here is a settled fact, long-established, the temperature of stone that has forgotten sunlight. Light arrives as rumor: a directionless greying that never quite becomes vision. Distances collapse into a brown-black that particles of silt keep rewriting. You know the plateau is high only because the land still believes it. The ground swells and recedes in the long, exhausted rhythms of old uplands—shoulders of mountains worn down to suggestion, hanging valleys that now hold only quieter water, a canyon whose walls have been rounded by centuries of the same slow current until they look almost polite.

This is not an abyss. The drop-offs exist, yes, far out at the edges of the plateau where the terrain finally surrenders and the water darkens into something that has never heard of height. But here, across this regional expanse, the bottom stays within a few hundred meters of itself, an open highland drowned in place. Silt plains stretch between the living stands, pale and almost featureless, marked only by the faint, parallel scoring of currents that have run the same paths since the flooding finished its work and the world agreed to this arrangement. The erosion is complete. Nothing sharp remains. Boulders are rumps. Ridges are rumpled bedding. The land has been in this state long enough to have opinions about it.

Vegetation occurs in isolated, almost ceremonial groves. Where it exists it is lush to the point of excess—layered, overlapping, dripping with itself. Tall ribbon-forms rise thirty meters and more, their blades the colour of old bone or bruise, so densely packed that the interior of a stand is a different country, a vertical thicket that sways in conflicting directions at once. Epiphytic films and hanging curtains of finer growth turn every stem into architecture. They are not kelp. They do not need the sun they almost never receive. Their chemistry is slower and stranger: they mine the faint mineral breath of the plateau itself, the residual warmth in certain seams of rock, the dissolved ghosts of whatever this place was before the water decided to stay. Between the groves there is almost nothing. Kilometers of open silt. The lushness is concentrated, spent lavishly in patches and then abandoned, so the overall impression is of a moor that learned to grow cathedrals and then forgot why.

Nothing moves here that is not water or plant. No burrows. No tracks. No sudden dart of something deciding to live. The ecology is a single kingdom conducting all its business in vegetable time. Reproduction is a matter of runners that creep under the silt for years before erupting, or of spore-clouds released in rare, coordinated events that turn the water into a hissing milk and then settle as a new film on everything. The groves themselves migrate. Not quickly. A stand will lean, send a cohort of explorers, allow its trailing edge to brown and slough, and over decades the entire forest will have walked a few hundred meters across the plateau, leaving behind a palimpsest of dead holdfasts that the silt slowly buries. The process is audible. Everything here is audible.

Sound is not background. It is weather. The constant low groan of millions of blades against one another builds into a choir that has no leader, a wet, leathery thunder that rises and falls with the larger currents. When a whole grove shifts its weight the noise becomes structural, like a building deciding to remember it is timber. Pressure fronts from distant storms—storms that happen in a sky no one here has seen—arrive as deep, slow detonations that make the silt jump in place. Ice, somewhere far above or in the hanging valleys, calves or fractures and the report travels for minutes. There are no animals to scream or click or sing, so the world has filled the available bandwidth with its own geology and botany. It is loud the way a cathedral is loud when empty: the space itself performing.

Scattered across all this, never clustered, never explaining themselves, are the built things. A ring of standing stones, each taller than a person, their faces softened into almost-faces by the same current that rounded the hills. A low wall of stacked slate that once perhaps marked a field and now merely interrupts the silt, plants using its lee as a nursery. A single corbelled hut, doorway still intact, interior drifted half-full of pale mud, the stones fitted with the patient accuracy of hands that had no metal to spare and all the time they thought they needed. Some surfaces hold the ghosts of tool marks—pecked grooves, a spiral worn nearly flush. They are primitive in the way a decision can be primitive: early, sufficient, already ancient when the water came. The groves treat them as topography. Sometimes a ribbon-forest will incorporate a hut into its architecture, growing through the doorway and out the smoke-hole that no longer means smoke. Sometimes the plants keep a scrupulous distance, leaving a neat halo of bare silt around a menhir as if the stone still has rights.

The plateau is active without hurry. Currents reverse on a cycle longer than weather and shorter than geology; when they do, every grove performs a slow, collective bow and the sound changes key. Sediment storms boil up from the canyon and travel for days as walls of moving night, louder than the plants, erasing and redepositing the top layer of the world. Certain seams in the rock exhale warmer water in pulses, and the vegetation there grows darker, faster, almost eager. The darkness itself is not static. It has weather: periods when the greying strengthens enough that you can see the next grove as a suggestion, and longer periods when even your own hands become hearsay. In the deepest of these the plants continue their noise undiminished, as if light had never been part of the contract.

There is a wrongness that does not announce itself all at once. The water has memory in excess of physics. Swim a path and later the current will prefer that path, faintly, for hours. The groves sometimes flower—if the word applies—in perfect silence, and the silence is louder than their usual roar, a held breath the entire plateau seems to take. Standing inside one of the stone rings you can feel the temperature drop another fraction, as if the stones are still performing a function no one assigned them. Time is viscous here in the same way the cold is. Events arrive late and leave residue. The high land, fully claimed by liquid, continues to behave as highland: it sheds its weather downward into the true deeps beyond the drop-off, it remembers drainage, it holds its elevation like a secret the water has agreed not to contradict.

You could walk—or swim, or drift—for a very long time and the character of the place would not change, only its local sentences. Another grove. Another empty hut filling with the same silt. Another canyon that was a valley. The scale is open enough that the mind keeps expecting a horizon and receiving only more of the same attentive dark. Nothing here is trying to end. The flooding finished. The wearing-down finished. The agreement between stone, cold, plant, and noise has been in force long enough to have weather of its own. It is a country that has already happened, and is still happening, loudly, in the dark, without witnesses of any other kingdom.
