The first thing that is not water is height.

You feel it before you understand it: a pressure-change in the chest, a thinning that should mean air and instead means *shallower dark*. The sea here sits on a table of unfinished stone. It is not a basin that was carved and then filled. It is a highland that has not yet decided it is land. The rock is still arriving.

It arrives the way milk arrives at the surface of tea—slow bloom, pale front, a thickening that remembers being liquid. In the dim, you can watch it if you are patient. A ledge that was not there last season now has the thickness of a palm. A wall that was slick calcium last year has grown a second skin of something harder, gray-green, tasting of iron if you put your tongue to it. The people who live here do not call this growth. They call it *the coming-up*. They do not speak of mountains. They speak of the floor rising to meet them, and of the water that refuses, mostly, to leave.

Almost everything is water. Not metaphorically. The atmosphere above the plateau is a second, thinner sea: warm rain that does not fall so much as occupy, a standing weather, a wall you walk through and never exit. Visibility is a few body-lengths of green-gold dusk. Below that hanging wet, the true water begins without ceremony. There is no clean meniscus, no tidy shore. There are densities. You wade, then you are swimming, then you are walking again on a roof, then you drop through a window into a room full of still, warm dark, and a child is sleeping in a hammock of woven root because the hammock is the only dry-enough place, and even the hammock is beaded.

The town is this: chambers and weirs and stacked basins, a settlement the size of a long valley if you include what is drowned and what is only half-drowned and what will be drowned next year when the coming-up pauses and the rain does not. It is urban the way a reef is urban. Passages have names. Intersections have customs. Someone has cut steps into a face of new stone that is still soft enough to take a thumbnail, and those steps are already being used as a market, if market is the word for people exchanging dried fiber, fish-bone awls, cakes of rendered fat, and coils of a black vine that holds a knot better than any cord. There is no metal worth speaking of. A few hammered nodules of something reddish, found where the new rock sweats, beaten into blades that dull in a week. Most work is stone on stone, bone on hide, tooth on sinew, hand on the living grain of the wet wood that grows here.

The wood is not wood. It is vegetation that has not agreed to be terrestrial. It is lush in the way a wound is lush—too much life for the available form. Forests hang. They hang from the undersides of the rising shelves, from the lips of drowned doorways, from one another. Fronds the color of old bronze, thick as thighs, weighted with bladders of gas so they lift and droop with a tide you can barely feel. Mats of a fine green hair that nets the dim light and turns it into a kind of slow heat. Flowers, if they are flowers, the size of bowls, opening not to insects (there are almost no insects) but to the current, sipping mineral. When you push through a street you are always pushing through plant. The density is not crowd-density. It is saturation. Surfaces touch. Layer rests on layer. Your shoulder leaves a dark print in moss that will close behind you by evening.

Evening is a rumor. Light here is a single long hour that forgot how to become day. It comes from above as a bruise-colored seepage through the rain-sea, and from the stone itself as a faint, sourceless pallor, as if the embryonic rock still carried some of the glow of whatever furnace it was poured from. No one has a strong shadow. Faces are read by the wet shine on cheekbone and lip. At what they call night the pallor withdraws into the deeper walls and the town becomes a system of remembered routes, hand following handhold, breath held through the longer duck-unders.

Sound is mostly refused. Water takes it. What remains is low and infrequent: the tick of a pebble releasing from a new ceiling and falling a long time; the soft collision of two hanging fronds; someone far off knocking a signal-stone against a signal-stone, three slow, then two, a language of intervals. Voices do not carry. People stand close to speak, forehead to forehead, and even then the words arrive as if wrapped. Children learn not to shout because shouting is a thing that happens to other kinds of air. The quiet is not peace exactly. It is the acoustics of a world that has not finished being a world, and so has not yet learned to ring.

Animals are scarce, and this is one of the strangenesses people argue about without heat, the way you argue about weather that never changes. There should be more. The plants are a feast. The water is warm as blood in the shallows and only a little cooler in the deep rooms. And yet: a pale eel once a month in the market, a bird that is not quite a bird seen hanging under a ledge, its wings more like wet paper, gone when you look again. Shells that walk, rarely. Something large that leaves a groove in the silt of the lowest street and never shows a body. The ecology feels paused, or exclusive, or not yet invited. The people do not hunt so much as wait, and mend, and grow the plants that will grow, which is most of them.

Time is the same temperature as the water. Change happens, but it happens the way the rock happens. A family will deepen a doorway over a decade because the floor came up and the lintel did not. A canal that was useful becomes a hallway; a hallway becomes a well. The town migrates vertically by inches, always negotiating with the coming-up and with the rain that will not stop occupying. You can stand on a high lip—high for here, a spine of raw geology still showing its pour-lines and its unweathered edges, almost no erosion yet, the planet’s first draft of a ridge—and look out across the whole of it: a dim, warm country of roofs just under the surface, of streets that are also channels, of gardens that are also lungs, of a settlement breathing through plants in a sea that sits on a mountain that is still being born.

The rules are practical and a little wrong.

You do not strike the new stone hard. It remembers the blow and weeps a milk that spoils food and numbs the mouth.

You do not sleep in a room that has not been sounded. The coming-up sometimes closes a chamber around a sleeper, not from malice, from inattention. The rock does not know the difference between a person and a pocket of water.

You share air the way you share bread. There are bells of it, trapped under inverted basins, tended, rationed in the deeper weeks when the rain-sea thickens and the true water rises to the second storey. People have opinions about whose lungs are wasteful.

You treat the hanging forests as architecture and as kin. Cut only what has already browned at the bladder. A cut that is too eager will bleed a sweetness into the water that draws the rare fauna, and the rare fauna, when it comes, is not always the size you hoped.

You measure wealth in dryness and in height and in the number of rooms whose walls have hardened enough to carve. A finished corner is an heirloom. A window that opens into actual air—true air, thin and astonishing, a brief lens of not-water on the rarest mornings when the hanging sea tears—is a civic treasure. People line up not to look at a view but to feel their own weight correctly for a few breaths, to hear their own voice arrive at their own ear without the wrap of wet.

And there is the other rule, the one that makes visitors, when there are visitors, go quiet in a different way.

Sometimes the water is older than the stone it covers, and sometimes it is younger. You can taste the difference. The older water has a mineral patience; it has been high here since before the plateau began to admit it was a plateau. The younger water is rain that has only just decided to stay, and it is restless in the mouth, almost sweet, almost empty. Where they mix, in certain stairwells, in the long drowned market, the current writes a slow helix and the plants lean in two directions at once. People born in those mixings are said to know, without looking, when a wall is about to become floor. They are given the work of sounding the rooms. They are listened to. They do not live longer. They live *next to* the coming-up, the way a hand lives next to a fever.

If you stay, the place will not rush you. Nothing here rushes. The rain will occupy you. The stone will offer you a lip, then a step, then a room that was not a room until you needed it and the rock, embryonic and indifferent and strangely courteous, closed a curve around your need. You will learn the muffled calendar: the week the bronze fronds release their spores in a cloud that is also a silence; the month the new ridge shows a seam of that reddish metal and the whole town becomes a line of kneeling figures, tapping, gathering, not quite praying; the long season when the hanging sea thins just enough that the dim brightens to the color of a bruise healing, and everyone climbs, dripping, to stand on the unfinished heights and remember that elevation was supposed to mean above.

It does. It also means under. Both are true at once, the way a word can mean itself and its opposite if you say it underwater, slowly, close to someone else’s mouth, in a town that is a reef that is a city that is a mountain that has not finished arriving, in a country of warm dark water that has nowhere lower to go, and so remains, and remains, and remains, lush and quiet and only beginning.
