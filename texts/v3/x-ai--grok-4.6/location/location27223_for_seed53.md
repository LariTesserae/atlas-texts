The place is a hanging basin no wider than a large cloister, a stone room open only at the top and at one notched lip to the west. You come into it over that lip and the world drops away on three sides into distances you cannot usefully think about. The air is already too thin for thinking much. What remains of thought arrives as glare.

The walls are old ice-scoured schist, dark where they are dry, steel-blue where melt has filmed them. They rise close and nearly vertical, fluted by a long argument between frost and grain. The argument is finished in the large; the cirque has its shape and will keep it. What continues is smaller: a grain popped from a face in the night, a wafer of rock ringing once on the floor, a wet dark line that has used the same hairline crack since before anyone stacked a stone here. The floor is not a floor so much as a packed wreck of plates and cobbles, frost-heaved, interlocked, glazed in the hollows. You move by choosing gaps.

What fills the room is ice, standing.

It stands in blades and keel-shapes and thin leaning slabs, some no thicker than a hand, some like the prows of buried boats. They crowd one another. In the densest middle you turn sideways. Their sunward faces are pitted and dripping; their shaded faces are still, milky, furred with last night’s rime. They all lean the same way, a few degrees off vertical, as if a congregation had been told to look west and had never been told to stop. Between them the light is not a single thing. It is a argument of transmissions: clear ice piping the sky down into the cobbles, fractured ice throwing it back as white fire, wet ice making wandering coins of it on the dark stone. The sky itself, what you can see of it, is a hard mineral blue that looks nearer to black at the zenith. There is no softness in any of this. Shadows have edges you could cut cloth on.

It is cold in the way of high places that have never held a forest: the sun is a pressure on skin and a lie in the lungs. Stand in a blade’s lee and the wet on your cuff goes rigid in a minute. Step into the open and the same cuff steams. The damp is not weather so much as a local economy. A seep comes out of the north wall at a stain of rust-colored mineral and spends the day as a thread, the night as a tooth of ice, the next noon as a thread again. Pockets in the bedrock hold water the color of weak tea, no deeper than a palm, skinned with plates that form and dissolve while you watch. Breath becomes a brief white architecture and falls apart. On the rare afternoon a cap of cloud tears across the notch and a fine grit of ice-needles slants through the grove, ticking on the blades, then ceases. There is no soil to hold any of it.

Nothing grows. There is not even the grey dust of lichen on the long-stable faces. The schist is too burnished, the season of melt too short, the night too absolute. What looks at first like moss in a crack is only a residue of iron and old leaf-stain from some altitude far below, blown here and trapped, a rumor of a greener world that does not apply. No bird crosses the notch. Once in a great while a midge, baffled, dying, writes a brief scribble in a sun-pocket and is gone. The place does not include animals among its facts.

It does include work, faintly.

Against the east wall someone dry-laid a crescent of slabs, shoulder-high, a wind-break or a shrine or a place to sleep sitting up. Two courses have slumped. In the lee the cobbles are paler, as if they were turned often. A hammerstone with a bruised equator lies where a hand left it; the rock it was dressing is a block of the same schist, one face pecked toward flatness and then abandoned. Higher, in a crack that takes the sun at noon, a pin of dark metal has been driven and has stained the stone in a long teardrop of rust. The head is simple, mushroomed by use. Nearby the wall holds a few pecked marks that might be a count or a name or a route, already weathering into the general fluting. On a ledge no wider than a boot, a short row of white quartz cobbles has been set with care. They are not native to the basin. Someone carried them. That is the whole of the built world: a pause, a tool, a stain, a small obedience to arrangement. The ice has grown around these things and receded around them many times. The stones do not mind.

Sound is a scarce material. The drip from the sun-faces has a precise, spaced grammar, different in the morning than at the height of the light. Wind, when it comes down the notch, is flattened and thinned by the blades until it is only a pressure in the ears and a faint glass-harmonica along the thinner keels. A blade that has leaned too far will, without preamble, unweight and fall. The fall is a single dry report, then a long granular sliding, then the drip again, now with a new note where the stump is bleeding melt. Your own breath is loud. Your boots, choosing gaps, make the only rhythm that is not the place’s.

The activity is all cycle, none of it hurried and none of it still. From first light the sunward ice begins its quiet spending: a hiss too soft to be sure of, a brightening of the wet, a slow change in the silhouette of a prow as its edge thins. By the middle of the day the air in the corridors smells of stone and of nothing, which is the smell of cold water in thin air. Pools fatten by a finger’s width and find the same ancient exits through the cobbles, darkening seams, vanishing into the body of the mountain. In the long blue afternoon the notch-wind arrives and combs rime off the crests in glittering smoke. Night is a hard retraction. The drips become threads, the threads become teeth, the teeth become the next day’s blades’ sharper versions of themselves. New feathers of frost grow in the stillest alleys, so dense that in the morning you break them with your chest. The leaning increases by amounts you cannot see in a visit. Over a season a corridor closes and another opens. Over many seasons the grove keeps the same general stance, the same westward attention, as if the basin had decided on a posture and the ice were only its weather.

The slight strangeness is not a secret and not a miracle. It is the way the place insists on being a room, and a crowd, and a clock, without inhabitants. The blades look like figures because the sun and the dry air always sculpt them that way here, and because the walls are close enough to make a nave of the air. Light behaves with more edges than you are used to; a turned head can blind; a pane of clear ice will set a wavering coal of sky on the back of your hand and hold it there while you walk. If you stand in the exact middle and face the notch, the wind-break and the quartz row and the rust-teardrop fall into a line you did not arrange. Perhaps someone else did. Perhaps the basin only has so many sheltered facts and they have settled, as the walls have settled, into the only balance available. You can hear your pulse in your neck. The drip counts something that is not for you. When you leave over the lip the brightness follows a few steps down the ridge and then, as if the room had a door, cuts off.
