The first thing is not the height. The first thing is that you cannot hear yourself think, and then you understand that thinking has always been a kind of listening, and the listening has been taken from you.

It is a continuous pressure, not a weather. It does not gust. It does not begin. Somewhere under the skin of the world a chord is being held that no throat could hold, a note with too many edges, and the highland is the instrument. Your ribs learn the pitch before your ears do. After an hour the bones of the inner ear stop reporting and start resonating; after a day you stop calling it sound and start calling it the medium you walk through. It is not wind. There is almost no air thick enough up here to be wind. It is the work of stone becoming stone.

You are very high. The shelf runs farther than weather, a regional sprawl of massifs and dry basins and rims that have not finished deciding they are rims. The sky sits close and dark-violet, as if dusk were a ceiling rather than an hour. What light there is comes thin and sideways, a leftover illumination with no obvious source you can point to and keep pointing to. Shadows puddle in the wrong directions and then, slowly, as if embarrassed, correct themselves. The temperature is the temperature of stored night: cool enough that metal, if there were metal, would sting the palm; dry enough that lips crack without ever tasting salt.

There is no water. There has not been. The basins are not lakebeds. They are simply low places that the rising has not yet filled, shallow dishes of pale grit and sintered dust, their floors still unmarked by any channel. Nothing has ever run here. No frost. No fog. The air holds a mineral taste, like licking a flagstone, and that is all the moisture the altitude will confess to.

And yet there are forests.

Not everywhere. The density of the place is a kind of courtesy: long reaches of bare, embryonic ground between the stands, so that you walk for a long time on nothing but the unfinished and then come, suddenly, into a thickness of pale growth that has no business existing. The growth is lush where it is — canopied, layered, greedy with surface — and vacant where it is not. From a ridge you can map it: groves occupying the loudest bowls, the amphitheaters where the birth-roar of the rock pools and thickens. Between them, emptiness, the color of raw plaster and old bone.

They are not plants in any hydrated sense. Break a stem and there is no sap, only a pith the texture of packed ash and a smell like struck flint. The leaves — if the word will stretch — are membranes, taut, slightly translucent, veined with darker mineral threads. They do not drink. They attend. Each membrane is a surface turned toward the deafening, shivering in standing waves so fine the eye only catches them when you stop trying to look. The groves feed on the chord. They metabolize becoming. In the thickest stands the sound is so completely eaten that you almost imagine a pocket of ordinary silence, and then the silence turns out to be a different pitch, lower, a sub-roar in the soles, and you understand the forest is not sheltering you from the noise but digesting it in front of you.

Roots go down into stone that is not finished being stone. Sometimes the roots arrive first. You will find a pale cable of growth hanging in air over a slope that has not risen to meet it yet, waiting with botanical patience for the ground to catch up. Days later, or years — the distinction is locally unreliable — the slope will have shouldered itself upward and closed around the root as if the root had always been a vein in the rock. The geology here is embryonic. Nothing is eroded because nothing has been given time, or weather, or permission to wear. Faces of cliff are still assembling their strata in public: you can watch, if you have the weeks, a dark band of something like basalt inventing itself along a wall, grain by grain, with the shy deliberation of a bruise. Joints appear overnight and then think better of their angles. A ridgeline that was a suggestion this morning is, by the dim afternoon, a decision.

Because the rock is still learning its own shape, it does not always learn Euclidean lessons. A spur will stand both before and behind the massif it belongs to, depending on which eye you close. Distances agree to be one length when you look and another when you walk; a cairn that sat on the horizon for half a day will be at your elbow when you next attend to your feet, and you will not have traveled. Some slopes are older than the mountains that carry them, their surfaces already thinking about age while their foundations are still hypothetical. Time is patchy. You learn not to trust sequences.

In the open, between groves, the ground is a pale crust over something that has not committed. It sounds — through the larger sound — like walking on a drum skin stretched over a cathedral. Your footprints remain. Nothing will wash them, and the slow rise of the land will only lift them, intact, into future strata, a small personal fossil of passage. Here and there the crust has split along seams that glow very faintly, not with heat, with the leftover light of matter making up its mind. The glow is the same dimness as the sky, as if the world and the air were using one lamp between them.

There are marks of hands.

Not many. The built things are scattered the way the groves are scattered, with long honest emptiness between. Low walls of uncut stacked stone, no mortar, the blocks chosen for how they already wanted to sit. Some of the walls have continued to grow after stacking; a course that was true has developed a slow botanical lean as the blocks remember they are still becoming, and the lean has been left, or lived with, or worshipped — you cannot tell. Cairns. Small corbelled hollows barely large enough to crouch in, their interiors lined with the membrane-leaves of the sound-trees, which damp the chord just enough that a person might sleep without coming apart. Listening cups: hemispheres pecked into boulders with stone on stone, their rims worn by palms, angled toward particular faces of particular ridges as if someone had been trying to separate the deafening into voices.

The workmanship is early and earnest. Percussion marks. No metal that you can find, unless you count the dull sheen in certain fresh strata that might become ore in a few geological moods, if moods are what strata have. Whoever stacked these things understood tools as extensions of patience. They did not hurry the rock. Hurrying would have been a misunderstanding.

You do not find the makers. There is no animal life to have been them, or to have been their livestock, or to have scavenged their leavings. The ecology is a conversation between unfinished stone and the lush, waterless forests that eat its cry. Nothing grazes. Nothing burrows. Nothing flies across the dimness. If there were ever bodies they have been taken up into the rising, folded into a band of darker rock, given the dignity of being landscape. The paths that remain are only decisions in the dust, un-eroded, leading from hollow to grove to the lip of a basin and then stopping, as if the destination had not been assembled yet.

Stay long enough and the weirdness stops being an event and becomes the grammar.

You will see a mountain from the inside while standing on its shoulder: a brief, unearned cross-section, strata hanging in the air like a diagram, then gone, the view snapping back to mere enormousness. You will hear, inside the chord, something very like syntax — not words, the promise of words, the shape speech would take if speech were a mineral. The groves will sometimes arrange their membranes into a single vast tympanum and the note will change key so slowly you only notice when you realize you have been grieving in a different direction. Reflections appear in stone that is not polished. They are not always yours. Some of them are the ridge. Some of them are the idea of a person who stacked a wall and then walked into a contradiction and did not come out on any side you can find.

Night, if the word applies, is only the dimness forgetting itself a little further. The membrane-forests take on a weak inward light, a colorless phosphorescence borrowed from the roar they drink. In that light the unfinished cliffs look newly poured. You can believe, briefly, that if you put your hand flat to the rock and waited a life, the rock would rise through your palm and continue, and you would be a joint in something that had not existed when you were born.

The processes are patient. A grove thickens by the width of a membrane in a season you cannot name. A wall leans. A basin deepens by a thought. The chord does not rest; it is the one present-tense in a country of gradualness, the scream of a birth that has been happening since before the shelf was a shelf and will still be happening when the stacked stones have been lifted into peaks that have not been imagined. You shout and do not hear it. You stop shouting. The place does not notice. It is busy becoming, and the becoming is very loud, and the forests stand in the thick of it with their pale hands open, lush and impossible, drinking a world that has not yet agreed to be real.
