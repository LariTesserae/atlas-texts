The cirque is younger than its own weather. It opened only recently — a high amphitheater torn free of ice, the last tongue of glacier having withdrawn in a single human lifetime, leaving the rock still bright, still angular, still remembering the weight. The walls are not yet rounded. Every ledge is a fresh fracture. Melt still weeps from seams that have not learned how to be dry.

Into this bowl the plants arrived as if they had been waiting just below the snowline for permission.

They did not colonize. They occupied. What grows here is not forest and not meadow and not any temperate idea of garden. It is a single continuous body of vegetation packed into every volume the stone will hold: mosses thickened into load-bearing pith, alpine shrubs fused at the stem into vaults, lichens laminated into sheets that hang like damp tapestries and then harden, slightly, into partitions. There is almost no bare ground. Where a boot would want to find dirt it finds instead a living mattress of root and thallus, cold to the touch, faintly springy, always wet. Water does not run so much as inhabit. It stands in leaf-cups. It threads capillary corridors inside the moss. It condenses on every underside and drips with a patience that has become the cirque’s clock.

The scale is that of a cathedral that forgot it was supposed to have a single nave. You can walk the whole of it in an afternoon if you know the galleries, and you cannot see the far wall from the near one because the living mass is in the way. Streets, if that word can be used, are tunnels pressed through the vegetation by the simple pressure of repeated passage — though almost no one passes. The tunnels are low, ribbed with stems, roofed in overlapping fronds that never quite dry. Light arrives already used: a high, thin daylight strained through cloud and then through leaf, so that noon is the color of steeped tea and dusk is only a further dimming, not a change of kind. At the basin’s lip the sky is a pale slit. Inside, the world is green-black and wet-silver.

The architecture is not metaphor. The plants have layered themselves the way a town layers itself — older growth below, compressed and darkened, becoming something like masonry; newer shoots above, still flexible, still deciding. There are chambers. Some are the hollows left when a shrub-mass died back and was overgrown rather than cleared. Some are deliberately maintained, though not by tools. Nothing here was cut or nailed or fired. Whatever intelligence arranged these rooms — if intelligence is the word — did it by withholding growth in one direction and encouraging it in another, by leaning, by grafting living stem to living stem until a lintel held. The result reads as urban because density forced it to: when every cubic span is claimed, occupation becomes floors and corridors or it becomes suffocation. The plants chose floors.

It is cold enough that breath is visible even in the stillest alcove. The water on the leaves is often a skin of slush that has not decided whether to freeze. You hear that decision being made — a faint ticking as films of ice form and unform. Otherwise the cirque is quiet in the way of a packed room after the guests have left. Drip. The slow creak of a stem taking on ice-weight. Wind, when it finds a shaft, does not roar; it is baffled into a hush by the sheer occupancy of the air. Somewhere far up the weeping wall a rivulet finds a new crack and the sound changes by a note, then settles.

Fauna is rumor. A pale moth once, too cold to fly, clinging. Something small and warm-blooded that uses the deeper pith as a den and is never seen, only smelled on certain still mornings — a musk like wet wool. Birds do not stay. The plant-city offers no open flight-paths and no easy perches that are not already someone else’s leaf. The ecology is almost purely vegetable, and it is busy without being loud. Growth here is not seasonal so much as tidal. After a wet week the corridors narrow. After a freeze they open again as tissues contract. New partitions appear overnight in the upper galleries, pale and watery, and by the next dim noon they have darkened and begun to bear. Decay happens in place, folded back into the mass; there is no clearing, no compost heap, no outside to put the dead. The dead are substrate. The living walk on their own ancestors’ cellulose.

At the center of the bowl, where the old glacier’s last water still wells from a raw spring, the vegetation has built something that can only be called a plaza if one is willing to let the word get strange. It is a depression lined in the thickest moss, circular, perhaps twenty paces across, and the surrounding walls of plant lean inward until they nearly close the sky. Water stands there ankle-deep, black-green, feeding everything. The air above it is a degree less cold and a degree more still. Condensation beads on every hanging surface and falls back. This is the heart, and it works like a heart: pulse of melt, pulse of uptake, the whole packed city drinking from one wound in the young stone.

No devices. No metal that was smelted. No fire that was kept. If there are inhabitants they are rare enough to be mistaken for the hush itself — figures who move by parting the living walls and letting them close, who sleep in chambers that grow a little smaller each year and do not mind, who have no word for tool because the world arrives already shaped, or shapes itself when leaned upon long enough. The rules are botanical and hydraulic. You do not cut. You do not burn. You do not open a way that the mass cannot heal, because an unhealed way becomes a drain, and a drain in this cold wet height becomes a vein of ice that splits the rooms apart. Weather is the only politics: a week of rain and the lower galleries flood to the knee and everyone (everything) climbs; a hard clear night and the upper fronds lock in rime and the city becomes, for a few hours, a single glittering lung, too brittle to enter.

It is still becoming. The rock is too new to have a finished relationship with root. You can find, if you push through to the perimeter, places where the plant-mass has not yet reached the freshest talus, and there the stone is raw and the water sheets off it uncaught, and the air is a little brighter and a little crueler. Those margins will not last. The vegetation is not waiting. It advances by the width of a season, packing itself into the last unclaimed angles, turning the cirque’s youth into a finished interior. Soon there will be no outside left inside the bowl — only the slit of sky, the drip, the dim, the cold that never quite freezes the heart-spring, and the quiet work of a city that grew instead of being built, and has no name for the difference.
