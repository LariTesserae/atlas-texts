The hollow sits lower than everything around it, a shallow collapse no wider than a room, sunk into the long-settled floor of an ancient floodplain. You do not find it by looking; you step off the last firm tussock and the ground simply gives, a living mattress of root and peat that has been yielding in exactly this way for longer than memory. The air drops half a degree and thickens. Warmth rises from below as much as it falls from above.

Everything here is plant first. The floor is not soil. It is a continuous, breathing mat of sphagnum, liverwort, and thread-root that has knitted itself into a single organism the size of the hollow. It flexes underfoot, slow and hydraulic, squeezing out water that never quite drains. The walls are the same living tissue grown vertical: overlapping fronds and aerial roots have fused over centuries into a seamless, weeping skin. Water beads constantly on every surface. It does not rain inside the hollow; the hollow itself sweats. Condensation gathers on the undersides of the highest leaves and falls in slow, fat drops that strike the mat with a sound like distant fingers on taut skin. The air is always just short of fog, warm enough that the moisture never chills, only clings.

There is space. That is the first surprise. For all the living mass, the hollow is not crowded. The great plant-body forms a basin and then stops. A few isolated columns rise from the floor—single, ancient stems thickened into pillars, their bark more moss than wood—and between them the air hangs empty. Light enters normally, filtered but not blocked, a even, green-gold that never quite becomes noon and never quite fails. Nothing here grows toward anything else. The vegetation has already arrived at its final architecture. It maintains itself.

The loudness is not of this place and yet it is the place. A low, industrial thrum arrives from somewhere beyond the living walls, constant as blood, never rising, never falling. It is the sound of engines that have run without pause for so long they have become weather. Pistons or turbines or pumps—something vast and regular—vibrate through the peat. The hollow resonates. Every hanging drop trembles at the same frequency. The plant-mat itself seems to breathe in time with it, a slow expansion and contraction you feel in your knees. The fauna live inside that sound.

They are everywhere and they are small. Frogs the color of wet bark cling to the weeping walls in numbers that should be startling and somehow are not; they do not startle. Their throats pulse in exact counterpoint to the distant engines. Beetles with iridescent elytra move along the stems in unhurried processions, their legs clicking against the living bark like tiny machinery. Midges hang in stationary clouds that neither thicken nor thin. Something larger—warm-blooded, unseen—shifts occasionally in the thicker peat, a rustle that is always the same rustle. Nothing hunts with urgency. Nothing flees. The populations are fixed. Eggs are laid, larvae emerge, adults die, and the numbers remain as they were. The hollow has no seasons and no nights that matter. It is a closed ledger.

Water is never far. It stands in shallow, tea-colored pools no deeper than a hand, their surfaces dimpled by the constant drip. It sheets down the plant-walls in transparent films that never quite become streams. It saturates the air until every breath tastes of chlorophyll and rust. The rust is the only hint of the world outside: a faint metallic note in the moisture, as if the engines that shake the hollow also warm its water, or feed it. There are no pipes here, no casings, no made things. The industrial presence is only pressure and heat and sound, translated perfectly into the living system. The plants drink whatever arrives. They have been drinking it long enough that the chemistry has become theirs.

You can walk the entire hollow in twenty paces. At the far side the living wall thins, not to an exit but to a membrane of interlaced roots through which the same filtered light continues, the same thrum. There is no beyond that feels different. The hollow is complete. It has weathered into this exact shape—the precise curve of the basin, the exact spacing of the pillars, the unvarying interval of the drops—and it will not weather further. Erosion here is a finished verb. The place is in its own state, a pocket of equilibrium tucked into the larger, louder world the way a seed sits inside fruit.

Stand still long enough and the distinction between the plant-body and the air begins to dissolve. The warmth is the same temperature as skin. The moisture condenses on you as readily as on the leaves. The frogs watch with lidless patience. The engines continue. Nothing will be different when you leave. Nothing was different before you arrived. The hollow simply is, wet and green and loudly, perfectly still.
