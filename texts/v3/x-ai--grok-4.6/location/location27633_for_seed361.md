The high country has been worn so long it barely remembers being mountains. What remains is a pale table of old stone, almost level, scored with the ghosts of drainages that have not carried real rivers in an age. Wind comes across it without obstruction. The sky is ordinary and large. Heat sits on the rock the way a hand sits on a sleeping animal.

You would miss the place. From fifty paces it is only a darker smudge, a slight failure of flatness. Closer, the smudge becomes a lip. The lip is no higher than a kneeling person. Inside it the world drops away into a shallow bowl perhaps forty strides across, its floor a single pour of dark, glass-smooth material that is not quite glass. It holds the day’s warmth and gives it back slowly. The air in the bowl is damper than the plateau by a margin you feel first on your wrists and at the roots of your hair.

Moisture does not arrive as weather so much as as a habit. At irregular intervals a film of water beads on the inner curve, condenses out of air that looks empty, and runs in threads toward a low point that is never quite the same low point twice. There is no spring. There is no outlet you can trust. The water appears, bright and brief, and soaks into a seam, or is taken by roots, or evaporates with a faint mineral smell like hot slate after rain. On some afternoons a true shower crosses the tableland and the bowl drinks it with an audible hiss, then goes back to its private sweating.

Plants occupy the cracks where the dark pour meets older stone. They are few. Each one is unnecessarily alive. A shrub with leaves the size of open hands, waxed and heavy, stands alone in a rake of gravel and looks like a lamp someone forgot to extinguish. Ferns, if they are ferns, unspool from a single wet fissure and hang in a green that is almost black at the midrib. Something like ginger, something like thistle, something that flowers in tight white knots and then folds the knots away again within an hour. Between them: emptiness. Pale grit. The dark floor reflecting a warped oval of sky. You can walk a slow circle and touch only two living stems if you choose. The lushness is not a carpet. It is a set of concentrated arguments against the dryness above.

The heat is gentle and constant, a highland warmth that does not quite match the altitude. It comes partly from the sun on the dark material and partly from below. If you put your palm to the floor at dusk you feel a slow pulse, not heartbeat-regular, more like machinery remembering a schedule. The pulse is old. Everything here is old in the settled way of things that have finished becoming what they are. The rim of the bowl is rounded as bone. The surrounding table is planed to a patience that predates maps. Erosion has done its last interesting work and gone home. This is the shape the land intends to keep.

What the bowl was for is not obvious and does not stay still in the mind. Scattered through it, and just outside the lip, are marks that do not belong to weather. A ring of dull metal flush with the glass, interrupted in three places, each interruption filled with a dust that will not blow away. A post no thicker than a finger, standing without a join, its upper end opening into a flower of foil so thin it reads as smoke until you try to pinch it. A shallow trench that refuses to hold shadow the way a trench should. At the eastern inner wall, half-buried, a plate the color of old ice. If you stand over the plate the air thickens, not hotter, not colder, simply more *decided*, as if the space immediately above it has been assigned a job. Insects avoid that air. Seeds that fall into it hang for a count of five before they drop. The engineering is past the point where you can reverse-engineer it from materials you know. It still functions the way a scar still functions: not as intended, but undeniably.

Light is ordinary daylight, slightly thickened by the warm air, slightly greened where it passes the big leaves. No theatrical glow. Shadows are honest. At noon the bowl is a bright coin. At evening the dark floor holds the last of the sky longer than the plateau does, a dim lake of leftover blue.

The fauna is the fact you cannot negotiate with.

They do not pack the ground. The ground stays spare. They pack the *volume*. From midmorning until well after the first stars the air over the bowl is a traffic. Midges in curtains. Beetles with copper elytra that strike the glass and ring it. Moths the size of a child’s sandal, cream and tobacco, blundering through the white flowers and leaving them sticky. Birds arrive in pulses, not flocks so much as decisions: a dozen then a hundred then a thinning, all of them loud in a way that the bowl’s curve turns into architecture. Their calls slap the inner wall and come back slightly late, slightly lower, so that every phrase has a tired twin. Something larger comes at the heat of the day and does not land—wingbeats you feel in the sternum, a shadow with too many angles, gone before the eye finishes assembling it. In the condensation film there are lives that are only visible when the water runs: threads that swim upstream against the beads, tiny bodies that flash once and are gone into the seam.

At night the overwhelming does not cease. It changes register. The bowl becomes a drum for things that live in the worn highlands and treat this dimple as a well, a temple, a larder, a gossip-stone. Frogs that should not be this high, if they are frogs. A mammal that sounds like fabric tearing, over and over, from three directions at once. The insects do not sleep; they change their metal. The air is a fabric being continually rewoven.

Sound is structural. The plateau above is not silent—wind over old stone has a dry hiss—but the bowl takes every noise and finishes it. Footsteps on the glass report like knuckles on a jar. A dropped pebble is a brief, shameful bell. When the condensation starts, it is a whispering crowd. When rain finds the place, the hiss becomes a roar contained, a single-room storm. The animals know this. They use it. Courtship here is an acoustic siege. Predation is announced. Even the plants, when the big leaves knock together in a gust that spills over the lip, contribute a leathery applause.

The place is active without being hurried. Moisture cycles on a private clock. Flowers open and shut as if answering a question you did not hear. The buried pulse under the floor skips, resumes. Birds empty the air and refill it. A thread of water chooses a new path down the glass and a fern adjusts, over hours, toward the change. Heat blooms and recedes in the dark material with the sun, but never quite to match, always a little behind, always a little more. Nothing here is abandoned. It is occupied in the way a tool is occupied by its use.

Rules suggest themselves if you stay.

Do not stand long over the ice-colored plate. The decided air is not hostile, but it is not for lungs that expect ordinary mixtures; after a minute the taste of tin arrives, and after two the edges of vision take on a patient shimmer.

Do not assume the low point of the water is a kindness. Things wait in the seam.

The metal ring is safe to cross and unsafe to sit on. Those who have sat on it report a day of hearing their own blood as a separate animal.

The foil flower at the top of the thin post turns, sometimes, toward sounds that have not happened yet. This is not a metaphor. You can clap and watch it fail to respond, then later, when you are leaving, see it incline to the memory.

The bowl keeps its weather. A wind that flattens the plateau may only ruffle the hanging ferns. A dry week above may still produce the beading, as if the device—whatever remains of the device—were still pulling water out of a sky that has already given what it can.

Strangeness here is moderate and local, the strangeness of a function that outlived its explanation. The plants are almost plants. The glass is almost geology. The animals are almost the animals of warm highlands everywhere, except that they treat the bowl as if it were a season, a god, a door. You have the sense that the original makers, whoever or whatever they were, did not build a garden. They built an instrument. Time and the grinding-down of the mountains have left the instrument playing itself: heat, damp, green, body, noise, in a cup small enough to be overlooked and old enough to have become a kind of weather.

Sit at the rim in late light and the place composes. Sparse dark plants, each one a surplus of life. The open dark floor between them, faintly pulsing. Condensation beginning its first bright threads. The air already thickening with wings. A sound like rain that is not rain yet, like a crowd that is not people, like a machine that has forgotten it is a machine and believes it is a valley. Above, the worn tableland goes on being finished. Below the lip, the hidden country keeps working.
