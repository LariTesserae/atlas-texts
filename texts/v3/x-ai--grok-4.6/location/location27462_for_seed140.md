The hollow sits near what used to be a summit. Ages of rain and freeze have planed the mountain down to a rounded high table, and this last chamber is all that remains of its interior: a single, cathedral-sized cavity whose ceiling has sagged into a low, weeping vault. The rock itself is finished. Every angle has been surrendered; the walls and floor are one continuous, almost featureless basin of ancient limestone so thoroughly dissolved and redeposited that it feels more like cooled marrow than stone. Water stands everywhere just beneath or just above the surface—thin sheets, ankle-deep sloughs, and a few still, black pools whose bottoms cannot be guessed. The air is warm and heavy, a constant exhalation of geothermal breath mixed with the mineral steam of perpetual condensation. Nothing dries.

You know the space more by sound and skin than by any other sense. The darkness is absolute; no seam of sky, no after-image, no bioluminescence. Sound, however, is lavish. Water does not fall so much as announce itself: each drop from the vault lands with a report that the empty volume turns into a short, ringing chord. Hundreds of them, never quite in rhythm, overlay a deeper, slower pulse—the almost subsonic gulp of water moving through unseen throats in the rock. When the fauna stirs the chords thicken. There are many lives here, but they keep to the surfaces and the shallows, leaving the greater part of the chamber a hanging void. Pale, segmented things click in the wet moss in sudden choruses; larger, slower bodies haul themselves through the marsh with wet, deliberate smacks; something winged or gliding occasionally cuts the air with a sound like tearing cloth and is gone. The abundance is real, yet the distances between any two events remain long. The place feels hollow even while it teems.

What grows does so without light and without hurry. Great, layered curtains of a flesh-like fungus descend from the vault in isolated columns, each one a dripping, labyrinthine garden of gills and folds, lush to the point of collapse yet standing meters apart so that the intervening dark is simply unoccupied air. On the basin floor, sparse stands of taller, jointed stalks rise from the peat—neither quite plant nor mushroom—their surfaces slick and warm, their interiors faintly yielding. They too keep their distance. Between them the marsh is only water, silt, and the occasional low cushion of something that might be moss if moss could live on heat and stone-dust alone. Everything is older than it looks; growth and decay have reached an understanding that changes only across seasons measured in decades.

Here and there the finished rock is interrupted. A low, incomplete ring of material that is not limestone sits half-drowned in one of the pools: a ceramic or metal whose surface temperature is a fraction higher than the water, whose edges refuse to dull. Nearby, a single upright pylon, no taller than a person, hums at a frequency just below hearing and seems, if you stand close, to subtract a little of the weight from your bones. These are the only built things—scattered, partial, long since surrendered to the same wet patience that finished the mountain. They do not light anything. They do not explain themselves. They simply persist, the last slow artifacts of an engineering that treated gravity and heat as suggestions.

Time here is a weak gradient. The drips have occupied the same trajectories for centuries; the fungal columns thicken by the width of a fingernail in a human lifetime; the unknown devices cool or warm by amounts too small to notice except by comparison with records no one is keeping. The whole chamber has settled into its final posture. It is not dying. It is simply finished, and the lives that remain have learned to live inside that finishedness—warm, wet, loud in their small ways, and almost unimaginably far from one another in the dark.
