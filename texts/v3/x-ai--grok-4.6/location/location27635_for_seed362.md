The first thing that is not a plant is the light, and even that is arguable.

It arrives as a white that has forgotten heat. Not glare exactly—glare implies a source you can turn from—but a saturation, as if the air itself had been milled into brightness and packed into every interval. Shadows exist, but they are thin as varnish, and they do not pool. They cling to undersides and then give up. You walk for a long time before you understand that the coolness is not weather. The coolness is a product. Heat is being taken somewhere, continuously, by something that does not advertise the taking.

There is almost no water. The tongue learns this before the mind does. The air has a mineral dryness, a taste of crushed feldspar and old paper, and the surfaces—every surface—refuse wetness the way glass refuses ink. What moisture exists is locked. It is bound in polymers, in crystalline hydrates nested inside cell walls that are not quite cell walls, in sealed viscera of machinery that grew rather than was assembled. Nothing drips. Nothing beads. If you cut a stem (and there are stems the width of transit tunnels) what wells is not sap but a cool, slow gel the color of weak tea, already half-set, smelling faintly of ozone and starch. It does not run. It occupies the wound and begins, with industrial patience, to become more stem.

You are in a low country. That is the only honest geography. There are no mountains because mountains were inefficient. There may have been mountains. What remains is a continental sheet of made ground, slightly dished, a basin so wide that its rim is a rumor and its center is not a place so much as a gradient of more. Elevation changes by terraces, and the terraces are leaves, or the leaves are terraces: platforms of living laminate, ribbed with vascular trusses, stacked in shallow overlapping courses that recede until perspective itself becomes a kind of agriculture. You can walk for weeks and climb less than a person climbs a hill. The world prefers the horizontal. Gravity here is a sorting principle, not a drama.

What you walk on is neither soil nor floor. It is substrate in the old industrial sense and the botanical one at once: a pressed, endless pith, fibrous, springy in places, glass-hard in others, stained with decades or centuries of tannin-dark exudate that has polymerized into a kind of lacquer. Roots—if they are roots—do not seek water. They seek structure, current, mineral feedstocks trucked or piped or grown along dedicated cambium-lines. The ground is a chassis. It is also a forest floor that never knew a forest. It has been this way long enough to have a weather of its own settling: abrasion-shine on the high-traffic laminates, a pilling of dead trichomes in the lee of stanchions, hairline checks in the older bark-plating where expansion and contraction have argued for a very long time and arrived at a truce.

The vegetation is not on the world. It is the world, and the world was built.

That sentence has to be lived in, not merely understood. There is no pre-existing rock showing through, no “natural” horizon under the green. Green is even the wrong word, though it is the word the eye reaches for. The mass is chlorophyll and its cousins, yes—acres, provinces, climate-zones of photosynthetic surface—but it is chlorophyll deployed as cladding, as roof, as load-bearing wall, as conveyor, as filter-bank, as the housing of engines. Leaves the size of plazas have been trained, or printed, or budded according to plan, into vaults. Their venation is a grid and also a circulatory map and also, if you stand in the right aisle at the right hour, a set of rails. Petioles thick as pilings go down through decks of older growth. The decks are still alive. Everything is still alive. Death here is a maintenance state, not an ending: browned tissue is shunted, composted in sealed dry-retorts, reconstituted as feedstock, extruded again as meristem, as panel, as the next season’s load-bearing petiole.

It is industrial the way a heartbeat is industrial if you listen long enough. There are factories. They are trees, or the trees are factories, and the distinction has been weathered away the way a maker’s mark weathers off a tool that has been used by too many hands. In one stratum—call it a canopy if you need the comfort of a forest word—you find galleries where palisade cells have been hypertrophied into reaction chambers. Light is piped in along fiber-optic xylem, concentrated, broken into useful wavelengths, spent. The products are not fruit. They are sheet, filament, pigment, structural foam, the next generation of leaf. Somewhere a press cycles: not a metal press, a press of turgor and ratchet-tissue, silent except for a sound so low it is almost a change in the way your knees feel. Somewhere else a line of identical buds opens in sequence, each bud a component, each component already threaded with conductive scar-tissue, already numbered in a script of raised lenticels.

The scale is the sort that invents its own meteorology. There are winds that are only winds of this place: slow lateral drainages of cooled air down the terrace-grades, pollen-storms that are not reproductive so much as logistical—spores of a designed organism that colonizes microfractures and seals them, a maintenance blizzard, pale and glittering, falling through the white light like a dry snow that never melts because it was never ice. There are pressure systems born from the collective transpiration that is not transpiration, the venting of waste gases through stomatal stacks the size of chimneys that do not smoke. The stacks exhale a faint sweetness and a fainter metallic tang. On certain days—though day is a weak concept under this light—the exhalation weathers into a haze that sits in the lower galleries and turns the brightness into a kind of milk. You can lose the middle distance in it. You can find it again as a wall of leaf that was always there.

Sound stays down. The mass eats it. Fibrous bulk, layered and near-touching, is an anechoic chamber the size of a nation. What remains is quiet work: a tick of contracting fiber, a sigh in a duct, the almost-not-there percussion of a bud-scar flexing. Occasionally, far off, something large adjusts—a whole vault resettling its weight as turgor is redistributed—and the adjustment arrives as a single deep syllable you feel in the teeth. Then the silence closes over it, not as absence but as the place’s native substance. People who have stayed too long report that they begin to hear the light. This is probably not true. It is also not quite false. The brightness has a pressure. The pressure has a frequency. The frequency is below language.

Fauna is rare enough to feel like a rumor that grew legs. There are things in the joints. Small, dry, the color of old paper, they move when the light-angle changes and are still when it does not. They may be insects. They may be shed parts that retained a habit of crawling. They do not drink. They graze the dust of sloughed cuticle, the fallen trichomes, the occasional drift of industrial pollen. Larger animals—if any remain from whatever time this place had a before—do not announce themselves. You might find a shed covering, keratinous, ribbed like a seed-case, large enough that a person could sleep in the curl of it. You might find bones that are not bones, calcified supports abandoned when a gallery was decommissioned by growth. The living rare thing, when you see it, tends to be at the edge of scale: a pale walker far down a nave of leaves, human or once-human, moving with the economy of someone who has learned that haste is a kind of noise. They do not travel in groups. Groups would be a different ecology.

The activity is plant-paced and machine-paced at once, which is to say it never stops and it never hurries. Growth is production. Production is growth. Whole provinces are in bud. Whole provinces are in a long senescence that has been designed as a harvest. Seasons here are not solar so much as scheduled: a dimming in one spectral band, a surge in another, a change in the mineral recipe arriving through the chassis, and ten thousand square kilometers of lamina reorient by a few degrees. The reorientation is a weather event. It makes a sound like a library inhaling. Then the new alignment locks, and the light, which was already too much, finds new interiors to fill.

You can live a long time in the first interpretation—that this is a factory that learned to photosynthesize, or a forest that was staffed and tooled until it became infrastructure. The place will let you. It has the patience of something established. The forms are settled. Edges are worn in the specific way of objects that have been touched by purpose for longer than a lifetime. Bark-cladding is polished along the human-height band. Handholds have been grown, then worn, then grown again around the wear, so that the grip is a collaboration between plan and use. There are paths that are simply the places the laminate is tired of being walked on, compressed to a darker sheen, slightly concave. The world is in-balance with itself the way a running engine is in-balance: not still, not decaying, just continuing.

Then the weirdness, which has been standing a little off to the side, steps into the aisle with you.

It starts as a problem of counting. A colonnade of petioles: you count fourteen. You walk the length. You count nine, or thirty, or the same fourteen in a different order, each pillar now bearing a different load of galleries, as if the building had been folded and the fold did not admit it. The light does not help. It is too even. Depth cues starve. A leaf-vault recedes and then is suddenly the floor you are standing on, and the floor you were standing on is overhead, still supporting your weight in a direction that has become optional. You feel no lurch. The body accepts the new down with a botanical calm, as if gravity were just another feedstock being rerouted.

Worse—or more itself—is the failure of made versus grown. You put a hand on a wall and feel the thrum of a process that is equally mitosis and assembly-line. You watch a seam. Is it a weld? A graft? A scar? The seam thinks about the question and decides to be a row of stomata, then a row of rivets, then a script of lenticels that, if you could read it, might be a work-order or a genome or the same document. Time stutters in the tissues. A bud opens through a sequence that includes, as one of its ordinary stages, already having been open. You remember walking through a chamber you have not reached. The chamber, when you reach it, remembers you back, and the memory is photosynthesized, laid down as sugar-analogue in a ring of wood that did not exist this morning.

There are places where the construction was too complete, where the living mass filled every interval, and density became a kind of logic error. In those districts the leaves interpenetrate without colliding, occupying the same volume the way two frequencies occupy the same air. You can put your arm into a wall and the wall is glad to have the company. You can walk through a press in operation and feel only a polite pressure, a suggestion of force, as if the machine were manufacturing the idea of compression and had not yet decided whether to include you. People who stay in the interpenetrated zones too long come out slightly mis-registered, their edges soft, their voices arriving a half-step before their mouths. They do not seem to mind. The place does not mind. Mindedness may not be the right category.

The rules, insofar as they can be stated without immediately becoming foliage, are something like this:

The world is a single continuously revised artifact. There is no outside to the artifact except more artifact, dimmer, older, still working. Water is a historical material, a solvent the design outgrew; its traces are archival, locked, not for use. Light is the currency and the weather and the load. Growth is labor. Labor is growth. What is built is alive; what is alive is on shift. Silence is structural, a load-bearing absence. Animals are optional and know it. Coolness is extracted and sent down, always down, into a chassis that may be the original planet or may be the first layer of planting, compressed now to something that thinks like bedrock and dreams like a root. Causality is vascular: it branches, it anastomoses, it can be girdled. If you need a thing to have happened already, there are districts that will grow you the history. If you need a thing not to have happened, there are other districts, slightly cooler, where the light is a different white and the records have been composted.

At the far, unhelpful edge of the basin—far enough that the word *edge* is a kindness—the laminate thins. Not to soil. There is no soil. It thins to a brightness with less furniture in it, a glare-plain of pale, dry, living film stretched over the chassis like a drumhead. Here the factories are recumbent, half-submerged in their own leaf, still cycling, still exhaling that faint starch-and-metal breath. The wind, such as it is, writes nothing. The film ticks as it expands. Occasionally a rare walker crosses, small as a seed against the white, and the film records the pressure for a while in a blush of darker pigment, then photosynthesizes the blush away.

Above—if above is still a direction—the light does not come from a sun you could point to. It comes from the work. It comes from the work’s idea of a sun. It comes from a sky that may be another deck, another province of lamina so distant it has forgotten it is green and remembers only that it is supposed to shine.

You stand in the cool white. You listen to the library inhale. You do not swallow, because there is nothing to swallow, and the gel in the cut stems is already closing, already becoming more of the only world there is.
