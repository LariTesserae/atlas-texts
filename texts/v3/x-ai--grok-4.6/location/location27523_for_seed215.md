The high country is still deciding its own shape. You feel that first in the ground: it is not old stone worn into patience, but something recently broken and lifted, its faces still angular, its bedding planes still raw. Ridges arrive overnight. A slope that was merely steep yesterday now shears into a new escarpment, pale and unweathered, weeping a film of melt and condensate that never quite freezes even though the air stays well below what should allow it. The water is always near. It beads on every surface, gathers in embryonic hollows that have not yet learned how to drain, hangs as a low, persistent mist that the wind never quite scours away. After rain—which comes often, sudden and cold, more sleet than downpour—the whole plateau glistens as if the rock itself were sweating.

Nothing here has had time to grow tall. Vegetation is a thin, stubborn afterthought: black-green cushions of moss in the wetter cracks, a few wiry sedges where meltwater lingers longest, pale lichens already colonizing the newest faces. They do not cover. They punctuate. Between them the stone remains itself, vast and mostly empty. You can walk for hours and meet only another low swell of unformed highland, another shallow basin filling with tea-colored water, another line of fresh fracture.

Yet the emptiness is crowded with life. Animals move through it in numbers that seem out of proportion to the forage. Herds of long-shanked, pale browsers—something between goat and antelope, coats the color of wet limestone— graze the moss in loose, shifting constellations that can number in the thousands. They appear and vanish into the mist as if the land itself were breathing them. Smaller things crowd the wet: amphibians the size of a fist that cling to weeping rock faces and sing only at the edge of hearing; flocks of gray, short-winged birds that ride the thermals rising from still-warm faults and never seem to land; insect swarms that hatch in the temporary pools and darken the air for an afternoon before the cold takes them. Everything is in motion, but the motion is quiet. Hooves on wet stone make almost no sound. Wings are muffled. Even the wind, when it comes, is a low continuous pressure rather than a voice.

The settlements do not cluster. They are placed at the same sparse density as everything else: a tower or a low complex every several kilometers, sometimes farther, linked by pale linear traces that might be roads or might be something else. The structures themselves look grown as much as built—smooth, slightly iridescent surfaces that follow the new contours rather than fighting them, as if the city had been poured into the same molds the geology is still using. Foundations adjust. When a ridge lifts, the buildings lift with it, or step aside, or briefly become translucent while the rock finishes deciding. No one here talks about the mechanism. The technology is simply the weather of the place: it works, it is everywhere, and it does not announce itself. At night the towers give off a steady, sourceless light no brighter than late dusk. During the day they are almost the same color as the mist.

Between the nodes the animals pass freely. Some of the larger browsers have learned to use the linear traces as easy going; the birds nest in the lee of the taller structures. The city does not exclude them. It is too thinly spread to exclude anything. People move along the same routes, often on foot, sometimes in silent capsules that ride a few centimeters above the stone and leave no track. Conversations, when they happen, are brief. Most of the sound is still the land’s: the faint tick of cooling rock, the drip from a thousand weeping faces, the distant, almost subsonic shift as another section of plateau continues its slow, unfinished rise.

Weather here has room to become its own system. A front can spend a day crossing from one horizon to the other. Mist thickens into low cloud, thins again, then returns as fine rain that never quite soaks in because the drainage is still being invented. Temperature stays cold enough that breath hangs and shadows hold rime, yet the constant film of water keeps the surface from locking into ice. Everything stays just this side of frozen, just this side of finished. The geology is still under construction; the city is still learning the new map; the animals are still finding the newest moss. Nothing has settled. The place is large enough that its unfinishedness has weather of its own, quiet and continuous, like a continent being born in a climate that has not yet decided whether to be tundra or marsh or something that has no name yet.

You can stand on a newly lifted ridge at what would be noon anywhere else and watch the light do ordinary things: catch on wet stone, flatten in the mist, pick out the distant pale geometry of the next settlement half a day’s walk away. The light is normal. The cold is ordinary cold. The strangeness is only the scale and the newness, the sense that the ground under you is still being written and that the city, the animals, the thin green, and the water are all writing themselves at the same time, quietly, with a great deal of empty room between the sentences.
