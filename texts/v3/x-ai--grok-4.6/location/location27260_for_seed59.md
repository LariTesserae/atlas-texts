The notch does not announce itself from the ridge. You come over wet stone and the world simply thickens.

A hanging basin, no larger than a cathedral close or a generous village green, opens under the cloud. It is a pocket in high country—old rock, long settled into itself, shoulders rounded by centuries of water rather than recent violence. The floor sits perhaps a few hundred paces across, steep walls rising on three sides into the same green mass that fills the air. Elevation keeps the heat off. Breath shows if you stand still. The temperature is the temperature of wet wool and cold stream-water on the wrists.

Water is not a feature here. It is the medium. It comes down as mist that never quite decides to be rain, then as rain that never quite stops being mist. It weeps from every face of stone. It stands in shallow, tea-colored sheets between root-knees. It threads the air so densely that distant trunks look as if they are behind glass. Gutters are unnecessary; everything is a gutter. Where people have cut steps, the steps are little waterfalls. Where they have laid walkways of split timber, the timber is permanently dark and slick, and the underside drips in a second, slower weather. After a heavier pulse—there is always a heavier pulse coming—the whole basin sounds like a single throat clearing.

The plants are the ground, the walls, the roof, and most of what would elsewhere be called furniture.

Nothing here is merely growing *on* something. The living mass is the substrate. Tree-ferns lock their skirts into one another until you cannot tell which crown belongs to which bole. Broadleaf evergreens—cool-climate things with waxed, dripping leaves—have been trained, pollarded, bent, and allowed to grow through one another for generations, so that the settlement is a single knitted organism with rooms in it. Moss is not an accent. Moss is plaster, insulation, mattress, and, in the thicker pads, a kind of slow furniture that takes the shape of whoever sat last and then forgets. Epiphytes occupy every fork. When you put a hand on a “wall,” your palm comes away green and cold and slightly springy. There is almost no bare mineral surface except where running water has kept the rock scoured, and even those dark wet faces are stitched with liverwort.

People live inside this. The built and the grown are not separate projects.

The hamlet is dense the way a wasp nest is dense: many lives per volume, near-touching, layered. Platforms are lashed into the crooks of living limbs and then the limbs are left to thicken around the lashings until the joints are botanical. Hearths sit in bowls of packed clay and river stone, hooded by living screens that have been woven wet and have taken the hood-shape as their habit. Ladders are not always ladders; some are aerial roots encouraged downward and notched with an adze. There are perhaps two or three dozen household hollows in the basin, plus common spaces—drying racks under the densest canopy, a long split-log table that is always wet at one end, a meeting platform around a trunk so old the bark has the texture of cooled lava. Paths do not so much connect these as thread them. You are rarely more than an arm’s length from someone else’s wall, someone else’s hanging storage, someone else’s dripping eave. Privacy is a curtain of leaves and a habit of not looking.

The work is hand work. Wedges, stone blades still in use beside a few simple metal ones—soft, often-resharpened things, more copper-glow than steel. Fiber everywhere: twisted bark, split vine, hair-fine root used as thread. They do not fight the wet so much as schedule around it. Wood is worked green. Bindings are retied as they slacken. A house here is not finished; a house is a conversation with something that keeps growing through the conversation. Children learn early which stems may be cut and which are load-bearing in the living sense. Elders walk the lanes after every hard rain and tap the joinery the way other people tap melon rinds.

All of that would be quiet if the animals allowed it. They do not.

Fauna is not a population here. It is weather with legs and wings and throats. The air is particulate with midges and small flies in the cooler hours, then with larger, blundering beetles when the light lifts. Frogs occupy every cistern, every folded leaf, every gap between plank and moss; their gelatin egg-masses hang like extra fruit. You cannot set a gourd down and expect to find it unoccupied. Birds work the vertical city in shifts—thin mountain species with loud, simple voices, mixed with thicker, wet-forest birds that move like thrown stones through the understory. Something mammalian and dark, about the size of a cat, uses the same aerial roots the people do, and does not always yield. At dusk the bats come out of the deepest hollows in numbers that make the mist look briefly solid.

The sound is continuous and stacked. Water first: drip, run, sheet, the hollow knock of a full spout hitting a wooden trough. Then the frogs, which do not so much chorus as occupy every available pitch. Then birds. Then people—voices carry strangely in the wet; a call from the far wall arrives both muffled and too close. Then the work: adze, the wet slap of beaten fiber, the creak of a living beam taking a new load. When a rain cell stands on the ridge and then slides into the notch, all of this is overwritten by a roar that is not quite thunder and not quite waterfall. Conversation becomes gesture. Children scream with pleasure and are not heard. The animals, if anything, get louder, as if competing with the weather rather than sheltering from it.

Nothing stays still long enough to compose itself.

This is the turbulence: not catastrophe, not ruin, but a kind of permanent bazaar of matter. A limb that was a roof-edge last month has put out a spray of new growth and now drips into a hearth, so someone is up there with a stone knife, redirecting. A path that was clear at dawn is a braid of armying insects by midmorning, and the people simply step around or through, cursing, laughing, slapping calves. Market—if you can call the daily spill of baskets onto the central platform a market—never quite starts or stops. Fish (small, cold, from the sheet-water and the feeder rills) are traded wet from hand to hand. Fungus, leaf-protein, bitter high-country tubers, packets of dyed fiber, a few hammered ornaments. Dogs if there are dogs; more likely the half-tame dark mammals, arguing. Someone is always re-lashing. Someone is always chasing a bird out of the grain. The mist thickens, thins, thickens. Light is ordinary daylight strained through one too many layers of leaf—green-grey, readable, never golden for long. When a shaft does find the floor it finds steam coming off backs and the sudden, shocking color of something flowering that you had taken for more moss.

The rock underneath all this is patient. You feel it in the few places the roots have not yet sealed: rounded, dark, lichen-mapped, the grain of a place that has been a basin for a very long time. The chaos is a film on an old face. People have been here long enough that the main ways are worn into the living wood like saddle-polish. Not long enough, or not willing, to replace the living with the cut and stacked. The rule of the world, insofar as there is one, is maintenance as worship, or worship as maintenance: keep the water moving, keep the bindings wet-tight, leave the load-bearing stems, do not try to dry anything that has not agreed to be dry, and accept that the night will rearrange the inventory of frogs.

Stand in the middle and turn slowly. There is no horizon. There is only layered nearness—leaf, platform, face, water, wing—and the cool on your teeth, and the sense that if you stayed a week you would be claimed in small ways: a moss print on your sleeping side, a familiar frog in your cup, a path that has already grown a little shut behind you and been cut open again by someone who did not need to be asked.

The cloud lowers another body-length. Rain finds a new pitch. The basin, which has always been this basin, becomes slightly more itself.
