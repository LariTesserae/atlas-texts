The plateau does not announce its height the way mountains do. There is no single summit, no cone against the sky. You are already on it when the air thins and the light hardens, and the ground underfoot becomes a packed geology of blades and basins that have been weathering into one another for longer than the idea of a visitor. The elevation is extreme enough that the sky, even at what ought to be noon, has a dark indigo rind around a white, almost mineral glare. The glare does not come only from above. Wet stone throws it back. Every surface is a poor mirror.

Water is everywhere and almost never a river. It beads. It films. It hangs in the air as a fine, cool condensate that never quite decides to be fog or rain. The rock weeps. Not metaphorically: the mineral itself exudes, as if the plateau were a lung turned inside out and left in cold air. Droplets fatten along hairline joints, run a finger’s width, and vanish into channels so old they look designed. The channels are not designed. They are the settled argument between gravity and a stone that does not quite agree about down. Some runlets climb a short way before remembering themselves. You learn not to stare at those.

There are no plants. There has not been, for a duration the erosion suggests is geological rather than merely historical. No lichen even, which is the first thing you notice after the brightness, because the mind keeps looking for a stain of green and finds only value-changes in the rock: iron blush, sulfur ghost, a blue-black glaze like the inside of a mussel shell, polished by water and by time and by nothing alive. What grows here is mineral habit. Columns stand so close they nearly touch, hexagonal in the large and then fracturing into smaller prisms, a forest of stone that you walk through sideways, shoulder to wet shoulder with the world. Between them, hanging sheets of sinter and silica, thin as parchment, ringing when the wind finds them. The density is not clutter. It is saturation. Every volume has something in it: a fin, a pendant, a bowl of standing water no wider than a palm, a second fin behind the first so that depth becomes a stack of edges.

The scale is open anyway. You can see a long way because the formations repeat their grammar for valley after valley, a regional habit of upright stone and shallow, brilliantly lit water. Distances lie. A notch that looks an hour off is under your hand in twenty steps; a cairn you could spit on recedes when you walk toward it and then is suddenly beside you, as if the ground had folded a pleat and then smoothed it. This is not a trick of thin air. The air is thin, yes, and cold enough that your breath writes itself and hangs, unwilling to disperse, but the unreliability is in the metric. The plateau has been worn so long that wear has gone through the cloth of place. Reality here is established and also threadbare.

Sound is loud in a way that does not require a single source. Wind slots between the columns and becomes a chord, not a howl—many pitches at once, an organ left with all the stops out. Water ticks and then, without warning, slaps, as if a hidden cistern had overflowed. Thermal cracks go off like dropped slates. Under that, a low, continuous pressure, the kind of noise a crowd makes in a stone hall, or a millrace, or blood in the ears after climbing. There are no animals to make it. There is no one. The sound is the place working. Sometimes a syllable surfaces in it, a name or a warning in no language you own, and then the syllable is only drip and wind again. You do not answer.

Light is the other weather. It is too much. It stands in the water-films as if light had weight and could puddle. In the brighter hours the whole highland seems overexposed, edges eaten, the dark indigo of the high sky the only mercy. Shadows fall in more than one direction, or fail to fall, so that a column’s base is brighter than its crown. At the rims of basins the wet stone throws secondary suns, small and savage, that crawl when the real sun does not. Night, when it comes, is not a relief so much as a change of instrument: the rock holds a residual shine, a slow phosphorescence in the glazes, and the condensate catches it, so you walk in a dim aquarium of stone. The temperature stays cool. Nothing freezes hard. The water remains a skin, a seep, a decision the air keeps making.

The built things are few and do not argue with any of this. They are the marks of hands that came with almost nothing and left almost nothing. Cupules pecked into weeping faces, each one a small dark mouth collecting a perfect lens of water. Stacked slabs in the lee of larger fins, not quite rooms, more the idea of a pause—three walls and a slab roof, the joints packed with grit, the interiors colder than outside and loud with drip. Grooves cut to lead water toward a basin, or away; some of the grooves now run backward, or fork into channels the cutters did not make. Here and there a tool: a pecked maul of the same stone as the plateau, a flake with an edge still cruel, a length of something like beaten copper gone green and then almost black, as if metal were only a rumor. Ochre in the deeper overhangs, not figures, only hand-sized smears and tallies that the seep has feathered into the rock so that counting and mineral are the same event.

No one lives in the shelters. Nothing nests. The ecology, if the word can be used, is process without organisms. Water moves. Stone sheds and is recemented. Light arrives, pools, departs, leaves a fatigue in the eye. Heat (what little there is) migrates through the columns and they tick and lean a hair, then lean back; the leans have been going on long enough that the whole field of pillars has a slight, shared list, like wheat in a wind that ended ages ago and never quite stopped existing. Mist rises from the basins in threads that do not braid with one another. Occasionally a thread stands upright, a pale vertical, and remains after the conditions for mist have passed. You can put a hand through it. The hand comes out colder, and for a few minutes the skin looks like wet stone, same glaze, same slow drip. It passes. You do not test it twice.

The weirdness is not an event. It is the climate of the place, as established as the erosion. Perspective is a courtesy the highland sometimes extends. Causality too. A stone you dislodge upslope may take a long time to fall, or may already be at the bottom when you hear it start. Pools show the sky of a different hour. In one basin, frequently, the reflection includes a figure that is not you and not present, standing as you stand, doing the small human work of looking. When you move, it does not always move. When you leave, you hear, very clearly, the drip of water into a cupule you are no longer near, and then your own breathing from a shelter you have not entered. The plateau is dense with presences that do not qualify as life. They are more like grammatical errors that the world has decided to keep.

Weather cycles through without drama and without rest. A brighter glare, a dimmer. A thickening of condensate until walking is like shouldering through cold cloth, then a thinning until every edge is knife-clean and the organ-wind goes sharp. Pulses of seep, when every column weeps at once and the channels fill and the whole highland becomes briefly, impossibly loud—a single instrument. Then the water finds its old routes again, the established ones, the ones worn into balance, and the noise steps down to its usual many-voiced working. Nothing here is new. Everything here is still happening.

If there is a rule, it is only this: the place has finished becoming what it is, and what it is includes the frayed place where becoming shows through. You can cross a valley of almost-touching stone in an afternoon, or in a duration that does not map to afternoon. You can drink from the cupules; the water is clean and mineral and cold and tastes faintly of lightning and of something like old blood, though there is no blood in it. You can sit in a three-walled pause and listen to the plateau think in wind and drip and the low crowd-sound of empty distance. You should not sleep if you can help it. Dreams here have the same metric problem as walking, and they weep, and they do not always stay on their side of the eyes.

Far off—or near, the fold will decide—another fin-field stands in the same white glare, same dark rind of sky, same wet grammar. Between here and there the ground is a pavement of broken prisms, treacherous, ringing underfoot, each fragment a small bright wound. No trail worth the name. Only the faint, stubborn marks of earlier crossings: a line of cairns that are not quite in a line, a scrape where someone dragged a slab, a single shoe-last of packed grit in a shelter floor, the print already filling with condensate, already forgetting the foot. The highland does not keep you. It does not expel you. It continues, densely, loudly, coolly, in extreme light, at the altitude where the world is thin enough to hear itself, and wet enough to shine.
