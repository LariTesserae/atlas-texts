The highland is still arriving.

It has not finished deciding its own slopes. Ridges lift in patient increments, a knuckle of new stone showing through the older skin every few decades, and the city is built on that indecision the way other cities are built on river bends. You feel the unfinishedness in the way water does not yet know its channels. It finds them provisionally, weeps them into being, abandons them, tries again. Every surface is a rehearsal for a drainage that has not been agreed upon.

Above, there is no sky worth the name. A lid of unlit vapor sits so close and so heavy that the place exists in a permanent undercroft, a regional darkness that is not night so much as occlusion. What light there is comes from the city itself, and even that is stingy, more a suggestion than an illumination: narrow bands of something that is not quite visible spectrum, more a pressure against the eye than a color, leaking from seams in the fabric of the walls. You walk by knowing, by habit, by the wet heat of other bodies. The dark is wet. The wet is cold. The cold does not bite so much as occupy, a tenant that arrived early and will outlast the lease.

Water is the primary architecture. It does not fall as rain; it condenses out of the lid and out of the breath of the city and out of the stone, which is young enough to still be exhaling. Faces of buildings run continuously. Stair-cuts become rills. Every lintel has a beard of ice so thin it flexes when the air shifts, and under the ice a slower water moves, never quite freezing, never quite free, a film with intentions. In the lower streets the films thicken into sheets, the sheets into ankle-deep lanes that do not flow so much as stand and shiver. Marsh-logic without marsh-plants: black mineral mud, slick plates of precipitated salts, the smell of wet iron and something like old electricity. Pools occupy basements that were never meant to be basements, only the ground rose around them, or the water table climbed to greet the new elevation, and now the lowest floors are tanks in all but name, their contents slowly circulating through the city’s own plumbing as if the distinction between cistern and street had been retired as a provincial idea.

There are no plants. There have never been plants here, or if there were they belong to a geology so previous that even their absence has no monument. Nothing green, nothing photosynthetic, nothing that waits for a sun that does not visit. In the weeping rock, occasionally, a fern-shape of mineral dendrite, white or iron-red, branching with a patience that mocks biology. People sometimes touch these as if they were lucky. They are not alive. What lives instead is animal, and it is everywhere, and it is the city’s other infrastructure.

They occupy the volume the way water occupies a sponge. Not a population so much as a saturation. Pale things the length of a forearm cling to every vertical, overlapping like shingles, their mouths working the mineral films for whatever chemosynthetic smear the darkness permits. Above them, and through them, and in the spaces their bodies do not quite fill, a second nation of flyers: soft-winged, ear-faced, navigating by a continuous shouting that is language and sonar and territorial claim braided into one unending braid. They do not roost so much as pause. The pause lasts a breath. Then the air is full of them again. In the standing water, things with too many joints move the way thoughts move in a fever, and larger shapes — shouldered, blind, patient — use the flooded understreets as roads, their passage felt first as a change in the pitch of the dripping, then as a displacement of cold against the shins. There are more kinds than there are names, and more individuals than the kinds would seem to allow. They eat the films. They eat each other. They eat the city’s shed heat, which the beyond-modern fabric of the place leaks in ways no thermal camera from an older physics would correctly parse: warmth appearing a half-step to the left of the pipe that should contain it, or a moment after the valve is shut, as if heat here were a guest that arrived using the wrong door.

The city is dense enough that “street” is a courtesy. Structures shoulder one another, share walls, share foundations that are still negotiating with a rising land. Walkways stack. Habitations socket into one another like a clenched fist of rooms. You can cross a district without seeing sky (there is no sky) and without walking more than a few body-lengths of true exterior, only the dripping in-between of two buildings that have not yet admitted they are one. And yet the whole of it opens: this is not a single pile but a basin-full, a town gone regional, districts repeating and varying across a highland the size of a small country’s vale, each neighborhood a slightly different answer to the same wet dark. From any high gantry — and the gantries are many, because the ground cannot be trusted to stay at last year’s height — you see not an edge but a continuation, lights like bruises receding into the lid, the rumor of more city where the vapor thickens.

What the city is made of is not fully available to older engineering. The load-bearing members look like stone until you notice they do not stain, do not spall, and occasionally forget to occupy only one location, a faint double-image at the corner of the stair as if the step were being remembered by the air. Water is taken into the walls and does not come out as water. Something is done to it that current textbooks would call a category error: the wet is sorted, the mineral burden precipitated into new rooms that were not on last decade’s survey, the leftover cold sunk into sinks that do not empty into any river you can find. The city grows the way a reef grows, except the polyps are devices, and the devices do not quite obey conservation in the ways a visiting physicist would bet their career on. People speak of the Growers the way other cities speak of masons, except no one has seen a Grower work, only the result, which is always slightly more city than there was, fitted into a geology that is itself slightly more geology than there was. The two accretions — land, town — proceed at similar speeds, a duet of slowness, and the inhabitants have learned not to finish sentences about “permanent address.”

Sound is the medium you actually live in. The flyers’ shouting never ebbs; it is a weather. Under it, the water’s thousand thin percussions, a continuous unspooling of drops on metal, on ice, on hide, on the drum-skins the larger fauna wear across their backs as if they had been designed as instruments. Under that, a bass that is the city thinking: a hum with no obvious source, felt in the teeth, shifting so gradually that a childhood and an old age might disagree about its note and both be right. There are no sudden noises because there are no sudden events. A collapse, when it comes, has been announcing itself for years in the changing drip-pattern of a particular court. A migration of the jointed water-things takes a season to commit and three seasons to complete, a rustling flood that rearranges which streets are passable, which are merely another kind of river. The loudness does not spike. It obtains. It has always obtained. Newcomers shout for a week and then stop, not because they have gone hoarse but because they have understood that speech is a tributary, not a rival.

Time here is a thick fluid. The lid does not brighten. The cold does not relent. The fauna do not thin. What changes does so across spans that make calendars feel nervous: a new ridge shouldering up under the eastern districts, lifting a whole neighborhood a man’s height over the course of a generation, so that doors become windows and windows become something you step down from, into the wet. The Growers adjust. The animals adjust faster, which is to say still slowly, abandoning a court over years, packing a new one until the walls themselves seem furred. Children learn the city as a set of tendencies rather than a map. Elders remember when the central sluice was a staircase, and are not believed, and are correct.

Rules, such as they are: do not trust a dry patch; dryness is a delay, not a condition. Do not strike the pale shingling creatures; there are too many behind the ones you see, and they share an alarm that the flyers will take up, and the flyers’ alarm is a sound you will not wish to have caused. Do not try to see. Seeing is a habit from places with suns. Here you listen for the shape of a room, feel for the temperature-that-is-not-where-it-should-be, taste the air for the iron that means a new seep is opening. Do not ask the walls to stay. They are in conversation with a highland that is still being born, and with a technology that treats matter as a suggestion. If you live here long enough you will dream in drip-rhythms, and you will wake unable to tell whether the shouting is outside or is the inside of your own head agreeing, at last, to be part of the saturation.

At the basin’s far lip, where the unfinished ridges are rawest, the city thins into scaffolds and listening posts, and the fauna thicken to a soup, and the water comes off the new stone so generously that people wear their breathing as a visible plume, a personal weather. Beyond that there is only more lid, more lift, more of the same dark wet mineral becoming, slowly, a place that might someday have a different name than the one it has now, which is not a name so much as a description said with the mouth half closed against the cold: the dripping, the packed, the unlit, the loud, the still-arriving.
