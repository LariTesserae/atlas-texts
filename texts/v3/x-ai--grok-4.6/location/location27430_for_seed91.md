The first thing is not the cold and not the green. It is the sound, which occupies the basin the way a sea occupies a sea-floor: total, pressure-bearing, without a source you can point to once you are inside it. It is a single industrial note held for years — compressors, a dewatering gallery still running in the dark under the north wall, a mill that never fully shut, all of it folded by the bowl of rock into one continuous roar. You feel it in the hinges of your jaw. Conversation is a mime. Birds, on the rare days they cross the rim, pass over without landing.

You come down by the old haul road, a switchback cut so recently the benches still have their quarry angles, unsoftened, pale where the last machines peeled the stone. The road itself has already been claimed. What you walk on is not gravel. It is a living thickness — mosses, liverworts, crowberry, a pelt of dwarf willow and cassiope packed so densely that your boots leave temporary wells which slowly close. Under the first few centimeters the mass is frozen. Deeper it is merely cold, and damp in the way a cloth is damp when it has been wrung and left in a cellar. There is no standing water to speak of. Moisture lives in the plant body: melt that never quite becomes a rivulet, condensation beading on the undersides of leaves at the few hours of dim day, a sweat of the living carpet whenever the air in the pit is warmer than the stone.

The pit is a town wide and a town long, maybe more. From a mid-bench you can see the opposite wall as a stacked geometry of pale cuts receding into the dim, each terrace wearing the same continuous hide of vegetation, as if the quarry had been lined with one organism that learned the shape of industry and kept it. The floor is not flat. It has the long, patient swells of spoil that was never hauled out, now indistinguishable from ground except that the plant-mass is thicker there, a peat in the making, still alive at the surface. Between the swells there is a great deal of empty air. The vegetation is total in the horizontal — nothing of the original floor remains visible — and sparse in every other sense. What rises from the carpet does so far apart: a solitary thicket of birch no higher than a person, black-twigged, glazed; a hummock the size of a cottage where the moss has built itself into a dome; the rusted upright of a conveyor, furred to the waist in the same green-black pelt.

Light arrives as a thinning, not a presence. The sky is a pale lid, often the color of dirty ice, and the walls take most of it. At the bottom the day is a long dusk. You see your breath as a continuous soft animal beside your face. Frost works the edges of every leaf that faces the open. In the hollows of the plant-mass, under the first skin, it is only damp and dark, a stored cold, smelling of iron and chlorophyll and the faint sweetness of things that rot too slowly to finish.

The remaining works are few and far between, which is why you hear them as one thing. Halfway across the floor, a pump-house still throws a white plume from a stack no taller than the birch. The plume does not rise far; it flattens, ices, and sifts down as a glitter that the moss drinks. Near the eastern wall a processing shed keeps a single bay lit, a sodium smear you can see from a kilometer off, and the note of its motors is the bone of the larger sound. A line of sleepers and rail, half-swallowed, runs toward it and gives up. There are no fences that matter. The plant-mass has taken the posts into itself and kept growing. Here and there a concrete pad shows a corner, or a length of pipe lifts from the carpet like a root of the wrong material, sweating. People came and did this and left most of it, and what they did not leave they left running, because water still wants the pit and the pit is not allowed to have it. The pumps have the patience of weather. They have not changed their work in a long time.

Change here is a gradient, not an event. The newest benches, high on the south side, still show raw stone in stripes between the first cushions of moss; give them a decade and the stripes will close. The living floor thickens by a finger’s width in a good year, less in a bad one. Ice lenses form and melt in the dark of the pelt and the surface rises and settles so slowly you would need marks and a lifetime to be sure. The sound does not vary except when the wind lies down in the bowl and the roar becomes, impossibly, more itself. Once in a winter a slab calved from an upper cut and the vegetation rode it to the floor and kept growing at the new angle. The scar on the wall is still pale.

Animals are rumors with footprints. A fox’s line, hours old, pressed into the moss and already lifting out. Ptarmigan sign near a thicket, white droppings, no bird. In summer — if the dim clarifies enough to be called that — midges hatch from the wettest pockets and last a day. You do not come here for life that moves. The life that matters is the one you stand on, which is also the walls, which is also, in time, the machines: a factory floor that learned to photosynthesize and then to wait.

Stand still long enough and the cold enters by the usual routes and then by stranger ones — through the soles, because the plant-mass conducts the stone’s chill as faithfully as any floor; through the sound, which is a kind of weather of its own. Your thoughts arrive slowly, as if they too had to cross the open. The place does not feel unfinished so much as paused in an early sentence: the cut still sharp, the green already entire, the engines refusing the idea of aftermath. It will look like this for a long time. The moss does not hurry. The pumps do not know how.
