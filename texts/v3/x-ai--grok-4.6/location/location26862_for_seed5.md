The hollow is easy to miss. The land around it is ordinary low country—flat enough that water would stand if water ever stayed—and then the ground simply forgets to continue at the same height. A shallow collapse, no deeper than a two-storey house, ringed by a lip of packed glacial till that has not moved in anyone’s memory. From twenty paces you would walk past it. From the lip you look down into a brightness that does not belong to the latitude.

Light pools there as if the air itself were a lens. It is not warm light. It has the colour and the insistence of noon on new snow, except there is no snow, only a continuous fine hoar that never quite becomes frost and never quite melts. Every surface sweats a skin of water that immediately stills. The damp is everywhere and nowhere: condensation on stone, a film on leaf, dark seams in the packed earth of the lanes. There are no streams. There are only the seams, and a few stone basins that have been full to the same exact line since the last person who cared about such things stopped measuring.

What occupies the hollow is a town. Not a village that grew careless and dense, but a town that was always this size and always this complete: streets that meet at proper angles, a central space that is almost a square, drainage channels cut with the patience of people who expected rain that never quite arrived. The buildings are low, shoulder-to-shoulder, roofed in turf and split stone and the grey-green thatch of a sedge that grows only here. They are primitive in the strict sense—no iron, no glass that isn’t a river pebble held to the light, walls of fitted fieldstone and rammed earth, lintels of bog-oak that were already ancient when they were lifted into place. Everything has been handled. Every edge is rounded by touch and weather into the same soft authority. The town has finished becoming itself. Erosion and construction reached an agreement a long time ago and have not spoken since.

There is no room left. Vegetation occupies every interval the builders left, and then occupies the builders’ work as well. Mosses of a dozen textures—some like wet velvet, some like the pile of an old carpet that has learned to photosynthesise—cover the north faces, the south faces, the insides of unused doorways. Ferns the colour of tarnished copper unfurl in the same permanent half-curl from every crack. A low, glossy shrub with leaves like polished horn fills the alleys so completely that the alleys have become tunnels of leaf. Where a roof has sagged, the sag is packed with a cushion of living matter that holds the sag exactly where it is. The plants are lush the way a closed fist is lush: no waste space, no gesture toward openness. They do not climb so much as replace. The town and the green have interleaved until neither could be removed without the other collapsing, and neither is collapsing.

It is cold enough that breath would show, if anyone were breathing hard. No one is. The cold is a settled fact, like the angle of the light. It does not bite; it simply remains. The plants do not mind. Whatever they are, they have been selected by this single unchanging temperature for longer than the stone has been cut. Their chemistry is a slow, bright metabolism that does not require thaw.

Animals are everywhere and make no sound. Pale voles thread the moss without rustling it. Something like a hare, smaller, with winter-white fur that never darkens, sits in the same doorway at the same hour—though hour is the wrong word; the light does not travel. Birds the size of fists, slate-coloured, occupy the thatch. Their throats move. Nothing comes out. Insects exist as a continuous minute glitter in the condensation films, walking on water that does not tremble. A few larger shapes—deer reduced to the scale of goats, antlered in velvet that never sheds—stand in the square as if posed. They have always been standing there. When they do move it is with the economy of something that has already decided the next thousand steps. Their hooves leave no new print that isn’t immediately filled by moss.

The silence is not absence. It is a positive condition, a pressure the air exerts against the possibility of noise. Footsteps would be absorbed by the living carpet before they finished happening. A tool striking stone would be a theoretical event. The few remaining inhabitants—if that is still the word—move the way the animals move. They wear the same greys and greens as the walls. Their tools are bone, antler, fire-hardened wood, a little cold-hammered copper that has been recast so many times it has forgotten ore. They repair what does not need repair. They harvest leaves that grow back in the same hour, or what the light insists is an hour. They do not speak. Speech would be a gradient, and the hollow does not permit gradients.

There is a well in the square. The water in it is the same water that beads on every surface: clear, tasting of stone and chlorophyll, never lower, never higher. People and animals drink from it with the same unhurried motions. Around the well the paving stones are worn into shallow dishes that hold the damp in perfect circles. Those circles have been perfect for so long that the idea of an imperfect circle has left the place.

What is unusual is not any single impossibility but the completeness of the agreement. The light does not come from a sun you can point to; it is simply the condition of being inside the hollow, as wetness is the condition of the air. Shadows exist but they do not lengthen. The town has streets that go nowhere except to other streets, and those streets have been sufficient. Time here is a held breath. The erosion that shaped the till lip, that rounded every lintel, that polished the well-stones, finished its work and then, having nowhere further to go, became a kind of stillness that looks like weather. You could leave and return in a decade and the hare would be in the same doorway, the same fern uncurled to the same millimetre, the same film of water on the same leaf. The place is not frozen. It is finished. It continues by not needing to continue.

Stand at the lip long enough and the brightness begins to feel like a sound you cannot hear. Descend the single worn path—more a crease in the moss than a path—and the temperature drops the last fraction of a degree that tells you you have entered a different climate, a pocket the larger world accidentally made and then forgot to unmake. The town receives you without curiosity. There is a bench of split stone under an eave. The moss on it is already the shape of a sitting body. The air tastes of wet iron and crushed green. Nothing will happen. That is the entire event.
