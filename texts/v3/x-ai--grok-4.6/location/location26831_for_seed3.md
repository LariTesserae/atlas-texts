The high places are still deciding their shapes. You feel it first in the stone underfoot: not the rounded, time-softened massifs of older ranges, but plates and blades of rock that have only recently finished arriving. Fresh fracture faces catch what little light there is—planar, unpolished, still holding the heat of their own birth in places, though the air itself is cool and the heat never lasts. These are mountains in the embryonic sense: not eroded remnants but accretions, slow upwellings that have not yet learned the grammar of weather. Ridges terminate in unfinished edges. Valleys are more like unfinished thoughts, broad and shallow, their floors still lifting in increments too patient for any single lifetime to notice.

Across this raw geology a city has been laid, or grown, or insisted upon. It is urban in intent and industrial in method, yet it never quite coheres into the dense hive one expects. Foundries, stacking yards, turbine halls and stepped housing terraces occupy the more stable shelves and saddles, connected by gantries and cable-ways that span emptiness rather than fill it. Between the clusters the land remains itself—bare, angular, sparsely vegetated except in the damp folds where moisture collects. The settlement is regional in scale, a loose archipelago of making that occupies an entire highland basin and the scarps around it, yet density stays low. You can walk half an hour between the last warehouse of one district and the first chimney of the next, the path switching from paved grit to raw talus without ceremony.

Water is not abundant; it is intermittent and opportunistic. High cloud banks, themselves almost stationary, wring themselves out in long, indecisive rains that arrive more as a change in the quality of dampness than as weather. Condensation is the truer hydrology. Every metal surface, every fresh rock face, every length of pipe sweats. Gutters and catchment lips have been bolted to the newest cliffs; they fill slowly, drop by drop, feeding cisterns that taste of iron and cold mineral. Small tarns occupy depressions that did not exist a generation ago. The vegetation that thrives here—lush by the standards of such height and such newness—clings to these wet seams. Mosses thicker than felt, ferns whose fronds uncurl with visible reluctance, dark succulents and hanging curtains of something like liverwort that should not survive the thin air or the cold. They colonize not only stone but the lower reaches of the machinery: boiler jackets, cooling vanes, the undersides of walkways. In the dimness they appear almost black, drinking what light there is.

The light itself is a perpetual late hour. No true night, no true day—only a thick, high overcast that never quite lifts and never quite darkens into black. Everything is seen as if through smoked glass or deep water. Shadows are long and uncertain. Lamps burn in the factories, but they are industrial lamps, utilitarian, their glow pooling rather than carrying. The overall impression is of a place that has forgotten noon.

Sound occupies a middle register: not silence, not roar. The factories keep a measured, bass-heavy conversation—presses cycling, conveyors that never hurry, the long exhalation of steam that has traveled kilometers of pipe. Wind finds the unfinished ridgelines and makes a low, continuous complaint. Over this, and through it, the fauna. They are abundant to the point of crowding the available niches. Herds of something like tahr or mountain goat pick their way across roofs and slag heaps with the same unhurried gait they use on the raw scree. Flocks of dark birds—or bird-shaped absences—turn in the dim air, their calls overlapping into a single textured noise that never quite resolves into individual voices. Smaller lives occupy every damp crevice: insects whose wings catch the weak light like mica, amphibians that have no business at this elevation yet persist in the condensation runnels, pale things that emerge from pipework at dusk (though dusk is continuous). They do not flee the machines. They use them. Nesting in cooling towers, grazing the moss that grows on warm housings, drinking from leaky valves.

The slowness is structural. Change happens, but it happens the way the mountains themselves happen: by accumulation so gradual it feels like stillness until you look away and look back. A new terrace of housing will appear over the course of a decade, its foundations poured into rock that was still rising when the first surveyors marked it. A glacier of rust will advance a few meters across a forgotten gantry. The animals shift their ranges by degrees too fine to map in a single season. Even the industrial processes have been tuned to this tempo—batch cycles measured in weeks, furnaces that are never fully banked nor fully roaring. The world here prefers the long gradient.

And then the breakdowns, which are not sudden either. They arrive with the same patience. Distances sometimes fail to add. A walk that should take twenty minutes deposits you three districts over and slightly younger, or older; the stone remembers a different sequence of construction. Gravity forgets itself in pockets: a dropped wrench hangs, rotating, for the length of a held breath before continuing downward as if nothing occurred. Reflections in the condensation on a window show rooms that do not exist on the other side of the glass, or show them occupied by versions of the workers who have not yet been born, or who finished their shifts a century ago. The fauna are not exempt. You will see a goat step through a solid bulkhead as though the metal were only a rumor. You will hear factory whistles answered from inside the rock itself. The air, already thin and cool and damp, occasionally tastes of other altitudes, other weathers—salt, or pollen from forests that cannot grow here, or the ozone of a thunderstorm that is still assembling itself somewhere far below.

The materials of the place keep their own counsel. Unweathered igneous rock, basalt and something paler, almost porcelain in the newest exposures. Riveted plate, brick the color of dried blood, concrete that never quite cured in the cold and therefore remains faintly plastic, taking fingerprints years later. Glass that fogs from the inside. Insulation that has become habitat. Everything is both built and still becoming. The city does not sit on the mountains so much as negotiate with them, an ongoing treaty written in expansion joints and sacrificial walls designed to crack along predicted lines as the land continues its slow, unfinished ascent.

At the edges of the settled shelves the raw geology reasserts itself completely: fields of shattered plates, embryonic cirques that hold nothing yet but shadow and the possibility of ice, ridgelines that simply stop, as if the sculptor set the chisel down and walked away. The wind is louder there. The animals are thicker. The dimness feels older. You can stand at one of those unfinished edges and look out across the next valley—already being occupied, already sprouting its first cable-masts and moss-slicked roofs—and understand that the whole region is a single, patient argument between making and unmaking, between the industrial will and the stone that has not yet agreed to stay still.

The rules, insofar as they can be stated, are these: nothing here is finished, including the laws that govern finishing. Moisture is more reliable than rain. Height is not merely a measurement but a condition that leaks. The abundant lives treat the machinery as another kind of cliff. Time is a substance that pools in the low places between districts and runs thin across the newest stone. And the light, such as it is, will not improve. It is enough to work by, enough to see the next gantry, the next herd, the next unexplained shimmer where a wall should be. Enough, just, to keep building.
