The place is a high cirque, newly torn. Not a valley grown old and rounded, but a bite taken from the mountain only a little while ago: a steep-walled cup of fractured basalt and pale, glassy rhyolite, its rims still knife-edged, its talus barely settled. Elevation keeps the air thin above it. The cirque itself is full. Water stands to the lip and a little over, a single cold lens perhaps two hundred meters across and deeper than it is wide, so that the bottom is a blue-black suggestion rather than a floor. There is no shore. The rock simply continues downward, slick and angular, and the water occupies every available volume the way a liquid occupies a vessel it has just been poured into.

Daylight arrives undiminished. At this height the sky is a hard, dry blue; the water takes that light and holds it, turning the upper thirty meters into a clear green-gold broth in which every suspended mote is visible. Below that the light remains strong but cooler, a steady aquarium noon that never flickers. Nothing grows here. No film of algae, no moss in the cracks, no drowned heather at the rim. The stone is mineral only—fresh conchoidal fractures, rust-dark iron stains that have not yet had time to bloom, veins of milky quartz that look poured rather than precipitated. The world is pre-vegetal, or post, or simply elsewhere.

What occupies the volume is fauna, densely, almost to the point of contact. They are not fish in any ordinary sense, though some of them swim. Most cling, crawl, or hang. Their bodies are pale mineral composites: plates of something like nacre over darker, fibrous muscle; jointed limbs that terminate in chisel-edges or rotating burrs; mantles that pulse with a slow peristalsis. Many have internal structures that flash when they turn—thin metal-bright septa, or chambers that briefly catch the light like the inside of a dynamo. They do not look assembled. They look grown, yet the growth has produced gears, ratchets, siphon stacks, and ranked filters. They mine the young rock. Not with tools but with themselves: a clustered mass of them works a single fresh fracture, grinding micron by micron, ingesting silicates and exhaling a faint milk of processed slurry that another caste immediately sieves. The work is industrial in scale and patience. A single face of the cirque wall may occupy the same colony for years. Nothing is hurried. The temperature of the water stays just above the point where movement would stiffen; everything proceeds at the speed of cold chemistry.

Sound fills the basin the way the animals do. It is loud, continuous, and layered. Closest is the dry click and scrape of thousands of mineralized feet and mouthparts on stone, a sound like a factory floor heard through water. Deeper is a low, regular thudding—internal valves or swim-bladders or whatever analogue they possess—beating in slow unison so that the whole lake seems to have a pulse. Superimposed on both is a higher, almost electrical rasp, the collective discharge of the animals that generate current as they filter. The water itself carries a faint metallic tang and a barely perceptible tingle. Bubbles rise in disciplined columns from certain vents the creatures have excavated; they break at the surface in a steady, industrial fizz that never quite becomes a boil. Wind above the rim adds nothing. The mountain is too newly made to have learned how to whistle.

The ecology is closed and strange. There are grazers that rasp the rock, filterers that hang in mid-water like living intake grilles, and larger, slower forms that drift through the crowded volume on membranes stiffened with cartilaginous spars, harvesting the smaller ones with a lazy, mechanical grace. Nothing photosynthetic anchors the chain. Energy appears to come from the stone itself—from the residual heat of its formation, from redox gradients in the fresh fractures, from the weak electric fields the animals generate and then feed upon. Reproduction is gradual and public: two or more individuals lock together for days, exchanging not gametes exactly but patterned mineral secretions that harden into new plates. The young, when they appear, are already armored and already working. Death is rare and local. A failed individual is dismantled by its neighbors with the same unhurried efficiency they apply to the rock; the components are reincorporated. The population density never thins. The cirque remains saturated, a single breathing machine whose parts happen to be alive.

Because the basin is small and complete, it has weather of its own, but the weather is slow. A weak seiche, perhaps once a month, sloshes the entire lens a few centimeters up one wall and down the other. Fine sediment lofted by the grinders takes weeks to settle. Light itself seems to age as it descends: the gold of the surface becomes a patient blue at depth, and the animals there are larger, quieter, more purely geometric, as if the pressure and the dimming have distilled them toward some final industrial form. At the very bottom, where the newest fractures still leak a mineral fog, the activity is densest and the sound is a continuous, submerged roar, yet even there nothing lunges. Everything waits its turn.

No artifact exists that was not grown. No wall was stacked, no pipe laid, no engine housed. The resemblance to manufacture is only the resemblance that certain chemistries, given time and a closed volume and a young mountain’s worth of unweathered stone, will always produce. The cirque does not remember being empty. It has been this full, this loud, this crowded with patient living machinery, for as long as the rock has been sharp.
