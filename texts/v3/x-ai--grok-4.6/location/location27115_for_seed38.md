The plateau does not begin. It has already been going on for so long that beginnings have worn off it like paint. You come up through mist that is not weather so much as exhalation, and the ground you thought you were approaching turns out to have been around you for an hour already: a slow lift of living matter, warm, giving slightly underfoot, veined with water that never quite decides whether it is stream or sweat.

What remains of the old high country is a table the size of a climate. Once there were ranges here. They have been planed, dissolved, ingested. The rock did not vanish so much as lose the argument. What stands in its place is not soil over stone but a single, continent-thick body of plant—lignin and cellulose and mineralized root, compressed and re-grown and compressed again until it has the patience of geology and the temperature of skin. You can walk for weeks and never leave the same organism, if organism is still the word. The surface is not a surface. It is a working thickness. Mosses the color of old bronze and wet slate stack themselves into benches and troughs. Liverworts glaze every overhang. Bracket growths the size of roofs shoulder out of the vertical, and the vertical is only a steeper kind of floor. Where something like a cliff exists, it weeps. Water does not fall here so much as declare itself, beading out of pores in the plant-rock, gathering in hanging lenses, sliding along ribs of bark that have forgotten they were ever trees.

The light is a dim, even broth. Not night. Not day. A green-gray that seems to come from the air’s own water, as if the mist were faintly luminous with the work of being mist. Sometimes a brighter smear moves far overhead—sun, or the idea of sun, filtered through a sky that the plateau has thickened for its own purposes. Shadows are rare and soft. Edges blur. You learn depth by humidity and by the way sound dies.

It is mostly quiet. Not sterile quiet. The quiet of a mouth full of cloth. Water makes the small sounds: a tick when a droplet finally lets go, a longer silk when a film finds a new channel. Leaves do not rustle; they are too wet, too heavy, too old. Once in a long while the mass itself adjusts—a fiber somewhere in the deep thickness giving up a tension it has held since before your language had a word for wood—and the sound that reaches you is less a crack than a remembered one, arriving late, already tired. Fauna is everywhere and almost invisible. Things the color of wet bark travel the undersides of shelves. Soft-bodied grazers, abundant as thought, work the constantly renewed film of algae and slime. You become aware of them as a change in the shine of a surface, or as a warmth that is not the plateau’s own warmth. Larger presences move in the hollows: slow, jointed, built for squeezing. They do not call. Their feet (when they have feet) are padded with the same spongiform tissue that makes the world. Wings exist but they are used the way you might use a sigh—rarely, and only to change rooms.

Because there are rooms. The living mass is architecture without architects. Over ages the plateau has grown galleries, cisterns, low halls whose ceilings are the root-thatches of whatever is living one storey up. Some chambers hold still black water that is not a lake but a gland. Some are lined with pale, photosynthetic skins that drink the dimness and give back a light even dimmer, a kind of vegetable dusk you can read a palm by if you wait. Air moves through these spaces the way water does: without hurry, by seepage and pressure, following gradients so weak they feel like mood. The temperature is a constant mild heat, the heat of compost that has decided to be a country. You sweat and do not dry. Cloth and skin take on the same faint green cast as everything else.

Water is near always. It stands in the shallow dishes the mosses keep. It threads the interior. It condenses on you as soon as you stop moving, as if the air had been waiting for a colder object. In places the plateau opens into marshes that are not lowlands—they are simply regions where the living floor has chosen to be more sponge than timber. You wade through warm tea-colored water that comes to the knee, the thigh, and the bottom is a weave you can feel breathing. Reeds here are not individuals. They are expressions of the same body, sent up the way a hand is sent up. Between them, the sky (such as it is) comes closer. Mist walks. On the far side of such a marsh the land does not rise so much as thicken again, becoming walkable, then climbable, then once more a sequence of wet rooms.

Erosion has finished its obvious work and begun a stranger one. There are no young shapes. Every contour has been reconsidered ten thousand times. The plateau’s upper weather—its own small climates, its local drizzle and its local stillness—has sanded the living mass into forms that look inevitable: long undulations like the backs of sleeping animals the size of provinces; sinkholes that are not collapses but mouths, rimmed with teeth of harder wood, slowly drinking the mist back into the dark circulation below. Time here is a horizontal thing. Change happens, but it happens the way a stain spreads in cloth already wet. A new gallery may take a century to open. A hanging lake may decide, over a generation of drips, to become a waterfall no taller than a person, and that waterfall may then be reabsorbed, leaving only a darker stripe and a smell of iron and sap.

Scattered through all this are the marks. They are easy to miss and harder to explain. In one hall, a ring of stones that are actually stones—true mineral, foreign, already being fitted with moss like a sleeve. They sit in a circle too regular for accident and too tired for intention. Elsewhere, a straight cut through a buttress of plant-rock, the faces glazed as if by old heat or old decision, the cut itself filling now with the same patient water as everything else. Once, a long way in, a chamber whose walls have been encouraged into steps, or have encouraged themselves, and on the broadest tread a depression the size and suggestion of a lying body, polished by something that was not rain. No metal. No tool-mark sharp enough to keep its name. Whatever used this place, or was used by it, did so in a tense the plateau has already composted. The structures do not feel like ruins so much as like pauses the vegetation has agreed, for a while, not to finish.

The strangeness is not an event. It is the grain. Distances lie. You walk toward a brighter smear and find you have been descending through the thickness, and the smear was a colony of luminous films on a ceiling you have just put above you. Directions revert. Water sometimes runs up the faint grooves in a wall, not quickly, not as a trick, but as if “down” were a local opinion the plateau is willing to discuss. In the deepest rooms the air tastes of something like lightning that never arrived—ozone and nectar and wet dust. Seeds (if they are seeds) hang in still pockets, turning so slowly you only know they have moved because you remember a different silhouette. Some of the larger hollows have weather of their own: a private drizzle, a private warmth, a fog that does not answer to the fog outside. You can stand in a doorway of living wood and put one hand in two climates.

Ecology here is a closed sentence that keeps being rewritten with the same words. Everything eats the plateau and is the plateau. Fauna returns its minerals in the same warm dark the roots have already reserved. There is no outside wood, no imported stone, no season that is not a modulation of damp and slightly-less-damp. The abundant lives do not so much occupy niches as secrete them, the way a shell secretes a shell. Predation, when it happens, is quiet and neighboring—an engulfing, a dissolving, a patient unmaking in a side chamber while the rest of the world continues not to notice. Growth and rot have the same color.

If there are rules, they are these: nothing is in a hurry except water, and water’s hurry is still a kind of waiting. Light is a guest, not a master. Sound is a debt that is rarely collected. The high country has become a single, vast, dim organism that remembers mountains the way a body remembers a fever—accurately, without nostalgia, as a condition it has finished with. You may walk it for a lifetime and only ever cross a few of its weathers. Behind you the galleries close at the speed of thought in wood. Ahead, the mist continues to be made. Under everything, the old rock, what is left of it, listens to roots that have been speaking to it so long they have become its voice.

At evening—which is only a further dimming, a thickening of the broth—the hanging water holds what light there is and the whole plateau seems to sweat stars that are not stars: just wet points, just the ends of leaves, just the eyes of things that have decided not to move until the next century. You hear one droplet finish its fall. Then the quiet takes the sound back, as it takes everything, into the warm, ancient, unfinished dark of the living ground.
