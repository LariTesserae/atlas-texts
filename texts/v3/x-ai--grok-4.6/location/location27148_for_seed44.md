The place is a mill-hamlet no larger than a cathedral close, set in a warm basin where the water table sits a hand’s width underfoot and sometimes above it. You never quite leave the marsh. The ground is not ground so much as a long negotiation between peat, root-mat, brick, and plank. After rain—and rain is frequent, warm, unemphatic—the paths shine and the brick weeps. Between storms the air still beads on iron and on the undersides of leaves. Everything is a little wet. Everything has been a little wet for a long time.

It is bounded. On one side a black canal, slow and the color of steeped tea, holds the settlement like a moat. On the other three sides the reed-forest closes in—canes thicker than a wrist, twelve and fifteen meters high, so dense that the hamlet feels cut from them rather than built among them. There is a single lock, a single road of crushed shell and slag that drowns twice a year and is patiently rebuilt, and a chimney you can see from the lock as a dark column furred with epiphytes. That is the whole town: the works, the drying sheds, the six or seven dwellings, the pump-house, the canteen, the narrow yards.

Nothing here was made in a hurry and nothing has been abandoned. The industry is old enough to have weather. Cast housings sit on pads of rammed marl that have settled a finger’s width in a century. Bolts have been painted, rusted, painted again. Belt-drives still run. A small dynamo in the pump-house keeps a few lamps and the winches alive; the rest is steam from a low-pressure boiler that drinks canal water and wood from the managed stands beyond the reeds. The smell is sweet-rot and hot oil and wet cellulose. The sound is continuous and unapologetic: the thump of the stamper, the long hiss when a valve opens, water falling from the overflow into the race, the slap of broad leaves when a gust finds the yard. It is loud the way a kitchen is loud, or a weir—layered, local, without echo because the plants eat the echo.

The plants are not decoration and they are not ruins. They are the rest of the architecture. A century of not quite winning has taught the settlement to treat living mass as a material with a maintenance schedule. The drying sheds are timber frames whose posts have been sistered with living fig and with trained rattan; aerial roots have been lashed, then allowed to lignify, then lashed again, until the shed and the tree are one load path. Roofs are not roofs so much as planted decks—sedum and a thick, waxy succulent the millhands call plateleaf—held in shallow trays of slag and canal-silt. They leak on purpose into gutters that feed the retting tanks. Walls of the older brick buildings are so colonized that the brick is substrate: moss first, then a fern that likes mortar, then a climbing aroid whose stems are as thick as cable. In the hottest months the aroids are cut back from the windows; in the wet months they are left, because they shed water better than paint.

Between structures there is almost no empty air. You walk in a slot. Leaves touch both shoulders. Pipes run at chest height, lagged in rotting canvas and new moss, hot enough that you learn the places not to brush. Overhead, guy-wires and steam-lines and the trained canes of the reed-forest make a second storey of crossings. Light comes through all of this as ordinary daylight, thinned and greened, never dim enough to need the lamps before late afternoon. On clear days a white disc of sun stands over the chimney and the plateleaf roofs throw a little glare. Most days are not clear. The air holds water. You see it.

The work is plant into product, slowly. They grow a fiber cane in the managed stands and they pull a second fiber from the wild reeds. Both are cut on a long rotation, floated in, laid in the retting tanks—long brick troughs let into the wet ground, always full, always warm, the surface a skin of pollen and drowned midges. The tanks smell like a riverbank in August. After weeks, not days, the stems are drawn, beaten in the stamper, washed in canal water, and hung in the sheds until they are only damp instead of wet. From there: twine, matting, a coarse paper the color of the canal, and a tannin liquor boiled down in open pans for the leather works two valleys over. None of it is hurried. The boiler is never pushed. The dynamo hums at one note from first light until they bank the fire. People move the way the machinery moves—along known paths, with their heads slightly ducked for the pipes, stopping to clear a gutter or to twist a new lashing around a root that has begun to lift a plank.

There are few animals. A frog sometimes in the race. Midges over the tanks at dusk. Once in a while a heron on the lock gate, looking as if it had taken a wrong turning. The density of plant and the residual chemistry of the liquor seem to have decided the matter. You hear insects more than you see them. You do not hear birds inside the hamlet; the reed-forest takes them a little farther out, where the stamper is only a thud.

Underfoot the construction is layered and honest about the water. First the living mat—roots, peat, the horizontal rhizomes of the reeds. Then brush and slag packed in old seasons. Then brick or sleeper, and on top of that the planks, which are always a little cupped, always dark. In the yards the planks have been replaced so often that the new ones are a different wood, paler, already spotting with mold. Between the planks the water shows, not as a view but as a glint, proof that you are walking on a lid. When a loaded cart crosses, the lid flexes. No one comments.

The dwellings are the same grammar at a smaller scale. Each is a brick box or a timber box, raised two steps, wrapped in the same vines, roof planted, windows shuttered against the afternoon damp. Laundry never fully dries; it is taken in smelling of steam and leaf. Interiors are warm in the way a closed greenhouse is warm. Walls sweat behind the furniture. People live with a cloth in the hand. At the canteen the tables are zinc, beaded, and the tea is always hot because the urn sits on a bleed from the works. Talk is close-range. The stamper fills the rest.

Established, here, means the place has finished arguing with its site. The canal does not flood the works anymore because the sills were raised after the bad year, and the bad year was long enough ago that the new brick has its own moss. The living posts have taken the loads they were meant to take. The reed-forest is cut on a plan written in a book with swollen pages. The boiler has a crack that is watched and has been watched by three successive hands. Change happens, but it happens as growth, as rot, as a plank that finally gives, as a cane that is ready. You could stand in the yard for a week and believe nothing moved except the belts and the water. Stand for a decade and the fig will have closed another window, and they will have cut a new one beside it, and lashed the wound, and gone back to the tanks.

At evening the light goes ordinary-gold for a little while, caught in steam and in the wet faces of leaves. The dynamo is shut down. The stamper stops and the quieter noises come forward: drip from the gutters, the canal against the piling, a valve ticking as it cools. Someone walks the plank to the lock with a lamp. The reeds stand black against a sky that is still pale. The hamlet is a small, loud, green machine sitting in a saucer of warm water, doing the same work it did yesterday, wearing its plants the way it wears its rust—not as failure, not as romance, as the condition of remaining.
