The highland sea does not begin. It has no shore you could walk and no moment when the water decided to be here. It sits on the planet’s raised back the way a thought sits in a skull: occupying, complete, already old. The light that reaches it is ordinary daylight, thinned a little by altitude, so that even at noon the water holds a cool mineral clarity rather than warmth. You see a long way. What you see is mostly interval.

Between the great bodies there are kilometers of open volume. The water there is not empty — it is faintly green with suspended cells, and the cells are plant — but it feels empty because the presences that count as presence stand so far apart. Each one occupies its own weather. Each one is a forest, a reef, a range, and a mill.

They are not trees in any coastal sense. They are the substrate. Their roots are the highland itself: a living basalt of compressed tissue, black-green and cool to the touch, pitted by the same current that has run across it since the current learned its path. The current has one path. It has never revised it. Where the tissue rises into the water column it becomes trunk, then chamber, then canopy, then the long trailing curtains that occupy the upper fathoms like slowly breathing ironworks. The tissue is photosynthetic and also mechanical. Vascular bundles as wide as river gorges take in cold water and return it warmer by a fraction of a degree that never varies. Inside the trunks there are stacks — not built, never assembled — of laminated living plate that hold charge the way a leaf holds sugar. The charge has a hum. The hum has a pitch. The pitch has not drifted.

You hear the place before you understand its size. The sound is loud in the way a cataract is loud when you have lived beside it long enough to forget that silence is possible. It is the sound of an ocean being used. Water through living turbines older than any mountain range still growing; gas exchanged in chambers the size of valleys; the low industrial thunder of electrochemical reduction happening at continental scale, forever, at the same rate. There is no idle and no peak. The plants do not sleep. They do not fruit in seasons. They do not drop leaves. Whatever once would have been growth has already happened, and whatever would have been decay has already been metabolized back into the same standing mass. The system is closed so tightly that time has nothing to push against.

Above, the surface is a single cool sheet. It does not chop. It does not darken for storms, because the weather of this sea is as settled as its floor: a permanent high overcast of its own making, water-saturated air that is almost a second, thinner ocean, raining a rain so fine and so continuous it cannot be distinguished from not raining. The rain has one intensity. Light comes through it the way light comes through frosted glass — present, sufficient, without drama. You could live a life under that light and never learn the sun’s disc.

Fauna is abundant in the statistical sense and sparse in the visual one, which is the same paradox as the plants. There are animals. They are many. They do not crowd. Shoals occupy the long galleries between trunks the way dust occupies a sunbeam — densely, locally, and then not at all for a day’s swim. Filter-feeders the size of dinghies cling to the outer bark in constellations that have not rearranged. Their feeding currents add a second, softer roar to the first, a nap of sound on the industrial nap. Things with too many fins hang in the charged water near the stacks and drink the field; their lateral lines glow a dull, constant line. Nothing hunts in a way that would empty a niche. Nothing blooms. The same mouths open on the same schedule that is not a schedule so much as a fixed angle of a wheel that does not turn because it has already arrived at every position at once.

If you press a palm to the living stone of a trunk you feel cool, and under the cool a vibration so even it reads as stillness. The bark is weathered into soft flutings, into bowls where water stands, into overhangs polished by the bodies of animals that have used them as roads for longer than roads have been an idea. There are no tool marks. There are no foundations, no pilings, no scuttled hulls, no wire. The industrial is not an addition. It is how the plants are plants. They refine. They pump. They store and discharge. They have the scale of factories because a factory is only a metabolism with ambitions, and these metabolisms finished their ambitions so long ago that ambition has weathered off them like a softer mineral.

Distance here is a kind of weather. Cross the open water between two of the great bodies and you are in a climate: a cool, lit, loudly humming desert of liquid, with the next continent-tree first a darkness, then a wall, then a city of leaves that are not leaves but heat-exchange vanes, endless, motionless, each one beaded with the same droplets that have always been there. Behind the vanes, halls. The halls are full of water. The water is full of the smell of wet iron and chlorophyll and something electrical and clean. Sound stacks on itself until it becomes a pressure. You shout and the shout is accepted into the roar without residue.

Deeper — not abyss, the highland sea is not bottomless, only deep enough — the light goes green and then the green goes grainy and the grain is still plant, a night-silt of living dust that has hung in the same strata since the strata formed. The seafloor is not mud. It is cambium. It bruises if you drag a keel, and then the bruise closes so exactly that the gesture might as well not have happened. Erosion here is not loss. It is the finished sentence of form. Everything that could be worn down has been worn to the shape that wears no further. The currents know those shapes by heart. They have no other heart to know them with.

At the rarest intervals a trunk does not rise but spreads, a living plateau just under the permanent rain, and on that plateau the water shallows to the height of a person and the light is almost air and the sound, strangely, is loudest of all: a white, even shout of water moving through a million mouths that are also leaves that are also turbines. Walk there — you can walk; the tissue bears weight the way old ice does — and you leave no lasting print. The surface heals behind you into the same dimpled, ancient skin. Fish travel with you in escort, then peel away on errands that are not errands. Far off, another plateau, another shout, the same shout. The world is large enough to have weather of its own and has chosen one weather and kept it.

There is a quality of strangeness that never quite becomes horror and never quite becomes wonder. It is the strangeness of a machine that grew, or of a forest that works, or of a sea that has decided to be finished. You keep expecting a change of shift, a season, a broken vane, a silence. None of these are available. The charge in the stacks holds. The rain continues not to be distinguishable from not-rain. The fauna complete their loops. The cool does not deepen toward night because night, if it occurs, occurs as a dimming so gradual and so identical each time that it is only another name for the same light.

Stay long enough and the loudness becomes a kind of quiet. Stay longer and you understand that this is not a metaphor. The place has no gradients left that matter. It is not frozen — the water moves, the mills turn, the animals feed — but it is invariant. Motion without history. Industry without a first day of operation. A highland ocean that is also a single plant that is also a factory that no one built and that will not, because it cannot, stop, start, or become otherwise. The elevation holds it up into the thin cool air. The water fills it. The living mass is the ground and the walls and the weather and the work. Between the great bodies, the long, lit, roaring rooms of emptiness go on, and on, and are already the same as they will be.
