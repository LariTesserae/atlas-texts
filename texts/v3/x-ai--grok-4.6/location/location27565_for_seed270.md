The pocket sits in the last remaining dimple of a land that has otherwise forgotten how to have height. Whatever mountains or ridges once stood here were planed away so long ago that even the memory of slope has been compacted into a single, almost-level sheet of pale mineral. The dimple itself is no deeper than a tall room and no wider than a neglected courtyard; from any distance it reads as a faint discoloration, a saucer of slightly darker grit. Step into it and the world contracts.

The air is cool and utterly dry. It smells of old iron filings, of salt that has never known rain, of something faintly oily that no longer evaporates. There is no moisture to bead on skin or to carry scent far; everything stays close, concentrated. The floor is a mosaic of compacted slag, wind-polished gypsum, and flakes of rust so ancient they have become a kind of ochre sand. Walls rise only a few meters before they fade into the surrounding peneplain; they are layered like sedimentary cake—thin strata of oxidized metal, compressed dust, and glassy sinter left by processes that ended before any living thing now present was conceived.

Nothing green has ever taken root here. The chemistry is wrong, the water budget is a rounding error, and the surface itself is too mobile, too constantly rearranged by the things that live on it. Instead the hollow is saturated with bodies.

They occupy every cubic centimeter that is not already occupied by another body. Beetles the size of clenched fists clamber over one another in slow, grinding traffic, their elytra the color of tarnished bronze and pitted with embedded grains of the same slag they walk on. Between their legs move rivers of smaller insects, mites, and things that have the look of earwigs redesigned by a committee that valued armor over elegance. Larger shapes—flattened, many-legged, the length of a forearm—slide through the lower strata like living plowshares, turning the mineral floor in slow furrows. Above them, in the narrow airspace, clouds of winged forms rise and settle, never quite flying so much as being continuously displaced. The density is such that motion is collective: when one creature shifts, a dozen others must shift, and the adjustment ripples outward in visible waves.

They eat the place and they eat each other. Mandibles scrape rust into paste. Palps harvest the microscopic salts that bloom on cooler nights. Predators do not chase so much as wait for the traffic to deliver a weaker neighbor within reach; the sound of a successful strike is a wet click followed by a brief, localized frenzy of smaller scavengers. Nothing here is waste for long. Even the dead are dismantled and redistributed before they can dry into permanence.

Scattered through this living congestion are the last industrial ghosts. A length of pipe, once perhaps a conduit, now serves as a communal tunnel; its interior is polished to a dull sheen by centuries of passage. A single gear the size of a cartwheel stands half-buried, still able to turn a few degrees when enough bodies push against its teeth; the resulting groan is one of the louder notes in the constant chord. A collapsed framework of I-beams forms a kind of multilevel plaza where the larger crawlers congregate at certain hours, their weight making the metal flex and complain. These remnants are not ruins in the romantic sense; they are simply another mineral stratum, slightly more organized than the rest, and the fauna treat them as topography.

Light arrives normally—an even, slightly cool daylight that does not dazzle. It reveals everything without drama: the overlapping plates of chitin, the dull gleam of old steel, the way dust hangs in the air only long enough to be breathed or brushed aside. Shadows are short and crowded.

The sound never stops. It is a layered roar of scraping, clicking, whirring, and the dry rattle of thousands of limbs against thousands of other limbs. When the gear turns, a deeper mechanical complaint joins the chorus. When a particularly dense wave of winged things lifts, the pitch rises into a harsh, metallic hiss. There is no silence here, and no single voice. The hollow speaks in the aggregate.

Processes continue without pause. There are feeding cycles timed to the slight daily cooling. There are vertical migrations: at the warmest part of the day the upper layers thin as bodies sink into cooler crevices; at dusk they rise again, turning the surface into a seething carpet. Territorial disputes flare and subside within minutes, marked by sudden knots of motion that dissolve back into the general flow. Eggs are laid in the sheltered undersides of the old metal and hatch into more of the same traffic. The ecosystem does not grow or diminish; it simply persists at saturation, an ancient machine of flesh and mineral that long ago reached its final, unchanging tempo.

The weirdness is mild, almost administrative. Some of the larger beetles have legs that end in tiny, functional sockets, as if they once interfaced with the machinery and never quite forgot the habit. A few of the winged forms leave faint magnetic traces that make nearby rust flakes twitch. These are details, not revelations. The place does not feel like a riddle. It feels like a conclusion—the last possible arrangement of matter and appetite in a landscape that has already finished every other story it knew how to tell.

Stand in the center long enough and the hollow begins to feel less like a location and more like a single, vast organism whose organs are other organisms, whose skeleton is leftover industry, and whose breath is the dry, ceaseless noise of things that have nowhere else to go and no reason to stop. The surrounding plain remains indifferent, almost featureless. Only this one pocket still remembers how to be busy.
