The cut is new enough that the stone still has an edge. You feel it first in the stairs: they are not worn into troughs, they have been sliced, and the risers still catch the sole. Twenty steps down from the glare and the stairs turn into a landing that is also a street, and the heat comes up through the masonry as if the bricks themselves were recently fired and never quite allowed to cool.

This is not a cavern in the old sense. It is a single working volume the size of a market square stacked on itself three times, roofed by the underside of the ordinary ground. The ceiling is low enough in places that a tall person learns the habit of a slight stoop. In other places it lifts into a nave of raw basalt, angular, unpolished, the tool-marks still visible as a kind of writing. Young rock. It has not had centuries to forget the violence of being opened. White mineral threads are already precipitating in the joints—salt, gypsum, something that tastes of metal if you put a finger to a damp seam—but they are thin, recent, like a first frost of stone.

Water here is not a body. It is a behavior. It arrives as seepage along three known faults and as breath. The air is so hot that every cool surface sweats. Pipes bloom with beads. The undersides of catwalks grow a constant dew that does not quite fall. Once or twice a day a finer rain starts in the upper dark: condensation shedding from the ceiling in a brief, localized shower that lasts a minute and leaves the iron ringing. People step under awnings without comment. There are no gutters in the civic sense; there are troughs, lipped into the fresh stone, that lead the water to cisterns and then to the boilers. The cisterns are never full. The boilers are never quiet for long, though “quiet” is the wrong complaint. The whole place is mostly quiet. The machines have been built to live with people in a room this size. They do not shout. They tick, they breathe steam through baffles, they make a padded industrial murmur that the rock absorbs. When a valve lets go it is a polite hiss, quickly gone. You can hear a conversation across the square if both parties wish it. You can also hear, if you stand still, the ceiling ticking as it expands.

There are no animals. This is not a metaphor and not quite a law, only a fact so complete it has become part of the climate. No moths at the lamps. No mites in the stored grain. No dogs, no rats, no pale cave things. The heat and the sterility of the new cut may be enough explanation; the people offer others, milder and stranger, and do not press them. Flies do not come down the stair. A crust of bread left on a railing will dry, then case itself in mineral film, and remain unvisited. The absence has a pressure of its own. It makes the dense human presence feel denser. It makes the sparse green things look like a decision.

Vegetation is allowed in the wet seams and nowhere else. A few species tolerate the heat if their roots can find the condensation line: a fern so reduced it is almost a green wire, a moss the color of old tea, a climbing succulent that people train along the cooler north faces of the tenements. These are not gardens. They are stains that happen to be alive. In one alcove someone has set three clay pots under a perpetual drip and grows a bitter leaf used in tea. That is the extent of cultivation. The rest of the green is accidental and sparse and clung to the architecture like a rumor.

The architecture is the point. Everything that can be stacked has been stacked. The settlement is urban in the way a ship’s hold is urban: organized, close, indifferent to the idea of a horizon. Brick tenements rise four and five floors and stop only because the ceiling insists. Between them the alleys are one person wide, sometimes roofed with gratings that are themselves streets for the level above. Pipes are a second city, a third. They run in bundles along every vertical, painted in a local code of heat and waste and steam-return, so that the walls are striped. Balconies overlap. Laundry, what little there is, dries in minutes and is taken in smelling of minerals. Workshops occupy the ground because the ground is warmest and the machines prefer it. Above the workshops, rooms. Above the rooms, more rooms cut directly into the surviving benches of stone, so that some dwellings have one wall of brick and three of living rock, still sharp at the corners, still bleeding a little salt.

Industry here is not a district. It is the metabolism. Small foundries, a pressing works, a shop that draws wire, a house that does nothing but re-pack bearings and send them up the stair. Electrification is partial and proud: filament lamps in cages, a few motors, a dynamo whose note you stop hearing after an hour. Much of the work is still heat and pressure and the patient violence of engines that can be understood with a wrench. Smoke is a problem they have not entirely solved. It layers under the ceiling in a slow brown weather and is pulled, when the wind on the surface is kind, toward two brick stacks that punch through to the world above. On still days the upper air in the chamber becomes a dim tea. People live in it anyway. Eyes adjust. The light was never going to be good. What light there is comes from the lamps, from the pink and orange mouths of the furnaces seen through hatches, from a few shafts where the surface has been pierced and glazed with thick green glass, so that noon arrives down here as a stained, distant coin. Shadows are long and numerous. Faces are lit from below more often than not.

The heat is the true government. It is not the dry heat of a desert. It is close, wet-edged, a heat that lives in the lungs. Metal left in the sun of a furnace alley will raise a blister. Stone remains tolerable because the condensation cools it in films. People move the way people move in boiler rooms: slowly, with a knowledge of which surfaces can be touched. Children learn the map of safe railings. There is a daily pulse. Before dawn (a word that means only that the glass coins go dark) the foundries bank their fires and the temperature eases by a degree that everyone can feel. A finer condensation falls. For an hour the place is almost cool enough to imagine another climate. Then the work starts and the heat climbs back into the body of the rock. The rock stores it. Night, such as it is, never fully retrieves what day has given.

Because the volume is small and the occupancy high, the settlement has the social physics of a single building that forgot to stop being a town. Everyone’s noise is potentially everyone else’s. Hence the quiet machines, the soft shoes, the custom of not calling up from a lower gallery. Arguments happen; they happen in rooms. Public space is for transit and for the brief, formal markets that appear on the central floor when a delivery comes down the stair: sacks, ingots, a crate of the bitter leaf, news. Currency is ordinary. So are debts. What is less ordinary is the feeling that the place is still arriving. The cut is young. New rooms are still being opened in the eastern wall; you can hear, some days, the short disciplined bite of tools, not blasting—blasting would be too much for a chamber this size—but patient removal, a fresh face of stone brought into the light. The rubble is crushed and fired into more brick. The settlement eats its own excavation. Sharp corners appear overnight. People take pride in how new a threshold still is.

A mild strangeness attends all of this, not enough to make a legend, enough to keep the mind from settling. Time feels slightly thick. Processes that should be sequential overlap: a wall that is being built is already staining with the first mineral bloom; a machine that is running is already being repaired by the person who will take the next shift; the condensation rain begins in one corner while the opposite corner is still dry enough to strike a match on. The absence of animals makes every human sound—a cough, a laugh, the set-down of a cup—arrive without competition, over-clear, like a word spoken in a held breath. And the surface, twenty steps up, might as well be another country. People go up. They come back down with dust on their shoulders that is a different color from the dust here, paler, wind-sorted. They do not linger in the mouth of the stair. The heat below has become the condition of being home.

Stand in the central floor at the hour when the furnaces are open and the glass coins are dull. You will see the whole of it at once, which is the privilege and the sentence of a place this size: the layered streets, the pipe-cities, the sparse green threads in their wet seams, the stacked windows the color of weak tea, the new white scars of recent cutting, the steam that is not weather and not breath and not neither. Nothing hunts. Nothing flies. The rock is still learning the shape it has been given. The boilers inhale. A brief rain starts, locally, as if a cloud had been born at arm’s reach, and then it stops, and the iron ticks, and the work goes on in the dim, in the heat, in a quiet so maintained it has become a kind of courtesy the town extends to itself.
