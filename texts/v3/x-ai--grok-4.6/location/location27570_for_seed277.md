The high dark is not a night that ends. It is a climate.

It begins as stone that forgot how to be mountain and settled, long ago, into something flatter and older: a continent of plateau, lifted and left there, so broad that weather forms on it the way weather forms on a world. Winds have whole lives before they reach an edge. Clouds, when they come, are born here and die here. The rock itself is the color of cooled slag and old iron, not black exactly, more the memory of black after centuries of wind have scoured it to a dull, absorbent dusk. Nothing gleams. Light, when it exists at all, is a rumor that arrives as heat more than brightness: a far infrared press, a warmth in the bones of the stone, as if a buried foundry still works far below and the plateau is only its cooling lid.

You feel the industry before you see any sign of it. The ground ticks. Not regularly. A slow, continental pulse of expansion and contraction, of water flashing to steam in sealed galleries and then condensing again, of minerals being sorted by heat and pressure the way a mill sorts ore. There are no buildings. There have never been buildings anyone could point to. What exists is process: vents that are simply holes the plateau has always had, exhaling a breath that smells of wet metal and warm sulfur; seams of rock that run hotter than the rest, where the stone has been annealed into plates and folds that look almost rolled; long, shallow basins where condensate gathers at dusk-that-is-always and then is wicked away into fractures, leaving paler salt-maps that the next wind will rewrite. The whole highland is a factory that no one assembled. It assembled itself by being old enough, high enough, and warm enough, and by never quite finishing the work of cooling.

Water is a guest with poor manners. It does not stay. Rain comes as brief, localized events—cells the size of drowned cities that wander the plateau like lost animals, dumping themselves in twenty minutes of warm, heavy drops that hiss when they hit the hotter seams. Afterward the stone darkens in patches, and for an hour the air smells of wet iron and something faintly sweet, like overheated sugar. Then it is gone. What remains is damp in the way a cellar is damp: condensation beading on the undersides of overhangs, films in the lee of every wind-carved rib, seeps that never become streams. In the lowest of the old erosion-bowls, water stands in black mirrors no deeper than a wrist, and even those are temporary, drunk by the stone or lifted back into mist when a vent field exhales. There are no rivers with names. There are only the rumors of rivers: stains, alignments of greener-black, the memory of flow written in the grain of the rock.

Vegetation keeps to those rumors. It is sparse not because the world is hostile but because the world is large and the wet is small. In the damp seams grow mats the color of old bruising—low, rubbery, more fungus than plant, or something that gave up the distinction. They do not reach. They occupy. A few upright forms persist in the more reliable pockets: jointed stalks with no leaves, only dark flanges that catch dew and fold it inward; cushions of something like moss that have learned to be almost stone, so that you can walk a long time before you realize the ground has been breathing very slightly under you. Nothing here is interested in height or display. Photosynthesis, if it happens, is a side project. Most of what grows is mining the warmth, the mineral film, the brief wet. The plateau does not green. It mottles.

And yet the place is full of animals.

They do not crowd. Density here is a kind of courtesy: much space between presences, long walks of empty warm dark in which you hear only the tick of stone and the far, soft roar of a vent field working through its cycle. Then a fold of land, a breath of different air, and there they are—not a herd so much as a weather of bodies. Things with too many joints, moving in practiced silence, their hides the same slag-dusk as the rock, heat-signatures more honest than their outlines. Grazers of the bruise-mats, if grazing is the word; strippers of mineral film; things that drink from the wrist-deep mirrors by kneeling in a way that looks like prayer and then are gone. Predators exist as interruptions in the quiet: a sudden absence of small sounds, a warmth that is not the ground’s warmth, the click of something hard set carefully down. The hunt is not loud. It does not need to be. The plateau is so large that even abundance is a local phenomenon. You can travel for a day in the dark and meet no one, and then spend an hour in a condensation-field so thick with life that the air itself seems to flinch and settle, flinch and settle, as bodies pass.

They use the industry. That is the mildly wrong thing, the one stitch that does not quite match the cloth. Not tools. Not structures. Use. Certain animals linger on the hotter seams until their bellies are banks of stored heat, then walk the long cold-dark between vents like portable summers, and smaller lives follow them for the climate they carry. Others have learned the timing of the steam-exhales and stand downwind with mouths open, sieving whatever the plateau is venting—minerals, midges, the protein-mist of things that live in the galleries below. There are species that nest only where the rock has been annealed into those rolled plates, as if the grain of the stone were a kind of grammar they can read. None of this looks like technology. It looks like ecology that has had a very long time to notice that the world is a machine and to take a wage from it.

Sound stays mostly in its room. The vastness eats noise. A cry travels a little way and then is thinned into the general warmth, the way ink thins in too much water. What you hear, if you stand still long enough, is layered quiet: the almost-subsonic working of the buried heat, the dry slide of grit along stone when a wind decides to exist, the occasional wet clap of a rain-cell beginning somewhere over the curve of the world, and under all of it the small wet noises of life doing life in the dark—mouths, feet set down with care, the ripple of a black mirror disturbed and then still again. When the factory below shifts into a more active phase, the quiet gains a throat: a long, even rumble you feel in your teeth, not alarming, more like a continent clearing its voice. Animals pause. The bruise-mats release a faint sweet smell. Then everyone continues.

Time here is made of cycles that do not ask permission. Heat-tides move through the stone in multi-day waves; the vents chorus and then fall back to ticking. Rain-cells have seasons that are only seasons if you live long enough to graph them. The fauna migrate not north and south but from warmth to warmth, following the buried weather, leaving the emptier tracts even emptier, so that the plateau seems to inhale life and exhale vacancy in slow breaths. Erosion is not a drama. It finished its big work ages ago. What remains is maintenance: wind polishing what is already smooth, water briefly finding the same old paths, the settled shapes of mesas and shallow canyons that look as if they were poured and then forgotten. The land is in its own state. It does not aspire.

Stand on any rise—there are rises, low and worn, like the knuckles of a buried hand—and the dark goes on in every direction until distance itself becomes a kind of material, thick and warm. No lights of settlements. No roads. The only geometries are the ones heat and time invented: the ruled lines of cooler fractures, the soft circles of old vent-fields gone quiet, the wandering pale of salt where water thought about staying. Above, if there is an above, there is no useful sky. Whatever illumination exists is the plateau’s own leftover, a darkness with a temperature, a darkness you can lean into. It is not hostile. It is simply the native condition, the way wet is the native condition of a sea.

You could walk a lifetime and remain in the same weather, the same industrial hush, the same scatter of lives making their living from seeps and heat and one another. The place does not climax. It continues—damp in pockets, green in stains, loud only when it must be, empty enough to think in, full enough to be eaten in, warm, high, and dark to its bones, a vast lid over a working that has no workers and no end, only the long settled motion of a world that learned how to run itself and then kept running, quietly, in the dark.
