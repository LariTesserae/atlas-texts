The hollow is older than the names anyone ever gave it. It does not announce itself. From the low country around it—the wet pasture, the blackthorn, the shallow clay that never quite dries—you would walk past the lip a hundred times. Heather and a few stunted hawthorns lean inward over the rim as if listening. Then the ground simply ceases. The drop is not dramatic at first: a slump, a darkening of the grass, a colder breath coming up. Ten steps more and the world has a hole in it.

What opens is a single chamber, roughly oval, perhaps sixty paces across at its widest and twice that in the long axis, its floor far enough below that the sky, when you can see it, is a pale coin. The walls are limestone that has been working on itself since before there were shepherds. They are fluted, bossed, undercut, stained in vertical maps of iron and old seepage. The stone is not raw. It has the settled, almost polite wear of something that finished its violence a long time ago and has been only breathing since. Water has written the same slow sentence on every surface: dissolve, redeposit, wait. There are no fresh scars. The hollow is in its own state.

Light does almost no work here. A thin, late, grey daylight sometimes finds the western undercut and lays a smear of pewter on the wet rock, enough to show the texture and then withdraw. The rest is darkness of different weights: the grainy dark of the upper walls, the thick dark of the inner recesses, the absolute dark under the biggest overhangs where the floor recedes into a second, lower notch that you feel rather than see. Cold lives in all of it. The air that pools at the bottom has never been warm. It smells of wet calcite, of old leaf-mould that washed in years ago and never left, of stone that has been damp so long the damp has become a material in its own right.

Moisture is not abundant. It is persistent and particular. There is no standing lake. There are pockets: a black pool no larger than a table under the north wall, its surface so still it looks like a hole in the floor until a drip strikes it; a film of water that never quite becomes a sheet, crawling the fluted grooves; condensation that beads on every overhang and falls when it is ready, which is not often and never in a hurry. After rain—real rain, the kind that finds this country only now and then—a thin fall comes in over the eastern lip. It is not much water. You could cup both hands under it and not fill them quickly. But the hollow is a bowl of stone and empty air, and that modest thread, dropping twenty times a person’s height onto a sloped apron of old flowstone, becomes a sound that owns the place.

The sound is the first thing and the last. It is not a roar exactly, not the white noise of a great river. It is a packed, continuous thunder with a hard bright edge, as if the rock itself were being rung and could not stop. It lives in the empty volume. It ricochets, stacks on itself, finds every hollow and every tight throat of passage and comes back denser. Speech is useless. Thought arrives already dented. After a time the sound stops being something you hear and becomes the medium you stand in, the same way water is the medium of a fish. When the little fall dwindles, days later, the silence that replaces it is almost worse: the chamber rings with the memory, and every new drip is a hammer.

On the walls, wherever a crack holds a pinch of soil or a constant seep, vegetation has done what the light and the cold allow, which is more than seems reasonable. It is not a forest. It is a hanging lushness, intimate and obstinate. Mosses in thick, dripping bolsters—some almost black, some the colour of tarnished brass—cover whole panels of stone. Liverworts glaze the wettest runnels. Ferns, pale and finely divided, unfurl in the gloom as if they had never needed a full day. In one recessed bay, where a little more sky reaches, there is a hanging garden no larger than a room: lady fern and hard fern and something with a reddish stipe, all of it beaded, all of it slowly breathing out a colder green. The volume of the chamber remains empty. Life is a skin. Between one living patch and the next there is only weathered stone and the huge unoccupied air.

Animals are rare enough that each one feels like an event. A pale cricket the length of a thumb may start from a moss cushion and vanish. Once in a long while a fox comes down the scree of the western side—there is a way, if you know it—drinks from the black pool, and leaves. Something small and colourless lives in the pool itself; you see it only when it turns. Bats use the upper dark in season, but not densely; their clicks are lost in the water-sound. The hollow does not teem. It permits.

People have been here. Not many, and not lately in any number, but the marks are clear if you let your hands find them. On a terrace of fallen blocks just above the worst of the spray someone stacked a rough shelter: three walls of unmortared limestone, the fourth open to the dark, the roof long gone. Inside, the floor is a little drier. There is a hearth-stain, a few shards of thick pottery, a bone needle, the rusted tongue of a simple knife. Higher, near the lip on the south side, a flight of steps has been cut—or rather improved, for the rock already offered ledges. The treads are worn in the middle, concave, older than the shelter. At the very rim, half swallowed by hawthorn, two upright stones lean toward each other; whether they were placed or merely spared is no longer obvious. In a dry niche, wrapped in what was once hide and is now something stiffer, someone left a bundle of flint blades and a lump of red ochre. The work is competent, unadorned, the work of hands that had no surplus.

Nothing here is in a hurry. The water writes. The moss thickens by the thickness of a fingernail in a wet year. The shelter loses another stone every few winters. The cold remains. The sound, when the fall is running, remains. The hollow has the patience of a thing that has already become what it is and is only continuing. You can stay long enough for your eyes to accept the dark and your skin to accept the damp and your bones to accept the temperature, and still the place will not have changed while you watched. It will have only gone on being the same absence, richly lined, loudly empty, hidden in the low ground like a held breath.
