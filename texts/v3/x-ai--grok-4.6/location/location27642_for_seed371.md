The cleft is new enough that the mountain has not yet decided its walls belong to weather. They still belong to fracture. You come to it along a ridge that has only recently finished being a single slab, and the path is not a path so much as a sequence of planes that have not learned to be rounded. Everything cuts. The rock is pale where it has just opened, darker where last week’s rain filmed it and last night’s frost did not quite take. Elevation here is not a view; it is a thinning. Breath goes short for reasons that have nothing to do with fear.

The cleft itself is small. Cathedral-small: a nave of stone, a crossing, a dark apse that does not open onto anything like a choir. You could shout across it if the air would hold a shout. It will not. The space is perhaps two hundred paces end to end, less than that from lip to lip at the narrowest, and the floor—if the word applies—is a steep, broken gutter of fresh talus, wet in seams, dry on the new faces. There are no buildings. There has never been a wall set here, no bolt, no cairn that has lasted. The only geometry is the mountain’s own, and it is still being drawn.

Light fails almost immediately. The walls rise higher than the sun’s winter angle, and even in the brief high season the cleft lives in a standing dusk the color of wet iron. What illumination there is arrives as a rumor: a pale smear on a hanging leaf, a momentary glint on a film of water that is already becoming mist. You see by texture more than by brightness—by the way moss drinks what little there is, by the way ferns hold a dim green that seems generated rather than received. Condensation is the local climate. It does not quite rain and it does not quite not-rain. Moisture beads on every surface, gathers, lets go, beads again. Occasional true rain arrives as a vertical argument, loud, brief, then the cleft returns to its private damp: drip, seep, the slow shine of water finding yesterday’s crack.

Vegetation has taken the dark as a kind of permission. It is lush in the way a closed room becomes lush if you leave a window cracked for ten wet years—except the years here have been fewer. Because the rock is young, the plants are colonizers with no patience: liverworts in sheets, mosses so thick they have a nap, ferns stacked in repeating fronds until the wall is more leaf than stone, pale-flowered shrubs that have no business at this height except that the cleft traps humidity the ridge cannot keep. They grow in layers, near-touching, root into root, a saturation of green that has nowhere to go except thicker. In the deepest shade the plants go almost black. You can put a hand into them and lose it to the wrist. There is no understory in the usual sense because there is almost no light-gradient left to stratify; there is only density, and then stone.

No animals. Not a bird in the updraft, not a insect-tick in the moss, not the small mammal that should be here by every reasonable ecology. The silence of bodies is strange against the rest of the noise. Either the cleft is too new, or too unstable, or the air that pools here after dark carries something the factories have given the valley wind, something plants will drink and animals will not. You do not smell chemicals so much as you smell wet stone and crushed chlorophyll and, under that, a faint heated-metal note that does not belong to geology. The plants keep growing. They have not been told.

The sound is the other climate. Wind is compressed by the walls until it has edges. It does not howl so much as tear. When it coincides with a fall of water the cleft becomes a single instrument, a reed of rock. Behind that, always, a lower register: the industrial valley you cannot see, thousands of feet down-slope, a place of crushers and night generators and the long continuous work of ore. The sound arrives without the buildings. It is a pressure in the ribs, a periodicity under the chaos, engines keeping a time the mountain does not keep. Sometimes a deeper detonation—blasting, or a slip in the workings—travels up the stone and the cleft answers with a shiver of loose chips. Nothing here was built. Everything here is used by vibration.

And the place will not stay. That is the rule, if it has one. The cleft is still happening. A slab lets go without preface and becomes the floor, and the floor becomes a new wall for whatever grows next week. Mist boils up from a seam that was dry an hour ago. A hanging garden of fern, entire, shears off in a single wet mass and is gone into the gutter, and already the scar is greening at the edges because the air is a greenhouse of drip and shade. The mountain lifts in increments too small to see and too large to live with; joints open; water finds them; frost, on the rare clear night, wedges them wider. Storms do not visit so much as occupy. They arrive as a change in the pitch of the wind and then the cleft is all water and dark and the industrial bass line grinding on underneath as if the weather were a shift change. Plants flatten, spring, flatten. Rock speaks. The air is cool enough that your skin never quite dries and never quite numbs. You could believe, standing in it, that the world is being manufactured in this one room—not by anyone, not toward anything—just the raw fact of formation: lift, split, wet, green, collapse, again.

There is a moment, if you wait through a lull, when the generators far below cycle down or the wind hits a node and drops, and you hear only the small sounds the density makes: water transferring from frond to frond, a stem straightening, the almost-inaudible creep of a root into a crack that did not exist at dawn. Then the mountain remembers itself. A stone the size of a chest detaches somewhere in the apse and the whole nave fills with the sound of its falling, and the plants close over the new bright wound as if brightness were a kind of injury, and the work—unbuilt, unceasing—goes on in the dark.
