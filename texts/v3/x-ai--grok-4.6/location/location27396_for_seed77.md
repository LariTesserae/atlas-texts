The heat arrives before any horizon does. It is not the heat of a desert that has had millennia to learn how to hold the sun; it is the heat of something still setting, a warmth that comes up through the soles as much as down from the sky. The ground is never quite cool. Even in the thin hours before dawn, when a skin of condensation beads along the raised seams, the plates themselves keep a residual fever, as if the pour is only days old and the world has not yet decided to be finished.

There is no native stone. That is the first fact the place insists on, once the eyes stop looking for mountains that will not come. Everything underfoot is made: a pale, fine-grained material the color of unfired clay and cooled bone, laid in slabs so large that their joints recede into weather. The slabs are not identical. Some are slightly thicker, their edges still showing the meniscus of a liquid that stiffened in place. Some carry the faint, parallel scoring of whatever last passed over them while they were plastic. A few, in the far middle distances, are still matte and darker, not yet fully cured, and from those the heat lifts in visible sheets. The whole basin is low. Elevation here is a rumor. You can walk a day and lose less height than a person is tall, and yet the curvature of the construction is planetary; the far edge of what you can see is not land meeting sky so much as the made surface falling away under its own weather.

Water is a visitor with poor manners and a short memory. It does not live here. It arrives as a brief, fat rain that walks in from the east in a single gray wall, hammers the plates for twenty minutes, and then is gone, leaving only a smell of wet mineral and a network of vanishing films. In the incomplete sectors—where the pour never quite met, or where a later settling opened a trough—the water gathers into long, shallow lenses, no deeper than a wrist, so clear they look like mistakes in the air. These linger a day, two at most. Their margins creep inward under the sun and leave white rims of whatever salt or binder the material sweats. Between rains there is only the night-damp: a condensation that prefers the seams, the slightly raised lips where one slab overrode another. In the morning those lips shine. By midmorning they are dry again, and the shine has become a paler stripe, a scar of repeated wetting.

Vegetation is an afterthought the construction did not budget for. It exists where the joints have begun to fail in the smallest way—hairline openings, not yet erosion so much as the first idea of erosion, embryonic cracks in a body that has barely started to age. Into these, something thin and gray-green has found a living. It is not grass. It is more like a lichen that decided to stand up, or a rush that forgot water. The plants are sparse enough that each one can be counted from a distance if you have the patience. They do not form a sward. They form punctuation. A clump here, a single blade there, a dark stain of moss in a depression that holds dew an hour longer than its neighbors. Their roots, such as they are, do not go down; they go along, following the joints, mining whatever dust and binder-dust has collected. Nothing casts a useful shade. The plants themselves look slightly cooked, their edges browned, as if the heat of the plates is a climate they tolerate rather than a home they chose.

There are no animals. The silence is not the silence of a held breath; it is the silence of a place that was never given the instruction for fauna. Wind moves. It has a long fetch across the basin and arrives already organized, a steady pressure that makes the sparse plants lean all one way and then, hours later, the other. When the rain-wall comes, there is the brief percussion of water on hard made ground, a sound like grain poured on a drumhead the size of a nation. Afterward: ticking. The plates contract by fractions as they shed heat into the evaporating wet, and the ticking travels. You hear it under you and then, a little later, far to the left, as if the basin were a single instrument still learning its pitch. Sometimes, over the darker uncured fields, there is a faint hiss, steam lifting from moisture that found a patch still chemically alive.

The light is ordinary in kind and extraordinary in quantity. A white sun in a sky that has nothing to interrupt it—no ranges, no high cloud factories except the ones the basin manufactures for itself. Heat rises off the plates in columns that tilt and wander. These columns organize. By afternoon the air over the central flats has built its own weather: a lid of haze, then a stacking of pale cloud that does not look like weather borrowed from elsewhere but like a local decision. The cloud does not cool the ground much. It only changes the color of the heat, from glare to a more even, oven-like radiance. Toward evening the convection collapses. The sky goes a long, uncomplicated blue, then copper at the rim, and the plates, which have been storing the day, give it back as a low infrared that you feel on the face and the insides of the wrists.

The construction is not done. That is the other fact, the one that keeps the place from settling into monument. At the margins of attention—always farther than a day’s walk, always somehow also underfoot—the pour continues by processes that do not resemble tools. No devices. No scaffolding. The new material arrives as a slow exudation from the seams themselves, a mineral milk that thickens in the heat and becomes the next plate, or as a fine, dry fall from a sky that sometimes carries dust the same color as the ground, as if the air were being asked to participate in the flooring. In a few places the surface is rumpled, not yet struck level, and these rumples have the look of something embryonic: folds that might become ridges if the making continued for a century, or might simply slump and be overwritten. Erosion has barely been invented. What little wearing exists is mechanical and recent—the polish of wind-blown grit in the most exposed centers, the first rounding of a lip that took the last rain too hard. The geology is raw because it is not geology. It is manufacture that has begun, just begun, to be mistaken for a world.

Scale does the rest of the work. The basin is large enough to have climates inside itself. One province of plates is a half-degree hotter because it cured darker. Another, slightly concave, holds its rare water a day longer and supports a denser scatter of the gray-green plants, a saturation that is still only a suggestion of green at the edge of sight. Weather systems are born over the heat-islands and die at the cooler, older margins where the material has finished its chemical work and lies paler, more brittle, more willing to tick in the night. You can walk toward a rain and never reach it. You can stand in a silence so complete that the only event is the change in the pitch of the plates as the sun leaves them. Distance here is not empty; it is low-saturation. Presence is scattered. Between one clump of vegetation and the next there is enough made nothing to forget that anything living was ever intended.

What the place is for has not been included in the pour. It has the feeling of a floor awaiting a use that did not arrive, or a lid on something that no longer needs covering. The emptiness is not desolate in the romantic sense. It is procedural. Someone, or something that does not use tools as we understand them, decided that this much world should be level, hot, hard, and almost dry, and then the decision kept being made, slab after slab, out to a curve that has its own winds. The first cracks are just now thinking about becoming valleys. The first plants are just now thinking about becoming a flora. Nothing has been hunted here. Nothing has been built on top of the building. The rules are simple and still slightly wet: the ground will be made; the made ground will be hot; water will visit and leave; life, if it comes, will come in the joints and remain sparse; the air will do as heat and distance require. The rest—names, purposes, the long future in which this embryonic surface might finally erode into something a geologist would recognize—has not been poured yet. The ticking goes on into the dark, a quiet, active sound, the sound of a vast thing becoming slightly more itself, and not hurrying.
