The basin was laid, not born.

You know this before you understand how you know it. The first thing underfoot is not soil. It is a skin of packed clay, still showing the flats of palms and the long ovals of heel-prints, still faintly ridged where someone dragged a comb of split reed to key the next course. The clay is warm. It is always warm. Heat comes up through it the way heat comes off a body that has been working since before you arrived and will not stop. There is no bedrock. If you scrape, if you peel a flake with a thumbnail, you find another layer identically marked, and another, and the marks do not fade with depth. The world is fabrication all the way down.

It is low country. Not a depression so much as a decision not to rise. What elevations exist are young and frank: low stepped platforms, knife-edged ramps, small ziggurats no taller than a house, their corners still sharp enough to catch cloth. Nothing has been rounded. Rain has not been allowed to argue with them. Wind has not been given a century. The surfaces glisten as if the last hands left only this morning, and the wetness never quite decides to dry.

Water is everywhere that is not quite a flood. It stands in shallow, ruled sheets across the clay flats, no deeper than the ankle, so clear the palm-prints show through it like fossils that have not yet died. It weeps from the vertical faces of the platforms—not from pipes, not from planned mouths, but from the material itself, as if the clay were a fruit under too much sun. The air is thick with it. You breathe a warm film. Beads form on your skin and do not evaporate; they only join other beads. The light, which is total, turns every standing film into a white blade. There is no shade that is not a made shade: a woven lean of reed mats, a cantilever of unbaked brick, a canopy of living thatch trained over a lattice someone tied with bark cord. Even those shadows are thin. The brightness eats them at the edges.

Vegetation is lush where it is allowed to be, and it is allowed only in islands. Between the platforms and along the wet seams, plants have been set—or have set themselves—in a density that would choke a riverbank, then stop, as if they had been told a boundary. The green is almost indecent against the pale clay: broad leaves fat with heat, hanging gardens of moss that drink the weep, stands of something like papyrus that have been planted in rows and then forgotten into riot. Vines find the tool-marks and follow them like script. Flowers open and stay open. Nothing drops a petal. The lushness is a held breath. You can walk a long time on the open flats—heat-shimmer, water-sheet, the ruled emptiness of a floor poured for a city that was never stacked—and then the green arrives all at once, a thicket so complete it has its own weather, a few degrees wetter, a smell of crushed stem and sweet rot that does not progress to rot.

The scale is the scale of a valley you could shout across if shouting worked. It does not. Sound comes unwillingly. Water should tick and run; it only stands. Leaves should rasp; they are still. What noise there is arrives from far off and already tired: a single drip with no source, a fiber stretching, something like a cough made of mud. The quiet is not peaceful. It is the quiet of a room in which everyone has agreed, without speaking, not to move.

Animals disagree with the agreement and also obey it. They are many. You see them in the water-sheets first—dark commas, pale discs, things with too many joints skating the meniscus, things that breathe at the surface and watch you with eyes set on the tops of their heads. In the thickets the leaves are full of bodies that do not startle: lizards the color of wet brick, moths as wide as hands that never quite land, birds that occupy the lattice-work as if they were extra knots. Larger shapes move in the middle distance, on the ramps, between the ziggurats. They have the outlines of cattle, of dogs, of people who have been walking a long time. They do not come closer. Their motion is real and also looped; you can watch one descend a stair and, if you blink, find it at the top again, or still descending, or standing in the water with its head down as if drinking a reflection that does not ripple. They make almost no sound. When they do, it is the wrong sound—a hoof like a fingertip on drum-skin, a cry like cloth tearing underwater. The air takes the noise and files it away.

Everything that looks like weather is a fixture. The light does not travel. It occupies. Morning is not a direction; it is a condition of the clay, a white-gold saturation that has no sun you can point to, only a brightness that seems to come from the surfaces themselves, as if the world had been fired in a kiln and the kiln left on. Heat sits. It does not pulse. There is no breeze to argue with the hanging water. If there were a storm it would be a painted storm, and it is not painted. The place is finished. Finished, and still wet. Finished, and still showing the last fingerprints.

The strangeness is not an event. It is the substrate. You notice, after a time that may not be time, that the construction does not stop at the plausible. The water-sheets are tiled beneath, each tile a smaller palm-print, and the tiles continue under the thickets, under the animals’ feet, under the idea of a horizon. A ramp ends in air and then continues, the same clay, the same comb-marks, as if the builder had not been told about gravity and had not needed to be. In one of the ziggurats a doorway is only a darker rectangle of the same material; step through and you are on the far side of a different platform, the valley rearranged, the same heat, the same held rain. The plants in the doorway have roots that go into the brick and become brick, fiber becoming course becoming leaf again. You cannot find a place that was not made. You cannot find a tool more complicated than a comb, a cord, a cupped hand, a stone used as a beater. The whole basin is the work of that poverty of means, repeated past reason, past geology, into a stillness that has the texture of a vow.

Stand long enough and the vow includes you. Your own wet footprints fill and do not fade. The light does not shift to tell you how long you have been here. Somewhere a woven mat ticks once, like a knuckle, and then remembers the rule. The animals go on being abundant and almost silent. The clay goes on being new. The water goes on not choosing a sea. This is the country as it was arranged: low, bright, handmade, dripping, green in islands, full of watching life, and finished so completely that change would be a kind of vandalism the world no longer knows how to commit.
