The cleft is narrower than it looks from below. You turn sideways, pack scraping wet stone, and the world pinches to a vertical slot of paler light. Then it opens.

What you step into is not a valley so much as a held breath: a hanging cirque no more than two hundred metres across, its floor a shallow, irregular bowl of fractured basalt still sharp enough to tear fabric. The walls rise in fresh, unsoftened planes, the kind of geometry that has not yet learned weather. Everything weeps. Water films every surface in a continuous, reluctant sheet, beading on the undersides of overhangs, tracing hairline cracks, freezing into translucent skins that last an hour or a minute depending on the wind that funnels over the rim. The air itself is a condensing medium, always on the edge of becoming visible. Breath does not dissipate; it hangs, then sags, then joins the general downward seep.

There is no soil, no lichen, nothing that has ever been green. The palette is wet slate, iron-black, the milky blue of rime that never quite decides to be ice. Occasional hydrothermal veins, relics of the same recent uplift that shoved this pocket into the sky, send thin plumes of vapour that freeze on contact with the ambient cold and fall back as a glittering, almost silent snow. The ground underfoot is a mosaic of these two regimes: warm seeps that keep small patches of stone merely wet, and everything else locked in a skin of ice that groans when the temperature drops another fraction.

Scattered across this bowl, never crowding, never quite regular, stand the buildings. They are few enough that you can count them without turning your head: seventeen primary volumes, plus the slender connecting spines. They look grown rather than assembled, as if the rock had been persuaded to continue upward in more deliberate forms. Surfaces are continuous, without joints or cladding, matte where they have been left raw, polished to a dark glass where traffic or function required it. Some rise as isolated towers, others as low, terraced masses that follow the contour of the cirque wall. Between them the space is generous, almost ceremonial: open expanses of wet stone and black ice, crossed by raised walkways no wider than a person, their undersides dripping steadily into collection channels that disappear into the rock.

The engineering is quiet and does not announce itself. Doors are simply places where the stone becomes briefly permeable; you feel a change in temperature and then you are inside. Interiors are the same material, continuous with the exterior, but the air is held at a precise, dry cold that does not bite. Light arrives through the walls themselves, a even, shadowless daylight that matches the thin high-altitude sun outside so closely you forget the difference until you notice there are no windows. Water is everywhere in the infrastructure and nowhere in the rooms: it is drawn, filtered, perhaps used, then returned to the seeps. You hear it only as a distant, regular pulse in the floor, like a slow heart.

The place is active in ways that never become noisy. Weather cycles last minutes rather than hours. A clear interval of pale sun will glaze every surface; then a ribbon of supercooled mist pours over the western rim and the world turns to white static, every sound muffled except the tick of new ice forming. The buildings adjust. Tiny shifts of alignment, almost below perception, keep the walkways level as the young mountain continues its infinitesimal rise. Once in a long while a slab of rime shears from a tower and shatters on the ice below with a sound like distant glass. That is the loudest thing that happens.

Life is scarce enough to feel like an intrusion when it appears. A single pale bird, some high-altitude specialist with almost no colour, may plane across the rim and vanish again. In the warmer seeps, if you crouch long enough, you sometimes see a flicker of something filamentous and mineral-white, neither plant nor quite animal, withdrawing the instant it is noticed. Otherwise the only motion is water, ice, vapour, and the almost undetectable breathing of the structures themselves.

At night the light does not fail so much as change register. The stone continues to glow at the same intensity, but the quality becomes the colour of late dusk. The dripping slows. The city, if it is a city, seems to listen to itself. You can walk the entire circuit of walkways in twenty minutes and meet no one, hear nothing but your own steps and the patient, inexhaustible weeping of the rock. The settlement is complete, organised, infrastructurally dense, and yet it occupies so little of the available space that emptiness remains the dominant material. It feels less like a place that was built here than like a place the mountain briefly consented to become.
