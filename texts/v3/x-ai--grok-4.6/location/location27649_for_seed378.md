The plateau holds heat the way a kettle holds steam: not as a surface condition but as a property of the air itself. You feel it in the sinuses first, a wet pressure, then in the lungs. Rain does not fall so much as arrive already warm, already decided. It comes in long, undecided sheets that never quite commit to downpour or mist. Between those sheets the stone weeps. Every face of rock, every vertical plane of old concrete, every length of pipe sweats a continuous film that gathers, loosens, and threads downward without sound.

There are no plants. There have not been plants for longer than the weathering of the structures can measure. The ground is a cracked pavement of sinter and black glass, pale mineral crusts, rust-colored evaporites that form overnight and dissolve by mid-morning. The crusts are the closest thing to a crop. They grow wherever water has stood and then left, thin as lacquer, tasting of metal and old batteries if you were foolish enough to put a tongue to them.

The city occupies the plateau the way a scatter of tools occupies a workbench: deliberately placed, widely spaced, still in use. Low processing halls sit half a kilometer apart, their roofs a dull, heat-softened alloy that never quite dries. Between them run the arteries—sintered-stone viaducts carrying both people and slurry, insulated pipes the color of old bone, occasional rail that clicks once every few minutes when a flatbed of ore or condensate passes. Nothing is close enough to another thing to cast a useful shadow at noon. The light is white and total; it comes from a sun that seems nearer than it should be, or from a sky that has forgotten how to be anything but glare. Wet surfaces throw it back in sheets. You walk with your eyes narrowed to slits and still the world is overexposed, every edge haloed.

The factories do not roar. They pulse. Deep in the halls, something cyclic happens—pistons the size of rooms, or centrifuges, or the slow collapse and re-formation of pressure vessels—but the sound that reaches the open air is only a pressure change, a thickening of the already-thick atmosphere, felt in the inner ear more than heard. Stacks release steam that is indistinguishable from the weather. At night the same stacks give off a low infrared that the fauna can see and you cannot.

The fauna is everywhere the buildings are not, and also inside them. They are not one kind of thing. Closest to the ground move the grazers: low, many-legged, their bodies the same mineral palette as the crusts they rasp away with mouthparts like files. They leave faint, glittering tracks that the next rain erases. Higher, in the perpetual film of water on walls and undersides of viaducts, live the film-walkers—flat, translucent, almost two-dimensional, their internal structures visible as darker branching. They travel by changing the surface tension of the water they inhabit; a building can be covered in them and still look merely wet until one of them decides to cross an open span of air, at which point it becomes a brief, trembling lens. In the brighter hours, flocks of aerial things—more kite than animal, membrane and vein—ride the thermals that rise off the sinter flats. They do not call. They align, separate, realign, their shadows the only moving dark on the blinding ground.

Everything here is in a long conversation with water and heat that has already reached its conclusions. The stone is rounded where it faces the prevailing weather, faceted where it does not. Metal has gone to a stable patina the color of dried blood or verdigris, depending on the alloy; it no longer flakes, only thins. The city’s original purpose—extraction, refinement, the turning of geothermal differential into something portable—continues because the differential has not gone away and because the machines were built to outlast the need to understand them. Maintenance is a kind of weather: crews appear, do what the manuals still require, vanish again into the spaced halls. They wear the same silence as everything else. Speech happens, but it is short and close; anything louder is swallowed by humidity before it travels twenty meters.

At the plateau’s edges the land does not so much drop as step down in long, water-slick benches toward lower country you cannot see for haze. Steam vents mark the steps, some of them old enough that the mineral terraces around them have become architecture in their own right, used now as overflow cisterns or as nesting ground for the larger grazers. The vents themselves are nearly inaudible: a soft, continuous exhalation that you notice only when it stops. When a vent pauses, the film-walkers on nearby surfaces freeze, then reorient, then resume. The grazers lift their heads. The kites above tilt as one. Then the vent resumes and the small adjustment ends. This is as close as the place comes to a clock.

There is a cycle you can set a watch by if you have patience. After the heavier rains the crusts bloom fastest. The grazers concentrate. The film-walkers thicken until whole walls seem to breathe. The factories increase their draw on the slurry lines, because the same minerals the grazers eat are what the centrifuges want. Then the air clears by a few degrees of humidity, the light grows harsher, evaporation accelerates, and for a few hours the plateau is almost dry—only the weeping remains, the slow internal water of the rock itself. In that interval the kites descend and walk, using jointed limbs you had not realized they possessed, and harvest something from the drying films that no one has properly named. Then the next sheet of rain arrives, already warm, and the whole arrangement inhales and begins again.

Nothing here is trying to become a forest or a meadow. The rules are mineral, thermal, hydraulic. Life (the abundant, quiet, unplant-like life) occupies the interfaces: wet/dry, hot/less-hot, dissolved/precipitated. The city is one more interface, older than its current occupants, younger than the plateau, weathering at the same rate as the stone it was poured against. You can walk for an hour between buildings and meet only the tracks of grazers and the occasional lens-body crossing a puddle of sky. The light never relents. The heat never quite becomes pain; it stays at the edge of possible, like the silence. If you stand still long enough you begin to hear the individual drips, each one a small, exact event, and then even those recede, and what is left is the feeling of a place that has already finished becoming what it is and is now only continuing.
