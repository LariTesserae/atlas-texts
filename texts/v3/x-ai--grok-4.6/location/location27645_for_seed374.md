The basin does not drain. That is the first fact the place offers, and it offers it without drama: a wide, shallow concavity in unconsolidated silt and river-cobble, low enough that the air itself seems to pool. The sky is a single ungraduated lid of cloud, the color of wet pewter, and it has been that color for as long as anyone here has language for color. Light arrives already used. It is not night and it is not day. It is the dim that lives between a held breath and a released one.

Water is not a feature. Water is the medium. It does not fall so much as continually arrive — a fine, warm precipitation that never thickens into rain and never thins into mist, a condensing that happens as readily on the inner surface of a hide as on the outer. The ground is saturated a hand’s depth down and remains so. Step and the print fills. Walls weep. Stone sweats. Every vertical plane has a dark skirt of damp a cubit high. There are no true puddles because there is no true dry from which a puddle could be distinguished; there are only shallows and lessershallows, sheets of water no deeper than a finger, and the occasional channel someone has scraped so that the overflow will have a preference.

Nothing green. The word for plant exists here only as a traveler’s rumor, slightly indecent, like a story about a second moon. What lives without blood is simpler and lower: slick films on the undersides of stones, pale thread-growths in the oldest midden seams, a gray bloom that people scrape from the inner faces of their sleeping-hides and feed to the smaller animals. The rest of the visible world is mineral and made and animal. Fresh silt the color of a bruise. Cobbles still wearing the shapes the last flood gave them, though there has been no last flood anyone remembers — only this perpetual arriving of water that never gathers force enough to rearrange anything. The geology looks poured yesterday and then told to wait.

They built anyway. They are still building, in the sense that hands still pack silt and still stack stone, but the city does not grow and does not weather. It occupies the same density it has always occupied. The settlement is a regional thing: you can walk from the first outer rings of low platforms to the crowded inner lifts and it will take you from what would be morning to what would be afternoon if either of those existed, and the character of the place will change by degrees — more bone in the framing here, more stacked slab there, a quarter where the channels have been lined with upright cobbles, a quarter where the platforms are so close their eaves of stretched hide almost touch — but you will not find an edge that feels like an ending. Beyond the last platform the silt simply continues, dotted with the same half-sunk stones, and then, farther, more platforms again, as if the city had been sprinkled rather than founded.

They do not have timber. They have bone, hide, sinew, packed earth, unshaped stone, and the simple metals that can be cold-hammered from the rare nodules the silt sometimes coughs up — a dull copper, a darker lump that takes an edge if you are patient. Tools are hand tools. A maul of river-stone bound to a femur. A needle of splintered rib. A scraper. The buildings are mounds first, always: silt packed in lifts, each lift left to weep until it will bear the next, then faced with cobbles set by eye, not by line. Onto these they raise frames of the long bones of the large wallowers, lashed with gut, and over the frames they pull hides still stiff with their own fat, sewn with sinew, weighted at the hems with stones so the perpetual moisture cannot lift them. Doorways are flaps. Windows are not a custom. Light is not a thing one invites in.

The density is the other fact. People live against people. Platforms share walls. Sleeping-hollows are scraped into the upper lifts so that one family’s roof is another’s floor, and the drip from above is a household sound, accounted for, like a relative. Animals are not kept to the side. They are the side, and the middle, and underfoot. The wallowers — heavy, hairless, the color of the silt itself, built low to the water — lie in the channels with only their nostrils and the ridged shelves of their backs showing; children walk across them. Packs of the small sharp-faced eaters live in the cavities between cobbles and come out at no particular hour because there is no particular hour. Birds the size of fists, slick-feathered, nest in the bone eaves and never stop. Their voices are a single braid of sound that does not resolve into individuals. Amphibians occupy every film of water. Their throats work without rest. Fish, if they are fish, thread the shallows in such numbers that a dipped hand comes up strung with them.

The noise is total. It does not crest. It does not recede. It is a standing roar composed of throat and water and hide slapping in the never-quite-wind and the continuous small percussion of cobbles settling a fraction because the silt beneath them is always a fraction less solid than it was a moment ago, and then a fraction more, and then less again, a vibration too slow to see and too honest to stop. You feel it in your teeth. Speech is mouth-to-ear, or it is the set of hand signs that evolved so that bargains could be struck across a platform without adding more sound to a world that has no more room for sound. Newcomers shout for the first day and then stop. The city has no silent district. Even the farthest rings carry the same pressure of voice and animal and drip, as if the basin were a bowl and the noise a liquid that has found its level.

Nothing here is finished, and nothing is beginning. The platforms look embryonic — raw lifts, stones still wearing river-skin, hides still the shape of the animals they were — and they have looked this way through every generation that has names. A wall that slumps is packed again to the same height. A hide that rots is replaced with a hide of the same cut. Children are born into the dim and the wet and the roar and they die in them, and the count of the living does not tilt. The wallowers calve in the channels and the calves are eaten or they grow to lie where their parents lay. The small eaters take the afterbirth and the fish take what the eaters miss and the birds take the insects that take the rest, and the people take the wallowers and the birds and the fish, and the middens grow a finger’s width and are then used as the core of the next lift, so that the city’s refuse is also its foundation and its foundation is also its refuse, and the height of the platforms remains what it was.

There is a craft of listening for the almost-change. Older people press an ear to the weeping stone and claim they can hear the silt deciding whether to hold. They cannot. The silt has already decided, long ago or never; the decision is the equilibrium. Fires, when they are lit, are small and hooded — fat-lamps of wallower tallow in scoops of clay — and they do not alter the dim so much as confirm it. The flame is a local orange that makes the wet on the nearest cobbles look like metal for a moment, and then the moment passes without having been different from the moment before.

Walk the inner lifts and the body learns a gait: the slight suck of the surface, the sideways step around a sleeping eater, the duck under a hide-eave heavy with condensed water, the pause while a wallower shifts in the channel and the platform under you remembers it is only packed earth. Faces come close. Breath is warm and the same temperature as the air, so that you know a person is speaking more by the movement of their mouth than by any cooling. Jewelry is bone beads and the rare hammered nodules, worn at the throat where they will be seen in the lean-in of talk. Children string the pale cartilages of fish. There are markets that are only widenings in the platforms, where the same things change hands in the same proportions: dried strips of wallower, needles, a better hide, a stone that has taken an edge, a bowl scraped from a shoulder-blade. No one is rich in a way that would show from a distance. Distance itself is a crowded thing.

At the very center — if a basin this even can be said to have a center — the platforms rise a little higher, not from ambition but from the simple accumulation of more generations of packing in a place people preferred. Here the bone frames are older, polished by hands, and the hides have been patched until they are maps of other hides. Here the sound, having less sky to spend itself in, becomes a physical weight. People move through it the way fish move through the shallows: by habit, by the pressure of others, by the knowledge that the way has not changed. There is a cistern that is only a deeper scrape, and the water in it is the same water as everywhere else, and they still come to it, because coming to it is what has always been done.

Beyond even the outer rings, where the platforms thin, the silt continues its unfinished look: ripple-marks that were never ripples, cobbles half-swallowed, the dark shine of standing film. Animals go there too. The city does not end so much as dilute, and then thicken again in the next scatter of lifts, and the roar follows, unperturbed, as if the air had been manufactured at this volume and will not accept a quieter recipe.

You could stay a life. Many do. The warmth never leaves the skin. The dim never lifts enough to make a shadow with an edge. The water never chooses a direction. What would be weather is a single climate with no memory of another. What would be time is the small human stubbornness of packing one more lift, sewing one more seam, lifting one more fish from a channel that will have as many tomorrow. The place is not waiting to become anything. It is the becoming, held. It is the first hour of a city, extended until the hour forgot how to end.
