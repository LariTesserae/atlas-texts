The place sits where the mountain forgets to keep rising. Not a summit—those are sharper, louder with wind—but a settled ledge, a worn saddle of rock that has been a high thing for so long it no longer performs height. The air is thin and cool and faintly wet, as if the stone itself were breathing out last night’s condensation. Light arrives ordinary and unspectacular: a clear, slightly pale daylight that does not flatter or conceal. What it shows has already been shown a thousand times.

There is a town here, if town is the word. It is small enough to be walked end to end before the damp on your clothes has dried. Streets are not generous; they are slots between walls, then they open without warning into courts that feel too large for the number of doors that face them. The built mass is real—stacked, organized, deliberate—but the saturation is low. Emptiness is part of the masonry. You can stand in a plaza of fitted flags and hear your own steps come back from three sides, delayed, as if the stone were considering whether to answer.

The construction is old in the settled way: not ruin, not maintenance, just completion. Walls of pale, coarse stone, dry-laid in the lower courses and bound higher up with a lime that has gone the same color as the rock. Edges have been taken down by weather until nothing is a corner anymore, only a rounding. Thresholds are dished. Lintels sag in the middle by the width of a thumb and have sagged that way since before anyone now living was born. Wooden parts are few and they are the same wood: a dark, close-grained timber brought from somewhere lower, swollen with the damp, never quite dry, never quite rotting. Doors sit slightly proud of their frames. Shutters, where they exist, are closed more often than open, not against weather so much as against the habit of being closed.

Water does not run here so much as visit. A seep in the eastern court keeps a stone basin dark. The basin is older than the walls that enclose it; the walls were built around the fact of it. Sometimes the seep is only a shine. Sometimes, after a night of high cloud that never quite becomes rain, it ticks into the basin with a sound like a slow clock. Gutters—shallow channels cut into the flags—carry that overflow a little way and then lose interest; the water disappears into joints and comes out again as dark patches on the downhill faces of houses, as a coldness you feel through your palm if you rest it on the stone. In the early hours there is dew on every horizontal, and the dew is the closest the place comes to abundance. By midmorning it is gone. The air stays cool.

Vegetation is an afterthought the rock has not entirely rejected. Lichen in greys and a tired yellow occupies the north sides and the undersides of sills. In the wider courts, where wind-blown grit has become a kind of soil, there are tufts of a wiry grass that never greens so much as bronze. One tree—if it is a tree—grows in the angle where two walls meet and the drip from a broken spout has made a private climate. It is no taller than a person. Its leaves are small, stiff, the color of old copper. No one prunes it. No one planted it, as far as the arrangement of things suggests. It is simply the amount of tree the place will allow.

Animals are almost a rumor. A bird will cross the open air above the roofs sometimes, high enough that its species does not matter, only the fact of a moving speck. In the basin, on rare days, something small dimples the surface from below and is gone. At the edge of the ledge, where the town stops being town and becomes slope, there are droppings that might be goat, and a path too narrow for people that switchbacks into the scree and does not come back. The quiet is not the quiet of absence so much as the quiet of a place that has already accounted for every sound it intends to make. Wind in the high streets. The occasional wooden complaint of a door. Water, when it decides to be water. That is the budget.

People are implied more than demonstrated. Smoke, on some mornings, from two or three of the higher chimneys—thin, pale, smelling of the same dark wood as the doors. A shutter opened and left open. A pot set out under the drip of a spout and taken in again. The streets are sized for more bodies than they ever seem to hold. You could invent a history in which the town was fuller, but the stone does not support invention; it supports only what is here. Tools, when you see them, are simple: a wooden rake leaning in a passage, a stone trough, a hand-mill set into a courtyard wall, its hopper empty, its grinding surface worn to a satin that no longer wants grain. There is metal—hinges, a knife-blade left on a sill—but it is the minimum of metal, dark, used, not displayed. Everything that can be done by hand and weight and patience has been done that way for long enough that the method and the result have the same weather on them.

The town does not grow and does not diminish. That is the deepest fact of it. Courses of stone end where they end. Roofs are the same roofs. The basin holds what it holds. If a slate slides in winter, someone puts it back, or the gap becomes another drip, and either outcome is already within the town’s idea of itself. There are no new houses and no fresh scars of demolition. Erosion here is not a process you watch; it is a finished sentence. The ledge has found the shape it means to keep, and the buildings have found theirs inside it, and the damp and the cool and the thin light have found the amount of each that the arrangement will bear. Time passes—the light moves, the seep ticks, the one tree holds its copper leaves or lets a few of them go—but nothing accumulates and nothing is spent. You could leave and return after a long absence and be unsure whether an hour or a generation had gone. The place would not help you decide.

From the western edge, where a low wall is all that stands between the last court and the drop, the rest of the mountain is visible as a succession of similar palenesses, receding. Cloud sometimes sits on those farther ridges and does not come here. Sometimes it does come, and then the town is a set of dark mouths in a soft grey, and the sound of the seep is louder, and the lichen darkens, and that too is not a change so much as one of the few expressions the face is allowed. When the cloud lifts, the ordinary light returns, and the streets are as they were: too wide, too quiet, fitted, damp in the joints, finished.

Stand long enough in the central court and the scale of the place settles over you. It is not a city. It is not a ruin. It is a single built thought, cathedral-small, hamlet-complete, with more stone than need and more space than company, held in a cool damp equilibrium at the height where weather becomes simple. The mountain keeps it. The town keeps itself. Nothing else is required.
