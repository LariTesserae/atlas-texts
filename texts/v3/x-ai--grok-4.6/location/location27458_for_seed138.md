The basin sits no higher than a man's knee above the water table, a shallow, newly torn depression in land that has only just finished arriving. The rock is young in the geological sense: angular, unweathered, still showing the planes along which it split. Fresh sinter and silica crusts grow in thin, brittle shelves over basalt and mudstone; they have not had time to round or stain. Every edge remains sharp enough to cut a boot. Water is everywhere and nowhere quite deep. It weeps from the rock faces in continuous, slow sheets. It stands in irregular pools no larger than rooms. It saturates the ground so that a footstep anywhere except on the raised walkways produces a soft, delayed suck. Steam lifts from the hotter vents in low, unmoving veils that never quite become fog and never quite clear. The air is a single heavy medium—hot, wet, faintly mineral, always condensing on iron, brick, and skin.

The settlement occupies the entire basin and nothing beyond it. There is no hinterland. A low rim of the same young rock closes the place like the wall of a dry dock or a crater, so the scale remains cathedral-sized: one could walk the circuit of the outer walkways in a few minutes. Within that circuit the buildings stand scattered rather than packed. Brick engine-houses, corrugated sheds, a pair of cooling towers no taller than three storeys, a water tower, a short run of covered galleries. Between them lie wide intervals of wet ground, black mud, and standing water. The emptiness is not vacancy so much as interval, the same interval that exists between the notes of a slow machine. Nothing is crowded; everything is placed with the spare logic of a diagram that has already been drawn once and will never be revised.

Light arrives already used. It filters through the steam canopy and the low, unchanging overcast as a dull pewter wash, never bright enough to cast a hard shadow, never dark enough to require lamps except inside the deepest sheds. Surfaces gleam dully—wet brick, wet iron, the slick mineral crusts—rather than shine. Colour is reduced to rust, soot, the grey-green of algae films, and the occasional ochre of iron oxide staining the weep-lines.

The heat does not vary. It is the heat of a body that has been running at the same temperature since it first started. Metal is always too warm to rest a hand on for long. Water that looks still is blood-warm at the surface and hotter at the vents. The atmosphere itself feels close to the dew point of flesh. There is no breeze to move the steam; the air simply hangs, condenses, drips, and is replaced by more of the same.

Sound fills the intervals the buildings leave empty. The dominant note is mechanical and perfectly regular: the slow, heavy pulse of a beam engine, the higher chatter of smaller pumps, the continuous hiss of steam escaping from poorly packed glands, the occasional deep clang when a valve seats. These sounds do not swell or fade; they occupy the same amplitude they have always occupied. Over them, and under them, the water contributes its own unceasing vocabulary—drip, suck, splash, the soft roar of a weir no wider than a doorway. The fauna add the rest. Insects are constant: a dense, low-pitched drone of midges and larger flies that rise in visible clouds from every still surface when something disturbs them, then settle again. Frogs occupy every margin; their overlapping croaks form a second, wetter engine that never misses a stroke. Water-rails and a few grebes move through the reeds that manage to grow in the least scalding shallows; their calls are sharp and repetitive. Rats and lean, short-haired dogs move along the drier walkways with the unhurried ownership of creatures that have never known a different arrangement. Nothing here is rare. Abundance is simply the default density of life that the heat and the water and the refuse of the works can support.

Vegetation remains incidental. Algae paint the wetter brick and the cooler pools in thin, slippery films. A few clumps of reed and a single species of tough, salt-tolerant rush occupy the least industrial corners. Nothing taller, nothing that would suggest succession or season. The plants exist the way rust exists: as a surface condition that has already reached its final extent.

The works themselves are industrial in the older sense—steam, iron, brick, belts, and the smell of hot oil and wet coal. Nothing looks new and nothing looks abandoned. Pistons rise and fall through the same travel they have always used. Belts slap the same drums. Water is lifted, used, and returned in closed circuits that have no surplus and no deficit. The entire settlement functions as a single, self-balancing machine whose output is simply its own continued operation. There are no seasons to interrupt it, no nights that truly fall, no mornings that truly begin. The young rock does not weather further. The water level does not drop or rise. The animals do not increase or diminish. The steam never lifts. Time here is not a river but a standing wave, perfectly reflected, perfectly contained by the rim of the basin.

One can walk the raised boardwalks and the short iron bridges and feel the place as a completed sentence. The heat presses equally from every direction. The sounds interlock without remainder. The water continues its slow, inexhaustible weeping. Everything that can happen has already reached the rate at which it will continue to happen, and that rate is the only climate the basin will ever know.
