The bowl keeps its people the way it keeps its heat: close, constant, and almost unused.

They are few enough that the city still feels empty when they move through it. You see them as interruptions in the glare—figures the same tarnished-brass, tea-stain, beetle-underwing color as the leaves they walk among, wraps wound tight against light that has weight. The cloth is plant fiber, beaten and layered until it holds a dull sheen; it does not hide them so much as finish the camouflage the forest already began. A bundle of cut stems over one shoulder, a stone blade at the hip, copper flashing thin as a thought where it has been hammered over some ancient cobble. They cross the high bridges on the off-beats of the wind, soles reading the quieter nodes the way other feet once read pavement. They recarve a step whose edge has forgotten its purpose. They restring a baffle whose cords have gone slack. They prune a sounding-chamber whose slit has drifted out of tune. The work is maintenance that has become liturgy. Nothing is ever finished because the wind and the dry forest and the stone keep rewriting the instrument, and the people answer by rewriting it back.

Speech is not gone; it has simply withdrawn. In the deepest rooms—those few interiors that face away from the noon erasure, brown-gold dusk where walls can be walls again—mouths still move. The words are short, private, almost ceremonial, as if sound itself were a scarce mineral they refuse to spend in the open air. Outside, language is hands: a grammar of fingers and wrists developed because the roar will not carry anything as fragile as a sentence. Children (there are still children, though they appear like the moths—briefly, at the edge of dusk, then gone) learn the signs before they learn to waste breath. A child can tell you the day’s wind-cycle, the next terrace that needs recutting, the cistern that clicked last night, without ever opening its mouth to the bowl.

They do not look for water. The word itself has become a kind of private weather they carry only in those deepest rooms, unused in ordinary hours. A spilled drop blackens a stem as if the plant had been burned; the stone drinks nothing and offers nothing. Their bodies have made their own dry accommodations. They drink the metallic taste the rock sweats at peak heat, the faint mineral film the leaves exude when they turn their pale backs to the sun. It is enough. They wrap their eyes at noon and work by the feel of edges. They move through light that has become a substance, a pressure, a second architecture.

The plants live more thoroughly than the people. They occupy every joint, every neglected terrace, every hollow the masons left and every hollow the masons never imagined. They are not decoration; they are the other population, the one that never thins. Rosettes, fans, hanging curtains of paper-thin leaves the color of old brass and leftover tea. Hollow, fluted, segmented stems that grew as instruments before anyone thought to blow through them. Roots that thread stone for mineral and purchase and that faint metallic sweat, never for moisture that does not exist. They drink light and warmth the way wetter forests drink rain. At noon they turn their pale undersides upward and the entire bowl becomes a second sun, intolerably bright, edges erased. You navigate by touch and by the memory of steps. The forest is lush the way a crowded attic is lush: every volume claimed by surface. It roofs terraces, fills courtyards, intercalates with masonry until a tower is also a trunk and a root-mass is also a foundation. No one bothers to say which anymore. The plants are the city’s lungs and its reeds and its memory of itself. Generations of pruning have tuned their sounding-chambers; the people cut stems to length the way other towns once cut timber, not for building but for pitch.

Animals remain a rumor with occasional, unprovable proof. In the deepest unused cistern—a well of actual shadow in a place that otherwise refuses shade—something small and jointed lives. It clicks, perhaps. The click is hard to certify against the city’s weather. Pale moths, or moth-shaped seeds, lift from the highest fans at dusk and vanish before the eye can decide. After the rarest nights, when the wind drops just enough for dust to hold a mark, tracks appear the size of a child’s hand and lead nowhere a person would go. That is the census. Nothing larger declares itself. Nothing needs to. The bowl has already decided what scale of life it will permit.

The city itself lives in the only way a place this old and this finished can live: by continuing to happen. Architecture and forest have weathered into one another so completely that repairs are indistinguishable from originals, edges softened into something like bone, a tower both built and grown. The wind is not weather; it is the city’s breath and its voice and its blood. Morning tearing, midmorning chord thick enough to lean against, afternoon when heat opens every slit-valve and the bowl becomes a single instrument playing itself, night’s lower grinding and the tens of thousands of cooling ticks like a fire that will not admit it has nothing left to burn. The remaining people do not fight this. They keep it. They recut the flutes when erosion dulls them. They accept that sealed rooms only make louder throats. They know the lip is climbable in an hour and that the silence beyond it is the wrong size—an unshaped quiet in which a person can hear their own blood and mistake it for the city calling them home. Most who leave come back before dark. A few do not. The forest grows through their stoops. The wind learns a new note in the gap.

You could live here a year and never fill a room. You could also never find a surface that had not already been touched, planted, carved, or deliberately left empty. The people, the plants, the rumor-animals, the stone, the wind: they occupy the same finished-and-still-happening tense. They are not waiting for anything. They are the bowl’s way of remaining itself. In the plaza at evening the flagstones tick as they cool—a sound too small to exist, and yet, in a trough of the roar, audible, like a clock the ground invented for no one in particular, and for everyone who has learned to listen with the soles of the feet.
