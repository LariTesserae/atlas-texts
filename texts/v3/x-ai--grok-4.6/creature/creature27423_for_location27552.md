The ones who belong to the hollow are few, and they have the look of something that grew into its socket rather than something that chose a dwelling. Two dozen at most on any count of the long rotation, plus the small constellations of kin that occupy the thicker hedges. They are not a village and not a crew in the ordinary sense; they are the hollow’s own circulation, the part of it that walks and cuts and dresses the belts.

Their bodies keep the record. Skin takes the peat-stain early and never quite lets it go; the creases of knuckles and the hollows under the eyes hold a green-brown that the upper weather only fades, never erases. Palms are permanently a little swollen, always warm, mapped with the white seams of old nicks from hook-knives and the finer splinters of living bamboo. Hair stays lank. It is never fully dry. They wear what the hollow itself supplies—coarse cloth beaten from the same bast that goes to the presses, leaf-wrapped and resewn until the seams are thicker than the original weave—and the cloth darkens in the same gradient as the stone, black-green at the hems where it drags the wet mat, paler only at the throat. Shoes are rare. Most go in split-reed sandals that last a week and then compost underfoot. A few of the older ones have gone bare for so long the soles have thickened into something like the rhizome itself, springy, indifferent to the constant film of water.

They move as the architecture requires: a habitual half-crouch that is not fatigue but courtesy to the vaults, a way of passing through the willow corridors without snagging the bindings. A hook-knife rides in every belt or hangs from a living spur of wood; they prune while they walk the way another people might fidget or smoke. Cuttings are pocketed without thought and pressed into any fresh wound in the peat. Most take. Children learn the stroke before they learn letters. The letters themselves arrive already soft, on newspapers that have begun to pulp in the hoist-shaft, and are read by lamplight the color of steeped leaf, then used to patch a leaking joint or line a niche.

Speech is close work. The beam never hurries and never stops; conversation happens cheek to cheek, mouths almost at the ear, words shaped more than spoken. Voices stay low even in the relative hush of the packing floor. Laughter, when it comes, is a short exhalation that does not try to rise above the slap of the wet belt. They have no animals to startle, no birds to answer. The absence is so complete it has become a kind of silence inside the noise, a knowledge that nothing else is listening. Infants are slung in dry reed that does not stay dry; they sleep to the thud of the engine the way other children sleep to rain. Fever is ordinary. The heat sits in the lungs like a second pulse. Those born here rarely lose the slight rasp, even after a stretch in the upper air.

Families occupy volumes that would be a single room elsewhere. A niche is pruned, lined, slept in, and pruned again when the walls leaf toward the bedding in the night. A couple and two children share a cavity whose living sides must be cut back from the mattress every few days; the cut material is fed to the presses or simply left to rot back into the floor. Privacy is a habit of turning one’s back, not of walls. Tools hang from hooks that are still growing. The water-main, that split and rebound liana, is checked the way another household checks a roof. When it weeps too freely someone grafts, someone waits, someone patches with clay that never fully dries. Repair is horticulture. Everything that fails is invited to grow around the failure.

They rotate on a long count—weeks in the hollow, a stretch out where weather still happens—but the ones who stay longest acquire a stillness that is not resignation. They stand in the center aisle with eyes closed and the whole millrace of sound becomes a single warmth: water finding limestone, iron complaining, leaf against leaf with no wind to move it, the generator’s low animal groan. Nothing here is trying to become anything else, and after enough seasons neither are they. The upper world is “the weather.” Supplies come down—coal, a new belt, salt, needle and thread, the already-soft paper. Goods go up. No one from outside remains. The air is too settled, the light too unwilling. Those who belong keep the vaults bound and the firebox fed and the cuttings pressed home. They do not speak of owning the hollow. The hollow occupies them, the way the plants occupy every interval, the way the stone weeps without ever quite becoming a fall. Evening is only a further dimming. The lamps are lit from the last green rumor of day. The engine is not banked. In the niches the walls tick as cells swell. This is the whole of them, and it has been the whole of them for a long time.
