They are not arrivals. They have the look of something the notch itself extruded in the same motion that raised its walls, a living remainder that learned, very slowly, how to stay.

At first the eye refuses them as animals. The notch is already a place of blades and fresh flakes; the pale overlapping that coats every surface simply reads as another texture the mountain has not yet decided to shed. Only when the rare brighter gray leaks in do the ridges declare themselves as shells, each one a clenched, gray fist the exact color of the stone it covers. They range from the size of a closed human hand to something nearer a heart, and in the oldest stretches they have ceased to be countable at all. There the edges have fused. What began as neighbors has become a single thickening mat whose surface still shows the ghosts of individuals—ridges that no longer quite match, seams that have been mortared over by later growth.

Each shell is slightly translucent. In that dim hour you can watch the dark fluid move underneath, not circulating so much as deciding, a slow internal tide the color of wet slate or old blood that has forgotten red. The fluid never hurries. It has nowhere else to be. The animals cling with a perfect circle of wet, muscular feet, a living gasket that leaves a faint glistening film the rime later claims as its own. To free one is to hear a sound like a plate lifted from still water; the underside comes away bearing a perfect negative of the crystal face it occupied, every facet and fracture line reproduced in living tissue as though the creature had been trying, for years, to become a more accurate version of the mountain.

They breathe through slits that open and close in no shared rhythm. A thousand small mouths, each taking the thin, already-used air in its own time. The sound they make is the notch’s true climate: a stacked, unending drone of wet inhalations, minute clicks, the rasp of shell adjusting against shell. It does not rise or fall. It has been this loud since the available stone was filled, which, to the stone, was yesterday or the day before. Newcomers—smaller, paler, their ridges still sharp—settle into the gaps the way grit settles into a wound. The dead do not travel. They are simply incorporated, their empty shells becoming the next layer of architecture. Every few seasons the mats thicken by exactly the width of one life.

In the densest patches the weirdness stops being individual and becomes structural. Hairline bridges of living tissue now connect neighbor to neighbor. The dark fluid crosses them. A pulse that begins at one dripping seam can travel a meter, then two, a dim wave visible beneath the overlapping shells. On the coldest days the entire south wall will fog-breathe as one, a single slow lung exhaling into the dusk. No one has remained long enough to learn whether this is thought or only shared plumbing. Their dark pits—eyes, perhaps, or only the places where eyes would be if eyes were needed—face inward, toward the stone. They do not look up when a dusk-colored bird hangs screaming in the updraft and is gone. They do not look at the pale, segmented worms that work the wetter seams beneath them, tasting mineral and whatever finer waste the colony discards. They eat what the height provides: the indecisive water that weeps down the same grooves every thaw, the moth that should never have climbed this far, the dust, the seed that will never find soil, and, when one of their number stills, one another. There is nothing else.

The lesser lives persist in the margins the colony has not yet claimed or cannot be bothered to claim. Black lichen beards the slickest cracks like a second, slower shadow. Cushions of moss the color of forgotten bruises hold a film of water in hollows no larger than a cupped palm. One wiry shrub, no taller than a knuckle, has kept a single leaf; the wind uses it as a clapper. Tick. That is the forest. The worms are the only other motion that belongs: blind, glistening, they thread the fractures the mountain opens and then ignores, perhaps cleaning, perhaps simply existing at a scale the shells cannot be troubled to notice.

Around the five parallel grooves cut into one fresh face, and around the gray, lichen-furred splinter of wood wedged in a weeping crack, the colony has grown but not covered. The space remains. It may be indifference. It may be the only courtesy the notch has ever shown. Nothing else of hands survives. The living stone has simply continued.

If you stay until your own breath falls into their drone, the distinction between inhabitant and place begins to dissolve. The notch is no longer a cut you entered; it is a throat, cool and unfinished, speaking with a thousand small mouths in a language that has no word for elsewhere. The shells drink. The pulses travel. The rime feathers and weeps in the pattern it has always used. Change here is only the thickness of another shell, the slow creep of a mat toward a better drip, the mountain lifting by amounts too small to see. They are the skin that will not be shed, the second geology that breathes and sings and has no need of a name. Whatever they are becoming, they have already become the only thing that lives here as if it were home.
