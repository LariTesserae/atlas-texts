The primary citizens of the basin are vegetal, and they have made the water their architecture rather than their medium. They do not wait along the margins or decorate a floor. They occupy. They treat depth as a continuum of soil, light as a currency to be intercepted and passed downward in diminishing coins, and time as something that can be spent in accumulation rather than in motion.

The column-risers stand first in any accounting. From drowned benches and hanging shelves they lift pale, ribbed trunks that stop a few meters short of air, each trunk a slow argument with gravity and with the water’s own slight insistence. Their surfaces are not bark in any ordinary sense; many precipitate a mineral film along the ribs, a pale armor that thickens while they live, so that living wood and incipient stone become the same object. Crowns open into plates and fans, shallow bowls that hold the last light and leak a green dusk into the rooms beneath. These forests do not compete so much as tile the volume. Between them the finer growths hang: hair-curtains of endlessly dividing filaments so dense a hand meets unanimous resistance at once, a soft pressure that is neither current nor wall. They part only when something larger drifts through, then close again over weeks. Dead matter does not fall; it lodges, becoming the next holdfast, the next substrate. Roots and rhizoids braid through one another and through the young fractures of the rock, turning the basin’s unfinished geology into a single, saturated fabric.

Near the surface the mats lock together into something walkable if you accept that the ground gives and that your legs come away wet to the knee. Small-leaved, interwoven, they form prairies or the taut skin of a fruit, copper-cast at dusk for reasons the light itself does not fully explain. In the first hour of morning they release clouds of spores so fine they read as a second, interior weather, a faint turbidity that is not dirt. Beneath them, on every horizontal, other layers persist: mosses that have unlearned air, blotched lichens the size of doors, gelatinous sheets that pulse on a schedule too slow to notice unless you look away and look back. In certain coves the water itself tastes faintly of cut stems and wet pollen. Flowers—if that is what they are—open dull white and shut again on a rhythm longer than a day, never many at once, never theatrical.

The microscopic democracies live on everything: films, spores, a continuous faint thickening of the water between bodies. They are the reason the flood is rarely empty even in its clearer lanes. Change happens on their terms and the basin’s: a mat tears along a hidden seam and drifts, over weeks, into a new configuration; hair-curtains grow toward one another across a temporary street of light; a fresh scarp, pale wound still showing, begins to accept the first holdfasts. Carbon simply accumulates, layer on layer. Nothing hurries to become a different place.

Animals are almost an afterthought, and they behave as if they know it. A shrimp-length shape the color of silt moves as though movement were expensive, pale and infrequent. Once, perhaps, a darker slow form against a far wall—fish or shed leaf, the distinction unhurried. Insects remain a rumor at the air-water skin and then not even that. Birds do not come. There is no startle in the ecology, no mouth that interrupts the patience. Sound is reduced to the tick of a bubble finding the surface long after it detached, the almost inaudible slide of one mat against another, drip from an island overhang, and, if you listen, your own blood.

The islands themselves, raw at their spines, carry only a crust of sun-tolerant moss before the vegetation resumes with the first splash. No one has built, cut, or marked. To move through is to become another slow object among slow objects. The inhabitants do not notice you except as a temporary rearrangement of their densities. After you have gone they will add another film, another grain of silt to the unhurried dark, the water remaining what they have decided it is: not a covering, but the land’s present tense, and they its only lasting tense.
