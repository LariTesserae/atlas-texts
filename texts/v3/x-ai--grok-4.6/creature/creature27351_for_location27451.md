It begins in pieces, the way the holding itself was laid: a life assembled from what the wet would keep and what the dark would hide. At first there is only the busy republic of the shallows—the newts that walk the drowned flags as if they were still dry, their skins the exact grey-green of old pug, their toes splayed like the last memory of hands. The fry stitch and unstitch the light that never quite arrives. Midges rise in those trembling columns and the columns have weather; they lean, they rain themselves back down. None of this is yet a who. It is only occupancy, the density the walls allowed.

Then the frogs take the ledges. They are the first that feel like intention. Clay-bodied, they wait in the exact colour of the joints until a mouth opens and the colour of meat appears, brief and shocking, as if the holding itself had decided to speak. Their voices are the ceramic clatter of a kiln cooling; they keep no night because the place has none, only thicker and thinner air. They work in relays. When one drops from a lip the sound is the old packed thud of pug being driven home, and for a moment the walls remember being made.

Something crabbed lives inside the drowned weir, sideways, clicking against the lashed poles as though testing whether the old duty still holds. It is never fully seen. You know it by the way the white apron of water sometimes stutters, by the silk it winds into the remaining lashings until the silk is stronger than the original cord. The bats (or whatever still wears that rumour of wings) sew the lattice overhead; their flight is a dry percussion under the wet one, low enough that the air of them brushes a cheek, high enough that they remain uncounted. They drink the indoor rain as it manufactures itself.

Deeper, the eels hang. They do not swim so much as they occupy the correct sequence, a script left in the water after the language that wrote it has gone. They are the first true continuity: long, dark, remembered rather than moving. You feel them as a single slow thought that has been thinking the same thing since the basins were cut.

And then the rectangle. The three steps that go down and do not return. Here the heavier thing lives.

It is not one shape for a long time. It is first a roll that arrives too late at the ankles, a pause that the shallows obey by emptying themselves in a single coordinated flinch you feel through the flag more than you see. Afterward the instrument of drips is briefly exposed, naked, and the city of small lives must rebuild from the joints outward, ticking, industrious, unsurprised. The thing is a rumour that occasionally consents to become a fact. When it does, the water climbs a finger’s width; films reverse themselves on the glazed tracks, arrive at a ledge, and pretend they were always puddles.

What emerges, if you stay until your eyes have finished their work and the emptiness has become the point, is long. Longer than the basin that holds it. Pale in the way things become pale when they have not needed colour for a very long time: the colour of wet slate, of bone left in a stream, of the underside of a leaf that never saw the sun it still remembers. It is muscled like a current given skin. Fins, if they are fins, are worn down to suggestions, ridges that once steered and now only remember steering. The head is blunt, almost kindly, the mouth a clean dark line that opens rarely and, when it does, shows nothing you could call teeth so much as the idea of a gate. Eyes are present but unused; they have the clouded patience of stones that have been underwater since they were placed. It breathes through the entire length of itself. The indoor rain is its weather. The circulation of the holding—the weep becoming rain becoming seep becoming the dark throat that returns the water to the walls—is its blood.

It has no name because the makers stepped back far enough that stepping back became leaving, and nothing that came after needed to call it. It is not the last of them. It is what the work became when the work no longer required hands: a patience that learned to hunger. It feeds when the harvest is thick—midges, frogs, the occasional sideways tenant of the weir—taking them the way the weir still takes its one duty, without drama, because the lip was placed true. Between feedings it hangs in the rectangle the way the eels hang, being remembered in the correct order. Sometimes it rises along a vertical face, a film of itself, and the water appears to climb; it leaves no new stain. Sometimes the drips fall in phrases and the frogs answer in the same meter and the whole envelope, for a little while, keeps time for a work-gang that is not there. That is the closest it comes to speech.

It does not maintain the holding. It simply occupies the hollow the way a throat occupies a mouth, and because it is abundant in its single, slow way, the made places that still work are leaned upon until leaning becomes a kind of repair. The moss meadow thickens because the seam weeps; the seam weeps because the thing in the dark returns the water; the water is returned because the thing is there, breathing the only engine the place has left. The stones, in certain weathers, smell like palms. That is its oldest scent, or the holding’s, or both; there is no longer a difference worth making.

It is not waiting. Waiting would imply an arrival that has not already happened. It is simply the shape the wet wore until the shape became a fact, the same way the walls decided their inward lean. Cool. Dim. Complete. You could walk every dry line in a hundred breaths and never meet it, only feel the roll arrive too late, only hear the instrument of drips exposed and then re-clothed. It lives there the way the pug lives in the joints: by having already been packed well enough, and by remaining. The air condenses and condenses, writing the same short word on every stone. The word is the same word the thing itself is: hold.
