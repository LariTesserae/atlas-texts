They live in the wet the way the wet lives in the rock: without apology, without quite finishing. You meet them first as pressure and then as faces, the way the chamber itself arrives—heat, then thickness, then the late assembly of features in the low orange smear. They are not pale exactly. The furnace-throats have given them a slow coppering along the cheekbones and the inner wrists, a mineral flush that never quite rinses out, so that even the youngest carry a faint tea-stain under the skin, as if they have been steeped. Their hair stays heavy. It is cut short or bound back with the same wet fiber they harvest, but it never lifts; it lies against napes and temples in dark ropes that shine when they turn. Eyes take a long time to find you. When they do they are the color of the lagging itself, the greened iron, the weak-tea glass.

They move as if the air has weight, which it does. Shoulders turn sideways in the alleys without thinking. A hand lifts a leaf-curtain and does not let it slap back. They walk the gratings the way other people walk streets, feeling for the places where the plate has gone soft, where a drip-line has been slung, where a vine has decided overnight to cross at shin height. Contact is constant and therefore unnoticed: a damp sleeve against a damp sleeve, the furred moss of a rail under the palm, the sudden cool of a pipe that has not yet been lagged. They do not startle. The oldest rule lives in their bodies now, older than speech. A sudden step, a raised voice, a stamp that is not the permitted one—these things are remembered in the tendons, so that even the children, who have never seen the rock open a new hand’s-breadth, still flinch inward when a pot lid drops.

They are many and they are not. Density is civic, yes, but it is also cellular. Families occupy lofts that share ceilings of trained vine; two households may sleep with only a curtain of pale leaves between them and will pretend, for years, not to hear the other’s breathing. Doorways open onto doorways. A cistern’s belly is someone’s sky. They have forgotten, if they ever knew, the luxury of an unused interval. Laundry hangs forever in warm flags, taken down only when it is needed again, never dry, only emptied of the day’s grit. They cook with the same patience as the turbines: kettles set on lagged throats, fiber-root mashed in the slow heat, a dark sweet tar used sparingly as both seasoning and lamp-oil. Meals are eaten close. Voices stay in the room that made them. A laugh, on the rare days one happens, is taken immediately by the leaves and the wet iron and does not travel.

They work the way the chamber works—drawing, condensing, waiting. The fiber harvest is not a date. When the thin leaves of the ivory-trunked trees begin to show the furnace-glow as a map of veins too clearly, when they hang a certain way, the walkers go out with bone knives and slings. They lift the broad pale curtains aside, cut what the plant offers, and the plant does not seem to mind; it is fever-lush, already replacing itself. The fiber comes away wet and green-brown, smelling of boiled rope. They boil it again. They twist it. They lag pipes with it, weave the slings, bind hair, patch the shuttered windows that keep out a brightness no one has seen. In the lower works the turbines turn and the belts creep and once, perhaps twice, in an afternoon the stamp reports its single felt concussion. They take the mineral flush and the tar. They mix mortar with geological manners and lay brick only where the young rock has, at last, offered a ledge. A new landing appears against a wall that was blank last year. No one comments on it as growth. It is simply that the chamber consented, and they kept pace.

Children exist in the intervals the adults have not yet claimed. They are told about the pipe-eel the way other children are told about wolves, but the telling has no heat in it. The eels, if they exist, have never taken more than a sock, and even that is half legend, half laundry mishap. The children themselves are quieter than the drip. They play games of stillness: who can stand longest beneath an unpatched seam without flinching when the drop hits the crown. They learn the condensation cycle before they learn numbers. They know which troughs have begun to sprout and which vines will hold a small weight. They age into the copper stain. They learn to keep their voices inside the leaves.

There are other lives, thinner, less certain. Once in a great while a pale undulation the length of a finger moves through an overflow and is gone, a rumor with a body. Cricket-song, or something that has decided to use the same intervals, happens inside the walls at spans too long to memorize. The plants are not other. They are the third architecture, the fever that filled every human interval of air and then kept filling. The ferns pack the cool sides of pipes in wet ruffs that must be parted like hair. The vines have trained themselves after they were trained, knitting loft to loft until two families share a ceiling and do not speak of it. The fiber-trees stand in their tubs, heat-drunk, trunks the color of old ivory, leaves so thin the light maps them. Nothing here has ever met a wind. Their green is the green of things that grew up already steaming.

And the rock lives. That is the strangeness that does not announce itself and then does. Millimeters in a year, a hairline in a decade, the vault continues to finish being born. Fresh shine appears along old cracks like a thought someone had in the night. They do not startle it. They lag it, they speak small to it, they let the stamp report only when the alternative is a pressure no one wishes to store. They build to keep pace with an opening that refuses to be finished. In that they are like the plants, like the condensation that chooses, eventually, to fall, like the painted bolt-head that travels the width of a thumb while a kettle boils. Time is not weather. Time is the cycle of heavier drip after the channel surges, the leaf that tells them it is ready, the almost imperceptible brightening of the fracture-planes when somewhere far above, in a dryness no one present has seen, a season of sun conducts a rumor downward. They age inside that rumor. Their faces, when they stand at the far end before the unlit lamp and the dark glass, look as if they have been waiting in the stone, damp, and are only now, reluctantly, coming out.

They are the Hollow. The chamber occupies them as much as they occupy it. There is almost no empty air, and they have made of that a kind of courtesy.
