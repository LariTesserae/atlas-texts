The first lives have no edges. They are the felt itself, a single patient body that has been thickening since the last ridges forgot they were mountains. What looks like ground is a knit of root and thallus and the compressed dead of everything that has ever fallen here, still faintly warm, still drinking. It beads at dawn and swallows its own sweat by the time the sun is high. It remembers pressure the way muscle does. A footstep is only a dimple that will be evened out before the next hatch. Rain is taken in and held in those black, room-sized mirrors whose rims are the same living tissue, so that the pools are less containers than organs. Nothing here is separate from the floor for long. The felt is always eating, always becoming floor again.

Inside it the second lives move as tides. They are segmented, some armored in overlapping plates the color of old resin, some soft and pale as the undersides of leaves that never see sun. They surface when the moisture or the vegetable heat or some signal from the distant titans tells them the hour has come, and a stretch of damp green that was only itself at noon becomes a rippling pelt by late afternoon, clicking, rustling, the sound of too many small mouths working. They feed, they mate in the brief wet dark, they sink. The mat closes over them. You can walk a day and never see one except as a sudden change in the color and texture of the world underfoot, a living weather that has weight.

Above, the air is never empty. The third lives are the ones that have weight in aggregate, continents of midges and nameless beetles and glittering motes that dim the clean high sun the way thinner cloud would. They have pulse. They move with the wind or against it or in great slow gyres that last days, and the birds (if they are still birds) work those currents the way fish work a river, long-winged, scissoring through the grain, rarely landing except at the dark pools or along the veins of a titan leaf. The air is a constant slight collision against skin, mouth, eyes. It is a second atmosphere, and it is never still. At the hatches the density becomes a kind of night that travels. Then the sun returns, ordinary, interrupted only by the next dark vertical weather of a trunk on the horizon.

Larger things come to the pools at dusk, fat-bodied, slow, their sides gleaming as they drink. They are the ones harvested: netted or speared in the last light, their richness dried in the brief hard sun and stored against the leaner weeks. Still larger things move through the mat itself, unseen except as a change in the lower register of the roar, a grinding that travels like weather under the feet. They browse or they wait. Their passage leaves temporary hollows that the felt slowly fills. When a titan sheds, all of them come. A leaf the size of a hillside lowers for a day and then meets the floor with a boom that is felt in the jaw. For a year there is a new country of rotting green, deafening, sweet, a temporary city of feeders. Then the mat takes it back. The emptiness resumes, which is to say the fullness that has no objects in it.

The titans themselves are lives so old they have become architecture. Hollow, ribbed, exhaling sweet heat, their bark a weathered outer climate of lichen and hardened resin. Things live in them: in the seams, in the leaf-axils where the exudate collects after rain, in the high umbels that cast twilight over a valley’s worth of felt. The trunks play when the wind finds them, adding the thin screaming edge to the chord. They are destinations. You walk toward one until evening and still do not arrive. They shed. They stand. They are the only verticals the plateau has permitted itself to keep.

And then the ones who walk with hands.

They are few, and they do not argue with any of it. They came up, once, from a lower world whose metal they still sometimes carry as a beaten ornament they have stopped needing. They stay. They learn the patience of the place, which is the patience of something that has already finished becoming what it is. Their structures are slight and already being overgrown: platforms of titan-fiber lashed into the lee of a trunk, black with old smoke, swaying with the slow vegetable breath of the wood; ladders of trained vine; low windbreaks of stacked chitin and felt-brick that last a season before the living ground begins to take them. Paths close. They do not build walls. There is nothing to keep out that is not already inside the sound, the air, the give of the floor.

They stuff their ears with the pale resin-wool that weeps from certain seams, yet the chord arrives anyway, through bone, through the soles. They have learned to hear with the body. A change in key means a hatch, a tide, a shedding, a shift in the continents of midges. They harvest the sweet sap after rain. They take the fat-bodied drinkers at dusk. They dry what can be dried. They move when they must. Their tools stay hand-scale: flaked stone, bone awls, cordage. Their garments, such as they are, are felt and chitin and the protective smear of resins. Their skin takes the stain. Their gait has learned not to fight the hide they walk on.

They are mostly silent, not from austerity but because the air is already full. When they speak it is close, mouth to ear, or they add a low note to the existing pressure and let it be carried. Children born here (there are children, sometimes) have never known a world without the held chord. For them the vibration is as ordinary as pulse. They do not call the plateau high. Height is only a quality of light, of distance, of clouds that are not allowed up. Some of them walk, once, to the rim, days out, to the place where the living ground forgets how to be flat and begins its long worn decline. They stand in ordinary afternoon, warm wind coming up out of the white gulf, the roar suddenly given a direction, and look down onto the tops of clouds. Then they turn back. The surface has no further ambition except to remain. They have become, almost, another rarity the plateau permits: as spaced as the titans, as temporary as a path, another kind of weather that learned, slowly, how to stay. 

All of it is the weather. The vacancy is only unoccupied by mass. What lives here is the fullness that does not need to announce itself.
