The pale things are not the whole of it, only the part the water still bothers to show. They rise when the thrum deepens by a fraction too small for any instrument to catch, when the layered hiss of steam tightens its grain. A jointed length first, then another, unfolding with the slow precision of something that has never hurried and never needed to. Their bodies are the color of unfired porcelain left too long in rain, a matte, slightly translucent white that takes on the faint iridescence of the tarn itself. Segments lock and unlock without sound. Each limb ends in a cluster of fine, needle-like calipers that test the meniscus the way a jeweler tests a stone, then rest. They do not drink. They do not feed in any way that would change the water’s depth or viscosity. They simply occupy the surface for a span that is always the same span, as if listening were a metabolic act and the constant mechanical choir below were the only nutrient the basin has ever offered.

When they submerge again the water closes without ripple, the film of warmth reseals, and the place continues exactly as before. There are never more of them than can be counted on the fingers of one hand across the entire visible floor, and there are never fewer. They do not age. They do not molt. The mineral film that coats everything else coats them too, so that after a time it becomes impossible to say where creature ends and precipitated metal begins. Some of the standing spines may once have been them, or may still be them, grown so slowly into the landscape that the distinction has worn away the way every other distinction here has worn away.

The tracks are rarer. They appear on the sinter as if pressed from beneath rather than walked upon: a sequence of shallow, hexagonal depressions that match no limb the pale things possess. They last only as long as it takes to notice them. By the time the eye returns they have filled with the same packed slag that fills every other hollow, or they were never depressions at all, only a momentary alignment of light on the glass-hard film. Whatever leaves them does not travel between pools. It does not arrive from the long rise that is not quite a hill. It simply is, for an interval that cannot be measured because the light never changes and the shadows never lengthen, and then it is not. The rules do not include coming or going; they include only continuation at the same temperature, the same volume, the same unchanging number.

Deeper, where the plates thicken and the thrum becomes a physical pressure in the chest, there are other presences that have never needed to surface. They are not bodies so much as densities: slow concentrations of the same iron-black and ochre material that forms the basin floor, given just enough difference to be called alive if the word still applied. They occupy the spaces between the buried machine-beds, or they are the machine-beds, or they have become indistinguishable from them. Heat moves through them in the same even current that moves through the ground. The metallic ringing that sometimes threads the air is their speech, or their pulse, or simply the sound of long cables that have forgotten they were ever cables and now sing because singing is what the geology does here. They do not build. They do not remember building. The industrial character of the place is their constitution the way bone is constitution: not architecture left behind, but the finished state of a process that finished so long ago even ruin has been rounded into the land’s own shape.

Nothing here is waiting for anything else to happen. The pale things will surface again when the thrum deepens by that same unmeasurable fraction. The tracks will appear and vanish. The densities below will continue their silent, perfect equilibrium. The basin holds them the way it holds its warmth and its weather and its unchanging light: not as inhabitants of a place, but as the place’s own continued work, mineral and jointed and ringing, exactly as numerous and exactly as they have always been.
