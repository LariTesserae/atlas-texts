The hollow holds its lives the way a cupped palm holds water that has not yet decided whether to stay. They arrived in overlapping waves, none of them old enough to remember another home as clearly as they remember the moment they first felt the warmth rising through the new stone.

The smallest came first and never left. Frogs the exact grey-green of wet limestone occupy every persistent film of moisture, their throats pulsing in time with the slow settling of the walls. They are so numerous that the ledges seem to breathe. Between them move beetles whose wing-cases still carry the polish of unused chitin; they tick against cane and leaf and vanish into hairline cracks that were not there the night before. Lizards claim the warmest facets, flattening themselves until they become another plane of glittering crystal, only the frantic beat of a throat betraying them. Insects stitch the dim air in short, bright sentences and then dissolve. At dusk the whole bowl fills with a low, continuous rustle that is more texture than sound—the dry collision of carapace on stone, the liquid click of a frog adjusting its grip, the almost inaudible sigh of a new crack opening somewhere high on the wall. Nothing here has learned to be loud. The stone drinks echoes before they can form.

Birds treat the hollow as a temporary room. They drop in from the rim in near silence, their wings making the sound of a page being turned in another chamber, take what they need (a seed, a beetle, a moment of held warmth), and leave again. A pair of ground-doves has claimed one thatched roof and is tolerated because they do not soil the water-catchments and because their soft cooing at first light feels like another kind of weather. They are the only visitors who return as if they already know the place.

The people came next, walking in from the older, drier country after the last long rain, when the ground was still deciding how to be ground. There are fewer than forty of them. They did not so much choose the hollow as recognize it: a sudden wetness, a held heat, a bowl that had not yet been claimed by anything larger than themselves. They brought almost nothing—digging sticks worn smooth by other soils, a handful of fire-hardened points, baskets of vine that already looked foreign against the new pale stone. Everything else they have made here, with hands and weight and the patience of fiber.

Their dwellings form a loose, stubborn ring just inside the gentler slope of the inner rim. Walls of packed earth still the color of the rock they were scraped from, roofs thatched with the same extravagant platter-leaves that rise from the seams below. The houses sit close enough that the space between them has already become alley, already shines with the oil of passing feet. A central platform of unmortared blocks—each block still bright with the scars of being broken free—serves as drying ground, meeting place, and the only surface flat enough to feel like a decision. Smoke from the morning fires is thin and pale; it hangs in the warm vapor and then forgets itself.

They are lean in the way of people who have walked far and then stopped. Skin the color of sun-darkened stone dust, hair bound with strips of the same cane they weave into walls, eyes that have learned to watch the ground for new cracks as carefully as they watch one another. Children move through the packed paths with the particular caution of those who have already felt the earth shift under a wall. They chase the glittering insects, collect the broader leaves for thatch, and learn the names of the frogs by the different ways they jump. The adults work with a quiet, continuous attention. Women mix grit and moisture into new daub, their palms knowing the exact give of earth that has not yet become soil. Men test the faces of the walls for loose crystal, stack another course on the platform, or follow the thin silver threads of rain that have not yet cut channels. At the catchments they have scraped into the rock they wait without impatience; water here arrives, lingers a day, and is gone.

Their voices stay close to the speaker, as if the air itself prefers not to carry them. They have begun to invent words for things that did not exist in the older country: a term for the particular warmth that wells from a fresh crack, another for the way moss cushions spring back after a foot has passed, a third for the clouded lens of water that sometimes persists in the lowest center and reflects nothing. They tell no long stories yet. The hollow is too new for history. Instead they speak of the work of the day, of which wall settled in the night, of the pair of doves, of a plant that was not there yesterday. Sometimes, standing on the platform at dusk, they simply watch the ragged circle of ordinary sky and listen to the faint, continual sound of water choosing its first habits.

They eat what the hollow offers and what they can take without spoiling the offering: roasted lizards whose flesh tastes faintly of mineral, the tender uncurling hearts of ferns, insects toasted on hot stone, the occasional bird. They drink from the catchments before the dark maps fade. They sleep lightly, because the ground is still speaking—hair-thin cracks appearing overnight, a little more warmth sighing out, a new seam of grit appearing where none was. When a wall shifts they do not curse; they simply take it down and pack it again a hand-span to the left. The place is restless and they have become restless with it, but it is a restlessness without fear. They have decided this new wetness is worth claiming, and so they claim it one packed path, one thatched roof, one shared silence at a time.

At night the hollow is never empty. The frogs continue their liquid notes. Beetles tick. Somewhere a body brushes cane. A child turns in sleep. The people lie in the dark listening to the stone finish cooling, or begin, and know themselves to be the first who will remember this place as home. Everything else is still outside, older, drier, and does not yet know they are here. They intend, quietly, to remain until it does.
