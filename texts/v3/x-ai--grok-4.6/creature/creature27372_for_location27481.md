The green is what first claims the name of living. It does not arrive or succeed or compete. It is already the thickness between water and stone, a cold saturation that has never learned to thin. Sphagnum rises in the basins until the peat itself walks, a yielding that is not soil but a second, slower water table; you step and the ground remembers the step from far below, then forgets it in the same motion. On every fresh face—every knife of quartzite, every brick that has never dried, every iron stanchion—the liverworts and ice-ferns lie in glass-wet mats, fronds that hold the light the way the mist holds the light, without absorbing it. Moss hangs in beards that tear under their own weight and re-form in the identical beard, the identical tear. Nothing flowers. Nothing fruits. Nothing browns. The plants do not colonize; they occupy the only tense the plateau will allow. In the drowned hollows they become almost a jungle of pads and stems that never seek the sun because there is no sun to seek, only the even pale that is the plateau’s single lamp. Then the land opens and the green is only a film again, a skin stretched over ten miles of shattered plates, dripping, waiting for nothing.

Water lives beside it, or as it. The air is a cold emulsion that condenses on every surface as if the rock were breathing out. Cliffs do not shed water; they wear it, sheets that glaze and thread and soak the next ledge and begin again, never quite waterfall, never ice. In the young basins the water stands in black marshes whose depth is a rumor—you walk and the answer comes wetly from a long way down. There are no mouths, no currents that remember a sea. Only levels, hanging silver on the next unbroken lip. The pour is continuous, a thousand faces becoming one unending occupation. It does not hurry because hurry would be a change of tense, and the plateau has none.

Nothing else moves of its own will. No tick of insect, no line of bird, no burrow that was ever kept. The peat is unmarked. If prints were once possible they were not a language this place consented to remember. The lushness feeds no one. It is a plant silence so complete that the absence itself has weight, a negative fauna that occupies the air the way moss occupies stone.

Then the towns, bolted into the fault-steps, and the question of whether anything lives in them or whether they themselves are the living. Brick the color of dried blood and wet soot, iron that rusts only to a certain brown and then stops, as oxidation too has found its level. Every street is a sluice, every roof a gutter. Stacks breathe a pale exhaust indistinguishable from mist. The sound is a chord that does not vary: turbines, trip-hammers, steam shouted on a schedule that has no author and no Sunday. Inside, pipe over pipe, catwalk over millrace, a town-thickness of wet iron. Dynamos hold a note. Looms throw the same shuttle. Foundry light is a copper stagnant glow that is color more than heat. Rails leave in arrow-straight gleaming wetness, completing loops so vast the curve is invisible, or simply continuing until the mist takes them and they are still continuing. Trains arrive on the minute they have always arrived, empty or loaded with the same pig-iron shapes, and leave. The rails tick back to cold.

Windows hold the same laundry, wet, hanging, never quite dry. Canteens hold steam on the glass. Boots stand by doors. Movement happens the way the hammers happen: as function. If there are figures they do not flock or startle or vary. They are the towns’ way of remaining on, or they are not figures at all and the laundry is another moss, the boots a habit of the threshold. Either reading is permitted. The plateau does not decide. What is made—ingots, cloth the color of overcast, components that fit the next town’s components, or only noise and water to keep the loudness constant—does not accumulate. Slag is a hillside that has always been that hillside, already mossed, already weeping. Smoke becomes the weather it came from.

Nothing here weathers forward. A break remains the shape of its breaking. A new wall, if one were raised, would look the age of every other wall by morning—not older, not younger, only included. The ferns are already uncoiled. The marshes hold at the edge of ice like a held breath. The light does not migrate. Cold is the medium. Wet is the medium. You do not dry. The water on your lips tastes of stone and a faint oil.

What lives, then, is persistence without succession. The moss that tears and re-grows the same tear. The mill that keeps the same hour of rain. The rail that completes its enormous circle and begins it again. The plateau itself, young and finished, a single upthrown slab that rose once and then, perfectly, stopped. Far out, where even the loops have not gone, it continues—sharp, dripping, green-black in the seams, loud with its own unending pour—until the mind refuses the scale and begins to think of it as a sky that can be stood upon. The mist thickens to a sea. Somewhere there is a rim. From the rim there would be only more of the same weather generating itself, and the drop, and nothing crossing the drop, and the light holding steady on a world that has already resolved its chord and is only still being heard.

You may walk a lifetime and the distances remain distances. You may enter a foundry and the heat will be the same heat the first hand felt, if a first hand ever was. The water will still be coming through the brick. That is what lives here: the refusal to become anything else.
