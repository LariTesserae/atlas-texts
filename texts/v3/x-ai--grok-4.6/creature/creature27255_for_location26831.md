The first lives you meet here do not announce themselves as inhabitants so much as continuations of the stone and the pipework. They move the way the ridges themselves move: by increments too small to startle anything. On the more settled shelves you find the workers—humans, or close enough that the distinction has grown tired. They are long-limbed and narrow, built for gantries and for the sudden, polite failures of distance. Their skin takes the perpetual late-hour light and keeps it, so they appear smoked or water-dark even under the factory lamps. Eyes are large, dark-adapted, often filmed with the same condensation that beads every metal surface; they blink slowly, as if the lid itself were another catchment lip. Clothing is layered felt and oilcloth, the outer garments already colonised by the same black mosses that prefer boiler jackets. They do not hurry. A shift may last a week of clock-time or three days of the stone’s time; they have learned not to argue with whichever calendar is currently in force.

They speak little. When they do, the words arrive the way steam arrives: measured, bass-heavy, already travelling some distance inside them. They accept the glitches as weather. A man will step through a bulkhead that was solid an hour ago, emerge on a terrace that has not yet been poured, and simply begin laying brick as though the wall had always been waiting for him. A woman will look into a fogged pane and recognise her own unborn grandchild staring back, already wearing the same oil-stained gloves; she will nod, once, and go on tightening valves. They keep the furnaces at that careful middle heat—never fully banked, never roaring—because they understand the mountains are still deciding, and a sudden temperature is a kind of argument the rock has not yet agreed to hear.

Among them, and around them, and sometimes through them, the other herds. The goat-things (tahr is close enough, though their horns are more like unfinished basalt blades) pick their way across slag heaps and corrugated roofs with the same gait they use on scree that is still rising. They graze the moss that grows warm on turbine housings. They drink from leaky valves. Occasionally one will step through a solid plate as though the metal were only a rumour the stone had not yet finished telling; the workers step aside without comment. The dark birds—or the bird-shaped absences—turn in the dim air in flocks so dense they become a single textured noise, a kind of audible lichen. Their calls overlap until you cannot tell whether you are hearing animals or the wind finding another unfinished ridgeline. Smaller lives occupy every damp seam: insects whose wings catch the weak light like mica flakes, amphibians the colour of wet concrete that have no business at this height yet persist in the condensation runnels, pale elongated things that ease themselves out of pipework at the hours that would be dusk if dusk ever arrived. They do not flee the machines. They treat cooling towers as cliffs, nesting in the warm updrafts, laying eggs in the felted moss that thickens on the undersides of walkways.

Stay longer and the categories begin to leak, the way height leaks here, the way time pools in the low places between districts. Some of the workers have the same unhurried, hooved gait as the goats; you will see a man pause on a gantry, lower his head, and crop a mouthful of boiler-moss as if it were the most natural pause in a long conversation. Some of the pale pipe-dwellers have fingers. Some of the reflections in the sweating glass are not echoes of people who have already lived or not yet been born; they are simply other inhabitants using the same rooms at a different viscosity of time. You learn to greet them. A dropped wrench hangs, rotating, and while it hangs a child who will not be conceived for another forty years reaches out, takes it, and hands it back to you with a polite nod. Gravity forgets itself in pockets; so do the living. A flock of the dark birds will suddenly occupy a volume of air that is also a volume of last century’s steam, and for a few held breaths the factory floor is full of wings that taste of ozone and pollen from forests that have never grown here.

Deeper still, in the embryonic cirques and the fields of shattered plates where the city has not yet insisted, other things keep their own counsel. They are harder to name because they are still arriving, the way the mountains are still arriving. You glimpse them first as a thickening of the dimness, a place where the perpetual late hour becomes almost viscous. Then a shape: something like a person, or a goat, or a length of pipe that has decided to walk, but unfinished—edges still planar, fracture faces still holding a little of their own birth-heat. They do not speak. They stand at the unfinished edges and look out across the next valley, already sprouting its first cable-masts, and you understand they are the argument itself: the slow negotiation between industrial will and stone that has not yet agreed to stay still. Sometimes they step into a worker and the worker continues laying brick, only now the brick is paler, almost porcelain, and the mortar never quite sets. Sometimes they simply remain, patient as rust advancing a few metres across a forgotten gantry, waiting for the geology to catch up.

Moisture is their common language. Everything that lives here drinks the same iron-cold sweat of rock and metal. The mosses thicken into felt that is almost muscle. The ferns uncurl with visible reluctance, as though even growth were a decision they prefer to postpone. The hanging curtains of liverwort drink the thin light and return it as a deeper black. In the end it becomes difficult to say where the inhabitants end and the habitat begins. A cooling vane is also a cliff. A housing terrace is also a new kind of scree. A person is also a slow upwelling that has learned, provisionally, to use tools. Nothing here is finished, including the ones who live here. They keep building because the light, such as it is, is enough to see the next gantry, the next herd, the next unexplained shimmer where a wall should be. They keep building because the mountains are still deciding, and someone has to be there when the shapes finally arrive.
