The inhabitants are the saturation itself. They do not occupy the water so much as compose it, a living pressure that has long since replaced the idea of emptiness. What first registers is not species but the continuous soft collision: flanks sliding past flanks, the faint rasp of a shell against a worn arris, the slow lift and settle of bodies that have never known current or chase. Density here is not crowd but condition, the way the stone is condition. Everything touches everything, and the touching is the entire social fact.

Begin with the pale ones, because they are the most numerous and the least individual. They move as a single decision along the drowned galleries, a school whose edges fray and re-form against the stone the way smoke re-forms against a ceiling. Their bodies are the color of old milk or of the bacterial film they graze; fins reduced to almost-transparent membranes that stir rather than stroke. Eyes persist as dark pits that no longer gather light so much as register the pressure-shadow of a neighbor. When the school turns, the colonnade they follow briefly becomes fish, then stone again, the distinction having grown unimportant. They do not startle. A large body passing merely folds them aside; they reoccupy the vacated volume as soon as it is offered back.

Lower, and slower, the walkers. Too many legs, each ending in a delicate, probing tip that tastes the stone as it descends. They feel their way down pillars the way a hand that has forgotten its own face still finds the familiar banister in the dark. Carapaces the shade of ivory left too long in a closed room, stacked sometimes three and four deep in the sockets that once held—whatever they once held. The stone does not remember statues, only the hollows statues would have required; the walkers occupy those hollows as if the hollows had always been waiting for exactly this occupancy. Their mouths work constantly, rasping the chalky film, then rasping one another when the film thins. Generations turn over years no one counts. Shed skins join the slow mineral snow.

Clinging to the undersides of lintels and the inner curves of vaults are the unsegmented. Soft, overlapping, tile-like, their edges lift and fall in a rhythm too patient to be breath. They are the color of the stone they cover, or slightly greasier, a living mortar. Some hang in clusters so dense the architecture itself seems to pulse. Others have flattened themselves into the last remaining tool-marks, filling scallops and lips until the masonry’s memory of the chisel is only another texture of flesh. They feed on the film, on the rain of pellets, on whatever smaller lives blunder against their slowly waving margins. Predators among them are simply larger versions of the same softness, mouths that open like slow tears in the dark.

Then the ribbons. They hang vertically in the warm volume like belts someone forgot to take down, tasting whatever the water has decided to be this century. Lengths vary; some are no longer than a forearm, others trail for body-lengths, their posterior ends lost in the gloom or casually looped around a corbel. A few carry a dull interior phosphor—not light, more a bruise of green-white that lets their own kind keep station in the almost-dark. Eyes gleam along their flanks and then are not eyes, only more bruise. They do not swim so much as adjust, a rippling that displaces almost nothing. When one of the truly large things passes, the ribbons lean aside in unison, then settle back into their hanging as if the interruption had been theoretical.

In the field of low troughs the larval forms wait. Each partition, though nearly erased, still holds its own pale occupant, a body that has not yet chosen its later shape or has chosen to remain this shape. They feed on the detritus that settles, on one another when the settling thins. The troughs were cut by hands that could not have imagined this tenancy, yet the fit is now perfect; after long enough, the stone might as well have been cut for them.

And once, or twice, or in a rhythm too slow to measure, the large one. You do not see it. You are displaced by it—a warm, unhurried decision the water makes to be elsewhere for a while. In its wake the smaller lives rearrange without comment, the way sand rearranges, except this sand is awake and already feeding in the new configuration. Its skin, when it brushes a gallery wall, leaves a brief warmer streak that the film colonizes within hours. What it is remains less important than the fact that the tank has room for it, has always had room, will continue to have room.

They are all quiet. Sound exists only as contact: the rare click of chitin, the silk-tear of a fin, the pressure-thud felt more than heard. No voices, no signals beyond the phosphor bruise and the incidental gleam. Communication is the arrangement of bodies, the way a school occupies a colonnade or a walker occupies a socket. There is no outside against which to be wild, no keeper against which to be tame. The fauna use the architecture the way the architecture uses the fauna: as the other half of a sentence that finished speaking a very long time ago.

Niches are dens. Troughs are guts. Pillars are the vertical habit of hanging. The bacterial grease and the mineral snow are the weather they have instead of seasons. Newcomers are investigated with the tips of things—antennae, barbels, the leading edges of ribbons—slowly, thoroughly, without hurry. The system is closed enough to have opinions. It keeps what it can use and incorporates the rest into the rain.

If you remain until your own body is no longer an anomaly of temperature, you begin to feel the larger continuity: that these lives are not descendants of the builders so much as the builders’ final, unintended masonry. The stone was assembled to hold water. The water was held until holding became the world. What lives here is simply the world continuing, warm, dark, densely itself, recycling the last possible shapes of a place that was made completely and then allowed to become only what it could keep. A click, somewhere. Then the silence that was never broken reseals around every flank, every shell, every hanging ribbon, every pale turning school. The water holds them the way it holds everything else it has decided not to lose.
