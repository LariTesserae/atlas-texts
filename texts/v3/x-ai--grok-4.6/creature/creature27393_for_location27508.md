The smallest lives are films. They coat every weeping joint and the underside of every duct, a slick of iron-eating bacteria and mineral-staining algae that turn runoff the colour of old blood. Mycelia bind the silt in the mortar-gaps into something almost soil; they glow faintly when the mist thickens, a cold foxfire that never quite becomes light. In the permanently flooded turbine halls the water itself is occupied—rafts of cress hide larval clouds, and the dark meres pulse with the slow respiration of things that have never known a dry hour.

Above that layer the opportunistic green has already been named, but it is not passive. The ivy whose stems are thick as wrists does not merely climb; it pries, it reroutes steam, it shares insulation with pipes until the distinction between vine and conduit is academic. Ferns unfurl from bolt-holes with the patience of a second architecture. In the hanging wetlands that were once loading bays, sphagnum and horsetail have learned the timing of the remaining conveyors: they lift and settle with the clank, their roots knitting through seized bearings so that when a belt finally tears it does so along a living seam.

Insects exist as pockets, never a haze. Water-striders dimple the geometrically perfect basins. Pale, elongated beetles tunnel the compacted root-mass between plates, their elytra the same rust-brown as the metal they mine. Midges rise in brief, local storms when a steam vent condenses, then vanish. There is a moth whose wings are the exact dull sheen of wet moss; it rests open on cooling-tower faces and is indistinguishable until it moves.

Amphibians have claimed the lower decks. Newts the colour of wet brick slip between rivets. In the reed-choked meres, frogs the size of a fist sit motionless for days, their throats pulsing in time with distant, muffled engines. They do not sing often. When they do, the sound is a single, echoing note that travels the green-metal canyons and then stops, as if the place itself has decided the song is finished.

Birds are rarer still. A heron sometimes stands in a flooded hall, absolutely still, until the moment it spears something pale and larval. Corvids of a greyer, wetter strain pick along the higher gantries, their calls thin and startling. They nest in the crooks where tree-branch and walkway have become the same structure; their young learn to grip both bark and rusted grate before they fledge. Once in a long while a larger shadow crosses the dim canopy—something with a slow wingbeat that does not belong, gone before the eye can decide what it was.

Larger animals, as the place itself seems to insist, are almost absent. Nothing that needs a stable den or a clear run lasts. Yet there are traces: a shed plate of keratin the colour of old brick, a scrape along a catwalk too regular to be wind, a smell of musk and wet fur that appears after heavy drip and is gone by the next lull. Whatever leaves these signs moves only when the mist is thickest, using the same routes as the steam lines, never lingering.

And then there are the ones who still turn the keys.

They live in the sealed halls whose outer walls have become hanging gardens. Generations have passed since anyone came from outside; the original operators’ descendants (or perhaps only their programming, worn into flesh) keep the remaining engines on their looping, failing schedules. They are pale, permanently damp, their skin taking a faint green cast from the light that filters through three layers of gantry and canopy. They move along the catwalks in silence, barefoot so they can feel the creak of stressed metal through the soles. Their tools are a mixture of the old—wrenches whose handles have been wrapped in living vine for grip—and the scavenged: a bracket torn from a collapsed bay, already sprouting. They repair what can be repaired, shore what is sagging, and accept the rest. When a section of deck slumps they do not curse; they listen, then add new steel that will itself rust and sprout within a season. They harvest water-cress and the pale inner pith of certain ferns. They drink the condensate that beads on the undersides of ducts, tasting iron and leaf as if it were the only vintage. They rarely speak. When they do, it is in a low register that does not carry far, a language of drips and pressures rather than words. At night (though night is only a deepening of the same gray) they gather in the driest of the sealed rooms and listen to the subsonic turning of the engines, the way other people once listened to rain on a roof.

They are not many. The place does not allow many. A child is born already knowing the sound of a root growing through a bearing. An elder dies and is laid in one of the geometrically perfect basins, where the reeds close over within a week and the body becomes another slow contribution to the silt. They do not leave. There is nowhere else that matches the specific gravity of this damp, this dimness, this continuous quiet upheaval. They have become another weather system inside the larger one: cool, patient, densely occupied, repeating their small catastrophes of maintenance and surrender across the terraces.

From the highest gantries you might see one of them, a thin figure moving through the lattice, indistinguishable at first from the dripping metal and the ferns. Then the figure pauses, lays a hand on a pipe that is also a stem, and the whole interlocking pavement seems, for a moment, to breathe with them.

The place does not remember it was ever anything else. Neither do they.
