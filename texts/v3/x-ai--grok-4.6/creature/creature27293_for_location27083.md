The first lives are the ones the stone already wears. They do not arrive; they persist in the agreement the masonry made with seepage long before any later tenant could object. Algae occupy the vertical maps, olive in the slower films, rust where the blocks themselves bleed iron. They are not a covering. They are a metabolism the walls now require, thinning and thickening with the capillary rise, photosynthesizing in the milk-light at a pace that matches the drip’s schedule. Moss follows, black-green, so waterlogged it has the give of flesh that has forgotten bone. It points the joints more honestly than mortar ever did. In the few courts that catch a paler hour, the single permitted shrub holds its waxed leaves against the almost-rain and sends a root the width of a vein into a crack that will never widen. That is the flora. It does not spread. It occupies the damp the way the city occupies the highland: completely, without surplus.

The mobile things are fewer, and they have learned the city’s shyness. The bird the color of wet iron-tea appears as a brief subtraction of daylight, dropping from lintel to drain-mouth with wings that look too heavy for the air and yet vanish into it. There are more of them than the single glimpse admits. They nest in the truncated towers and the booming vaults, lining cups of moss and the translucent bones of what they take from the water. Their flight is always downward or inward. They do not cross open sky for long; the height and the even light make them conspicuous against themselves. You hear no call. The hiss of committed rain, when it comes, swallows whatever sound they keep.

In the cisterns and the standing pools—the tea-colored ones, cellar-cold, skinned with pollen that cannot have been made here—something dimples and then thinks better of it. The dimplers are pale, thumb-length or hand-length, finned or merely writhing, grazing the submerged algae or hunting what is smaller still. They live in water that has already been used by the roofs and the courts and the mineral crusts. When a plaza sheets over at midafternoon they are lifted with it, stranded in the silt as the outlets remember their work, and the bird takes some while others ride the overflow to the next terrace, the next fractionally warmer air, the next climate the city manufactures for itself. In the broader lagoons of the flatter quarters they grow larger, slower, the size of a forearm, hanging in the still reflections like the memory of a current. They do not school. They occupy.

Tracks appear in the gray deposit of sunken forums after the blueing of evening. The print is four-toed, tentative, the weight of something that keeps its belly close to the wet. Not a fox, though the resemblance is a convenience. Whatever leaves it drinks at the watermark’s edge, takes a stranded dimpler if the bird has not, and withdraws into the pinch of alleys where the sky is only a ribbon. Its coat, if it has one, holds the same stain as the walls. It is scarce because the city chose scarcity; the cisterns could feed a denser hunger, the moss could hide insects enough for a different equilibrium. This one does not increase. It persists at the density of rumor, one set of prints per many districts, fading as the next sheet of water rearranges the silt.

Deeper, in the cuttings where the living rock sweats and the pale crusts grow ring upon ring, there are films that do not need light. They feed on the stone’s own slow unmaking, precipitating the city’s second masonry from the inside out. They are life as deposit, as the opposite of collapse. You do not see them move. You see that the rings are still being added, microscopically, in the dark.

That could be all. Emptiness remains load-bearing. You can walk a week of courts and lanes and hear only your coat and the scheduled drip and the occasional larger report of water changing terrace. The scale dilutes every population to the edge of absence. And yet the density of the built thing—wall to wall, room opening into room that was never meant as passage, platforms stacked across older roofs—creates a vertical interior the avenues never reach. Lives that think the way the water thinks can occupy it without ever using a street.

They are not the original hands. Those ceased, and left no hinge, no oxide that was not already the stone’s own rust, no glass. What remains, if it remains, has the finished quality of the arrises: rounded, stained, in its long afternoon. They would be few, perhaps one habit of being per climate the city contains. They move when the hydrologic breath moves—climbing the walls by the same capillary patience, descending with the overflow—because the alternative is to be seen against the ordinary daylight. In a high window the color of wet stone they are only another truncation. At night they walk by the gleam the masonry gives back in the wrong direction, and their feet make the same tiny tick of grit. They eat what the bird and the dimpler and the shrub reluctantly offer. They add nothing that would remain as tool. Their enclosures are the same enclosures; their doors are the missing ones. You would pass them in a deep well of walls and take their stillness for masonry, their rare displacement for a sheet of water detaching somewhere out of sight.

Or they are not separate. The longer you stay, the less the distinction holds. The films, the moss, the shy flesh, the mineral rings, the possible watchers in the stacked silhouettes—all of it is the city’s other respiration, the one that is not weather. In and out, at the pace of a season rather than a front. Cold, clean, scheduled, unable at last to get dry. They occupy the volume they were given the same way the stone does: completely, without surplus, including their own scarcity as part of the load-path. Nothing here is trying to be strange. It is only very large, and very finished, and alive in the only way the highland would still permit.
