The first lives you notice are the ones that refuse to be noticed. They occupy the film between drip and steam, the thickness of a leaf’s shine. Midges and pale crane-flies hang in motionless veils above every weeping overhang; their wings do not beat so much as tremble in the convection, and when they land they vanish into the same wet brown as the moss. Their larvae live inside the warm sponge of the peat, mining galleries that collapse behind them, feeding on the mineral taste the rock sweats. You feel them before you see them: a constant, almost weightless pressure on the skin, like being breathed on by something that has no lungs.

Lower, in the tea-colored shallows, the frogs have given up the idea of leaping. They are flattened, almost two-dimensional, their throats the exact color of drowned leaf litter. When they sing they do it in pulses that lock to the vents—three notes, rest, three notes—so that a newcomer mistakes the entire marsh for geology. Their tadpoles hatch already warm, already tasting of metal; they graze the bacterial veils that coat every drowned stem, veils the color of rust and old ivory. Some of the adult frogs have learned to sit inside the bleached rings around the quieter fumaroles, letting the heat loosen parasites they no longer bother to carry. They do not startle. Nothing here startles for long.

The birds are compact the way stones in a river are compact: no wasted surface. They move through the steam in short, decided hops rather than true flight, landing on the tree-fern crowns and folding themselves into the geometry of the fronds until they become another wet silhouette. Their calls, when they give them, are single notes dropped like the heavy drips from high leaves—sparse, unrepeated. They eat what the air condenses: midges, the soft new uncurling of fern croziers, the occasional slow moth that has spent its whole life never drying its wings. Nests are not built so much as accepted: a hollow already padded by last year’s bromeliad, still holding yesterday’s rain.

Between these layers something larger keeps the thicker steam. It does not hunt in the way the word usually means. It displaces. A dark, low mass eases through the succulent mats; stems lean, then close. You catch the suggestion of a flank the color of wet basalt, a ridge of what might be fur or might be living moss, a single slow blink of an eye that has the same tired, true color as the overcast. The creature’s blood is only a few degrees cooler than the water it wades. It feeds by standing still until the small warm fish and the larval clouds come to the shade of its body, then it simply closes around them. When it moves on, the marsh forgets the shape in less than a minute. There are several. They do not gather. They do not call. Their young are born already the temperature of the peat and remain half-submerged for the first year, indistinguishable from the floating root-mats until they decide, without hurry, to stand.

Deeper still, where the water clears and the drowned trunks stand upright like the ghosts of a forest that finished growing, other lives turn over in the slow columns of bubbles. Pale, almost translucent eels no longer than a forearm thread the drowned wood, their skins tasting the same cooked-metal note as the droplets. They have no need of eyes; the convection itself tells them where the next vent will exhale. Around the hottest margins, mats of something that is neither plant nor animal—colonial, gelatinous, faintly luminous at night if night ever fully arrives—pulse in time with the boilers underground. They consume the sulfur and the iron and give back a faint sweetness the frogs have learned to lick.

Nothing here has named itself. Nothing has made a path that lasts longer than the next rain-that-is-not-rain. The treaty between heat and height has simply continued long enough for every occupant to become another way the basin keeps itself wet and warm. The rims still hold the weather in. The rock still radiates. The cloud still condenses on itself a few meters above the ground and is taken back. And through all of it the lives move at the pace of a kettle that has been left on since the last ash cooled, unhurried, inevitable, already home.
