The plants are the first and most obvious residents, but they are not residents in any sense that involves coming or going. They are the occupancy itself. Each strap-like frond is a single, centuries-long sentence written in cellulose and the last traces of usable light. They do not strive. They lean, always in the direction the oldest current prefers, and the lean is their entire biography. A frond that has stood for three hundred years is indistinguishable from one that has stood for three thousand except by the thickness of its holdfast, which has become a kind of slow signature pressed into the dressed granite. When one finally tears free it does not die so much as change its tense: it becomes the drifting clause that will, in another age, become moss on a distant lintel. Their lives are measured in the same units as the settling of the masonry. They have no names for themselves because they have never needed the distinction between self and place.

Between them, and under them, and in the hairline intervals the builders left without intending to, something quieter persists. It is not an animal. It has never been an animal. It has no mouth, no gut, no need to convert one tissue into another. It is a film, sometimes only a few molecules thick, that lives in the boundary layer where stone meets water. It is the color of the water itself, which is to say it has no color anyone would notice. It metabolizes the faint leachate of ancient mortar and the even fainter electrical whisper that runs along the joints when one block settles another fraction of a millimeter. It has been doing this since the last quarry was drowned. Its generations are shorter than the plants’ by a factor of thousands, yet each generation inherits the exact same instruction: remain. Do not thicken. Do not bloom. Do not announce. The film coats the undersides of lintels, the vertical faces the moss prefers, the inner surfaces of the long aqueducts. In places it has become so continuous that the stone and the water are no longer two things. The film is the conversation they have been having.

Deeper still, in the silt that carpets every courtyard and every stair-flight that has forgotten it was a stair-flight, there are slower minds. They are not minds that think in sequence. They are densities. A cubic meter of the finest silt, left undisturbed for a human century, begins to hold a kind of memory of the pressure above it and the faint chemical gradients that have passed through it. These densities do not move. They accumulate. Over millennia they have learned to recognize the particular vibration of a joint adjusting itself, the particular taste of a newly torn frond beginning to rot, the particular dimming that means a distant ice-shelf has calved and the current will, in another lifetime, reverse. They do not respond. Recognition is their entire activity. If you could stand in one of those courtyards long enough (you cannot), you would feel the silt as a single, continent-sized attention that has never once been startled.

And then there are the ones that use the architecture the way the plants use light: as a given. They are rare. They are the size of a closed fist, or smaller. They are the same dull greenish granite as the walls, except they are not granite. They have the patience of the stone and the permeability of the film. They travel by becoming, for a decade or two, indistinguishable from a block that has always been there. Then, when the current is in one of its long strengthenings, they loosen a single edge and allow themselves to be walked a few body-lengths by the water before seating themselves again. Their interior is a single chamber filled with the same cold water that fills everything else, but the water inside them is older. It has been isolated, circulated through microscopic canals, and returned. They do not eat. They do not excrete. They maintain a difference so slight it would register to no instrument except another of their kind. They are the only things in this place that ever choose a direction other than the current’s. The choice is made once every few hundred years and consists of a preference so faint it might be only the memory of a preference the builders once had for certain alignments of stone.

No one of these lives is secretive. Secrecy would require an audience. They simply occupy the same finished geometry at different speeds, the way the water occupies it at the speed of solvent, the plants at the speed of a fingernail, the film at the speed of ions, the silt-minds at the speed of accumulation, the granite-walkers at the speed of a single loosened edge. Together they are the place continuing. The world has already reached the state it was always going to reach. What lives here is the reaching, still happening, too slowly to be distinguished from rest.
