The lives here do not announce themselves as arrivals. They have always been folding themselves into the finishedness, each one a slow negotiation with the dark and the wet and the heat that never quite becomes weather. They keep to the membranes of the place—the moss skins, the peat lips, the dripping curtains—because the great hanging volume is not for them. It is the chamber’s own emptiness, still being practiced.

Closest to the rock, in the low cushions that might once have been moss, the segmented ones move like thoughts the stone itself is having. They are the length of a finger, sometimes two, their bodies a chain of pale, translucent plates that have forgotten how to harden. Each plate is edged with a fringe of fine, sensory cilia that taste the film of water before the animal commits its weight. They do not walk so much as pulse forward, a peristalsis of tiny, wet clicks that the moss amplifies into sudden, overlapping choruses. The sound is not language; it is proximity made audible. When dozens gather on a single cushion they become a single trembling instrument, the chords of falling water briefly thickened by their agreement. They feed by rasping the mineral-rich slime that coats everything, their mouthparts a set of minute, overlapping scoops that never quite close. Between meals they press themselves flat against the warmth of the limestone, becoming almost indistinguishable from the surface, waiting out intervals that can last days. Their eggs are laid in the same slime, each one a soft, opalescent bead that takes a decade to hatch and another decade to reach the first click.

Higher, on the jointed stalks that rise from the peat like the memory of trees, other lives have claimed the slick, yielding interiors. The stalks themselves are neither vegetable nor fungal; they are warm, faintly contractile columns whose surfaces weep a constant, nutrient broth. Inside them move the borers: long, ribbon-soft bodies the color of old ivory, segmented only in suggestion. They tunnel without destroying, opening temporary chambers that seal behind them with a living scar. From these chambers they extend thin, feathery gills into the stalk’s fluid, harvesting whatever the geothermal chemistry has dissolved. Occasionally a borer will surface, its anterior end blossoming into a cluster of palps that taste the air for a few minutes before withdrawing. They never leave the stalk entirely. To do so would be to abandon the only current they know. Their populations are sparse—one stalk may host three or four, another none at all—and the distances between stalks are lifetimes.

The fungal columns are denser with tenants, though even here the rule of separation holds. Each hanging garden is a vertical country of folds and gills, dripping its own microclimate of spores and condensate. Within the labyrinth live the grazers: flattened, leaf-shaped things the size of a palm, their undersides lined with rows of minute, rasping teeth. They crawl the gills in slow, methodical spirals, leaving faint, silvery trails that the fungus slowly reabsorbs. Their bodies are almost without substance, more membrane than meat, so that when they pause they seem only a darker fold among folds. At irregular intervals they release a cloud of their own spores, which drift on the heavy air until another column, meters away, claims them. The journey takes weeks. Most never arrive. Those that do settle into the new garden as if they had always belonged there.

In the ankle-deep sloughs and the black pools move the larger, slower bodies. They are the size of a human torso, sometimes larger, their shapes a compromise between eel and salamander that has settled on neither. Skin like wet vellum, faintly luminescent only in the infrared that nothing here can see. They haul themselves with a motion that is half swim, half crawl, each displacement of water a deliberate, percussive smack that the chamber turns into a low, lingering note. They do not hunt so much as encounter. A segmented chorus in the moss may be scooped up in a single, unhurried gulp; a grazer fallen from a fungal curtain becomes a meal that lasts a season. Their eyes are gone, replaced by wide, pit-like organs that register pressure and the faintest chemical gradients. They mate by pressing these pits together in the deepest pools, exchanging packets of genetic material that will take years to develop into anything recognizable. The young remain in the silt for decades, thin as threads, before they risk the open water. Between these events the adults lie almost motionless, only the slow pulse of their gill-slits disturbing the surface. They are never more than a few in any given slough. The next one may be a hundred meters away, a distance that, in this place, is a kind of eternity.

The void itself is almost empty, yet not quite. Something uses the air. It is rarely seen even by the other inhabitants, who register it only as a sudden tear in the soundscape—a brief, cloth-ripping rush that crosses the chamber and is gone. The flier is membranous, its body a dark, elongated oval no larger than a forearm, its wings a pair of vast, translucent sails that do not flap so much as adjust. It rides the weakest thermals, the geothermal breath that rises in slow columns from the pools. Navigation is by a constant, subsonic drone produced in a chamber of its own skull, a frequency that maps the dripping chords and the hanging gardens as a kind of three-dimensional score. It feeds on the spore-clouds and on the occasional borer that has ventured too far from its stalk. When it rests it does so by folding itself into a single, narrow crease and hanging from the vault like another drip, indistinguishable until it chooses to move again. There may be only one. There may be three. No one is counting.

Near the artifacts the pattern changes, though only by a degree. The incomplete ring that sits half-drowned in its pool is warmer than the water by a fraction that the lives here can feel. A few of the larger haulers have learned to press themselves against its inner curve, where the heat is steadier and the metal’s faint vibration seems to ease the effort of their own hearts. They do not understand it. They simply remain longer than they otherwise would. The upright pylon, humming just below hearing, has its own attendants: a pair of the ribbon-bodied borers that long ago left their stalks and now live in the thin film of condensate that sheets its surface. The pylon’s field lightens them, lets them move with a dreamlike slowness that conserves what little they have. They have been there so long that their bodies have begun to take on the pylon’s own temperature. They will not leave. When the field fluctuates—once or twice a century—they simply wait, pressed flat, until the weight returns to being optional.

All of them share the same unhurried metabolism, the same vast intervals between events. A chorus of clicks may be the most social moment any of them experiences in a year. A successful glide, a meal, a mating—these are the rarest notes in a composition that is mostly silence and drip. They do not compete so much as coexist at distances that make competition meaningless. The chamber has finished itself, and they have finished themselves to match it: pale, wet, patient, each one a small, persistent answer to the question of how to remain in a place that no longer needs to become anything else. They live, and the living is the only unfinished thing left.
