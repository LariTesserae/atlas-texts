The ones who remain do not fill the streets. They occupy the town the way the seep occupies the basin: sometimes only a shine, sometimes a slow ticking that the stone has already budgeted for. You infer them first from interruptions that are almost not interruptions at all—a shutter left ajar on a morning when the cloud sits elsewhere, a dark pot set precisely under the broken spout and gone again before the overflow can find the gutters, smoke the color of the timber itself lifting from two chimneys, never three, never one. The third chimney stays cold. That, too, is part of the accounting.

They are few. The courts were built for more, and the emptiness is not recent; it has the same weather as the lime. A person crossing the central flags does so with the delayed echo already in mind, as if rehearsing the stone’s reply. Their steps are not hurried. Nothing here requires haste, and the air, thin and faintly wet, does not reward it. Clothes are the same close-grained dark as the doors, swollen, never quite dry, cut in shapes that have forgotten fashion the way the ledge has forgotten to keep rising. Edges of sleeves and hems are rounded by the same long abrasion that has taken the corners from the walls. Skin, where it shows at wrist or throat, has the pale coarseness of the lower courses, mapped with the tired yellow of lichen in the creases.

They keep the hours the mountain keeps. Before the dew lifts they are about: a hand on the basin’s rim to feel whether the night’s condensation has been enough, the rake lifted from its passage and set down again without having raked anything that needed raking. The hand-mill is touched, its satin surface no longer wanting grain, yet the hopper is checked as if habit were a kind of hunger. By midmorning they have withdrawn. Shutters close not against weather but against the habit of being closed. The streets return to their slots and their sudden, oversized courts. You can stand a long while and hear only the wind in the high ways, the occasional wooden complaint, the water when it decides. Their absence then is as deliberate as their presence, another fitted flag.

There is no children-sound. No new voices testing the echo. The town does not grow and they do not diminish; the two facts have worn the same groove. If there were once more of them, the stone does not record the leaving. Thresholds are dished to the width of the feet that still use them. Those feet are not young. They have the patience of weight. When a slate slides in the winter that is only a change of light and damp, someone—always the same someone, or someone interchangeable with the last—sets it back, or does not, and either choice was already inside the town’s idea of itself. The gap becomes another drip. The tree in the angle keeps its copper leaves or lets a few go. They do not prune it. They did not plant it. It is simply the amount of tree, and they are simply the amount of people.

They eat what the place will allow and no more. Something dry from stores that last because spoiling here is as slow as rounding; a bitterness from the wiry bronze grass after it has been crushed and left; the rare dimple in the basin that is not insect and is not spoken of. Grain, when it comes, comes from below, carried up the path that switchbacks into scree and does not return, and it is never enough to fill the hopper or to change the satin. They drink the seep. It is older than the walls. Their thirst is the basin’s thirst: sometimes a shine, sometimes a ticking.

At the western wall, where the last court ends and the drop begins, one of them will stand the way the wall itself stands—low, finished, considering the receding palenesses. Cloud may sit on those farther ridges and not come. When it does come, they do not hurry indoors. The town becomes dark mouths in soft grey; the lichen darkens; the seep is louder. That is one of the few expressions the face is allowed, and they allow it too. Their own faces, in that light, are the same set of dark mouths. They do not call to one another across the courts. Speech, when it happens, is the sound of a lintel that has sagged the width of a thumb since before anyone now living—yet they are the ones now living, and the sag is theirs.

They have become difficult to tell from the masonry. Not metaphorically. The damp is in their joints. The cool is the temperature they keep. You could leave and return after what feels like a generation and find the same three smokes, the same pot, the same figure at the western edge, and be unsure whether the figure had moved or merely been the wall all along. They would not help you decide. They have already accounted for every sound they intend to make, and a visitor’s questions are not in the budget.

At night—if night here is more than the ordinary light withdrawing—the condensation breathes out of the stone and out of them equally. They sleep in the higher rooms, or they do not sleep so much as settle, the way the ledge settled. In the first pale hour they are again the interruptions that prove the quiet is not absence. A shutter. A pot. The rake. The knife-blade left on a sill, dark, used, the minimum of metal, as if even sharpness had been weathered down to what the place will bear.

They are the town keeping itself. The mountain keeps them. Nothing else is required, and they have not required it for so long that the not-requiring has the same completion as the dry-laid courses. Stand long enough in the central court and you feel the scale of them settle over you as well: not a people, not a remnant, but a single inhabited thought, cathedral-small, hamlet-complete, held in the same cool damp equilibrium, finished.
