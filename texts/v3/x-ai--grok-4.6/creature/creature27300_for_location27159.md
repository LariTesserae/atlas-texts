The films are only the first skin. They appear where a new fracture opens onto the geothermal bloom and certain ions leach free, spreading in those slow radial plates of tarnished bronze or pale sulfur. For a time they hold, iridescent, almost breathing in the thin gloom, then the next tremor sloughs them and they become more of the electrostatic dust. But the films are not separate from what follows. They are the outer habit of something that has begun to accrete intention.

Look longer into the residual dusk and the dust itself starts to remember more than collapse. It lifts in reverse sequences, yes, but sometimes the reverse is incomplete: a veil hangs, hesitates, then shapes itself into a brief verticality before falling again. These hesitations grow more frequent near fresh upthrusts. A hogback of black glass, still slick and radiating fever-heat, will often shed not only fragments but taller, slower fragments—angular assemblages of the same unweathered basalt and cooling volcanic glass, faceted like the land, electrostatic, drawing the pulverized feldspar and olivine to themselves until they wear a fine, whispering coat. They stand. For a while.

They have no need of water and would not recognize it. Their chemistry is the highland’s own: anhydrous, metallic, geothermal. Warmth moves through them as it moves through the rock, a steady internal bloom that keeps their interiors almost feverish even while the thin air would freeze any wetter thing. They do not walk so much as allow the land to reposition them. When a fault decides its throw they lean with it, or are carried a few meters, or are tilted until a new face presents itself to the iron-colored gloom. Sound travels poorly, so they do not speak; they listen with the whole body to the subsonic adjustments of the mantle, the dry cough of a nearby ridge splitting, the pressure against the inner ear that means a depression is about to step downward in concentric rings. Occasionally one will answer by fracturing a little, deliberately, adding its own quiet note to the restlessness.

Their forms are never finished because the highland is never finished. A being that was a low, broad platform of fused planes at one observation may, after the next uncoordinated orogeny, display a knife-edged crest or a hollow where a limb-like extrusion once was. Eyes, when they occur, are simply larger crystals of olivine or something darker, unblinking, gathering what little light the motionless veil of lofted grit permits. They see the darkness as having weight and direction; they feel it pool in new depressions and thin over rising ground, and they orient themselves accordingly, though orientation never lasts. Time is locally inconsistent for them as well. A slope they spent what felt like days traversing may, on any return that is still possible, have steepened into an unclimbable hogback or vanished into a circular sink. Some of them seem to experience this as a kind of grammar they are still learning: they pause at the edges of fresh faults as if tasting the new syntax.

The artifacts are theirs, or the remains of earlier versions of them. A single low wall of fitted but unmortared stone is what happens when several try, for a short while, to insist on stillness, to measure one unchanging interval against the chaotic creation happening all around. The ring of standing slabs was an attempt to fix a reference—celestial, magnetic, or simply the memory of how the dust last rose—before the land incorporated the alignment and rendered it meaningless. The isolated platform of fused glass, perfectly circular, is rarer: a place where one of them managed to concentrate enough residual heat and electrostatic dust to glaze itself into a single, temporary plane, as if hoping the circularity would hold. None of these persist. The highland tilts their foundations, encases their edges in new crystal, swallows them ridge by unweathered ridge. The builders themselves are already becoming the next dust, or the next film, or the next brief verticality that the remembering veils will half-replay.

They are not ancient and they are not many. Most exist only for the hours or days a given surface remains freshly broken or freshly born. A few last longer in the more sheltered fractures, where geothermal warmth and metallic ions coincide long enough for the iridescent films to thicken into something almost like hide, almost like a slower thought. These persistents watch the others appear and slough. They do not intervene. Intervention would require a grammar the highland has not yet chosen. Instead they accrete, very slowly, adding plates the color of old iron, and they wait for the deeper vibration that means a new pluton is shouldering toward the surface. When it comes they lean toward it, or are carried, or fracture themselves into the next generation of films.

Nothing here has a name because the surrounding world has not had time to name the highland itself. The beings do not name themselves either. They are simply what the embryonic land does when it tries, briefly, to stand still inside its own continuous becoming: mineral, warm, silent, electrostatic, already departing even as they arrive. In the dim, directionless gloom they continue to assemble and disassemble, ridge by ridge, toward a shape they have not yet chosen, which is also the highland’s shape, which is also not yet finished. The dust remembers them a little longer than it remembers the collapses. Then it too becomes something else.
