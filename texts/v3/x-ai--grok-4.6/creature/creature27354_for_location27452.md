The pocket does not keep a single tenant. It keeps a density that has learned the difference between occupancy and residence. Everything that stays has already accepted that the available volume is not a room but a negotiation, measured in leftover millimeters and the brief intervals between one cycle of the heat exchangers and the next.

The liverwort comes first in any accounting, though it never claims precedence. It is the color of tea left too long in a forgotten cup, mat-thin, clinging to the ochre clay band where the fill sheared. It does not root so much as adhere, its underside a slow chemistry of holdfasts that dissolve the clay just enough to drink the seepage without ever becoming soil. In the warm fog it swells a fraction, each lobe taking on a faint translucence so that the rust vanes behind it show through like bones under old skin. It advances by forgetting its edges: a finger’s width between visits, the new growth already looking centuries older than the concrete it borders. Nothing eats it except the isopods, and they graze so lightly the silver tracks they leave are gone before the next condensation.

The two or three dock stems are different. They remember another life, one with more light and more argument. They found a crack in the broken slab and rose six inches before the pocket taught them the new rules. Now they are woody all the way down, their leaves reduced to stiff, dark flags that no longer unfurl. They do not grow; they persist, holding a shape that once meant “up” in a place that no longer has a reliable direction. Rain never reaches them. The fog does, and they take it the way the pipes take heat: without gratitude, without waste.

Between these green holdouts the rest of the life is the color of the break itself. Beetles whose elytra were rolled from the same industrial green that still clings to the nameless elbow of pipe. Their bodies are short, compressed, built for the spaces that already exist rather than the ones that might. They do not hunt so much as occupy. An isopod must pass a certain joint in a feed line; the beetle is already there, its mandibles simply closing on whatever arrives. There is no chase because there is no elsewhere. When they feed the sound is that wet-paper tear, brief, almost polite.

The isopods themselves have become specialists in the film. They graze the liverwort and the condensate equally, their legs leaving those temporary silver scripts that the next fog erases. They are the color of wet ash with a faint sulfur bloom along the segments, and they have learned the timetable of the heat plant better than any clock. Twice a day the concrete sweats, the pocket fills, and the isopods lift their bodies just enough to let the warm moisture run beneath them. They correct their paths around any larger intrusion—your boot, a fallen crust, the stiffened glove in its niche—without hurry and without memory of having been displaced.

In the coil of abandoned cable the many-jointed things wait. They are longer than they first appear, folded into the residual hum that still ghosts the wires when the exchangers cycle. Too many joints, each one capable of an independent flex, so that a single body can occupy three different crevices at once. They taste vibration more than they taste flesh. When the cable thrums they tighten, and whatever small life has wandered too close is simply incorporated, joint by joint, into the same packed geometry. You feel them more than you see them: a pressure against the teeth, a suggestion that the metal itself has grown extra articulations.

At the lowest corner, where the film never quite becomes a pool, the pale ones congregate. Flattened, neither slug nor fish, their bodies pulse with a rhythm that matches the temperature more than any heartbeat. Mouths open and close along their margins, tasting the glycol ghost, the warm iron, the faint river-silt memory that still lives in the older layers. When the fog arrives they brighten—an interior light, sourceless, as if they were storing the condensation as luminescence. When it thins they dim, folding flatter until they are almost part of the mineral crusts. In the wet weeks, when the real river rises and the film becomes actual water for a day, they become frantic. They thread the interiors of pipes that were empty the visit before, using the metal as a temporary river. Predation among them is only a matter of who occupies the same cubic inch when the water drops. The crusts hiss and dissolve, then grow back sharper, and the pale ones rearrange themselves inside the new geometry as if they had always known the next shape.

Nothing here is finished. New sulfate plates appear overnight, blade-thin, ringing when a drop hits, occupying surfaces you would have sworn were already claimed. Rust organizes itself into vertical vanes that share volume with the pipes rather than replacing them. The animals shift to match. A valve wheel that was seized open last season now carries a chain of the pulse-things along its spokes. The glove in its niche remains untouched by crust, as if the pocket has decided that particular vacancy is already occupied by a shape that no longer needs to be a hand.

You cannot stand among them without adding to the accounting. Your breath thickens the fog. Your heat is a new timetable. The isopods adjust. The pale ones brighten a fraction, tasting you the way they taste the exchangers. After a while the quiet stops being absence and becomes the sound of every small process agreeing that noise is a luxury the millimeters cannot afford. The pocket is still cutting its own walls. It is still filling every vacancy the moment a vacancy appears. What lives here is that unfinishedness given bodies: warm, damp, overfull, and already older than the break that made the room.
