The people of the basin are the color of the silt they pack, or a shade paler, a bruise-gray that never quite dries. Their skin stays slack and faintly pebbled from the perpetual film of warmth, as if the body itself has learned to weep along with the walls. Hair, when they have it, lies in thin dark ropes that never lift, plastered to scalp and nape; many shave or pluck it young so that nothing extra holds the moisture. Eyes are large and the same ungraduated pewter as the lid of cloud, adapted to a light that never sharpens. Pupils stay wide. They do not squint. They lean. Faces are close by necessity and by custom: mouths move more than voices travel, and the warm breath that leaves one body is indistinguishable from the air that already coats the next. You know speech by the shape of lips and the small flex of throat more than by sound.

They are compact, low-centered, built for the suck of the surface and the sideways dodge around a sleeping flank. Joints stay loose; knees and ankles remember that every platform is only a suggestion of solidity. Hands are broad, fingers slightly spatulate, permanently wrinkled at the tips, good for packing lifts, for scraping a needle from rib, for the lean-in bargain across a dripping eave. Children are born already damp, already listening, and they learn the gait before they learn the signs: the slight give, the duck, the pause while the packed earth beneath them considers its next fraction of slump. Their first jewelry is cartilage strung on sinew, pale and flexible, clicking softly against collarbone in a world that has no more room for extra noise.

They do not stand apart from what they eat and what eats beside them. The wallowers are the architecture of the channels and the underfoot of the children. Massive, hairless, the exact bruise-tone of fresh silt, they lie half-submerged with only the ridged shelves of their backs and the two dark wells of their nostrils showing. Their skin is thick and faintly pebbled, always filmed, always warm. They shift by slow increments, a tectonic shrug that travels up through packed earth into the soles of anyone standing above. Calves are born in the same water, slick and silent, and are either taken or grow into the same long occupation of the same scrape. People walk across them the way they walk across platforms: as surface, as given. The wallowers do not startle. They have never known a world without weight on their backs.

In the cavities between cobbles live the eaters, packs of them, sharp-faced and low, the color of wet stone or a shade darker. They are all muscle and angle, no larger than a forearm, with needle teeth and eyes that catch the fat-lamp’s local orange and throw it back as chips of metal. They emerge without schedule because schedule is a rumor here. They take afterbirth, they take what falls, they take the small amphibians whose throats never stop working, and they take each other when the count tilts. Children learn early not to leave a hand unattended in a gap. The eaters do not hunt so much as occupy the same density the people occupy; they are the side and the middle and the underfoot, a second traffic moving through the first.

The birds never leave the bone eaves. Fist-sized, slick-feathered, they are the same wet pewter as everything else until they open a wing and show a dull iridescence that looks like oil on standing film. Their nests are packed with the same silt, lined with shed hide-scraps and the pale threads they pull from midden seams. Their voices are not separate cries but one continuous braid that has no beginning and no individual strand; it simply is, another layer in the standing roar, another liquid that has found its level. They take insects from the films, they take what the eaters miss, they take the smallest fish when a hand is not quicker. They do not migrate. There is nowhere else the light or the water or the noise is different.

Every sheet of water no deeper than a finger holds amphibians. Their bodies are the color of the underside of stones, pale or mottled gray, throats pulsing without rest, a second, slower percussion beneath the cobbles’ settling. They occupy the dark skirts of damp that climb every vertical plane. Fish, if they are fish, thread the shallows in numbers that turn a dipped hand into a living net. They are narrow, almost translucent, tasting of the silt itself. People lift them by the handful, string them, dry the strips on the inner faces of hides where the perpetual condensing never quite lets them become brittle. What is left goes to the eaters, to the birds, to the gray bloom that people scrape and feed to the smallest animals, a closed loop that does not tilt.

The simpler lives are older and lower still: the slick films on stone undersides that people sometimes lick for the faint mineral sting, the pale thread-growths that lace the oldest midden seams and are harvested with scrapers of splintered rib, the gray bloom itself, neither plant nor quite animal, a rumor of green translated into something the basin will allow. These are fed, scraped, packed back into the next lift. Nothing is waste. The city’s refuse is its foundation; the foundation is already becoming refuse again.

They live against one another the way the water lives against the silt: no true edge, only shallows and lessershallows. A sleeping-hollow scraped into an upper lift means one family’s drip is another’s ceiling sound, accounted for like kin. Animals are not kept; they are the keeping. A wallower’s shift is a household event. An eater’s pack moving through the cobbles is traffic. The birds’ braid is the air. Markets are only widenings where the same strips, the same needles, the same better hides change hands in the same proportions, bone beads at the throat catching the lean-in light of a fat-lamp that confirms the dim rather than changing it. No one is rich at a distance because distance itself is crowded, a pressure of bodies and breath and hide.

At the higher central lifts the bone frames are polished by generations of hands, the hides mapped with patches of other hides, and the people move through the heavier sound the way the fish move through water: by the knowledge that the way has not changed. They press ears to weeping stone not because they hear a decision but because listening is what has always been done. They pack the next lift to the same height. They sew the next seam. They lift the next handful of fish from a channel that will have as many tomorrow. Children are born into the roar and the wet and the dim and they die in them, and the count does not tilt. The wallowers calve. The eaters take. The birds braid. The amphibians pulse. The films persist.

They are not waiting. They are the held becoming, the first hour extended until the hour forgot the trick of ending. The warmth never leaves the skin. The water never chooses. What lives here is the basin, thinking itself in flesh and hide and packed earth, over and over, without remainder.
