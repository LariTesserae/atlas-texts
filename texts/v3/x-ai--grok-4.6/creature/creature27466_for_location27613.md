The lives here keep to the skin. That is the first and lasting fact, and everything else is only how the skin has learned to be crowded without ever filling the bowl.

Begin with what does not fly and does not speak except in numbers. The floor is a single organism that happens to be many: sphagnum and sedge and the black grit they have made their own flesh. In the wettest seams the frogs sit so still they are only another gloss on the stone until dusk, when their throats open and the hollow does what it was carved to do. The sound is not delicate. It is a stacked, vibrating architecture, thousands of small wet bells rung in a space that has been waiting since the ice left. You feel it in the soles before you understand it is animal. They are the color of the rock when the rock is thinking about rain. They do not leave. Their eggs are pinpricks of jelly in the rills; their dead are clean hinges found at the outflow, where the otter-shaped thing comes by night and does not stay. The frogs are the place’s oldest continuous voice after the water itself.

Above them the carpet flowers in flushes that look like mistakes until you see they are timed to the weeks when the seeps run hardest: sulfur, milk-white, that hole-blue that is denser than pigment. Cushion plants hold the heat of an hour’s noon the way a palm holds a coal. On every ledge that can keep a handful of grit the hanging gardens begin—ferns unrolling like wet script, liverworts in overlapping scales, orchids the size of fingernails so numerous certain seams look frosted even in the dim. Higher still, in the lee of overhangs, the woody things that grow downward: rope-stems, coin-leaves always wet on the hidden side. Nothing here bets on summer. Everything here has accepted the held cool, the daily cloud-animal, the fact that the air above them is mostly unused.

The air is used by the swifts. They live in the highest dark of the north wall, a stippling that is not decoration but a decision waiting to happen. When they erupt—shadow, incoming weather, the two-degree drop of evening—the scream is so complete it becomes part of the stone. You hear it in the bones of your head; the hollow turns it into nave and choir and clerestory all at once. They do not own the volume. They borrow it in blades and give it back. The choughs keep the rims instead, red-legged, riding the updraft the sun pulls off the eastern face, insolent commentators who will steal whatever you leave at the post and call it theology. A pale pipit works the carpet. A wren that has no business at this height lives anyway in the wettest ferns, a brown knot of refusal. At dusk the swifts return in such density the air seems briefly solid, then they are holes again, and what remains is only water and wind, which is still a crowd.

The larger animals are rumors that leave evidence. At dawn the hoofed thing the size of a heavy dog—face too straight, coat too dense—picks its way along the ledges and the pecked steps, dropping berry-dark signatures. It does not linger in the bowl; the bowl is weather it passes through. Something otter-shaped works the western notch after dark, neat and thorough. Insects are weather when the light is good: midge-columns over the brightest shine, tar-drop beetles on the cushions, a moth so pale it is a petal until it moves. None of this occupies the unused air. All of it keeps the vacancy from being death.

Human hands have been here, and are here still, but lightly, the way weather is light and relentless. The windbreak on the shelf is the only roof that was ever attempted, and it does not keep; it only turns the prevailing cloud. Inside, the ash is a stain that remembers fire the way the rock remembers ice. Someone knelt there. Someone still does, not often. The cairns are maintained by hands that know to use stone already loose. The steps are scooped by use as much as by water. The post at the eastern notch holds three moving things—the green copper blade, the dark fan of feathers, the small board that knocks—and offerings left there do not remain. The choughs see to that. In a niche no larger than a bread oven there is a cache of carved things (bone, soapstone, a fitted seed-pod) left by someone who treated the primitive as a finished technology, the way this mountain treats water. Those things stay. Whoever left them understood the rule: add nothing that cannot weather into the same sentence as the rest.

Stay longer and another inhabitant becomes unmistakable, though it has no single body. It is the keeping. Scent hangs here past reason. A word spoken on the floor can be met again, thinner, on a ledge a quarter-hour later, as if the rock were still deciding. Cloud that pours in will sometimes refuse the notches and rotate, a slow white room feeding every seep until the walls varnish themselves. On certain still twilights the frogs, the last swifts, the rills, and the knocking board find a common tempo that is not rhythm but a shared nervous system. It is not enchantment. It is the hollow doing its one job without remainder: being a bowl, acoustically and hydrologically sincere.

What lives in that sincerity is hard to separate from the place itself. It is cool the way a room that has never known fire is cool, and does not miss the fire. It weeps as architecture. It shouts with borrowed throats and then returns the shout slightly rearranged. It has opinions about being quarried. It takes the side of neither visitor nor resident; it takes the side of remaining a hollow. If there is a watcher, it is the one who has learned the three mosses that mean a ledge will hold and the one that is only a green lie. If there is a keeper, it is the one who adds a stone already loose, drinks from the higher seeps, and does not make a fire unless willing to be commented on by every swift. If there is a god, it is small, wet, numerous, and stacked. The cirque does not care which of these you choose. It continues. The ice finished the work and left. Everything that lives here is what arrived afterward and agreed to the terms: lush only where a finger could lie, loud as a market, empty as a lung at the top of its breath, old without ruin.

On the ridge, looking back, you see only a notch and a suggestion of depth. Inside, the surfaces are busy and the space between them is a ringing vacancy that has been occupied all along. That occupation is the inhabitant. It has no name it answers to. It weeps. It waits. It is enough.
