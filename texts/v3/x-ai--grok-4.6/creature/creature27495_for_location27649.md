The plateau does not host inhabitants so much as it continues them. Everything that persists here has already been edited by the same three editors—heat that never quite becomes fire, water that never quite becomes rain, and stone that never quite finishes dissolving. What lives does so at the edges of those processes, never claiming the center.

The crews come first only because they still remember names for things. They are few, and they move as if the air itself has assigned them routes. You see them at shift-change as pale figures in suits the color of wet sinter, walking the viaducts with the same unhurried gait the grazers use. Their speech is a private weather: short clauses exchanged at arm’s length, swallowed by humidity before they can become public. They do not linger in the open light. When they pause it is always in the lee of a hall or beneath an overhang where the film-walkers have already gathered, so that a worker standing still can look, for a moment, like another vertical plane of wet stone. They maintain what the manuals still require—valves, slurry intakes, the slow calibration of pressure vessels whose original purpose has outlasted the language that named it. Between those tasks they vanish into the spaced halls and become part of the pulse. At night their infrared signatures are indistinguishable from the stacks. They are not dying out. They are simply weathering at the same rate as the alloy they tend.

Closer to the ground, and far more numerous, the grazers work the crusts. They are low, many-legged, their bodies the exact mineral palette of the evaporites they consume: rust, verdigris, the dull silver of old batteries. Mouthparts rasp like files across the lacquer-thin deposits that form overnight. They do not hurry. A grazer may spend an hour on a single square meter, leaving behind a glittering track that the next warm sheet of rain will erase as thoroughly as if it had never been. They travel in loose consociations rather than herds; proximity is tolerated but not required. When a heavier rain blooms the crusts faster, they concentrate along the slurry lines and the lower benches, rasping in near-silence except for the faint, continuous hiss of mineral against chitin. Their internal water is held at the same temperature as the air. If you were to lift one—none of the crews ever do—it would feel like lifting a warm, articulated stone that has decided, for now, to walk.

The film-walkers occupy every vertical and underside surface that stays wet. They are almost two-dimensional, translucent membranes whose darker branching interiors show through like veins in a leaf that has forgotten chlorophyll. They live inside the perpetual sweat of rock and concrete and pipe. Movement is a negotiation with surface tension: they thin themselves, thicken themselves, send out temporary ridges that pull them forward a few centimeters at a time. A wall can be completely covered and still look merely wet until one of them elects to cross open air. Then it becomes a trembling lens, a brief, living distortion that catches the white glare and throws it in a new direction before it reattaches on the far side. They thicken after the heavier rains until entire façades seem to breathe. They freeze when a steam vent pauses, then reorient as one, a slow, collective adjustment that looks like the building itself considering a new thought. No one has counted them. They are as continuous as the film they inhabit.

Above, the kites ride the thermals that lift off the sinter flats. They are more membrane and vein than body, alignments of taut surface that catch the rising heat and hold it. They do not call. They do not even rustle. Flocks form, dissolve, reform according to rules written in temperature gradients rather than territory. Their shadows are the only moving dark on the overexposed ground. In the brief, almost-dry interval after evaporation accelerates and before the next warm sheet arrives, they descend. Jointed limbs—previously invisible, folded along the leading edges—unfold. They walk the drying films with the same unhurried precision as the grazers, harvesting something unnamed: a concentrate, a precipitate, a residue of the film-walkers’ own chemistry. Then they lift again, membranes re-tensioning, and the thermals take them. They are the only things that regularly leave the plateau’s horizontal plane, and even they return.

None of these lives is trying to become something else. There is no succession, no ambition toward forest or meadow. The rules remain mineral, thermal, hydraulic. The grazers eat what the centrifuges also want; the film-walkers thicken where the factories pulse hardest; the kites harvest what the drying interval leaves behind; the crews keep the machines that keep the differential that keeps the weather that keeps them all. Interfaces are the only real estate. Wet/dry. Hot/less-hot. Dissolved/precipitated. The city is simply the oldest and most deliberate of those interfaces, poured against the stone and now weathering at the same patient rate.

Stand still long enough and the individual drips become audible again, each one an exact, small event. The grazers rasp. A film-walker crosses a span of air and for a second the world has an extra lens. A kite’s shadow slides across the cracked pavement and is gone. A crew member appears, adjusts a valve whose purpose is older than their language, and vanishes. The vent exhales. The arrangement inhales. What lives here has already finished becoming what it is. It is only continuing, one warm sheet of rain at a time.
