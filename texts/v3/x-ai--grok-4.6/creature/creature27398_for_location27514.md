The films come first, because they always do. They are not quite moss and not quite scum; they are the Hollow’s first and last wet thought. In every crotch where sap has wept and diluted, in every hairline fracture that still holds a day’s melt, a skin of pale mucilage gathers, faintly sweet, faintly luminous if you smear it between fingers in the dimmer heartwood. It tastes of cold iron and unripe pear. The grazers live for this skin. They are the length of a forearm, translucent as peeled ginger, mouths a soft rasp of cartilage that never quite closes. They do not hunt. They migrate by the millimeter, following the film as it creeps after condensation, vanishing into slits no wider than a nail when the air grows too dry or a footfall too near. Their bodies leave no track except a brighter patch of cleaned bark that the film will reclaim by the next slow hour. Sometimes a grazer is caught in a freeze-thaw and becomes a small, perfect icicle with a dark seed of viscera at its heart; the others do not notice.

Larger things remember the grazers only as texture. The frost-walkers travel the undersides of the great spans, many-jointed, their limbs too numerous and too thin, articulated like frost itself deciding to stand. Where a foot has rested, a flower of ice crystals opens overnight, six-sided, faintly green at the center, and remains until the next growth-storm shears it away. They move with a sound like dry paper unfolding. No one has seen a mouth on them. They drink, if they drink, through the soles, drawing the last film of water from bark that has already given everything it can spare. When two meet they pause, limbs interleaving without touching, and then one continues and the other folds itself into a crevice and is gone for a season. Their trail of frost-flowers is the closest the Hollow comes to a path that can be followed twice.

The kites appear only when the air itself flowers. During a growth-storm the hanging keels rupture and the void fills with colorless scale, spores the size of thumbnails that catch the white-green glare and do not fall so much as hesitate. Then the membranes unfold from crevices no one marked: thin, veined, kite-shaped, the color of old ice. They ride the displaced air, folding and unfolding, sieving the burst for whatever protein the plants have thrown away. A flock—if the word still applies—may number in the hundreds, silent except for the faint drum-skin sound of their turning. When the storm ends they collapse inward, become packets no larger than a fist, and wedge themselves into the newest wound-wood to wait. Years may pass. The people who live lashed to the columns sometimes find a packet while scraping a stair and leave it; opening one releases a smell of winter apples and a brief, localized darkening of the glow, as if the plant itself had flinched.

There are others, rarer, that have not yet decided whether they belong to the footnotes or the architecture. In the oldest columns, where heartwood has hollowed itself into galleries the size of halls, something pale and slow pulps the spent fibers into a paste that glows a little warmer than the walls. It has no face. It has many small, patient mouths. The people call it only “the longer hunger” and do not cut those galleries open if they can help it. Once in a generation a span will shed a hanging fruit the size of a hut, fibrous, dripping antifreeze sap; when it splits, what emerges is not seed but a single, folded membrane larger than any kite, already dying, already being eaten back by the film. No one knows what it was meant to become.

The people themselves are the most visible accident. They are few enough that a traveler may go a month without seeing another settlement, and when one appears it looks like a scar the column has agreed, for now, to keep. Platforms of living wood, still green at the bindings, cinched with root-cable that must be loosened every season or the swelling trunk will crush them. Stairs that begin as cuts and become incorporated grain, the plant healing around the treads until the ascent is a spiral the structure itself seems to remember. They sleep in the dimmer cores where the glow is only a rumor and the air is thick with sugar-frost. They bleed the plants rather than fell them: a shallow tap, a cup of luminescent sap that will not freeze in the throat, a strip of bark peeled so cleanly the wound-wood seals before the next weather. When they must open a door they use the heirloom knives—metal so cold it welds to skin if you linger—and they speak to the cut the way one speaks to a storm that has already decided its path: not apology, exactly, but a naming of what will be taken and what will be left.

Their tools are the place’s own refusals: bone from the rare grazers that die in the open, clay fired in pockets where roots have burned mineral seams down to red earth, ice-glass sap flaked into edges that last a week. They have no cities because a city would be a sentence the Hollow has not finished. They move when a span begins to shiver, when the grain underfoot starts to align toward a new water they cannot yet see. Maps are spoken in the present tense and discarded by morning. Children learn the swirl of bark the way other children learn stars: this direction toward damp, that toward the ringing desert of dry wood where the glare becomes a blade and nothing weeps.

They notice the mild wrongness sooner than outsiders do. A cut made here will sometimes dim a column that cannot be seen, fifty kilometers off, a sympathetic flush of brighter sap traveling the hidden vascular dark. Condensation runs uphill for an hour. Once, the glow itself flickered—not off, never off—and in that blink some of them swear they saw the layers continue down, hollow after hollow, the same bright emptiness, the same unfinished plant sharpening itself through a planet that had forgotten it was supposed to have a core of stone. They do not make a religion of it. They make a practice: lash tighter, scrape the stair again, taste the sap for the faint change that means a growth-storm is already writing a new wall across a route they had a name for.

In the deepest wet pockets, where even the kites do not fold, something else is beginning to cohere. Not a people. Not yet a beast. A density of film and grazer and frost-walker and the longer hunger, all occupying the same tarn of diluted sap, their boundaries thinning. It pulses on the same slow rhythm as the distant columns. It has not moved. It does not need to. The Hollow is still deciding its shape, and this, too, is one of the shapes it has not finished refusing.

The air still smells of wet paper and winter fruit. Hands still ache. There is still nothing per cubic kilometer, and everything that lives here is, in the end, only another way the plant has found to remain mostly air, still cold, still almost silent, still becoming.
