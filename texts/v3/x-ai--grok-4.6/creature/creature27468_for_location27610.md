They come down from the ordinary grass in ones and twos and then in files that never quite become processions. They are not a people yet, only a density that has chosen the basin the way water chooses a low place. Their skins take the same pale dust the stone gives off when it is first wounded; after a few weeks the dust is no longer on them but of them, a second, mineral complexion that cracks at the corners of mouths and the creases of knuckles. Hair is bound or cropped short because anything longer would catch in the ropes or drink the lime-sweat from the walls. Eyes stay narrowed even in the cool shade; the habit of watching for a shifting block or a fraying lash outlives the daylight.

They move as if the air itself were another unfinished street they must not block. Shoulders angle, hips turn, loads swing in practiced arcs so that two files can pass on a ledge the width of a single body. Speech is spare and happens at the level of the work: a grunt that means the roller is binding, a click of tongue that means the next face is ready for the wedge. When they do talk of other things it is in the sleeping-rooms after the lid of night has come down, voices kept inside the teeth the way they were taught, because a raised word still feels like waste. Children learn this first. They play in the chip-heaps with the same hush, stacking flakes into miniature terraces that the next haul will erase, or chasing the pale lizard that sometimes appears and is gone before a hand can close.

There are families, but the word is still being cut to fit the place. A man and a woman and two small ones may share a rectangle of shade whose ceiling still shows the first wedge-marks; they sleep in shifts so the floor can hold more bodies than it has space. Hides serve for doors, for beds, for the slings that carry the smallest children while the parents work the same terrace. Newcomers arrive with the look of people who expected a town and found a wound; they are given chip-baskets until their hands understand this particular grain, until they stop trying to stand as if the ground were finished. Some leave after a season, climbing back to the rim with the same dust on them and a new stiffness in the shoulders. Most stay. The basin keeps them the way it keeps the seepage: not by force but by the absence of anywhere else that is being made this urgently.

They eat what can be carried: grain that arrives in sacks already pale with stone-flour, dried fish whose smell never quite leaves the lower ledges, a little oil that they guard more carefully than water. Fires are many and very small; wood is a rumor from the river that is not this place. Smoke threads up the walls and frays. In the mornings breath shows and fingers stiffen on the tools. They work until the light leaves, then work a little longer by the memory of the last cut. Some already live in rooms whose outer faces are still being dressed; they wake to the sound of chisels that are also the sound of their own walls becoming thinner, more precise.

There are no elders who remember another way. The oldest among them are those who have seen the basin deepen by the height of a man. They are the ones who argue, in gestures, about the cisterns, who mark the blocks that must not be taken, who teach the children that the weeping-place is not to be fouled. They have begun to carry themselves as if the stone’s calendar were also theirs: how many faces opened, how far the lowest street has dropped. A few have started to dream of the day the ramps will become streets that stay, of beams that have not yet been felled, of a well-ring raised high enough that the spring can be trusted. They do not say these things aloud. The basin has taught them that hope, like a raised voice, is a kind of rudeness toward the work that is still happening.

And there is the other thing that lives here, quieter even than the people. It is not the lizard, which is only a rumor with scales. It is not the moss that tries the new seams and mostly fails. It is the habit the stone is teaching them: to become architecture the same way the rock is becoming it, one unweathered face at a time, crowded, downward, almost silent. They are the gesture that has not yet decided it is a town. Tomorrow they will cut again. The geology is younger than some of their children. When they put a palm to a wall they feel the slightly greasy cool of rock that has only just learned air, and for a moment they cannot tell whether the pulse they feel is their own or the basin’s, still being born.
