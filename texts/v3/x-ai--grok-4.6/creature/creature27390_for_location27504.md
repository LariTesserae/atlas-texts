The groves do not announce themselves as beings. They simply occupy, the way a lung occupies a chest: densely, without remainder, as if emptiness itself had been an error they corrected long ago. Each island is a single crowded argument against the stone. What first reads as leaves is often not leaf at all but flattened conductive tissue, stacked and re-stacked until the interior of a grove becomes a kind of slow circuitry that happens to photosynthesize. The fronds are the color of wet iron because they are, in part, iron. They take the metal up in solution, plate it along veins so fine the eye registers only a darker sheen, then shed the excess as those rose-copper crusts and blue-black glasses that accumulate in the inner rooms. The mosses are load-bearing. They have thickened into cushions that hold the weight of everything above them and still manage, somehow, to bead with condensate; the beads are not decoration. They are the grove’s circulating river, a closed, vertical weather that never quite falls.

Inside that weather the smaller lives keep their own clocks.

The mid-canopy browsers move as if time had been diluted. They are not sloths; sloths still remember trees that once competed for sun. These have the elongated grip of something designed around a single, inexhaustible meal. Six long digits per limb, each tipped with a keratin that has mineralized into a dull metallic claw, lock onto stems that themselves pulse with faint current. The animals do not climb so much as adhere and wait. Their pelts are not fur exactly: a dense, hydrophobic pile that traps the same film of moisture the fronds trap, so that each creature wears a second, temporary skin of cold water. They feed by rasping. The mouth is a ridged, almost crystalline pad that scrapes the metal-rich epidermis and then, with a slowness that looks like thought, dissolves what it has taken. They do not swallow in any ordinary sense. The slurry travels a short, electrically active gut and is sorted there: sugars and water retained, surplus metal precipitated into tiny nodules that are later excreted as the glassy coins found lower down. They taste constantly. Taste is how they navigate the grove’s internal gradients, how they know which stem is still drawing from a rich vein and which has begun to slough. They never call. Sound would be a waste of the thin air they have so little of.

Lower, where the slag-rooms open like cold forges, the grazers work the residues. These are paler, almost translucent in the standing dark, their bodies flattened and low, as if evolution had decided height was an extravagance. The mouth is a living spoon: a broad, slightly concave keratin plate that scrapes along the alloy crusts with a sound too soft to carry more than a body’s length. They move in loose constellations, never quite a herd, each animal following the faint thermal and chemical traces left by the one ahead. Their droppings steam because the metals they have concentrated still hold a remnant heat, a last chemical restlessness; then the steam freezes and the coins remain, little convex mirrors that catch the dull forge-glow of the larger stems and throw it back in fragments. Over seasons that are not seasons the coins accumulate into shallow pavements. The grazers walk those pavements without ceremony. They are recycling the grove’s exhaust, returning a fraction of the refined metal to the root-felts so the whole island can keep its books.

Higher, where the canopy thins toward the bruise-colored lid of sky, the fliers turn the electric weather into map. They are the size of an open hand, membranous, almost two-dimensional when at rest. The membranes are veined with the same fine metallic traces as the fronds; they flex not by muscle alone but by minute shifts in charge, so that a flier can hang in the still air of a grove as if the air itself had decided to hold it. They navigate by tasting the faint currents that run along the stems—the same currents that make the larger trunks glow at the deeper register of night. A flier will hover, dip, correct, hover again, always just outside the strongest fields, feeding on the smallest condensates and on the mites that live in the bead-rivers of the moss. They are numerous and almost invisible until one of them is taken.

The taker is a long fold of the same wet-iron color as the leaves. It spends most of its life vertical, compressed against a stem or a hanging mass of tissue, indistinguishable from the grove until the moment it is not. The unfolding is silent and nearly instantaneous: a body that had been a stripe becomes a length, a mouth that had been a line becomes a precise, unhurried aperture. The flier is gone before the mind that watches has finished assembling the event. The predator does not chase. There is nowhere to chase to, and the air will not support a long pursuit. It simply arrives at the same coordinate the flier occupied and then, still without sound, folds the body inward, toward the warmer, more conductive currents near the big stems. Digestion there is another kind of refining. What remains is a small, darker slag that the grove will eventually grow around.

Out on the open stone the lives are larger and farther apart, as if the highland had decided that density was a privilege of the islands.

The walkers appear first as a regularity in the frost-dust: prints that are not quite the size a human mind expects, spaced with a patience that belongs to thin air and long metabolic ledgers. Some lineages have six limbs, some four; both work. The extra pair, when present, is held close to the barrelled chest and used for the sudden damp—adhesive pads that can lock onto polished metamorphic the instant a film of melt appears. The chest itself is a compromise written in bone and cartilage: enough volume to pull what little oxygen the standing dark affords, not so much that the animal becomes a sail for the thin wind. They travel between groves on schedules that have nothing to do with light. They come for the condensate that beads on the outer fronds, for the salts that bloom on the slag-falls, and, some of them, to return a body. Death among the walkers is a delivery. An animal that has reached the end of its own chemistry will choose a particular island—always the same one, across generations—and enter it the way a shipment enters a warehouse. The grove does not refuse. The body is taken apart with the same unhurried electrolysis the plants apply to ore. What can be used is used. The rest becomes another terrace of intentional residue.

A few of the walkers carry their own banked glow, a deep-body phosphor that is the same dull industrial color as the stems. These are the lanterns. Others, darker, travel at a courteous remove, using the first kind as moving hearths. Predation on the stone is almost liturgical. Two shapes arrive at the same pocket of melt. There is a brief, formal rearrangement of mass and heat. One shape leaves heavier. The blood freezes in scallops that the next rime will sand away. No cry carries. The highland has no interest in punctuation.

There are smaller lives still, almost too slight for the census. In the momentary films of moisture, things like nematodes but threaded with metallic inclusions flex and feed on the dissolved traces the groves have already concentrated. On the undersides of the oldest fronds, colonies of a colonial organism that is neither plant nor animal pulse in slow synchrony with the stem-currents, adding their own faint increment to the glow. Even the rime itself is not sterile. When it sublimes it leaves behind a dust of spores and mineralized husks so fine the wind cannot quite lift them; they wait in the lee of every lip of rock for the next upwelling.

None of this is waiting to be seen. The groves continue their long extraction. The browsers rasp. The grazers spoon. The fliers correct their charge. The long predators unfold and fold again. The walkers keep their quiet deliveries across distances that would be loneliness if loneliness were a concept the place had ever needed. What lives here has already finished negotiating with the stone. It has taken the mountain’s leftover veins and its intermittent water and its civil geothermal heat and turned them into a metabolism that looks, from a certain angle, like industry, and from another like the only kind of forest this altitude was ever going to allow.

If you remain, the images stop arriving separately. They begin to share a single slow pulse: the tick of a stem, the scheduled brightening of a lantern-walker far out on the cirque, the almost inaudible unpeeling of a frond that has just taken another micron of metal from the dark. That pulse is what lives here. It has been living here long enough that the difference between inhabitant and place has worn as smooth as the stone itself.
