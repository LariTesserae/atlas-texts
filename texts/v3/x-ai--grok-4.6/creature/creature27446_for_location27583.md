The people here are compact in the way of things grown under low pressure and constant moisture. Shoulders slope inward, spines hold a habitual slight curve as if listening for the next lift in the floor. Skin takes the color of the stone after three days of cooling—matte, faintly greened in the creases of knuckles and eyelids, never quite dry. Hair is worn short or bound close; longer strands collect condensate and the copper moss that seeds itself in any stillness. They dress in layered, dark cloth that has been oiled and re-oiled until it shines like wet bark, the hems weighted with thin slates cut from last month’s vents. Nothing is new for long. Patches of fern-fiber and rust-dark leather accumulate until a coat becomes a map of the wearer’s last several years of adjustments.

They do not walk so much as thread. Alleys are measured in breaths and shoulder-widths; a person learns early to turn the body into a key that fits the day’s particular gaps. Conversations happen at the exact moment two walls have not yet closed, or in the steam that lifts from tea-stalls wedged beneath conveyor belts. Voices stay low because the air itself is already full—of drip, of the subsonic press of new stone, of the faint mineral hiss that means a vent is still remembering how to be solid. Names are short, often shared, and frequently abandoned when a tenement rotates or a foundry is left to moss. People introduce themselves by the current lean of their floor or the particular vent they worked last week.

Children are born already knowing how to wait. They play in the temporary tarns that form in yesterday’s foundation pits, catching the pale frogs whose skin tastes of iron and algae. They learn the three-day rule before they learn numbers: newest stone is still speaking, still deciding whether it will hold a nail or a root. A child who touches it too soon comes away with fingers that stay cold for hours and a faint ringing in the ears, as if the rock had tried to tell them something they were not yet shaped to hear. They grow into the same economy as their parents—hands that can jack a floor level in the dark, eyes that read the bioluminescent edge of certain mosses the way other people once read street signs.

The other lives are just as densely arranged. The silent birds—dark, short-legged, never calling—nest in the lee of heat exchangers and ride the gantries like living ballast. Their nests are lined with shed moss and the metallic wing-cases of the pale insects that graze the algae on every permanently wet surface. Those insects themselves live in vast, quiet colonies; their wing-cases catch the thinned daylight and throw it back as a dull, mineral shimmer that the night-shift workers have learned to use as additional signage. The fox-things (longer in the spine, quieter than any fox has a right to be) thread the alleys only when the mist is thickest. They eat rust flakes, the small frogs, and occasionally the shed skin of the newest stone before it hardens. People leave them offerings of cooling slag; the arrangement is old enough that no one remembers who began it.

Cats, of course. They have claimed the warm pockets behind transformer housings and the vibrating pipes that never quite stop. Their fur holds the same damp sheen as everything else; they walk the shifting geometry as if it were the only ground they have ever known. Nothing here is wild. Everything has made a contract with the density, the damp, the slow embryonic shrug of the basin that has not yet learned to be still.

There are also the ones who stay longer than a human life. Certain mosses have been cultivated across generations until they function as living infrastructure: copper strains that glow at the exact frequency the night crews need, grey mats that bind loose grit when a new blister rises too fast, a dark, almost black variety that the oldest workers chew to keep the mineral taste of the stone from settling permanently in the back of the throat. The low, densely needled trees that grow from foundry roofs and half-swallowed smokestacks are not quite trees anymore; their roots have learned to drink the still-warm runoff and their needles shed a fine, iron-scented dust that the people sweep into the canals as a kind of offering. The ground itself is treated as a slow, inattentive neighbor. You do not build higher than the mist will hide. You speak to a wall that has begun to occupy your window, or you wait, or you plant ferns across the closing gap until the geometry changes again.

No one claims to know what the basin will be when it finishes. The people live inside the sentence, densely, quietly, rebuilding the same streets from the same unfinished stone. They jack, they recut, they grow moss across the newest cracks. At night the city glows in isolated, practical patches and the fox-things move like thoughts the stone has not yet decided to keep. Everything is mid-formation. The inhabitants are simply the part that has learned, for now, how to breathe the wet iron air and call it home.
