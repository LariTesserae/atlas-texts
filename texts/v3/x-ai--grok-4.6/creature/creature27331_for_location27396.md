The first lives here are not yet sure they are lives. They occupy the only interval the pour left them: the joints, the hairline refusals, the places where one slab overrode another and never quite forgot the seam. They are the gray-green things, neither lichen nor rush, a decision the chemistry made when a little dust collected and a little night-damp lingered an hour too long. Each stands as if it has already been cooked and has chosen, after the cooking, to remain upright. Their color is the color of old copper left in rain, or of a bruise that never quite yellowed. They do not shade anything. They do not collect into meadows. They punctuate. A solitary blade will occupy a hundred meters of joint, its edges browned to the exact tint of the paler plates, as though it has learned the local climate by becoming a slightly more organized version of the heat itself.

Their roots do not descend. Descent has not been invented here; there is nothing beneath the plates but more of the same intention, still warm. The roots travel laterally, mining the binder-dust, the salt rims, the microscopic grit the wind has already begun to polish into existence. In the thin hours they drink the condensation that beads along the raised lips. By mid-morning the shine is gone and they stand in their own small drought, waiting. When the rain-wall arrives they do not rejoice; they endure the percussion, then drink what the films leave behind before the sun takes it back. A few, in the slightly concave provinces that hold water a day longer, have managed clumps—three or five stems together, a darker stain at their base that might be moss or might only be the first rumor of moss. These clumps lean with the organized wind, all one way for hours, then the other, as if practicing a gesture they have not yet been given a reason for.

Smaller lives occupy the same interval and do not stand. In the vanishing lenses—those wrist-deep mistakes of water, clear as air that forgot to be empty—something briefly organizes. It is not visible as color. It is a faint increase in the water’s reluctance to vanish, a skin that forms and is gone by the second afternoon. Whatever it is tastes the binder, the residual fever of the darker uncured fields, the salt the plates sweat. It does not persist. Persistence has barely been budgeted. Between rains there is only the night-damp and the ticking, and in that ticking the plates themselves perform a kind of respiration: contraction, a fractional settling, the sound of a body that is still deciding its temperature. The uncured patches hiss when moisture finds them. The hiss is not voice. It is chemistry still alive, steam lifting from a pour that has not yet agreed to be finished. If there is a metabolism here larger than the plants, it is this: the slow exudation that thickens into the next slab, the dry fall of same-colored dust from a sky that has been asked to participate. The making continues without tools, without hurry, a mineral milk rising in the seams as if the joints themselves remember how to pour.

No one walks the plates except the wind, and the wind does not live here; it only organizes itself across the fetch and then forgets. Nothing hunts. Nothing has been hunted. The first cracks are still only thinking about becoming valleys; they have not yet offered shade or soil enough for anything that would need either. What lives does so by remaining sparse, by following the failures, by treating the residual heat as a climate rather than an injury. In the far middle distances, where the material is still matte and the heat lifts in visible sheets, even the gray-green things thin to almost nothing. There the lives are smaller still, or slower: films, stains, the suggestion of a root that has not yet found a joint worth following. Scale keeps them separate. One clump cannot see the next. Distance here is low-saturation, a made nothing in which presence itself feels like an afterthought the construction did not quite refuse.

Toward evening the convection collapses and the plates give back the day as a low infrared that the sparse plants do not flinch from. They have already been browned; they have already chosen this oven. In the dark the ticking travels farther, a quiet active sound under the still air. It is possible to stand in that sound and feel, without quite naming it, that the basin is inhabited by its own unfinishedness—the exudation, the contraction, the first idea of erosion, the plants that are punctuation rather than flora. Nothing has been poured yet that would give them names or purposes. They remain what the joints allowed: thin, gray-green, cooked at the edges, mining what little the made ground sweats, waiting for the next brief rain and the next fraction of settling. The rest—the long future in which these embryonic lives might thicken into something a later world would recognize—has not arrived. The heat keeps rising through the soles. The plates keep their residual fever. What lives here does so by staying almost as new as the ground.
