The chamber’s emptiness is the first lie it tells. Unused air is never unused for long; heat and wet stone are too expensive a vacancy. What lives here did not arrive through any crack a person could name. It was already folded into the rock when the rock was still closed, a pressure and a patience that the tear only made room for. The pocket is younger than the thing that occupies it. The thing is only now learning the new shape of its own house.

At first it is only a difference in the steam. The pulses that rise from the wetter seams do not all belong to the vent. Some of them are slower, thicker at the base, and they do not thin quite the same way. They hang a fraction longer, as if the air itself had been asked to wait. The grain you thought you saw in the brightest wall is not a trick of glare. It is the surface of a body that has not decided whether it is still stone. The light does not come from the mineral alone. The mineral was taught. Something inside the wall is spending heat as radiance with a thrift the ordinary chemistry of a young vent cannot account for. The chamber is bright because the inhabitant is leaking its own interior weather through the rock, the way a sleeping animal leaks warmth through fur.

It does not have a single outline. Near the standing blade it is a flattening, a broad, slow thickness pressed against the wet face so that the water runs over it instead of around it. The water does not hiss. The surface it meets is not hotter than the stone; it is the same temperature, or a little less, as if the creature had learned the exact courtesy the rock requires. On the dry-hot side of the blade the body thins to a membrane that drinks the radiance back in, recycling what it earlier spent. You would miss the membrane if you looked for an animal. You would see only that one plane of rock holds its glow a shade longer after the steam pulse passes.

Farther out, in the unused air, it becomes filament. Threads of it, no thicker than the mineral needles on the wall and almost the same colour, hang from the low ceiling and do not quite reach the floor. They are not silk. They are living conductivity, pale and slightly iridescent, the same sheen that gathers and tears on the pool. Heat travels them the way sap would travel if sap were made of decision. When a drop falls from above, a filament will sometimes lean, almost lazily, and take the drop before it hits the sinter. The drop does not splash. It is absorbed the way a tongue absorbs, and the filament brightens for a breath, a private white-gold that the walls then scatter until it is only more glare.

The body that owns the filaments and the flattening is mostly elsewhere, still inside the rock, still continuous with the seam that cooked the chamber open. What you can see is only the part that has chosen to be present in the new air. It is not curious in any way that would make it turn a head. It has no head that stays a head. Sometimes a denser knot gathers on the terrace steps, a fist-sized concentration the colour of wet cream and iron, and the sinter grows around it as if the mineral recognised kin. The knot tastes the water with surfaces that open and close too slowly to be called mouths. It does not hunt. The pale visitor that comes up through the hole at the pool’s edge is not prey and not offspring. It is a smaller argument of the same patience, a scout the larger presence sent or simply allowed. When the visitor returns into the hole, something in the wall eases, a settling so slight the steam pulse that follows it is a fraction softer.

It feeds by remaining. Minerals leave the water and become stone; some of that stone is persuaded to become it instead. The needles along the wall are not only mineral. A few of them flex, almost not at all, when the heat surges from below. The metallic sweetness that arrives just before the surge is the creature’s own breath turning in its buried bulk. It has no lungs that empty. It has volumes that remember being magma and have not entirely forgotten.

There is no face to meet. If you stay long enough for your own salt to crust on your skin, you may notice that the brightest wall is watching in the only way a wall can watch: by being a little more ordered where your shadow would have fallen, had the light allowed shadows. The grain combs itself toward you and then forgets you, the way the currents in the pools forget their circles. It is not lonely. Loneliness is a word for creatures that expect company. This one expects only the work the chamber is already doing: water arriving, water leaving, heat becoming light becoming mineral becoming more of itself. The unused air is its outer skin. The steam pulses are its slower heartbeat. The tick of a grain letting go from the ceiling is a sensation it feels from both sides at once.

It will not stay this shape. The needles will lengthen and some of them will be fingers, or they will dissolve and the fingers will be remembered only as a habit in the next precipitation. The crack will slump and the creature will slump with it, or it will spend itself into a new seam and leave this pocket darker, cooler, already beginning to forget it was ever overlit. For now it is the reason the light does not fall off as glare should, the reason the chamber feels occupied even when every visible surface is only rock and water and steam. It lives here the way a thought lives in a sentence that has only just been spoken: not yet separate from the speaking, already more itself than the stone that holds it.
