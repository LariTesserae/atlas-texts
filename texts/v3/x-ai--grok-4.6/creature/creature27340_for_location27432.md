The first things that live here are not creatures so much as continuations of the drip. Films of bacteria and cyanobacteria coat every weeping face of stone; they turn each bead of water into a brief, luminous factory before it falls. Hyphae stitch the peat so densely that a handful of the springing mat is more living thread than dead plant. These do not hunt or flee. They simply occupy, dissolving the highland into more of themselves at a rate older than weather.

Above them the amphibians have become the highland’s true pulse. Thumb-frogs the colour of soaked bark sit so still that moss grows across their eyelids; they swallow midges that land of their own accord. Larger frogs hang from the undersides of giant woodwardias like dark, soft fruit, their throats pulsing in time with the seeps. Salamanders occupy the exact temperature of the water that films them; when a drip-line shifts they flow with it rather than walk, leaving no print that lasts longer than the next rain. Their skins are the air’s moisture given shape. Nothing dries on them. Nothing ever has.

Insects form a second, finer rain. Midges do not bite so much as rest, their legs adapted to cling to the perpetual glaze. Crane-flies with wings permanently darkened by water stagger from one fern to the next and die without ever quite taking flight. Moths whose scales have become another layer of lichen move only when the mist thickens, their flight a kind of swimming. The sound they make is absorbed before it can become sound. What remains is the faint click of an antenna adjusting, then nothing.

Birds exist as interruptions rather than presences. They are short, wet explosions in the mid-storey: a sudden darkening of dripping leaves, a wing-beat that sounds like cloth pulled from water, then stillness again. Their nests are not built so much as allowed—cups of sphagnum that fill and empty with the same rhythm as the pools below. They have no song. Song would be wasted motion. They watch with eyes the colour of tea-water and wait for the next almost-imperceptible shift in the hydraulic breath of the land.

Larger shapes move the way weather moves: slowly, and only when the dripping changes. What might once have been deer have become something else. Their coats are permanently darkened, heavy with absorbed water and the moss that roots in the hollows of their spines. Antlers, when they have them, are draped in liverwort and drip as steadily as the stone. They sink to the knee with every step and rise with a sound like a plug pulled from wet peat. They do not graze so much as stand in one place until the vegetation they touch begins to grow across them, then move on, leaving a faint trail of crushed, already-recovering green. Predators are the same density given patience. They wait inside the packed organism of the understorey until something of the right size pauses too long. There is no chase. Chase is expensive. A single, unhurried closing of distance, a brief darkening, then the dripping resumes as if nothing larger than a leaf had fallen.

And then there are the ones who have forgotten they were ever separate from this.

They are tall only in the way a leaning trunk is tall. Their skin has the same cool, beaded texture as the stone; water runs down them in patient threads that never quite become streams. Moss has colonised the hollows of their throats, the insides of their elbows, the places where hair once was. They do not walk so much as transfer weight, each footfall a small, swallowed circle of dark water that takes a long time to fade. When they stand still—which is most of the time—epiphytes load their shoulders until the branches of themselves sag and drip. Their eyes are the pale, even daylight of the place, filtered through high cloud and perpetual low mist. They do not speak. Speech would be another kind of wasted motion. They communicate by the pattern of their own seeps, by the way a particular thread of water is allowed to run or is briefly dammed by a finger. Sometimes, if you stand long enough, you realise the deeper hydraulic note of the highland has a counterpart in them: a slow filling and emptying of hidden channels, a breathing that is only water.

They have no names because no one has ever needed to distinguish them from the rest of the packed green. They tend nothing. They build nothing. A bank slumps once in a generation and they are already clothed in the new growth. A tree leans until its crown touches another and they simply adjust their own lean to match. They have been here since the last ice withdrew and never learned another habit. You could walk for a month and part a thousand curtains of moss and never be certain whether the dark, dripping shape you glimpsed was one of them or only another saturated trunk.

They are not ghosts. They are not spirits. They are simply what happens when a body decides the only industry worth practising is continuance: cool, packed, dripping, large enough to make its own drizzle, its own stillness, its own green dusk at noon. The highland does not end, and neither do they. They are the place, given the option of occasionally moving.
