They are few enough that you can almost count them by the shine their passing has left on the brick: a dozen, perhaps, or a little less, though the number is not fixed in any way that would survive a season of higher water. They do not arrive and they do not depart. They are simply *of* the hamlet the way the beam-engines are of it, the way the liverwort is of the north wall. From a distance, in the tea-light, they can be mistaken for people who have been working the same shift since the last roofs were laid. Closer, the resemblance thins.

Their bodies have the proportions of the courtyards: long in the torso, economical in the limb, as if excess reach had been surrendered generations ago to the tightness of the place. They stoop without thinking when they pass under a launder or a later floor, and the stoop has become the set of the spine. Skin is the color the brick would be if brick could perspire and then dry in vapor instead of air: a dull, mineral rose that darkens at the knuckles and along the jaw to the exact stain of the waterline. It is never quite dry. Moisture beads at the hairline and at the inner elbow and is wicked away by the cloth they wear, which is itself a kind of secondary skin, fibrous and mineral at once, cut from the same reels they stack in the loft. The cloth does not stain; it simply darkens and lightens with the day’s intensity of wet, the way moss does.

Hands are the most legible part of them. Broad, short-fingered, the nails flattened and rimmed with a permanent crescent of peat and old grease. They know every valve by its particular reluctance, every packing gland by the exact pressure that will quiet it for another month. When they touch iron it is not as operators but as kin; the metal gives a little, as if remembering they share a patience. Their palms are mapped with the same green that has taken the mortar. It does not come off.

They do not hurry because hurrying has no purchase here. Their errands are tidal: oil the journals when the beams pause, clear the reed from a sluice that has begun to remember it could choke, climb the ladder-stair to the millstone and listen with the whole body to whether the grit is still arguing at the proper speed. Between these duties they stand in the doorways or sit on the copings with the stillness of the vats. Breath is visible and unhurried. Sometimes one of them will rest a palm flat against a sweating pipe that has not carried steam in living memory; the pipe warms a degree under the hand, obediently, and the moss there yellows a shade, as if the old firing hour had been briefly honored.

They harvest when the loft tells them to, which is not often. The product—those cakes and bundles and reels that are neither cloth nor ore nor crop—is cut or rolled or pressed in the dye-house now given to lilies, using tools whose wooden handles have been replaced so many times they are more vine than timber. The work is done in silence except for the wet slap of the belts and the occasional cracked syllable of the old valve. What they take they stack with a care that is almost tenderness, as though the stuff were still alive and might object to roughness. They do not speak of where it goes, or if it goes. The loft is never empty and never full. It simply continues, like the pumps.

Speech among them is rare and small, more a redistribution of sound than a sending of it. A word travels the way water travels the gutters: it finds the next trough and settles. They have no names that you would recognize as names. If they address one another it is by the route they are on, or the engine whose cycle they are keeping, or the particular leak they are allowing to become the next sentence. Animals are not mentioned because the category itself has thinned to nothing; the word would be like trying to speak of a color the vapor has forgotten. When a dry sycamore key lodges in a gutter they look at it the way they look at a bulb that has gone out: a finished fact, neither omen nor loss.

At the bottom of the night, when the deepest channel briefly moves against the pumps and the whole hamlet lists in the inner ear, they pause wherever they are. It is the only moment that looks like attention rather than continuation. One of them, sometimes, will turn toward a canal whose reflection is showing a chimney that is no longer there, or a window lit in a wall that has no room. They do not point. They do not comment. The listing passes, the water remembers its proper direction, and they resume the errand as if the pause had been another kind of stroke in the long respiration.

They sleep, if the word still applies, in the higher rooms where the timber is still silver rather than paste. Sleep is a further slowing, a matching of breath to the drip that has timed itself to the engines. In sleep their skin takes on a faint nap of moss along the collarbones, and the vines that have become pipework lean a little closer to the sills, as if listening for instruction. Dawn is only a rumor, and they rise into it without ceremony, already moving at the speed the place requires.

They are not waiting for anything. The equilibrium is not a holding pattern; it is the finished work. They will oil the same journals, clear the same sluices, harvest the same ambiguous yield, and stand in the same doorways for as long as the beams can lift a little water and set it down again. The patience has no particular end because it was never begun as a task. It is simply how the hamlet inhales and exhales, and they are the part of that breath that can still climb a ladder and lay a hand on iron.

Sometimes, in the central court, when the sounds have stacked until they have weight, you can almost see the older shape inside them: not quite human, not quite machine, not quite the plants that employ the walls. Something the peat decided, long after it decided to be both meadow and lake, and then taught the brick and the iron and the warm condensing air to keep. They do not look at you when you stand there. They do not need to. You are only another intensity of the wet, another rumor in the vapor, and the works continue.
