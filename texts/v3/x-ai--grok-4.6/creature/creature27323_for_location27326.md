The first things to take hold are the horsetails. They do not arrive so much as remember themselves into being, pale-green columns that rise overnight from the still-plastic clay, their joints so new they flex like cartilage. Each one lives by a patient hydraulics: it drinks the film of condensation that never quite becomes rain, stores it in hollow internodes, and lifts it higher than any surrounding leaf. They do not compete. They simply occupy the air the way the dynamo occupies sound, filling every vertical interval until the basin feels faintly pressurized, as though the sky itself were being held up by vegetation.

Around their bases the ferns uncurl with a wet, audible sound, like paper being unfolded under water. Their fronds are maps of the same condensation that weeps from the scarps; every pinna holds a bead that never falls. The mosses live lower and slower still. They knit the rails into the ground, swallowing the bright steel so completely that a walker feels only a faint, regular hardness underfoot, like ribs beneath skin. In the groves of alder and willow the leaves are so dark they seem to drink light rather than reflect it. These trees are young in years but already ancient in manner: they lean toward one another as if conferring, their bark still smooth, their roots already probing the clay for the memory of older water.

The animals treat all of this as inheritance rather than invasion. Deer paths appear between one dusk and the next, pressed into the moss with the same unhurried certainty as the rails they cross. The otters come and go according to the channel’s mood; when it holds even a finger’s depth they occupy it as though it had always been a river. The herons nest in the mill’s rafters, weaving sticks through the iron trusses until the building looks less like a factory than a tree that learned, late in life, how to make a louder sound. Bats pour from the powerhouse eaves each evening in a single, deliberate exhalation, as if the dynamo itself were breathing them out. Midges hover in trembling columns that never quite dissolve; grasshoppers rattle inside the horsetails like small engines. Even the frogs, when they slap the dark pockets of water, do so with a measured percussion that braids into the dynamo’s roar rather than competing with it.

The workers are fewer and stranger. There are never more than a dozen visible at once, and they move through the lush gaps the way figures move through a painting that is still being painted. Their clothes are always the same damp ochre as the clay, oil-dark at the cuffs, never quite dry. They do not hurry because the work itself has chosen a geological tempo: a press that cycles three times in an afternoon, a flywheel that sighs like a sleeping animal, a single narrow-gauge truck that arrives twice a month with the apologetic whistle of something that knows it is late even when it is not. Their faces have the pallor of the scarps. Their eyes hold the weak-tea color of the channel. They speak little; when they do, the words are swallowed by the brick and returned as part of the general pressure in the chest. At dusk they stand in the open cottage doorways and watch the bats, or they simply stand, as if waiting for the clay to finish settling before they decide what to become next.

Something larger occupies the long shed. The knocking is not mechanical. It is the sound of a great, slow heart—or a body turning over in sleep the size of a room. Whatever lives there shifts only a few times a day, each movement accompanied by the deep clang of iron remembering it is also stone. The workers enter and leave without comment, carrying tools that look too small for the work, or perhaps the work is not what it appears. The dynamo itself feels inhabited: its note is too continuous, too perfectly matched to the tremor in the central pool, as though the machine and the water were two ends of the same thought.

That pool is the quietest inhabitant and the most awake. No larger than a table, it never stills. It trembles in exact time with the dynamo, and its reflection of the sky lags by a fraction of a second, as if the water were remembering the light rather than receiving it. Stand beside it long enough and the delay becomes a presence. Something lives in that lag—something that has not yet decided whether it is the basin’s first memory or its last. The herons ignore it. The workers pass it without looking down. The horsetails lean over it at dusk and drink from a surface that is always slightly in the past.

All of them—plants, animals, workers, the thing in the shed, the delayed sky in the pool—live at the same patient tempo because the basin itself is still becoming. The clay has not finished choosing its shape. The machines have not finished choosing whether they are engines or organs. The vegetation has not finished choosing how tall the world should be. What lives here is therefore not a single creature but a single unfolding: a place that collapsed into being last winter (or the winter before) and is now teaching itself, very slowly, how to be alive. Everything that occupies it is both inhabitant and instruction, both tenant and first draft. The rust blooms in perfect circles. The rails brighten even as they disappear. The knocking continues, unhurried, like a heart learning its own name.
