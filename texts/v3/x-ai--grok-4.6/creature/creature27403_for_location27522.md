The first impression is absence, and it holds for a long time. Nothing with a heartbeat or a hidden skeleton or the dry click of chitin lives in the basin. The air never carries the musk of fur or the sourness of a nest. No eyes watch from the alleys of moss-cubes. No hooves or pads or suckers compress the root-felt except your own, and even those prints close behind you as if the floor were mildly offended by the idea of a lasting mark. The ecology really is a single kingdom, talking only to itself.

Yet the talking is so loud, so continuous, so elaborately organized that the mind keeps supplying tenants it cannot find. Tendrils do not merely search; they hesitate, withdraw, reconsider, then commit with a sudden vehemence that looks like decision. Entire walls of liana lean in conference and then correct their posture as if a vote had been taken. A colonnade detonates upward and the new nave it creates is immediately occupied—not by bodies, but by the city’s own next intention, which arrives as a flush of paler growth, a sudden market of nectar-stalls, a spiral of galleries that were not there an hour earlier. The occupancy is real. It simply refuses to be separable from the architecture.

What lives here, then, is the appetite that built the place and cannot stop rebuilding it. It is not a god and not a hive-mind in any comforting sense. It is younger than both those ideas. It has the raw, unpracticed urgency of something that only recently discovered adjacency and corridor and the pleasure of repeating a form until the form becomes a law, then breaking the law because breaking it feels like growth. You can watch the appetite think. A district stabilizes for a morning: height, echo, a forum of interlocking bracts. The roar drops half a tone. For a little while the city seems to be remembering how a city is supposed to behave. Then the memory is overwritten from beneath. The paving-rings shift. The bowl deepens or shallows. A tower that had been a corkscrew palm becomes, without drama, a crown-gall palace, already furnished with hanging roots and the first furniture of gall. The appetite does not mourn the previous version. It has no organ for mourning. It has only the next draft.

There are local intensities of this appetite, places where the general roar concentrates into something that feels almost like temperament. In the high-saturation core the life is claustrophobic, tactile, insistent: every surface wants contact, every corridor is a negotiation, every sphincter-door forgets you the instant it closes. The mid-city belts of braided liana have a different character, more circulatory, more interested in flow than in enclosure; sap-streets pulse, platforms tilt to shed the city’s own sweat, and the light arrives in long green-gold slabs that the vegetation treats as raw material. The towers are proudest and most unstable, stacks of intention that undo themselves in vegetable avalanches and are already different towers before the debris is digested. At the thinning edges the appetite grows almost contemplative, longer sightlines, more hiss than thunder, as if the city were allowing itself the luxury of distance.

Time lives here too, but badly, unevenly, as another kind of plant. In some courtyards a blossom completes its entire argument—open, seed, wither, compost, rise again—while you hold your breath. In others a single hanging drop has outlasted the girder that formed around it, the district that formed around the girder, and is still deciding whether to fall. The inhabitants of those stretched or compressed hours are not separate creatures. They are the same appetite experienced at different speeds. A leaf that is a law in one courtyard is only a momentary rumor in the next.

The fog is the closest the basin comes to a guest. It climbs against the heat, drafts rooms that do not yet exist, and then waits, patient as weather, until the plants grow the rooms in fact. When the fog burns off, the rooms remain, already inhabited by the city’s next thought. Sometimes the standing beads of condensation show streets that are not the ones in front of you—older drafts, or drafts the city has not grown yet, or drafts in which the leaves are a color no leaf has earned. Those reflected streets are occupied, but only by the same green argument, conducted at a slightly different volume or in a slightly different tense.

Night does not bring rest or different citizens. The residual green glow and the dull lamps of night-flowers merely make the turbulence easier to see. Blocks heave. A tower rehearses collapse and rebirth in the same gesture. Streets reroute like a mind changing its subject. If anything sleeps, it sleeps the way a fever sleeps: the process continues, only the light has changed.

You can stay long enough to feel yourself being edited. The floor will not be this floor. The room is already practicing being a forest. The sound does not distinguish between making and unmaking, and after a while neither do you. What lives in the basin is the argument itself—nature and city conducted entirely by plants, at a volume that leaves a ringing in the skull. It has no name that lasts, because names last and this place is a draft. It does not want inhabitants in the ordinary sense. It wants only the next sentence, written in a medium that cannot stop revising, under a light that still insists, reasonably, that day is day.

The unreason is everything else, and it is alive.
