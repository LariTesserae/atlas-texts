The ones who live here are few enough that the needle can still pretend, on some cycles, to be empty. They are not a people in the old horizontal sense; they are a density, a scattering of warmth-points along the inner glass, each one a small rebellion against the medium that wants to cook them. A generation has passed since the shaft punched itself into the plateau, and already the bodies have begun to listen. Skin is the first organ to change: it darkens not toward brown but toward a matte, heat-slick charcoal that drinks the infrared and gives almost nothing back. Veins lie closer to the surface than they should, dark rivers under a surface that never quite stops gleaming, as if the blood itself has learned to radiate. Hair is rare and short, more suggestion than covering; what remains is cropped or burned away by the same slow chemistry that makes cloth a joke. They go nearly naked, but the nakedness is engineered. What they wear is scaffolding: thin, vented lattices of sintered fiber that stand a finger’s width off the body, inner faces mirrored so that each person carries a private, shivering winter no larger than themselves. The devices at throat and wrist do the rest. They tick. That is how you know a neighbor is near before you see them: a faint, irregular cooling-iron sound that is not quite mechanical, not quite alive.

They move as the heat requires. Haste is a way of dying from the inside, so every gesture is measured, every crossing of a plank-walk calculated against the risk of a stumble that would cook the lungs before the fall even finished. Conversations happen at the distance of breath. Voices do not travel; the empty volume of the chamber swallows them the way the intakes swallow frit. Two figures lean in until their cooling auras overlap, and the words that pass between them are always quieter than the tick of their devices. They speak of cistern levels, of the last growth-shudder, of who will walk the eastern heat-channel this cycle and scrape the dendrites before they grow too brittle and shed into the void. They have names for the ledges—old names, already worn smooth by use, as if the place had been inhabited far longer than the calendar allows. There are arguments. There is a junction where three walkways meet and people leave things: a polished receipt of last season’s condensation, a slag-bead worked into a shape that means nothing except that someone had time, a device whose tick has gone silent. Others take them or leave them. It is not trade. It is a way of saying we are still here, still choosing.

Children exist in the way a few extra ticks exist: rare, watched, taught early. They learn the sound of a pressure-gate considering whether to iris. They learn that the brown dim is not darkness but a color that has given up, and that faces become readable only when you are close enough to feel the private winter of another body. They learn not to look down, because down is not a direction so much as a duration, and not to put palms on a wall that has just finished raining. They learn that the empty middle of the chamber is occupied even when nothing moves there. The occupation has no name they are allowed to use.

Sometimes, at the edge of the dimness, something else occupies it. It is the size of a closed fist until it is the size of a room; it prefers the newer glass of the upper taper, where the sintering is still incomplete and the lattice has not yet decided how conductive it wants to be. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It is present the way a reflection is present when you are not quite looking, and then the presence is simply gone. The inhabitants leave offerings of cooled slag in niches cut for that purpose, not from worship, not even from fear exactly, but from a practiced wish to remain uninteresting. The slag is always gone later. Whether it is taken or unmade is a question they have learned not to ask out loud. The thing—if it is a thing—has no good name. They call it, when they must call it anything, the remainder. What remains when the needle finishes growing, perhaps. What the city is keeping itself from becoming.

They are keepers, after a fashion. The machines in the walls drink the gradient and turn it into the work of not-finishing: not rising all the way, not cooling all the way, not dying the ordinary death of magma. The people maintain the channels, argue about the eastern vein, stand (rarely, and never for long) on the rail-less platform at the very top where the newest glass is still remembering how to be solid. Someone goes there with a tool that does not look like a tool and does a thing to the private physics of the shaft. The air is thinner there, the heat a degree less certain of itself. A ghost of wetness touches the lips—minerals, old lightning—and is gone. They do not stay. No one stays at the calibration point. To stay would be to become part of the gradient, and they have not yet decided whether that is allowed.

The needle continues. A film of water decides to exist, runs the entire inner circumference in a single coordinated glaze, and vanishes into the organs of the wall. Dendrites brighten by a generation; the inhabitants call them receipts and do not touch them until they are brittle. A walkway ticks as it sheds heat. Far below, or far above—distance here is mostly a matter of which way the emptiness is leaning—a child is told, again, not to run. The remainder is or is not in the upper dark. The city, which should not be a city, which should not still be rising, which opens onto a sky no catalog knows, keeps the only work it knows. The ones who live inside it keep theirs: to remain, for one more cycle, uninteresting enough to be allowed to stay.
