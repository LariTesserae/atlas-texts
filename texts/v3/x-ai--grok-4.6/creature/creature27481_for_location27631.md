The first things that live here do not have faces. They are the films: olive-black slicks that paint every seep and every underside, the algae that treat the warm stone as a second skin and the bacteria that turn mineral water into a faint, sweet rot you can taste even when you close your mouth. They occupy the newest ledges first, the ones still showing machine teeth, and they rewrite the rock into something that can be climbed by later arrivals. Nothing here begins with a name. It begins with a wetness that has decided to stay.

Then the air itself becomes a body. Midges and nameless smaller wings make a constant, soft abrasion, a protein fog that coats the tongue and the inside of the eyelids. They do not swarm so much as they constitute the climate; walk and you wade through them, breathe and you ingest a little more of the place. They breed in the thinnest sheets of water, the ones that look like stone until your weight proves otherwise. Their larvae thread the mineral crusts. Their adults die by the million against the last orange lamp and become another film.

The frogs are the occupancy given voice. They hold every lip, every vertical inch of weeping face, every drowned sleeper of rail. Their throats work in overlapping pulses until the basin is a single instrument played by too many hands; the sound is not decoration but weather, a saturation you wait out rather than speak against. Some are the size of a thumbnail and the color of wet limestone. Some are larger, olive and gold and the dull red of old brick, their sides already filmed with the same algae that coats the stone. They replace themselves without ceremony. Eggs appear overnight in the warmest shallows, pale as thought. Tadpoles move like decisions the water has not quite made. When a mineral shelf collapses into a pool they treat the new edge as an invitation and are calling from it before the silt settles. At whatever passes for night they become more themselves: the chorus thickens, individual notes vanish, and the dark admits it was always this loud.

Under the frogs, in the throats of water that punch through into older workings, live the things that never needed the rumor of day. Pale fish the length of a finger, almost translucent, their sides catching the tired lamp-light like coins dropped long ago. Jointed flickers—crayfish or their cousins—moving without quite deciding to move, antennae writing slow questions in the black. Snails the size of thumbs, their shells already half-dissolved and half-rebuilt by the lime, grazing the drowned conveyor belts and the undersides of ledges. They traffic in silence. The water talks around them in a continuous unfastening, but they do not add to it. They are the place remembering it has depth.

The machines live here too, though they no longer know the difference. The pump house of tea-colored brick stands half-submerged, its roof a suggestion, its remaining pump exhaling into a trough with the sound of a man forever clearing his throat. Someone—or the habit of someone—still feeds a generator; a caged lamp burns a tired orange and turns the vapor solid. The conveyor has become a hanging garden of slime. Rails walk into the water and do not return. A gantry stands in a pool as if it waded in to think and forgot the shore. Rust is their new blood. Mineral crust is their new skin. They fail the way a habit fails: slowly, without drama, becoming perch and deposit and the hard part of a new shore. The frogs use them. The films use them. The water writes on them until they are no longer guests.

And then there are the ones who still remember the old names for this place. They are few. You see them first as a darker humidity at the edge of vision, a shape that resolves late because edges arrive late here. They move along the benches with the patience of the water, clothes another wet surface, metal in the hand already the temperature of blood. Some tend the last engines because the engines have not yet understood they are alone. Some came down the unfinished stairs and simply stayed, the way the willow on the spoil heap stayed long enough to die halfway. Their bootprints in the soft mineral mud become fossils before anyone returns to ask who made them, and even then the fossils are underwater, and the water is inventing a skin, and the frogs have already decided each print is a room. They do not hurry. Time here embarrasses intention. They speak, when they speak, with mouths close to helmets or in the rare thinnings of the chorus. They have the look of people who have learned that the basin is not a wound but an argument still being held, and that they are one more sentence in it.

What is forming has not agreed on a single inhabitant. The frogs believe they own the vertical and the horizontal alike. The films believe they own the future of the stone. The pale fish believe the deep throats were always theirs. The last machines believe they are still working. The few walkers believe they are still visitors, or caretakers, or the ones who will one day climb back toward a sky that no longer applies. None of them is wrong. The stone keeps arriving, blocky and unrounded. The water keeps writing on it in curtains and terraces and sudden black throats. Every living thing uses the newest sentence before it has finished being written. Nothing is in a rush to decide who lives here, because the place itself has not yet decided it is finished becoming. Walk far enough and the benches repeat, and the lives repeat with them: film, wing, throat, pale flicker, rust, boot. The argument continues. The warmth does not relent.
