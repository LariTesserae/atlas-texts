The first lives here are the ones that do not need to move. They arrived with the water, or they were already sleeping in the rock and woke when the ground unzipped and the heat found them. In the shallow saucer they form a living skin: white floc that is not mineral alone but a city of cells stacked in towers and veils, each tower a gradient of temperature and chemistry so steep that the organisms at the base live in a different world from those at the crest. They take the dissolved metals and the sulfur and the faint organic smear that comes up from deeper pipes, and they lay down new stone even as they eat. The oil-sheen is theirs as well—an iridescent monolayer that flexes when a drip strikes, then reseals. Nothing larger drinks because the water is still learning how to be water in this air; the microbes have already decided it is home.

On the wetter faces the dark algae is older than the hollow by a little. It is a stain that thickens by the week, a heat-tolerant film that drinks the glare and turns it into a dull, almost black velvet. Where the sheets of weep-water run thinnest the algae thins with them, becoming a watercolor wash; where the water pauses it piles into small, rubbery cushions that feel like the inside of a cheek if you were foolish enough to touch. These cushions trap the falling mineral dust and slowly bury themselves, so that the living layer is always the newest, always climbing over its own dead.

The few rushes in the palm-sized crack are accidents that became decisions. Their yellowed blades are half-cooked, yet they hold. Each blade is a wick: it pulls the slightly cooler seep upward and transpires it into the haze, a private weather no larger than a breath. Roots thread the rubble and exude acids that etch the new mineral skin, making tiny pockets where more of their kind might one day stand. They are not a colony yet. They are a negotiation.

The skeletal shrub on the high shoulder is the oldest visible citizen and the most exhausted. Its leaves have already become scales; its wood is more mineral than cellulose now, petrifying from the inside while the bark still tries to shed salt. It does not photosynthesize so much as endure. Insects have not found it. Birds have not perched. Its only companions are the orange fans of rust that creep along the welds below, and those rusts are themselves half-alive—iron-oxidizing films that bloom, darken, and become the next layer of the hollow’s growing floor.

Inside the valve-house the gloom is not empty. A slower life works there: salt-tolerant molds threading the mortar, a glaze of biofilm on the twitching gauge glass, filaments that sway in the convection that rises from the brick itself. The gauge needle’s almost-imperceptible motion is the only clock they have. They do not care that the building no longer has a purpose. They have made the purpose residual heat and the slow rain of evaporated minerals that sifts through the missing window.

Nothing walks. Nothing flies. Nothing burrows except the invisible threads of hyphae and the twitching of cilia too small to cast a shadow. The loudness is theirs to ignore; they live beneath it, in the frequencies of chemistry rather than sound. Over months the hollow will thicken around them. The saucer will become a pool, the pool a terrace of sinter, the terrace a roof. The lives that are here now will be the foundation, sealed in like flies in amber, except they will not be dead. They will have become the new rock, and the new rock will remember the heat.

And still the pipes keep feeding them. The collapse is only a season or two old, yet already the place has a metabolism. It inhales steam and exhales crust. What lives here is that metabolism given form—patient, microscopic, already ancient in its habits, and utterly unconcerned that the rest of the world has not yet noticed the hollow exists.
