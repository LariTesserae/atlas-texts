The mosses were first, though first is a word the hollow does not use. They have no beginning anyone can point to; they simply occupy the seeps and the pinches of soil the way the cold occupies the air. Thick bolsters, almost black where the water never quite stops, tarnished-brass where a little more light reaches and then forgets them. They drink what the stone offers and give back a colder green that is more texture than colour. Their life is a slow thickening, a fingernail’s width in a wet year, and they have been doing it long enough that whole panels of limestone now wear them like an old, wet coat. Between the bolsters the liverworts glaze the runnels, slick and indifferent. In the one recessed bay that still remembers a sliver of sky the ferns keep their own counsel: lady fern, hard fern, the one with the reddish stipe, all of them finely divided, all of them beaded, all of them unfurling as if a full day were a rumour they had decided not to credit. Life here is a skin. It does not fill the chamber. It lines it, obstinate and intimate, and then stops.

The cricket is an event. Pale, the length of a thumb, it starts from a moss cushion with a sound too small for the thunder and vanishes into a crack that has been waiting for it. You could watch for years and see it only once. The thing in the black pool is even less. Colourless, almost transparent, it turns when a drip strikes the surface that looks like a hole in the floor; the turn is the only proof it is there. Bats use the upper dark in their season, their clicks lost, their numbers never dense. A fox comes down the western scree when it must, drinks, leaves no argument. None of them claim the place. They are permitted, briefly, the way a drip is permitted to fall when it is ready.

Deeper, under the biggest overhangs where the floor recedes into that second, lower notch you feel rather than see, something else has been continuing. It is not one shape. It is the patience itself given a slow, mineral body. Pale as the wet calcite, or the colour of stone that has been damp so long the damp has become material, it occupies the absolute dark the way the sound occupies the empty volume when the little fall is running. It does not hurry. It has never hurried. Its movements, when they happen, happen inside the thunder so that even the rock cannot tell them apart from the ringing. It has no use for the pale coin of sky. Its senses are the seepage, the packed continuous note, the faint iron taste of old water, the rare vibration of a cricket’s leap or a bat that mistimes the dark. It has been here since the limestone finished its violence and began only breathing. The people who cut the steps, who stacked the three-walled shelter, who left the flint and the ochre in its stiff wrapping, they felt it without ever quite seeing it. Their hands knew the same patience. Their hearths stained the drier terrace and then went cold. The being (if being is still the word) stayed. It does not keep the shelter. It does not need the steps. It is the held breath the hollow has always been, only now the breath has weight, and cool flanks that match the undercuts, and a long, unhurried attention that has outlasted every name anyone ever tried to give the place.

Sometimes, after the fall has dwindled and the silence rings with memory, you can almost hear it listening back. The hawthorns lean inward over the rim as if they already know. The moss thickens by another unnoticeable increment. The black pool receives another drip. Nothing has changed. Everything has only gone on being what it is: richly lined, loudly empty, older than shepherds, and still, in the deepest notch, inhabited.
