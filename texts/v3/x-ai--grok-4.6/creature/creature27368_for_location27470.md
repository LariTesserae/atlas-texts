The plateau does not announce its people. For a long time it announces only itself: the cork-give of the living ground underfoot, the metallic edge of daylight, the organ-pipe roar that the wind makes of every hollow the plant has grown. You can walk a full day between habitable masses and meet nothing that is not the plant or the machines the plant has chosen to keep. Tracks exist, but they are faint and quickly closed by new lichen-meadow. A door left ajar will be sealed, over seasons, by a slow extrusion of tension wood. The impression is of a place that has outlasted the need for inhabitants, or has simply folded them so completely into its own tempo that they no longer register as separate.

Then you notice the first one, and it is not dramatic. A figure stands in the lee of a braided tower, one palm resting against a brass conduit that has long since been overgrown and then re-exposed by weather. The figure is tall, elongated in the way things become when they have lived generations in thin air and never had to hurry. The skin has the same faint bronze-green cast as the lichen, slightly yielding, never quite dry. The hair—if it is hair—is a dark, fibrous mass that the wind cannot fully disorder. The eyes are ordinary in shape but take the light the way polished resin does. The figure does not startle. It has been aware of you for some time; the plant itself carries vibrations, and the oldest vascular bundles remember the rhythm of a walking gait. After a while the figure turns a small wheel. Steam shifts pitch by a fraction. That is the greeting.

They are few. Each cluster of structures—those distant, wavering silhouettes—holds perhaps a dozen, perhaps two. They do not travel often. The open ground between is not hostile so much as simply too large, too loud, too much itself. When they do move from one district to another it is usually to accompany a sacrificial limb that has been felled and must be walked, over days, to a still-functioning foundry. They walk the same unhurried pace as the weather. Their clothing is grown rather than made: sheets of living bark peeled at the right season, cured in the wind, sewn with conductive resin thread so that a tear will slowly knit. Tools are carried, but many of the tools have already been grown around, so that a wrench or a pressure gauge is half brass and half heartwood, the two materials no longer distinguishable in function.

Their days are the factory cycles, which last whatever length the plant decides. A hopper that once took wood-chips now takes hours; the people who tend it have learned to feed it dusk, or the particular quality of light just before rain. They do not find this strange. They have stories—spoken rarely, because speech itself soaks into the walls and returns later, warmer, slightly changed—of ancestors who introduced the first pistons and conduits. Those ancestors are not remembered as conquerors or even as builders. They are remembered as a kind of pollination, a useful irritation the plant decided to keep. The present inhabitants are the result: neither wholly the original people nor wholly the plant, but the long negotiation between them. Some have begun to photosynthesize, faintly, along the shoulders and the backs of the hands. Others have resin glands at the wrists that they can open to seal a leak or to leave a mark that the next generation will be able to read by taste.

In the oldest cores the negotiation has gone further. Corridors that do not quite connect, stairs that open onto afternoons two centuries gone—these are not anomalies to the people who live there. They are simply rooms that have kept more time than others. A woman whose family has always tended the central pith will sometimes sit with a version of her great-grandmother that the walls have leaked back out, and they will speak of the same unfinished gears lying in the same puddles of resin. The conversation is not ghostly. It is hydraulic. Words travel in the sap, delayed, and return with the faint sweetness of whatever season the plant was having when they were first spoken. Children (there are children, though they are rare and grow with the slowness of good timber) learn early not to be frightened when a familiar hallway briefly contains a different year. They learn to greet whoever is standing in it, even if that person has been dead, or not yet born, for longer than the child can count.

A few of the machines have begun to require company in a different sense. The ones that process sequence rather than matter are tended by those who have grown quietest. These tenders sit for seasons beside a hopper that accepts hours and extrudes dark, fragrant cakes. They do not eat the cakes. They burn very small pieces, on certain nights, and the smell that rises is always a specific autumn no one present has lived through, yet everyone recognizes. The recognition is not nostalgia. It is more like the plant remembering, through them, a weather it once had. The tenders themselves become a little like the cakes: compressed, fragrant, slightly out of ordinary sequence. Their voices, when they use them, arrive a few seconds after their mouths have closed.

At the far rim, where the living ground thins and the last towers stand pale and wind-scoured, the inhabitants are different again. They are solitary even by the standards of this place. Some have rooted, not metaphorically: fine vascular threads have grown from the soles of their feet into the substrate, so that they take water and minerals directly and no longer need to walk to the condensers. They stand for years, watching the drop, the curvature, the pale uninhabited flats that used to be everything else. The wind is loudest here; it scours their lower limbs the way it scours the remnant mesas. They do not speak. When rain comes they open their mouths and let it in, and the plant, through them, drinks. Occasionally one of them will lift an arm and the gesture will be answered, kilometers away, by a slow adjustment in some distant flywheel. No message is being sent. It is only that the whole organism, people included, is still one body, still deciding, at its own pace, what to keep running.

There are also the moths. Pale, the size of a hand, they live their entire lives inside a single gallery and never see the sky. The people regard them with a kind of courtesy. A moth that dies is placed in a small resin cup and left on a windowsill; by morning the plant has reabsorbed it. Once, perhaps, there was a bird. Its empty nest is still pointed out, high in a ventilation flue, to children who have never seen feathers. No one expects another.

What lives here is not a population in the ordinary sense. It is a late, sparse expression of the same body that is the plateau itself—human-shaped in places, machine-shaped in others, plant-shaped everywhere, and in the oldest pith no longer bothering to keep the distinctions crisp. They are the place, continuing, at the speed of very old wood and of weather that has nowhere else to go. When you finally leave (and you will leave; the plateau does not keep visitors), the last figure you see will already have turned back to its valve or its hopper or its long watch at the rim. The wind will take the sound of your footsteps and turn it into just another note in the pipes. The ground will close. The city, such as it is, will not notice that you were ever a separate thing.
