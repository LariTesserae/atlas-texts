The plated things come first into view because they refuse to hide. They occupy the living skin the way dunes occupy a desert, in slow continental herds whose leading edge can be a day's walk from the trailing one. Each animal is a low fortress of overlapping mineral plates the same tarnished-gold and old-bone palette as the mat they graze. The plates are not armor in the usual sense; they are successive layers of their own shed epidermis, compressed and silicified until they ring like struck stone when the wind finds an edge. Between the plates, darker seams of living tissue pulse with the same dark phytolymph that the Growths move in their canyon-wide veins. You feel their calls before you hear them: a subsonic pressure that arrives in the sternum and the soles of the feet, perfectly offset from the drop-forge rhythm of the nearest Growth so the two percussion lines never quite coincide. The braid they make is the closest thing the world has to music.

They do not eat the skin so much as rasp it. Rows of grinding plates inside the mouth, themselves mineralized, reduce the fibrous mat to a dry flour that is then mixed with a trickle of their own internal lymph and fermented in a series of chambers that run almost the length of the body. What they extract is not sugar or protein in any familiar ratio but metals, silicates, and the faint electrical charge the skin itself generates. The waste they leave behind is a finer, more compacted version of the same mat; in a few seasons the wind will plane it into new ventifacts and the cycle will close. Their young are born already plated, the size of boulders, and they walk within hours. Mortality is almost entirely mechanical: a calving face of a Growth can bury an entire cohort, or a sudden resin-fall can lock their joints until they starve standing up. The survivors simply continue. They have no destination. The intervals between Growths are not barriers to them; they are merely longer stretches of the same food.

Above them the membrane-sails appear first as absences of light. A shadow slides across the bright plain, too regular to be weather, too slow to be a pollen wall. Then the wingbeat declares itself: a vast, translucent plane of stretched polymer that the dryness has turned almost glassy, ribbed with veins that carry the same dark fluid. Some are large enough that their passage dimms the merciless white for minutes at a time. They do not flap so much as trim and spill, riding the thermal columns that roar off the photosynthetic lofts of the Growths. Their bodies hang beneath like afterthoughts, compact and heavily plated against the sandblast of pollen and shredded fiber. Eyes, if they have them, are recessed pits shielded by nictitating layers that polarize the bleaching glare into usable contrast. More often they navigate by the charge in the air and the standing waves of sound that the Growths throw off; a shift in the factory roar or the sudden crackle of building lightning is a map to them.

They feed on the pollen-storms themselves, sieving the gold-brown walls with trailing filters that look like torn banners. Some dive into the wound-galleries to drink from exposed lymph-seams, risking the borers and the sudden slam of a vascular contraction. Their mating flights coincide with the rare alignments of several pollen-plumes; the air fills with a higher, tearing note as hundreds of sails lock and spiral upward until the charge they generate triggers their own lightning. The survivors drift down days later, some of them already carrying the next generation as internal cysts that will drop onto the living skin like seeds.

Inside the Growths the distinction between inhabitant and architecture dissolves. The borers are the most numerous. They are not insects; nothing here molts in the ordinary way. They are elongated, segmented forms whose anterior ends are rotating rasps of the same mineralized tissue the grazers use. They follow the resin veins and the older, half-dead galleries, opening new voids that the wind will later flute into instruments. Behind them come the drinkers: softer, almost gelatinous until they contact the phytolymph, at which point they stiffen into living pipes, anchoring themselves in the flow and filtering metals and charge for months or years. When a conduit slams they simply ride the pressure wave, some of them torn free and carried kilometers through the dark before they re-anchor or are crushed.

The ones that pupate are stranger still. At a certain density of nutrients or a certain frequency of the surrounding roar they seal themselves inside hard, porous casings that look like the amber glass of old resin. The casings lodge in constrictions or in the hanging curtains of dead vine. Over seasons they become part of the filtration architecture, their internal tissues reorganized into labyrinths that process sap, air, or the constant rain of shed scales. Some of these filters remain alive in a reduced sense, slowly replacing their own material with the minerals they concentrate. Others are simply occupied after the original occupant has been metabolized; new borers or drinkers move in and continue the work. Nothing is wasted because there is no outside to waste it into.

There are smaller pulses as well. Films of photosynthetic bacteria ride the living skin in the brief hours after a pollen-fall, staining it a darker gold until the wind or the grazers erase them. Aerial motes, neither quite plant nor animal, condense in the electrical weather of the upper lofts and rain down as a fine, conductive dust that the flyers and the Growths both harvest. In the deepest, oldest galleries things that have never seen the white pressure live by the vibration alone, their bodies little more than tuned cavities that feed on the subsonic roar and the occasional trickle of lymph.

None of them name anything. None of them build. They occupy the turbulence and are occupied by it in turn. A grazer herd will vanish for a generation inside a newly opened wound-gallery, emerging later plated with a different mineral sheen because the local lymph ran richer in one metal than another. A membrane-sail may ride a planetary wave of wind for half a “season,” crossing intervals that would take a walker months, only to fold itself into a hanging curtain and wait for the next thermal. The pupated filters become, over centuries, indistinguishable from the Growth’s own tissue; when that face finally calves they fall with it and the cycle begins again on the plain.

What lives here is the equilibrium itself, expressed in bodies that have had geological time to become inevitable. They are as loud as the world they inhabit, as brightly lit, as unwilling to stop. The high places have forgotten water, but they have not forgotten motion, and everything that persists has learned to be a kind of weather.
