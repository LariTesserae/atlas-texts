They come up out of the same wet that made the place, and they do not distinguish themselves from it at first glance. You see oilskins first, always: black, shining, cut long so the hems drag and leave dark commas on the flag. The people inside them are spare, as if the constant fine rain and the metal taste in the air have leached anything extra. Faces are turned down or turned aside; eyes are the color of the meres, tea-weak, and they do not widen at the noise because the noise is simply the weather they were born under. Their speech, when it happens, is a series of short, practical clicks aimed at the nearest pair of teeth—shift times, valve settings, which weir is running cloudy. Anything longer would be washed out before it reached the other side of a gallery.

They move as the belts move: not hurried, not idle. At change-over they appear on the high catwalks in twos and threes, then dissolve again into the pump halls or the drying sheds. Children exist, but they are a rumor you confirm only in the flood-courts at the slack hour before second shift, launching tin-and-cork boats that never travel far because the current is everywhere and nowhere. Their voices stay low; they have already learned that volume is a waste. The older ones—those whose names have already been used once on a piston and will be used again—keep the civic knoll, selling vinegar-grain and keeping the temple hours that no one hears. They do not look like a people who remember silence. They look like a people who have decided silence was never the point.

Stay longer and the animals begin to feel less like rumor and more like another shift. The pale dog at the slurry gates is real enough: thin, the color of wet limestone, appearing only when the screws change pitch. It does not bark. The water-rats in the deep culverts are larger than they should be, yes, and pale, and they do move when the big screws alter their note twice a year; whether they sing is a matter of how long you have been listening. Birds still refuse the ledges. Once a generation the heron story returns, and someone always adds that it stood as if it belonged, which is the closest anyone comes to saying the place might have room for something that was not made here.

Then the stone itself starts to feel inhabited. The bloom on the older walls is neither salt nor lichen exactly; if you press a palm to it in the deep levels you feel a slow, regular tick that is not the nearest machine and not your own pulse. Some of the weirs have worn mouths. No one prays to them. It is only that after enough years the city includes a few extra rooms, and those rooms have occupants who do not punch clocks. They are the patience of the pistons given another shape, or the groundwater deciding it has stood here long enough to grow a kind of attention. You catch them at the edge of vision in the steam-shadow of the cooling towers: a thickening of the black moss that is briefly more upright, or a reflection in a mere that does not quite match the chimney it doubles. They do not speak. They do not need to. The whole plateau is already saying one long sentence, and they are simply the parts of it that have learned to listen back.

At night, when the red lamps bleed and the weirs keep their own counsel, you can no longer tell which figures on the galleries are carrying lanterns because the electric has failed again and which figures are the lanterns the place itself has decided to walk. Both kinds move at the same unhurried speed. Both kinds belong. The city is still precipitating itself out of the wet, brick and iron and these quieter tenants alike, and none of them is in any hurry to become anything the water has not already agreed to.
