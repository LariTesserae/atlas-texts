They are the motion the stone still permits, and they have been permitting themselves for so long that the distinction between inhabitant and process has worn as thin as everything else.

At first they are only the way the wet iron shifts when you look too long. A living grit the size of thumbnails and smaller, the precise colour of the rain-darkened bedrock they rasp. They travel in sheets that can cover an acre and vanish into a hairline seam in the time it takes the pewter light to fail. Their undersides are all mouth and hook, a continuous rasping that lifts the molecular skin the rain keeps offering—the leachate, the dust of their own dead, the faint mineral bloom that is the closest this world comes to a harvest. When a sheet turns, the plain itself seems to flow, a sudden satin motion under the roar, and the sound they make is a dry seethe, millions of tiny abrasions that never quite rise above the rain and never quite sink beneath it. They are the base, if a place this old still remembers what a base is. They eat the stone. They eat one another. They are eaten. The cycle has no beginning anyone can point to; it simply continues, a kind of weather with legs.

Among them and above them move the mid-sized ones, the ones whose armor looks assembled from mouths and joints by a maker who had never been shown any other parts. Some are flattened like limpets that learned, late and reluctantly, to run. Others are vaulted like overturned bowls, their plates overlapping in rasping fans that click and stridulate until the air itself has a serrated edge. They open and close those plates along their flanks and the plates are musical in a way that has nothing to do with comfort or warning; it is simply the sound of being this shape in this rain. At the pewter hour they call together. The distances between them do not matter. The entire plain becomes one stretched instrument, a single skin vibrating with the same dry, urgent note. They feed on the grit and on the pale swimmers that flicker through the sheet-water—frantic commas of flesh, more gape than body, lives so brief and so aligned to the pull of the flood that they seem less like animals than like thoughts the water is having. The mid-sized ones herd them, or simply wait, their many legs locked into the stone until the lens of deeper water arrives.

The large ones you mistake for the land itself. They are the size of boats left keel-up, or of the stacked huts, or of both at once. Their backs accumulate the living grit the way outcrops accumulate rain; when they shift—rarely, heavily, like a change in the slope—the grit pours off them in brown weather. An eye opens, slow as condensation forming. They feed on whatever comes within reach of the paddle-limb: mid-sized armor cracked like wet slate, swimmers scooped in glittering handfuls, even, sometimes, the stacked plates of a hut that was built too close. When they boom they slap the standing water and the report travels farther than sight, a condition rather than a message, a reminder that mass still exists here. They move so seldom that entire generations of grit are born, rasp, and die on their backs without ever knowing the world has another shape.

And then there are the ones who stack.

They are the same colour as the weather, wrapped in the cured skins of the mid-sized ones—skins that never quite dry, that take on the same cool weight as everything else. Their faces are reduced, economical: eyes the dull interior shine of the standing water at noon, mouths that do not waste themselves on speech. Gesture is posture. They point with the whole body—the set of the shoulders, the faint lean toward or away from the slope. Density is low. You can walk for days and see only the facts of them: a hut of stacked plates, a weir of unshaped bedrock, a catch-basin ringing a warmer seep. Inside the huts the same iron stone, flaked sharp because this rock still remembers how to part. Hooks and scrapers made from shed plates. Tallies cut into the inner wall that might be days or floods or booms or all three at once. Ash in a ring of smaller stones, the fire a temporary, smoky disagreement with the wet that smells of chitin and mineral and does not last.

They know which basins will hold when the sheet-flood comes and which will be erased and must be rebuilt. They know the taste that arrives in the rain an hour before the pulse, the change in the boom-pattern that means a large one has shifted. They sleep against the bruises of residual heat in the stone, the world’s last interior warmth pooling where it can no longer afford mountains. They do not explain the comfort. Culture leaks both ways, or the land is suggestible: the mid-sized ones will sometimes arrange themselves, at the pewter hour, into rings that match the catch-basins, living weirs that hold nothing. The same tallies appear on isolated slabs miles from any doorway, as if the stone were practicing the marks. Tools left overnight in a warm seep are sometimes a fraction larger in the morning, or a fraction more themselves, edges clarified. No one relies on this. No one is surprised.

They are still people, if that word can stretch to cover a craft this reduced, a patience this exact, a density this low. Or they are what people become when the experiment of green is long closed and the only remaining grammar is the slow stacking of plates against a roar that will never stop. Their children, when there are children, learn the slope with their ankles before they learn it with their eyes. They learn that the rain is not always obedient: that sometimes a column of it hangs and then climbs, drops reversing in a chimney of silver, and every living thing on the plain—grit, mid-sized, large, stacked—freezes and turns to face the same unseeable point. Afterward they resume. The gear slips and then catches.

They lift a flake of iron, use it, set it down again. A small bright disagreement. The polish will, in time, forgive it. They know this. They do it anyway. The booms come farther apart at the dimmest hour, like a heart deciding. They continue. The rain does not consult them. The stone weeps, and weeps, and they stack another plate, and the living grit turns beneath their feet, and the place goes on being itself, which is all any of them have ever been asked to do.
