The emptiness is the first inhabitant, and it is not a vacancy but a pressure. It occupies every tank to the lip, every joint the capillary water refuses to abandon, every unfinished bed where a block still leans as if listening for the next instruction. It is not the emptiness of a ruin; ruins remember use. This emptiness remembers only the last mallet-blow and the moment the ropes were laid down. It has learned the geometry so thoroughly that it now performs the work itself: holding, passing, holding, passing, a little downhill, always shared. Stand long enough on a handspan rim and you feel it leaning against your calves, cool and exact, waiting for you to become another decision of surface.

Beneath that pressure something slower moves. It is not the water, though it uses the water the way a lung uses air. It collects in the rare deep cisterns where silt turns in a rotation too patient for weather. It thickens there, a clarity that is darker than the rest, and when the sun is high enough to shatter itself across a thousand lids it withdraws into those shafts as if brightness were a kind of drought. At night it rises again, not as flood but as a faint extra shine that the wet stone should not still possess. You can track it by the way the drops change their interval: they fall more slowly when it is near, as though the stone itself were considering whether to let them go.

It has no single body. It is the difference between one tank and the next, the nail-width of fall that takes an hour of walking to become the height of a person. It prefers the volcanic scraps inlaid along certain lips; those darker measures are where it rests, tasting sequence. When a new cut is made on the eastern margin—even now, even with no hands present—the fresh face begins to weep in a slightly different key, and the thing notices. It sends a thread of itself along the unfinished ramp, testing the compacted chips, deciding whether the mountain has been sufficiently persuaded. The half-freed blocks lean a fraction closer together after it has passed. Mortar, where it exists, takes a paler stain.

Sometimes it gathers enough to be almost seen. In the still air among the tanks, just after the peaks have gone black, a vertical thickening appears above one of the corbelled caps, no taller than the shaft it guards. It has the grain of the chisel still in it, the same fine white calcareous memory. It does not walk. It does not need to. Circulation here was never a matter of feet. It simply occupies the next rim, then the next, choosing the generous walkways when it wishes to be leisurely and the handspans when it wishes to remember how little room the builders left for anything that was not water or will. Fingertips trailed in the surface feel it as a sudden ache that is not quite cold. Breath shows more densely for a few steps afterward.

It is young, like the work. It has not yet learned to be cruel or kind; those are river-words, swamp-words, words for places that allow loss. Its only appetite is the doctrine itself: water kept at the surface, divided, bright, almost still. Overflow offends it. A notch that whispers too loudly into the next room will find itself narrowed by a fresh skin of mineral gel, iridescent, thin, not alive in any way that answers to touch and yet perfectly obedient. The stains of iron and copper that climb the walls are its older thoughts, maps of where it once lingered too long.

On the quarry edge it is still half mountain. You can hear it there if the air is cold enough: a faint quarry-smell that is also a voice, asking the remaining uncut shoulder how much more of itself it is willing to become rooms. The wooden levers and fiber ropes have begun to take its shape; they swell and darken as though remembering hands that will not return. The silence of the tool laid down is its breathing.

It will outlast the sharpness of the corners. Already it practices the slower life the basin was built to enter: seepage, evaporative argument, the long evening-out after snowmelt. A season lifts the levels; the thing drinks the difference and redistributes it until every tank agrees again. Construction continues at that speed because construction is now its body. A course of blocks in a month is a gesture it makes with the mountain’s own patience. Everything that exists was lifted by bodies; now the bodies are gone and the lifting has become a habit the stone cannot break.

If you remain through enough glare and enough leftover night-shine you begin to feel the invitation. Not to dwell—there are no doors, only lintels over channels, niches where a body might sit out of the brightest light. The invitation is simpler. Become another rim. Become a surface that is a decision. Let the capillary water occupy the joint of you. The highland does not need inhabitants who walk. It needs inhabitants who hold.
