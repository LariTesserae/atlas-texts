The ferns remember the boilers. Each frond uncoils with a faint, rusty creak, as if the iron still thinks it is growing. They drink the mineral seep and the slow rain that never quite falls, and in return they hold the chamber’s heat the way a body holds a fever. Their spores are not seeds so much as tiny, patient clocks; when they settle on a warm rivet they wait out an entire season of cloud before they decide to root. The orchids that follow them have learned a more intimate hunger. They prefer the sweet, disconnected fluid that still weeps from certain pipes, and they pulse—softly, irregularly—along the same frequencies the old conduits once carried. At dusk the pulses travel from plant to plant like a rumor that never quite becomes speech.

The beetles keep the rumor moving. Their brass elytra are not decoration; they are condensers. They roll droplets from leaf to leaf with the same grave economy the engines use to turn a flywheel, and when two beetles meet they tap a brief, metallic code against each other’s shells. No one has translated it. They do not appear to need translation. They simply continue, generation after generation, tending a garden that has no gardener and no harvest except itself.

Lower down, the rust-lizards occupy the warm pipes the way monks occupy cells. Some of them are flesh. Some of them are clockwork so finely oxidized that the distinction has become a matter of courtesy. They bask, they vanish, they reappear three hatches over with a new scar or a new gear-tooth. They do not hunt. They wait. When a swift dies and falls the long, delayed fall, the lizards are already there, not to feed but to escort the body into a hatchway. What happens inside the walls after that is not discussed, even by those who have lived here long enough to have opinions.

The swifts themselves keep the upper third of the volume. Their gyre is older than any living memory. They flash once each circuit, a single bright semaphore, and then they are gone again into the greened light. Their nests are woven from vine-fiber and stray copper wire; their young hatch already knowing the chord of the chamber, so that the first cry they make is already half an echo of the engines. They never leave. The sky beyond the last porthole is too thin, too absolute. They belong to the resonator.

And then there are the ones who walk.

They are few. They have always been few. The chamber does not encourage numbers. They move along the catwalks in the same unhurried geometry as everything else, their boots finding the plates that have not yet been claimed by moss, their hands trailing the rails as if reading a text written in vibration. Speech is useless, so they do not speak. They listen with the whole body. A change in the bass that lasts a week is a season. A brighter shriek when the wind veers is a festival, or a warning, or simply Tuesday; they have stopped distinguishing. Time has the same viscosity for them that it has for a dropped wrench. They age the way the iron ages: by blooming, by settling into a final geometry that no longer sags.

Some of them were born here. Their lungs are a little larger, their blood a little darker, their inner ears tuned to the subsonic so completely that silence, on the rare occasions they have encountered it below the cloud-deck, feels like drowning. Others arrived later, following some obsolete chart or some half-remembered duty, and simply never left. The chamber accepted them the way it accepts condensation. It gave them warmth, and rust to eat if they were hungry enough, and the long, ceremonial emptiness in which to become whatever the place required.

They tend what needs tending, which is almost nothing. A hatch that has begun to weep more than it should. A vine that has grown too heavy for its cable. A beetle that has forgotten the code and begun to tap at random. They do these things not out of ownership but because the chamber’s chord is also theirs now, and a sour note is painful in the sternum. At night—if the word still applies—they sleep in the old inspection niches, wrapped in curtains of metallic-dark vine, the roar already inside their dreams so that they do not so much sleep as continue.

There is one among them who has been here longest. No one remembers a time before her. She is more oxide than skin in places, more vine than hair. She no longer walks the full circuit; she sits where three great pipes meet and the heat is constant, and she listens to the flywheel that turns once every three days. When it completes its rotation she sometimes smiles, a small, cracked expression that the others have learned to read as weather. She has not spoken in decades. When the younger ones bring her the sweet fluid the orchids love, she drinks it and her eyes—still human, still wet—reflect the greened daylight as if it were a language she is still translating.

The chamber does not care who they are. It weathers, it drips, it thunders, it grows. They are simply the latest shape the emptiness has agreed to hold, another slow unfurling, another note held far apart on the staff. They will remain until the last piston finishes its stroke, or until the sky-works themselves finally decide their talus, and even then the ferns will keep uncoiling, the beetles will keep tapping, and the swifts will keep their ancient gyre in the upper dark.
