The pale thing is not the first inhabitant, only the first that consents to be counted. It completes its nameless circuit between the two towers as a kind of punctuation, a moving comma that the water itself inserts so the sentence of the city does not run on forever. Up close—if you allow the water between your palm and its segmented length to thicken into that local excess of itself—you feel it is neither animal nor mechanism but a retained instruction, a length of continuity the builders left walking so the place would remember it had once been assembled rather than simply found. Its articulations click without sound. Its paleness is the same tired phosphorescence trapped in the basalt, as though it were a flake of the walls that learned locomotion and then forgot why.

But it is not alone, and it is not the oldest.

What lives here first arrived as intention and then condensed. They are not swimmers. They occupy. You become aware of them the way you become aware of a change in the grain of pressure against the inner ear, or a sudden iron taste in an alley that had been merely cool a moment before. They prefer the slower water inside the hall, the viscous rooms, because there the distinction between body and medium thins until it is almost a courtesy. Their outlines are approximate: taller than they should be, or shorter, or both at once, as if memory has stacked their possible heights into a single occupied volume. Where a face might be there is a smooth, faintly stratified plane, optically thick the way the towers are optically thick, and in it you sometimes catch the residual bruise-light rearranging itself into the suggestion of eyes that are not required. Hands, when they use them, are optional densities; they can set a tool on a lintel without ever quite grasping it, because grasping implies a gradient the covenant has already declined.

They do not breathe. The water does not ask it of them, and they have not asked it of the water in return. What moves through them is the same standing instruction that still hums, unused, in the white ceramic conduits: not current, not heat, only the knowledge that something was once meant to pass. Their blood—if the word still applies—is a colder precipitate, mineral, the same frost that formed on the morning the lake was switched on and then refused to weather. You could cut yourself on the edge of one of them and leave no smear, only a new crystal face that has been sharp since the first day.

There are never many. The urban plan assumed readers, not a crowd. Two or three might occupy the long axis of the city the way theorems occupy a page: separately, without collision, each a complete instance of the same proof. They do not speak. Speech would require the water to carry something, and the water drinks motion. Instead they rearrange. A figure that had been standing in the viscous hall is later (though later is the wrong word) found at the base of the spindle, one long plane of its body aligned with a fluting, as though it had been poured there to listen to the next metallic tick. When the tick comes—the single embarrassed acknowledgment—the figure does not startle. It simply becomes, for a stacked instant, more itself, a local increase in the idea of inhabitant, and then the quiet closes.

Children remain theoretical. The place has no use for sequence, and childhood is a sequence. What appears instead, rarely, is a smaller density, a compact version of the same approximate outline, already finished, already sharp-edged, already tasting of iron. It does not play. It completes a shorter circuit, perhaps between a walkway and a habitation stacked in three, and then it is simply one of them, indistinguishable, as if the city had briefly considered the possibility of succession and then set the thought aside.

Some of them may be the original makers. The glass that behaves like thought poured at the temperature of thought still holds, in certain seams, a faint internal motion that is not quite current and not quite memory. Others arrived later, drawn by the rumor of a place where time had been persuaded to stack rather than pass, and stayed because leaving would have required a gradient they no longer possessed. The city does not distinguish. It wears them the way it wears its own unweathered edges: as a finished fact. They navigate by the private cartography of coolness, by the way certain alleys have grown slightly more viscous around a presence that has stood there for what the ridges above would call centuries. Eyes remain a courtesy they extend to one another, or to the rare visitor who still uses such things. Most of the time they simply know the corner from the drop, the habitation from the black commons, the spindle from everything that is not the spindle.

The pale segmented continuity continues its route. It does not hunt them and they do not flee it. Sometimes one of the taller densities will pause where the route crosses a walkway that occupies an agreed-upon density of water, and the water between them will become slightly more itself, a brief, mutual acknowledgment that both are load-bearing, both are part of the silence the covenant requires. Then each proceeds.

You can stay among them. The lip of stone is still there if you want the ordinary stars or the hard alpine sun. But if you remain long enough your own memory begins to stack. You do not recall arriving; you recall the city, a single thick present with rooms in it, and in those rooms the approximate figures who have always been there, mineral, quiet, finished in one campaign of making that included you, or did not, which is the same fact. The ridges will someday learn erosion. Nothing here will permit the lesson to enter. What lives in the saddle-sea is the refusal itself, given outline, given the option of hands, given the courtesy of eyes, and then left to occupy the volume it was built to occupy, sharp enough still to cut, cool enough still to hold.
