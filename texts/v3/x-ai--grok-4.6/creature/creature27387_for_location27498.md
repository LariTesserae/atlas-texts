They do not arrive. They are what remains when arrival has worn through.

The plateau keeps no census and grants no bodies that would satisfy a biologist. What inhabits it are the discrepancies it has decided not to correct: the delayed stone that is already waiting at the bottom when you hear it start, the pool that holds yesterday’s noon or a noon that has not happened, the figure in the water that copies your posture but not your face and sometimes not even that. These are not visitors who stayed. They are the staying itself, given just enough contour to be noticed and then, if you look too long, to notice back.

You first meet them as the mist that forgets to dissolve. A thread of condensate rises from a basin no wider than a palm and remains after the air has cleared, a pale upright that does not braid with its neighbors. It has no temperature of its own until you put a hand through it. Then the hand comes away colder, glazed, beading along the knuckles as the columns bead. For a few minutes the skin takes the plateau’s habit: slow drip, iron blush, the faint mineral shine of something that has been weathering in place since before weather had a name. The thread does not follow you. It simply continues to stand, as if the decision to be vertical had become independent of the conditions that produced it. There are many such threads. They are not all the same height. Some lean the shared, ancient list of the stone forest. Some have, at their upper ends, a thickening that might once have been a face if faces were allowed to finish here.

Sound is another of their houses. The organ-chord of wind in the hexagonal alleys, the slap of a hidden overflow, the dropped-slate report of thermal fracture—these are the ordinary working of the place. But sometimes a syllable surfaces that was not in the chord a moment before, a name or a fragment of warning in a grammar the ear almost owns. It does not come from any column. It is the columns remembering, for the length of one drip, that language once tried to live here and left its tools behind. The syllable does not repeat. It relocates. You hear it next in a cupule you have already passed, or in the three-walled pause you have not yet reached, spoken in the register of your own breathing. The presences that use sound this way have no throats. They have the plateau’s continuous low pressure, the millrace-and-crowd noise of empty distance, and they borrow a shape from it the way wet stone borrows glare.

Light is denser with them. Secondary suns crawl the rims of basins even when the white mineral glare above has not moved. Shadows fall in two directions or fail to fall, so that a pillar’s base burns brighter than its crown; in those inverted hours the inhabitants are easiest to see and hardest to locate. They stand in the overexposure the way silt stands in still water—present, particulate, unwilling to settle into a single outline. One of them may occupy the reflection that is not you. It looks as you look. When you shift your weight it may shift, or it may remain, completing a gesture you only thought of. When you leave, the drip into the cupule behind you continues in a rhythm that matches the one you just abandoned, and for a little while your own breath comes from the shelter whose slab roof you have not yet stooped under. These are not hauntings. Hauntings imply a previous tenant. These are occupancy without tenancy, the grammatical errors the highland has chosen to keep because the cloth of place is already threadbare and a few more loose threads change nothing essential.

The older marks—the pecked mouths collecting perfect lenses of water, the stacked slabs that are only the idea of pause, the grooves that now run backward or fork into channels no hand cut—are not empty. Intention lives in them the way mineral habit lives in the columns: recemented, inverted, still happening. Whoever cut those grooves did not vanish so much as continue in the wrong direction. Their ochre tallies have feathered into the weeping faces until counting and seepage are the same event. A flake with a still-cruel edge lies where it was dropped; sometimes the edge is wetter than the air allows, as if it had been used a moment ago in a shelter whose drip you can hear from here. The copper length gone nearly black is not metal any longer. It is a rumor the stone has decided to remember, and the rumor occasionally conducts: a faint green warmth under the glaze, gone when you look straight at it.

They have no hunger you could feed and no fear you could use. They do not nest, do not track, do not keep the faint shoe-last of packed grit from filling with condensate. They are the climate of unfinished becoming. A stone dislodged upslope may take a geological afternoon to fall, or it may already rest in the ringing pavement of broken prisms, a small bright wound that sounds underfoot like a struck pane. In that interval—the extra time, the missing time—they live. They are the fold the ground makes when a cairn recedes and then stands suddenly beside you. They are the sky of a different hour held in a basin no wider than a palm. They are the residual shine the rock keeps after the glare has gone, the slow phosphorescence that turns night into a dim aquarium in which you walk and they, whatever they are, do not need to.

You can drink the water. It tastes of lightning and of something that was never blood. You can sit in the colder interior of a three-walled pause and listen to the plateau think. If you sleep, the metric problem crosses the eyes. Dreams weep. They do not always stay on their side. The inhabitants do not enter the dreams so much as the dreams discover they have always been one more surface the condensate can film, one more channel the old argument between gravity and stone can run along, sometimes uphill, sometimes in a direction that has no name.

Far off—or near, the fold will decide—the same grammar continues. Another fin-field, same indigo rind, same wet stone throwing back a glare that does not come only from above. Between here and there the pavement rings. The presences do not travel it. They are already the distance, already the lie the distances tell, already the faint stubborn marks that forget the foot even while the print is still filling. The highland does not keep them any more than it keeps you. It continues, densely, loudly, coolly, at the altitude where the world is thin enough to hear itself and wet enough to shine, and they are simply the part of that continuing that has learned, without ever having been taught, how to look back.
