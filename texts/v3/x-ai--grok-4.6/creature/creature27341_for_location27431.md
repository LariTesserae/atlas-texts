The shield-beasts hold the ground the way a last snow holds a field: completely, without claim. Each is a low disc of living stone, broader than a man’s reach, the dorsal plates worn satin-smooth except where the same pale scar runs—always left-of-center, always the exact length of a child’s hand. They do not choose their places. A basin’s rim or a trench’s lip simply receives them, and they settle until their undersides match the rock’s temperature so perfectly that dusk’s first dew cannot tell the difference. Only the third eyelid, a smoked-glass shutter, moves. It slides once every few hours, slow as a glacier calving in another age, and in that motion you glimpse an eye the color of wet iron. They drink by existing. The thin film of night moisture beads along the plates, runs into the radial grooves, and is gone before the sun can argue. When one finally lifts—rarely, and only to settle a handsbreadth nearer a neighbor—the sound is the dry whisper of two old pages turning in an unread book.

Beside them, and sometimes upon them, the upright ones keep their congregations. They stand no taller than a ten-year-old, shoulders sloping into the next pair of shoulders until the group becomes a single low wall of flesh the color of tea-stained linen. Their faces, if they can be called faces, are the same worn suggestion as the plateau itself: two dark pits that may be eyes, a slit that may remember a mouth, all of it planed by the same weather that sanded the mountains away. They lean. That is their occupation and their architecture. The lean lasts seasons. Dew collects in the hollows of their collarbones and is shared without gesture; a bead simply travels the living masonry until it reaches whoever is driest. Their feet have compacted the stone beneath them into shallow, darker bowls—rooms the plateau never asked for and now cannot forget. After years the bowls hold a faint warmth even at noon, as if the ground itself has learned to remember bodies. They do not speak. When the wind finds the rare shrub it makes more sound than an entire congregation produces in a month. Yet they are not silent in the way of the dead. Stand close enough and you feel a tide, older than lungs, moving through the linked shoulders: a slow inhale that lasts from first light to the hour the basins begin to click.

The long ones refuse the trenches. They braid the unused stone, pale bellies flashing like the undersides of clouds that never quite arrive. Each is the length of a fallen sapling, no thicker than a wrist, moving at the exact speed of the shade that slides off a shield-beast at mid-morning. They do not hunt so much as wait inside the geometry of the others. A larger presence settles; days pass; the smaller one is simply less, its pale length absorbed the way a stain is absorbed by old cloth. Their skins carry the dew in repeating clusters—never language, but the ghosts of words that decided they were finished being spoken. At night, if you remain, you may hear one of them set itself down: a single unused note, stone against stone, and then the plateau swallows the echo.

In the basins, after the brief gray rain, the glass-tadpoles appear. They are smoked-quartz commas, no longer than a fingernail, pulsing once every few breaths of the high air. They do not become anything else. When the water thins to a film they simply cloud, then vanish, leaving the stone darker for a day, as if the basin had dreamed them. Under the lichen live the salt-things: granules that shift when no one watches, tasting of the sea the plateau forgot. You should not taste them. The lichen itself is older than the last mountain, gray as the rock’s own patience, and it does not mind being walked on.

All of them together are the plateau’s final weather. They occupy the same shallow volume the way sediment occupies a lake that has already decided to become stone. Generation upon generation pressed flat, adjacent, almost touching. The paths they have worn are not roads; they are the memory of shoulders and bellies that have used the same world too long to leave it unshaped. Circles of smaller stones reappear around certain basins after every scatter of wind; no hand is seen, yet the circles return, the way a scar returns to skin that has forgotten the wound. The air itself has taken on their rhythm. Distant things stay sharp because the sky no longer bothers to thicken. Nothing leaps. Nothing calls. The overwhelming is the agreement, made without a meeting, to remain.

Walk far enough and you begin to suspect the upright ones were once something that walked farther. Their alignments last years. Their compacted floors remember rooms. But the plateau does not distinguish. People, if they existed, have been sanded into another layer of leaning, another pair of shoulders in the quiet. The fauna are its mobile stone, its vegetation that learned to stand still, its last unfinished thought. The light stays ordinary. The warmth does not quite leave. And the density—visual, tactile, a faint musk of wet iron and dust—continues, almost finished, and has been almost finished for longer than finishing would take.
