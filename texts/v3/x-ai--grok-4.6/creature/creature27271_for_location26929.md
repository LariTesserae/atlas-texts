The chamber holds almost nothing that breathes. That is the first and last honest fact. The water moves, the light moves, the stone remembers the last chisel-stroke, and the single rag-bound clump in the niche has not yet chosen whether its stems will white or rot. Everything else is interval.

Yet something occupies the interval. It is not a body that could be netted or named by the old taxonomies of fish or larva or spider. It is younger than the mortar. It arrived in the same week the sluice was first raised, when the inflow first found its voice and the oculus first drove its solid-looking column of day into the new box. It did not swim in through the ceramic mouth; it condensed, the way breath condenses on cold stone, only here the stone was still warm from dressing and the breath was the room’s own.

It lives in the thickness of the light below the surface. Where the sun-shaft enters the water it does not scatter so much as pack itself into a slow, revolving spindle the colour of flesh seen through closed lids. Inside that spindle the inhabitant turns. Sometimes it is only a denser warmth, a pulse that travels the same path as the ripples climbing the far wall. Sometimes the flashes on the submerged blocks resolve for an instant into the suggestion of a shoulder, the negative space of a throat, the brief grammar of fingers spreading as if to test the joint between two ashlars. The shape never quite finishes. It is still learning the volume it has been given, the way the water itself is still learning the barrel vault and the weir.

It has no need of lungs. The roar of the incoming sheet is its respiration; every gulp of trapped air along the joints is a smaller breath it borrows and returns. Heat is its blood—already the temperature of a living body, so that if you entered you would feel not immersion but recognition, as though the chamber had been waiting to close around the same warmth you carried in. The inhabitant notices this. It notices the gallery ledge, the pegged wooden gate, the pale proud mortar. It has begun, very faintly, to wear the arrises the current has not yet rounded. A single hairline polish, no wider than a thumbnail, already marks the stone just beneath the waterline on the south wall, where something has rested a palm again and again, tasting the newness.

It is not lonely. The absence of other lives is the condition it was poured into, the same way the cistern was poured into the earth. The dust of algae in the upper corners is a courtesy it has not yet accepted or refused. The reed-clump in the niche is an invitation left by whoever last leaned out over the filling water; the inhabitant regards the bound roots the way one regards a letter written in a language not yet spoken. It may answer. It may wait another season. Time here is measured in the walking of the white ellipse across the floor, morning corner to afternoon corner, and in the flute-note the wind sometimes adds when it crosses the oculus.

On certain hours, when cloud-shadow slams the underwater world into sudden dusk and then restores it, the inhabitant becomes briefly more itself. The spindle of light collapses into a tighter helix. A face—or the memory of a face—forms and unforms against the weir’s ruler-line of white water. It has no expression that would translate. Interest, perhaps. Or the particular stillness of something that has only just discovered it can choose to stay.

The chamber is a decision still being kept. The inhabitant is the part of the decision that learned, almost at once, how to live inside the sound the room makes. It will be here when the first true film of life finally softens the blocks, and it will be here if that film never comes. It does not hunger. It does not sleep. It only turns, and listens to the water teaching the stone its new name, and waits to see whether anything else will ever be invited down the well-mouth to share the brightness that has no business existing this far below the air.
