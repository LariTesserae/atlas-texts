The mesa does not hold residents so much as it holds a single, unfinished occupancy. Everything that lives here arrived with the rock itself, or was already inside the stone, waiting for the shove that made a tableland out of a wound. What you meet when you climb the still-sharp rim is not a collection of species but a density that has decided, in the space of one long human life, to become rooms.

Stand in one of the sun-struck plazas and the first citizens you notice are the ones that refuse to move. Fist-sized, they cling in constellations across the plated trunks, the same tarnished-copper and dark-honey colors as the bark they feed on. Their armor is not carapace but a continuation of the plant’s own scales: overlapping, slightly translucent, locked so tightly that the boundary between feeder and food is only a change in the angle of light. They graze by sitting. Over months a single individual will etch a shallow, perfect scar the shape of its own underside, then shift a finger-width and begin again. The scars accumulate into faint bas-reliefs that the plants later overgrow, so that the walls themselves carry a slow, raised writing no one reads. When two of these armored bodies meet they do not fight or mate in any visible way; they simply press together until their plates interlock and, years later, one of them has become a slightly larger version of the other. They are the masonry that breathes.

Above them, in the vaults and the loft-work of paler stems, the gliders keep the air from being empty. Their bodies are almost nothing: two or three paper-thin vanes the color of old ivory, a central knot no bigger than a knuckle that holds the necessary chemistry. They do not fly. They allow themselves to be forgotten by gravity for a few minutes at a time, drifting from one honeyed opening to another on thermals so faint they register only as a change in the quality of shade. Their passage is the closest thing the mesa has to weather. Sometimes a glider will settle against a living wall and remain there for a season, vanes folded into the architecture, feeding so slowly that the plant incorporates it the way a building incorporates a nest. When it lifts again it leaves behind a slightly paler patch, a ghost window. The gliders never collide. Their paths are the negative space of the city, the corridors that remain open because something already knows how to occupy them without occupying them.

In the deeper chambers—the darker, slower quarter where the trunks form rooms that feel older than the mesa—live the pulser. Soft-bodied, forearm-long, the color of wet clay that has never seen rain. They lie in hollows the plants have left for them, or that they have persuaded the plants to leave. Once or twice an hour a pulse travels the length of a body, a single peristaltic thought that recycles the almost-nothing the system produces: a film of mineral dust, a flake of shed scale, the faint chemical exhalation of a thousand sealed metabolisms. The pulser do not eat so much as they remember what has already been eaten. Their motion is the only clock the mesa keeps. Watch one long enough and you begin to feel the settlement of the land itself—the imperceptible tilt the plants correct by growing against it—as a corresponding, equally slow contraction in the pulser’s flesh. They are the city’s digestion and its memory of having been born in violence.

Between these three visible orders the rest of the population exists as a continuous, almost mineral presence. Minute faceted rosettes that look like frost until they shift a fraction in the dust. Threads of living basalt no thicker than hair that run through the powdery floor and knit the plazas together. Things the size of a thumbnail that live entirely inside the translucent scales of the larger plants, photosynthesizing the light that has already been used once. Everything is sealed. Everything is thrifty. Nothing sings because singing would be waste. The hush is not silence; it is the sound of chemistry that has learned to keep its own counsel.

What is strange is not that they live this way but that they live it as if they have always known the plan. The plants grow streets the way a crystal grows facets, then discover, years later, that the streets already had names in the arrangement of their own cells. The animals occupy the rooms as if the rooms were made for them, which they were, though no one made them. There are districts: a quarter of deeper, almost subterranean chambers where the pulser gather in slow constellations and the light is the color of old honey held in a fist; a loft-work of quicker, paler growths that the gliders treat as an extended sky; a façade-line of ranked trunks whose hollows have become doorways that no one ever closes because nothing here has ever needed to keep anything out. Competition for light and mineral has produced the same solutions so consistently that the result looks like intention. It is intention, of a kind that does not require a mind larger than the mesa itself.

The place is still becoming. A new chamber opens when a trunk hollows from within, and the armored ones are already clinging to its inner walls before the last plate falls. Two neighboring stems lean a finger-width closer; a decade later they have fused into an arch that the gliders treat as a permanent current. The mesa settles another hair’s-breadth; the entire living structure grows against the tilt the way a sleeper, without waking, shifts the weight of a dream. No one here is old. The oldest living trunk is younger than the rock it stands on, and the rock is younger than a long human life. Yet they behave as if they remember a city that existed before the shove, or as if they are the city that the shove was always going to produce.

Turn from the cliff-edge—the drop still rude, the surrounding country still cracked—and the rooms close around you again. Warm. Dry. Full. The lives that do not need to speak continue their geologic conversations: a scar deepening on a wall, a vane unfolding in a shaft of ordinary sun, a pulse traveling the length of a body that has never known thirst. They are not waiting for anything. They are the occupancy that arrived the moment the land finished rising, and they have only just begun to live in it. The mesa is a room that forgot its ceiling. What lives here is the decision, still being made, to furnish it completely.
