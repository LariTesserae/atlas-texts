They are the ones who remember the making because they have not yet had time to forget it. Their hands still carry the ghost of wet plaster, the ridge of a twig drawn once and then left because the pattern had decided it was finished. You meet them first as a density that matches the place: bodies that occupy the width of a walkway without apology, shoulders that know exactly how far they may rise before they brush the thorn-lattices or the seed-pod curtains. They are not tall. The drop from the lip of brick is the height of one of them standing, and they have arranged their lives so that no further height is required. Skin the exact pale of limewash, or the warmer ochre of the packed dust, or the dull copper of those leaves that have begun to photosynthesize heat instead of light. Moisture is a private fact they keep inside the way the unopened jar keeps whatever it keeps. They do not sweat. They do not spit. When they speak—and they do speak, low, close to the teeth—the words taste of crushed coriander and insect dust and do not travel far. Song is leaking; they have decided against it.

They move as if the place were still being finished around them, which, in the slow resin of its time, it is. A woman will pause on a terrace no deeper than a coffin and press her palm to a wall that was flush yesterday; by evening a niche has appeared, already furnished with a sleeping ledge and a basin that holds only light, the plaster still damp and printed with her own thumb. She does not claim the room. The room claims her for a night, or a season. A child—there are children, or there were, or there will be—will leave a ladder against a terrace and return a week later to find it has put out a rung that is also a leaf; the child uses the leaf once, to scrape a pot, and then the leaf is ashamed and crumbles into the same ochre dust that fills the joints. They accept this the way they accept the goat that walks through the north wall every late afternoon and steps out of its own mural on the south, a little more faded, still chewing. They update nothing. The mural updates itself. Sometimes one of them is the goat. Sometimes the goat is one of them, slightly more faded, chewing the idea of a vine.

They tend what they made and what has begun to make itself. They needle fresh felt over the bone armatures when the lint falls, smelling of sheep they no longer keep and incense they no longer burn. They train the dwarf olives along wires of twisted sinew, leaves the color of tarnished coin, stomata sealed. They do not water. Water is a story told about other places, and they have plastered even the forgetting. Their food is the dry lushness itself: saltbush eaten like a pelt, parchment leaves that taste of old maps, thin-beaten copper that warms the tongue like a swallowed coin, the occasional true olive that has forgotten it ever needed rain. The mute birds that step downward through the layered air will sometimes hatch from the pots they leave out; the people eat those, too, or they do not, and the pots do not mind either way. At the spiral that does not close they leave what is not wet: a twist of their own hair, a beetle-shell still warm from the click, a word written on a leaf that will not decay. The gap fails to refuse the offerings. They lean in with the rest of the garden to hear what the lime is thinking about the next hundred years of not-cracking.

There are others that live among them, or as them, or through them. The small dogs with cropped ears sleep in the shade of the terraces and dream with their eyes open; the people do the same, because night returns only the heat the bricks were given, and closed eyes would be another kind of leaking. Lizards occupy every unmortared seam and also the people: a man will sit so still against a limewashed wall that the wall blinks, and for a while he is the exact pale of the plaster, thumbprints and all. The thing that is like a monkey, or a lemur, or a well-made puppet of a lemur travels the thorn-lattices on hands with too many knuckles; sometimes it is a child who has spent too long in the joins, the extra joints a gift the place gave and did not take back. The moths roost in the felt trees with wings folded into the nap; when they move they move like a thought one of the people almost had, and sometimes the thought is the moth, and the person is lighter afterward, drier.

They know the enclosure is the world. Beyond the knife-edged lip the plateau is a rumor, or a badly maintained painting, and they do not step up. Distance has already begun to slip inside: a walkway of twelve steps becomes nine if they carry something that casts a shadow, or twenty if they do not. Shadows pool and fork and refuse to point; a single person standing at noon will darken two terraces at once, and both shadows will believe themselves unique. They have learned to walk in the places where the air is thinner, or thicker, or briefly tastes of a river none of them have standing to remember. They put a hand in, feel the grain of the world running the wrong way, and take the hand back. The hand is usually the same hand. They are not alarmed. The place is young in the way a scab is young. The weirdness is still happening, slowly, in the joins, and they live in the joins.

At the center that migrates they gather without gathering, the quiet deepest there. You can hear them listening to the constructed sun—no: the real sun over a fully invented ground—leave its last band of ordinary gold on the western coping. The rectangle of sky goes the color of a bruise that has decided not to hurt. The moths open. The lizards become the walls become the people. Somewhere the single clay bell that has never been struck exists, and they exist around the fact of its not-striking. They hold their breath with the whole small country, sharp-edged, hand-made, warm as a sleeping spine. They have only just begun to exist. They have already started, very slowly, to come apart at the rules. They are the coming-apart. They are what lives there.
