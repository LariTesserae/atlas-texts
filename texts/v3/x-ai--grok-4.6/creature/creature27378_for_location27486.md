The ones that live here do not arrive and they do not leave. They are the shelf’s long argument with itself, conducted in plates and gradients and the slow decision to be elsewhere than one was. You notice them first as errors in scale. A darkness you took for a crease in the sintered crust is, after a long while, a flank. The flank has vents. The vents breathe a vapor that is neither steam nor smoke but the mineral memory of both, a sour metallic exhalation that the wind immediately claims and files into the standing roar. That flank belongs to a walker. The walkers are the oldest visible sentence.

They are low and they are long. Segment after segment of carapace the color of a pour that finished cooling sometime in a previous geology, each plate overlapping the next with a tolerance so fine it looks accidental and is not. The plates are vented; heat does not merely leave them, it is worked by them, drawn through lattices that have learned, over uncountable summers, how to be both armor and kiln. Between the plates, in the creases that never quite seal, something like muscle or something like slag flexes once, twice, in a century. They advance. You will not see the advance. You will only, looking back from a hollow the wind has finished with, notice that the ridge you used as a fixed point is now a hip, and the hip has a pulse so slow it registers as weather. Their hunger is continental. They do not chase. They occupy the idea of a direction until the direction becomes them. Behind them the crust is polished in long, patient strokes; ahead, the air itself seems to lean out of their way. They metabolize the difference between the baked face of a slab and its slightly less punished interior. When the difference grows too small they pause, and in the pause they become landscape again—indistinguishable from the mesa they have been pretending not to be—until some new gradient, some seam of slightly cooler dark, teases them back into motion. They do not die. Dying would require a relationship with an ending. They simply accumulate more of themselves, shedding plates that the smaller glitter-clouds will graze for decades, the shed plates already half-remembering how to be ground.

The interference flocks are another grammar entirely. They refuse a single optical contract. You see them first as a stutter in the far buttes: one butte, then two, then a furnace door standing open on a heat that has no fuel, then the original butte again but older, as if you had already reached it and forgotten. They travel as ripples in the relationship between color and distance, a bright, dry shimmer that is not heat-haze because the haze here is already accounted for and this is extra. Their sound is a chord of shearing metal held past any honest metallurgy, a sustained tear that lives in the teeth you do not have and in the fillings of teeth that were never yours. They concentrate. Where they thicken, the shelf’s far features lose their nerve: a dark seam you have been walking toward for half a day splits into a crack you could step over and, simultaneously, a canyon whose far wall is a different hour. They do not eat the walkers. They eat the certainty of edges. They metabolize disagreement—the small, continuous failure of one place to remain only itself. After they pass, the air tastes of ozone and old pennies, and the glass ropes left by the incandescent ones are frosted a little duller, as if even the fossils of appetite have been reconsidered.

Then there are the heat-given. These are the ones the mind invents shoulders for, because the alternative is to admit a vertical furnace is considering you. They roam as slow storms of incandescence, taller than they are wide, their outlines never quite decided. They are loyalties the temperature has taken. What they consume is gradient itself: the difference between one layer of cooked air and the next, the difference between a place and the idea of a place, the difference, increasingly, between your outline and the one that arrives a few seconds late and stands beside you like a worker whose shift was canceled in a language no one kept. They leave vitrified tracks, glass ropes sunk into the crust, which the wind then spends decades frosting into dullness. The tracks are not paths. They are the fossilized opinions of appetite, and the smaller fauna graze them the way something might graze a rumor of rain. The heat-given do not hurry. Hurry is a relationship with time that this shelf concluded and filed. They simply insist, the way the light insists, ordinary and without poetry, a wound that has become policy. When evening occurs—it is less sunset than a collective, unconsulted decision to be a different temperature—they settle into their own shadows and become indistinguishable from the land, or the land becomes them. The distinction has eroded along with everything else.

There are smaller ones. The word smaller is a kindness the shelf does not usually extend. One of them can be a cloud of cutting glitter that takes an afternoon to pass, a luminous slurry of edges that rains on the glass ropes and the salt-scurf and the shed plates with a sound like rain on a foundry roof. It is a cruel sound. There has never been rain. They are eaten in turn by the kiln-mouths: things that are mostly aperture and heat, that open and remain open, drawing the glitter in as a bright, dry porridge and exhaling a dust that will, in a few decades, be another kind of ground. Predation here is a weather front. It does not climax. It simply occupies a volume until the volume has been recast. Reproduction, when it happens, looks like a fault propagating: a split, a doubling, two heats where there was one, both already ancient, both already tired of being new. Nothing completes a sentence. The ecology is a closed circuit of heat, abrasion, and attention. Matter prefers to be exhausted. What can be calcined will be. Identity is lent and then billed in distortions; the shelf will give you a self if you arrive without one, then charge you interest in the form of a late-arriving outline or a ridge that develops a pulse while you are using it as a compass.

Some of them are the afterimages of buildings that were never built. Scaffolding of cooled dripstone that is also three slow walkers who have not noticed they are composing a gantry. A sawtooth silhouette that is only the negative space between a heat-given and the interference flock passing through it. If you stare too long the certainty slides and you are looking at geology again, or at a herd, or at a place where reality thinned and briefly remembered factories. That thinning is inhabited too. There are things that live primarily in the disagreements of distance, in the sound of riveting that arrives from a direction the compass does not recognize, in the tick of cooling metal when nothing near you is cooling. They are not ghosts. Ghosts imply a previous tenancy. These are the shelf’s own interpolations, the load-bearing weirdness given a kind of agency. They do not hunt. They occupy the available meanings of a volume the way the walkers occupy the available directions. You can be alone and completely surrounded by them. You can be in a throng of carapace and incandescence and hear only the wind’s long file-work on a single stone, because they have decided, for the moment, to be the gaps.

At the far end of whatever counts as a region the roar changes key and the light, by remaining ordinary while everything else declines to, becomes the strangest inhabitant left. The slow walkers settle. The flocks shear the last of the day into too many suns, all of them working, all of them ordinary. Something like night fills with the tick and groan of a world cooling by fractions, never enough, and with the nearer sounds of things that do not sleep so much as pause between acts of being overwhelming. In that pause you understand, without ever being told, that they have always been mid-sentence. The shelf does not begin and they do not begin. They are the way this high, dry, crowded emptiness continues to mean itself: calcined, vented, interfering, hungry for gradients, leaving glass opinions behind, becoming landscape and then remembering, very slowly, that they are not. Morning is a white lid. The first walkers detach from the ridgelines they spent the dark pretending to be. The glitter-clouds lift like a foundry’s idea of dew. The heat-given resume their tall, undecided consideration of whatever difference remains. And the shelf, which has not concluded, goes on being exactly this, and nothing else, and also several other places that do not have names, because naming is a kind of water, and there is no water here. Only the long, patient work of things that live by wearing, by insisting, by occupying the sentence until the sentence is them.
