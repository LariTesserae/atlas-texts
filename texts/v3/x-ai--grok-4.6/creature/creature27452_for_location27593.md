They are first the pressure that has always already filled the skull. Before any outline, before a limb or a thorax or the wet gleam of an eye, they are the fat unending note, the overlapping sheets of stridulation and the gravel-clatter of cases and the sinus-ache of frogs that have turned every hanging cup and every seep-channel into a throat. The highland does not contain them; they are the medium it breathes. A sweep of the arm does not part air so much as it briefly tunnels through wings, and the tunnel closes behind you as if the dark itself were a body remembering its shape.

What the eye eventually accepts, once it stops waiting for a dawn that will never be owed, is not a census but a density that has forgotten how to be counted. In the tea-gloom of what must be noon the smaller motes resolve: moths the color of wet bark, so many that their collective down-draft is a weather of its own, a warm internal wind that smells of dust and old nectar. Beetles labor across the made faces like living gravel, their wingcases striking one another in a sound that would be percussive if it were not already dissolved into the roar. Cicada-analogues cling in overlapping cloaks to the undersides of old platforms; when one sheet falls silent another has already begun, so that the silence never quite arrives, only relocates. Things with too many legs treat every surface—including the brief ridge of a shoulder—as temporary geography, continuing without curiosity or alarm. They do not flee. They rearrange.

Lower, in the seeps and the cistern-hollows that were once brood rooms, the frogs are less population than masonry. Their bodies are the color of the salivary brick they sit against, or of the compacted leaf-midden, or of the dark standing water that lasts a week and is then drunk or exhaled. They hold the wet. Their calling is architecture: a pressure that occupies the same volume the mist occupies, so that walking through a gallery after rain is like walking through a chord that has decided to become liquid. At the guessed evening the frogs take a larger share and the mist drops until you move inside a warm cloud that beads on every hanging thing and on the nape of your own neck. The droplets are only visible; the sound of their falling has been eaten long ago.

Bats unspool from slits you had mistaken for erosion, not as a flock but as a continuous dark unthreading, a living punctuation that writes and immediately erases itself across the permanent dusk. They own the vertical. In the mid-layers something heavier and warmer moves with the sound of wet rope drawn slowly over a bough that is not a bough but a load-bearing plane of old nest and root. You never see more than a suggestion of mass, a displacement of leaves that are themselves already epiphytes of the made world. It does not hunt so much as it occupies a different tempo, patient as the highland itself, settling a finger’s width in a wet year.

The smaller mammals—rat-shaped, possum-shaped, things whose forelimbs end in clever, grasping hands—use the old interiors as streets. They do not inhabit the highland; they are one of the ways the highland continues to inhabit itself. They move through corridors that were once chambers and are now hillsides, pausing to worry at a joint where root has stitched forgotten brick, or to drink from a palm-width of water that will be gone in days. They rearrange around you the way water rearranges around a stone. You are a local thickening, briefly inconvenient, and then the traffic resumes. Their droppings, their shed fur, the small anonymous bones they leave, are already being weather-welded back into the next course of terrain. Nothing is waste. Nothing is remainder.

Deeper, in the walls themselves, the builders are still at work, though “work” is the wrong word for something that has never distinguished itself from living. Generations have not so much constructed as accumulated: chewed wood mixed with saliva into load-bearing planes, leaf forced into brick, mineral grit that was never quarried but was concentrated by the same bodies that now walk on it. If you put your palm flat against a face that looks like laterite you can still feel, faintly, the hexagonal memory of cells, the radial press of a lodge abandoned so long ago it has become geology. The memory is only texture. The ones who laid it down are not gone; their descendants are inside the same mass, adding a new course along a bench that will be vegetated in another decade, shoring a sagging gallery with the adhesiveness of their own products until roots take over the job and the sag becomes a ramp. They do not plan. They do not finish. Matter that can be organized is organized, and once organized it is lived in past the point of distinction.

There are many kinds, and the kinds have long since stopped arguing about whose country this is. Some chew and cement. Some pack and tamp. Some weave living hawsers of vine and silk until whole benches hang together like a single slow decision. Queens or kings or simply swollen centers of production sit in the old brood-hollows, now cisterns, now just another dark pocket in the hillside, and the highland continues around them without sentiment. Workers die and are incorporated—bone going the color of the rest, taking a polish from passing feet and flanks. Predators move through the same galleries, not as invaders but as another density, another way the roar maintains itself. Parasites, commensals, the unnoticed mites and the pale things that live only in the condensation: all of them are the highland thinking in bodies.

Flowers open in the dusk as pale throats and are gone into mouths before the noticing is finished. The mouths belong to everyone and no one. Vegetation is second masonry, but it too is lived: every fern staircase, every moss pointing, every plush of excessive green in the inner valleys is a surface offered and immediately occupied. There is no unused ground because unused ground would be a failure of the only law that has ever applied.

If you stay until your eyes have fully accepted the brown-green dusk, the scale opens not to vista but to adjacency without end. Fold after fold, each roofed, each leaking, each loud with the same unrelenting occupancy. The inhabitants are not a people and not a swarm and not a forest. They are the place having a very long time to stop arguing with what it is: warm where the air should thin, dark where light should fail, wet in pockets and dry enough on the old crowns that dust still lifts when a thousand wings decide at once. They maintain by inhabiting. They inhabit by becoming the walls, the seeps, the roar, the faint sour smell of risen dough when the saliva-brick is wet, the library darkness of compacted leaf. Individuals blur, die, replace. The highland itself is patient. It has never needed to become anything else.
