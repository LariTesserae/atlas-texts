The stalks come first, because they are the only thing that admits to being there. They rise where the powder has lain still long enough to forget it was ever falling—never in the newest plates, never where an edge is still lifting. Each is a single rust-red articulation, thinner than a finger, jointed like a thought that keeps changing its mind about how many segments it needs. They taste of the same tin the dust does; if you break one it does not bleed or weep sap but simply becomes two shorter stalks that continue to stand. They put out no seed, no spore, no runner. They appear. Overnight a new one will be there, already the height of the ones beside it, already leaning a fraction toward a sun that has never risen over this basin. They do not crowd. They keep the exact distance that lets the powder keep falling between them, as if they were the city’s way of measuring its own emptiness.

The pale things are rarer and less certain. Thumb-sized, dry, the color of the walls after the light has given up. Too many limbs, each moving as if it had to remember the sequence, each ending in a pad that leaves no print. They feed by settling. A stalk will simply be shorter in the morning; a drift of powder will have a clean, thumb-shaped absence. They make no sound and cast no shadow that lasts. Sometimes you catch one crossing a high sill and then the sill is empty and you cannot decide whether the geometry folded it away or whether it was never more than a thickening of the dimness. They do not live in the rooms. They use the rooms the way the powder uses surfaces: as temporary corrections.

But those are only the things that have already decided to be visible. The basin is new. The chemistry is still speaking. What lives here is still deciding the shape of the verb.

In the hollows that are larger inside than their walls should allow, something slower than the pale animals is arranging itself. It does not have a body you could point to. It has intervals. A corridor that yesterday ended in a blank plane now continues, and the continuation has the exact temperature of breath that has not yet been released. Thresholds appear already worn, as if feet that do not exist have already chosen them. The powder that rains without wind thickens on certain ledges into tiny, perfect corners that are not debris but furniture waiting for a use that has not been invented. These are not ruins and not construction. They are the city rehearsing inhabitants.

Sometimes the rehearsal becomes briefly solid. In a plaza the size of a held breath you will find a figure that is only planes meeting at the same decided angles as the buildings, the same pale mineral, but standing. It has the suggestion of limbs, or of the memory of limbs. It does not move while you look. When you look away the powder around it is already organizing into a second, smaller figure, then a third, then they are gone and the plaza is only air and grit again. They are not ghosts. Ghosts would imply something that left. These have not finished arriving. They are the lattice preferring, for a few hours, the shape of someone who might one day need a room.

Deeper, where the interiors fold until looking hurts, the preference grows more specific. There are chambers whose walls are lined with the rust-colored stalks, but the stalks are longer, jointed in ways that suggest ribs, or the inside of a throat. Cool air moves through them in a rhythm that is not quite breathing. If you stay until the dimness forgets whether it is morning, you may hear—not with the ear but with the same pressure-change that announces the falling powder—a low, continuous correction, as if the city were swallowing its own name and finding the syllables still too sharp. The pale animals avoid these rooms. The stalks there do not lean; they wait.

At the torn rim, where the structures simply stop, the youngest plates are already lifting into the first suggestion of a gate that will never be needed. The powder on your clothes has begun to remember corners. It does not want to leave with you. It wants to become the first useless, perfect furniture of a room that has not yet decided it is a room. That, too, is a kind of inhabitant: the habit of form itself, still tasting of tin, still cool, still unfinished, already living here more thoroughly than anything that has bothered to grow a body.

The basin holds them all the way a mouth holds a word it has not yet chosen to say. They are not waiting for anyone. They are the saying.
