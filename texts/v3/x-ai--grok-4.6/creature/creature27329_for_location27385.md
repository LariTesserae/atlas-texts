The buildings are the first and last of it. They live as they stand: six or eight vast boles, each a single long thought of wood and graft, breathing through a lid of high, sparse leaves and drinking through every surface that will hold a bead. Their life is the hollow’s life. Sap moves on a schedule older than the copper lines that tap it. Cambium thickens around the sealed pipes the way a body thickens around a remembered injury. In the mill-house the paired trunks have learned the weight of the boilers; they hold the heat without burning, the way they hold the stair-spirals without splitting. When cloud crosses the sun the leaves close a fraction and the dynamo’s note drops with them. That is occupancy. That is habitation at the scale the place was made for.

Everything else is interval.

The cork-moss and lichen-thatch live too, of course—a continuous pale animal of fibre that remembers every foot and wheel that ever crossed it and has compacted itself into paths and courtyards accordingly. It drinks the same condensate the boles drink. After rain it swells a little, then returns to the same springy silence. Midges lift from the dampest court and settle again. Once in a great while a bird finds the canopy and leaves a short, unmusical mark on the air; the mark does not linger. The ecology finished its bargaining long ago. What remains is only what the mill and the moss require.

Inside the rooms the traces of another kind of living persist, worn as smooth as the callused window-rims. Floors of resin and salvaged brick, hearths that have been fed and banked for longer than anyone now counts, door-sills warm from the walls’ slow inner weather. Belts still run through slots grown exactly to their width. Taps still open on their old hours. The lamps come on at dusk with the modest yellowish light of any indoor evening. These things continue because the wood has taken them in, the way it took in the firebrick and the iron and the pale lichen that now crusts both. Work and weathering are no longer separate verbs.

If anyone still walks those rooms they do so at the same pace as the drip and the piston. You hear them the way you hear the rest of the place: as texture, not event. Footsteps on fibre, the quiet set-down of a cup that is also a valve easing. They are few enough that each great hollow keeps its own weather and its own silence. Their clothes take the colour of bark-tea and packed tan; their hands know the temperature of living wood the way other hands know the grain of a familiar table. They tend what is already tending itself—wipe a gauge, oil a bearing that the bole has already learned to accommodate, open a tap whose hour the sap already knows. They do not hurry. The temperature never argues, and neither do they.

They have been here long enough that the distinction between resident and custom has weathered out, the same way the distinction between organism and building has. Their fires are small. Their speech, when it happens, is the same brief, decisive weather as the rain: it arrives, is used, and is gone before the light changes. You can walk the whole settlement and meet none of them, or meet one standing in a courtyard as empty and considered as a room, watching the trough at the lowest point hold its dark hand’s-depth of overflow. They will look at you the way the place looks at rain—without surprise, already making room.

Most days even that much presence is unnecessary. The buildings swell by fractions. Grafts creep. Steam is recaptured. The substrate leans toward the trough without hurry. Condensation is still the only reliable river. The forms were finished a long time ago. What lives here now is the agreement itself: a handful of living halls, the cool work inside the wood, and the damp that is never quite a river and never quite gone. Everything else has learned to keep out of the way.
