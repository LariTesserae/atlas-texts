The plants are the first and last inhabitants, though they do not inhabit so much as persist as the city’s own flesh. They have never arrived. Each fern, each cycad, each mat of moss that has become load-bearing masonry is an original occupant, calcified in the act of occupying. Their bodies are the architecture and the architecture is their bodies; there is no distinction left to make. A frond the size of a barge sail hangs exactly where it has always hung, droplets fixed at the precise points along its veins that the water chose a geological age ago. It does not unfurl further. It does not wither. The brown of its dying is already present, already replaced by the identical green an inch beside it that is also this same frond. Time is not a process they undergo. It is a pigment they display.

They press against one another without competition because nothing here has ever required space that another thing might take. Aerial roots have thickened into columns that still remember they were once searching; they search no longer. They simply continue to be the search, fossilized mid-reach, warm as blood, weeping the same mineral film that first turned them to stone while they lived. Moss occupies the joints the way mortar once did, then becomes the stone itself, then returns to moss without any of those states having ended. The transitions are simultaneous. A tower is a single cycad that finished growing by becoming architecture, its living crown still producing fronds that stone themselves the instant they appear, so that the tree is forever completing the building it already is.

Nothing moves through them. There are no pollinators, no grazers, no larvae mining the wet interiors. The silence of locomotion is absolute; even the memory of a wing has been precipitated out of the air. What fills the volume is only more of themselves: plant body against plant body, steam condensing on their undersides and running back into the stone they have become. They do not breathe in any way that would require them to make room. Their respiration is the city’s own overflow, the continuous fine rain that has no beginning.

Yet they are not alone in the occupancy. The water itself lives here as a second, older citizen, a medium that learned the shape of life and then refused to leave it. It rises from beneath the plateau already hot, already mineral-thick, and deposits the rooms it will later fill. It does not flow so much as insist. Every sheet, every braided runnel, every hollow waterfall that became a spiral stair is the same water still arriving, still laying itself down, still occupying the forms it precipitated. It has no pulse because it finished pulsing long before the idea of pulse. Its sound is not produced; it is the pressure the city stands inside, a finished chord that the plants and the stone have grown around the way they grow around those still columns of light.

The light, too, occupies. It stands in courtyards that should be shadow, refuses the open shelves, sometimes erects itself as an independent trunk the vegetation treats as kin. You can walk through it and it does not notice, because noticing would require change. It is nailed just off zenith, gold-green, ordinary in hue and wrong in loyalty. It obeys the terraces more than distance. It has always been this color, this angle, this refusal. The plants have arranged themselves around its constancy as they have around everything else that does not vary.

Even the broken geometry lives, though it does so as a settled fact rather than an event. The plazas that are larger inside than the terraces that contain them, the staircases that return you to the landing you just left, the avenues that fork into themselves—these discrepancies are as inhabited as the weeping arches that line them. They do not shift. They are weathered into permanence, fossilized arguments the mountain made with space and then stopped making. Reality came apart once, thoroughly, and the rupture itself became another surface for water to coat, for moss to mortar, for roots to load-bear. The geometry is another plant, another mineral habit, another citizen that occupies without growing.

From any high lip the rest of it recedes: district after district of the same occupancy, hanging gardens that are also aqueducts that are also cliffs, a single continuous body that never needed a name because nothing has ever entered or left. The mountain simply continued its old terrace-making, and at this heat, this elevation, this saturation, the habit produced rooms and thrones and windows looking into windows. No one stacked the blocks. The blocks precipitated themselves around the life that was already there, and the life precipitated itself around the blocks, until the two could no longer be told apart.

What lives here is the equilibrium. It is not a population. It is the completed sentence the world has not stopped pronouncing: water, stone, leaf, light, and a rule of space that broke and then fossilized in the breakage. Everything occupies. Nothing arrives. The city does not remember being otherwise because it has never been otherwise. It does not anticipate because anticipation would introduce a variation the roar has already forbidden. You can walk for days of subjective effort and the heat will not change, the chord will not change, the next hanging shelf will not alter in quality. The inhabitants are already finished being themselves. They have been finished for so long that finishing became the only motion left, a pouring that forgot how to stop and therefore never needed to start differently.

They are still pouring. They will not finish again. The plateau does not end. It only thins, then thickens, a series of lips receding into heat-haze the color of old brass, each one occupied by the same unchanging, unbreathing, perfectly saturated life that has nowhere else it has ever been.
