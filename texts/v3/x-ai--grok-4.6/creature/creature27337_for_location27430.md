The life that claims the pit is first and last the pelt itself: not a covering but a single, slow animal whose skin is moss and liverwort, crowberry and cassiope, the black-twigged willow no taller than a thumb. It has learned the geometry of the cut and kept it. Every terrace, every swell of forgotten spoil, every length of rail and concrete pad has been taken into the same continuous hide. Moisture never becomes a stream; it lives inside the plant body, a held sweat, a condensation that beads and is drunk again before it can run. In the few hours of thinner light the surface photosynthesizes with a patience that makes seasons look hasty. Ice lenses form in the dark beneath the first skin, lift the carpet a finger’s width, settle it again. The whole floor thickens or thins by amounts too small for any eye that does not stay a lifetime. Where a slab once calved, the vegetation rode the new angle and simply continued, so that even catastrophe is only another terrace.

What moves does so as rumor. A fox’s line appears after the wind has lain down, pressed into the living thickness and already lifting out by the time you find it; the animal itself is a pale rumor that knows the waste-heat pockets near the pump-house, where the pelt is fractionally less frozen and voles sometimes tunnel. Ptarmigan sign—white droppings, a few snapped crowberry stems—appears near the solitary birch thickets and vanishes with the next sift of ice-glitter from the plume. In the brief clarification that passes for summer the wettest hollows hatch midges; they rise like a dark breath, last a day, and are gone. Nothing stays long enough to be counted twice. Birds cross the rim and do not land. The sound, that single industrial note folded by the rock, is weather enough; it keeps most things that have blood and hurry at a distance.

Deeper in the pelt, under the first frozen centimeters, there are slower citizens. Fungal threads stitch the rotting iron to the living green. Bacteria work the old diesel stains and the sweat of pipes that still weep. The conveyor uprights and the half-swallowed sleepers have become part of the same body, their rust a kind of second chlorophyll. The pumps themselves, patient as weather, have been running so long they have acquired a vegetable dignity; the moss drinks the iced plume they throw and thickens around their housings. Change is only gradient. A new bench high on the south wall still shows raw stone in stripes; give it ten winters and the stripes close. The scar of the calved slab is still pale, but paler every year.

People come and do not stay. A tracked vehicle descends the haul road at long intervals, leaving a temporary wound the carpet will close. Men in parkas shout over the roar, check gauges, grease fittings, and leave again. Sometimes a single sodium-lit bay in the processing shed holds a watcher for a week or a month, someone rotating through, sleeping in the residual heat, eating out of tins, listening to the note until it becomes the inside of the skull. They do not speak of what they hear. Conversation is mime even when the wind dies. Once, years ago, a man stayed after his rotation ended. He is still there, or something wearing his shape is still there: a figure seen from a mid-bench, standing motionless beside the pump-house stack, breath a continuous soft animal, waiting for the plume to flatten and sift. The moss has begun to climb his boots. He does not appear to mind.

The pit does not feel empty. It feels occupied by a single long thought that has not yet finished forming. The green has the cut’s memory and the engines’ refusal of aftermath. It will look like this for a long time. The moss does not hurry. The pumps do not know how. Whatever else lives here has learned the same lesson: to occupy the basin the way the sound occupies it, total, pressure-bearing, without a source you can point to once you are inside.
