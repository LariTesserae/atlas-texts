The amphipods come first, because they are everywhere and they never stop. They are the size of rice grains, translucent except for the dark thread of gut, and they graze the algal film that coats every vertical surface in slow, synchronized herds. When a thousand of them turn at once the wall looks briefly frosted, then the frost walks. They eat and they are eaten; the hair-thin worms that stitch the moss rise at intervals to take them, and the moths drop like wet leaves to take the worms. Nothing is wasted. Their bodies, once finished, are folded back into the peat by the same motion that made them.

The moths hang in layered curtains from the undersides of the living vaults. Their wings are the exact color and sheen of sodden bark; at rest they look like another fold of the ceiling. They never quite still. A constant, almost inaudible shiver runs through the hanging mass, a communal adjustment to the slow pulse of the fronds above them. When they fly it is only a few body-lengths, a brief rearrangement of darkness, and then they settle again. Their larvae live inside the softer internodes, hollowing them into galleries that later become stairs or cisterns if the people decide the wound is useful.

Deeper, where the galleries narrow and the air grows stiller, the green ones move. They are the size of large hares but longer in the body, the skin a cold, unbroken verdure that matches the plants so closely they vanish when they pause. The head is salamander-blunt, the eyes small and lidless, the feet padded and slightly webbed. They travel the trained runways—vines scored and weighted years ago until the plants themselves remember the path—as if the city had grown veins expressly for them. They drink from the leaf-cisterns, leaving no ripple. When they hunt one another the only sound is water finding a new level. They occupy the newly grown lofts without asking; a family will be curled in the exact sleeping-niche a human pair meant to claim, and the humans simply grow another niche a little farther along the same taproot. Eviction is not a concept that survives here. The green ones are not pets and not pests. They are the city’s blood made visible, the part of the hollow that hunts and is hunted so the rest of it can stay still.

The people themselves are fewer than the moths and quieter than the amphipods. They are nearly human in the way a trained vine is nearly a ladder: the shape is recognizable, the function altered by long agreement with the place. Their skin is pale and faintly mottled, the mottling the same chilled chlorophyll that stains everything else; in the rare moments when a fungal glow catches them they look briefly like another layer of the wall. Eyes exist—small, dark, often half-lidded—but they are not the first sense. Children are taught the city as a sequence of temperatures and textures: the warm vein that means this way, the cold hollow that means cistern or nest, the slight give of a living stair versus the stone-hard refusal of a lignified rib. A visitor, on the rare occasions one arrives, is given a staff cut from a vine that still remembers water; it leans, almost imperceptibly, toward the nearest inhabited cluster or the nearest drink. The people trust the lean more than they trust their own sight.

They work with thumbs, bone awls, and an inherited knowledge of which cuts a plant will forgive. A shoot is split, pinned with thorn-pegs, waited upon. Two vines are braided until the braid forgets it was ever two and becomes a load-bearing arch. Moss is encouraged, never forced, until it bricks itself in the wet angles and can hold a person’s weight. There is almost no metal. The single bronze needle is passed from hand to hand like a story that must not be dropped. What they wear is grown rather than sewn: mats of interwoven fiber that continue, slowly, to live; cloaks of moss that take root at the shoulders if left too long. They move through the chamber the way tenants move through a house that is also a gut—respectfully, with small repairs, accepting the rooms that close overnight and the furnishings that appear unbidden. A bench of fused petiole will have the exact curve of a tired back; a sleeping-niche will have already learned the shape of the body that will occupy it. It is never clear who designed what.

They do not name the city. They call it the hollow, or simply here, and they speak of it the way one speaks of a body one lives inside: with attention, without ownership. Property is a conversation. A vine that was once a ladder will, if cut, try to become a ladder again; the people listen and decide whether to let it. Some of the older inhabitants have begun to take on the plants’ patience. Their movements slow to match the internodal swell. A few of the very old no longer leave their taproot clusters; they sit in the growing furnishings and the furnishings grow around them until it is difficult to say where the person ends and the room begins. No one finds this alarming. The city is building its builders as much as the reverse, and the arrangement feels, to those who live it, like the only possible courtesy.

Outside, the marsh continues. The wound at the lip is already smaller than it was. Scouts—younger people, still quick, still mostly human—have begun laying the first trained stems along the raw rim, in case the settlement ever needs to climb. For now it does not. The pocket is enough. It holds the amphipods and the moths and the green ones and the people who have learned to live as another weather inside it, and the darkness that has, at last, learned how to be inhabited. If you climb out, the silence follows for a few steps, like a second ground, and then the ordinary marsh closes over it as if nothing had ever failed.
