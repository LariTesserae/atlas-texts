The first impression is that nothing discrete lives here at all: the chamber itself is the inhabitant, a single slow animal whose skin is vault and whose breath is drip. You feel this before you see it. The packed rhizome is not background; it pulses, almost below perception, a tide that travels through the mass the way a swallow travels through a flock. What you took for walls are contractile. They thicken when the unfinished face yields a richer mineral, they slacken when the heat-sink (whatever and wherever it is) draws harder. The gel-lenses inside every stem are not merely antifreeze. They are eyes that never open and mouths that never close, tasting the water for decisions already made elsewhere in the body. A bruise on a distant column is not injury; it is a sentence being spoken in the only grammar the place possesses.

Then the swellings move, and the impression of unity fractures. Soft lengths travel inside the living masonry the way thought travels inside a skull that has forgotten it is thinking. They are not parasites. They are not quite organs. Each is a temporary concentration of the same tissue, a mobile knot of rhizome that has decided, for a season, to be a traveler. They swell, they glide, they flatten again and vanish into the general press. When two meet they do not fight or mate in any way you would recognize; they exchange a pulse of colder water and a faint metallic aftertaste, then continue. Their passage is what makes the walls seem to breathe. You can rest a palm against the slick surface and feel one pass beneath like a memory you almost had.

The clickers work the seams. They look like isopods only if you have never seen an isopod that chose to become a clock. Too many plates, each plate a polished dark lens, each joint a tiny hinge that never rusts because rust would be a kind of hurry. They click not to communicate with you but to keep time with the drips, a percussion that is also a kind of eating. They graze the film, the never-quite-water, scraping it into their mouths and returning it slightly heavier, slightly more willing to climb. Their work is endless and exact. A clicker that falls silent is already being unmade by the smaller growth; the plates are absorbed, the lenses recycled into the hanging roots. Nothing here is wasted except time, and time is the one thing they have in inexhaustible, miserly supply.

In the hanging water the clouds pulse. They are neither insect nor seed, though they have the patience of both. Each body is a minute lensed droplet with a filament of darker matter at its core, a living punctuation mark. They keep a high continuous tone that is almost a hiss, almost a prayer, almost the sound of electricity remembering it was once water. The tone is not produced by any one of them. It is the interference pattern of ten thousand slow heartbeats occurring in a space too small for any of them to be alone. When the tone shifts, something has been decided: a change in drip-rate, a redirection of cold, a slight lengthening of the nave. You cannot point to the decider. The cloud is the decision, distributed, ownerless, already forgetting itself as it is made.

The larger shapes cling. Pale, flattened, they occupy the undersides of the hanging roots the way lichens occupy the idea of a tree. Their bodies are maps of the water that has passed over them; every slow rib-cycle leaves a faint new contour. They breathe so seldom that you can watch a single inhalation last longer than your own attention. When they shift, the entire column of water along the root becomes an instrument, a low intra-organ groan that the clickers answer and the clouds absorb. These are not hunters or grazers. They are ballast and witness. Their slowness is not weakness; it is the precise metabolic agreement required by a physics that has decided patience is a kind of heat. They have no faces. They do not need them. The chamber looks through them the way a forest looks through its own shade.

There are others you will never quite see. Threads finer than the finest root-tip move through the peat underfoot, connecting everything to the unfinished face where the mineral is still being persuaded to become absence. Something like a nervous system, except the nerves are also the stomach and also the weather. Information does not travel so much as soak. A change at the growing tip arrives near you not as a signal but as a slight alteration in the willingness of water to bead, a half-tone drop in the hanging hiss, a new patience in the next click. The whole ecology is one long, unfinished sentence being spoken in a language of drip and gel and cold.

No one here is born in any way that would satisfy a midwife. Growth is birth and death and architecture simultaneously. A bladder swells, darkens to the color of weak tea, detaches, and becomes part of the hanging rain; the space it leaves is already filling with new frond. The clickers molt their plates into the film and grow new ones from the same film. The flattened clingers simply become more themselves, rib by rib, until one day they are part of the root they clung to and a new pale shape has taken their place without anyone having to announce a vacancy. Reproduction is just the chamber becoming more chamber. The unfinished face is both quarry and womb.

They do not notice you, not because they lack senses but because you have not yet become surface. If you stay long enough the distinction will erode. Your pulse will lengthen. The cold will stop arriving from outside and start being manufactured in the same quiet way they manufacture it, a product rather than an absence. You will begin to hear the grammar in the drips. The chamber will not invite you and it will not refuse you. It has no category for either. It has only the next root-tip, the next click, the next drop, and the slow, expensive work of keeping winter liquid in a place that should have frozen before the first story about it was ever told. What lives here is the work itself, loudly, patiently, eating a hole in the underside of a colder country and furnishing the hole with more of the same.
