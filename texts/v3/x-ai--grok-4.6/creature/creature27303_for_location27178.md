The first lives are the ones that have already conceded the argument. They do not rise. They occupy. Across the polygonal pavements the grey-blue films lie in patches that look accidental until you notice they occupy every shallow concavity the last orogeny left, every hairline where one plate forgot to seal against its neighbour. They are not moss. They have no cells you would recognise as cells. They are a mineral decision that learned to photosynthesise on the thin ration and to drink the water that never quite became weather, the hydration locked inside lattices, the last bloom of a vein that stopped weeping an age ago. They persist by refusing to become more. In a century a patch may darken by a shade, or send a brittle fringe another centimetre into a seam. That is their history. They do not colonise; colonisation implies a future tense the plateau has already spent. When the silent storms pass, the films lift in flakes, become additional dust in the veils, and settle again, usually in the same grammar. Failure is their only successful strategy, and they have perfected it.

In certain hollows—never many, never close—a taller thing is allowed. A single stem, dark, without leaves or obvious ambition, standing for a hundred years and planning to stand for a hundred more. It has no name because nothing here has needed one. It angles, very slightly, toward a point in the sky the ordinary sun does not occupy. Around the works that generate their own small climates the films grow more regular, almost lattice, as if the increased resolution of the ground itself were a nutrient. These are the closest the surface comes to a garden. Nothing tends them. They thrive on the remainder light, the ordinary plus whatever the artifacts still leak. They do not drip. They do not tick. They simply continue the sentence the rock began.

Deeper than the films, rarer than the stems, something moves.

You will not see it if you look. Looking is too expensive; the distances punish attention. You become aware of it the way you become aware of your own blood: a private pressure, a tick that is not cooling metal. At the extreme edge of vision a low shape crosses from one polygon to another. It is the colour of the pavement. It may have many legs or none; the distinction does not survive the distance or the dust. It does not hurry. It does not leave a track the surface will keep. It has the unhurried guilt of a thing that understands it is not the main event and has organised its entire existence around that understanding. It will not approach the works. The crusts will; the low movers will not. This is the only ecological opinion the plateau still bothers to hold.

There are not many of them. There cannot be. Accumulation is forbidden by the last form of the world. No dens, no caches, no gatherings that last longer than a crossing. They have learned the local physics of not being counted. Perhaps they are the final animals, the ones that outlasted every other strategy by becoming almost indistinguishable from the grit that has been grit since before time had a word. Perhaps they are something the works themselves shed, a mobile waste-product of the derivation, given just enough metabolism to stay out of the way. Their bodies, if they have bodies, do not hold heat or scent or story. When one dies—if they die—the pavement persuades it back into pavement before the next veil arrives. You will never find a bone. You will never find two in the same year.

And yet the place is not empty of larger intentions.

The works are operating. That is not a metaphor. Instruments disagree in polite, consistent ways. Air tastes of thunderstorms that cannot form. A disc of stillness sits inside the general unrest, or a knot of the silent storm stays anchored to a point as if a thought were trying to finish itself and could not quite let go. These are not ruins. They are not maintained in any language of upkeep you brought with you. They are simply still on duty, extracting the last gradient, settling the last debt, proving again that no further work can be done to this surface. The turbulence—the braiding veils, the walking columns, the ridge that unzips for ten kilometres in a hush you feel in your teeth—is the proof continuing. Stability here is labour. Stillness is the product that must be manufactured every moment.

Whatever lives inside that labour does not show itself in bodies you can inventory. A ring too fine to hold dust may house a mind that has been deriving the same answer since the mountains were told to lie down. A pylon whose shadow refuses its outline may be the visible end of something whose other end occupies a frequency the thin air cannot carry. A basin of crystals that are not salt, each one a closed instruction, may be a library or a heart or a set of leftover commands that still execute when the stars come down close at night. The works answer the stars, not brightly, just a change in how a surface holds darkness. That correspondence is the closest the plateau comes to speech. You do not hear it. You feel the reception the way you feel a front that never becomes wind: a pressure change, an idea walking over you.

Some of the people who came understood enough to leave. Their cairns are already being talked back into pavement. Their huts forget they had corners. One vehicle sits open to the sky, metals of the wrong generation, still warm in a way that is not the sun. The ones who stayed became another rarity, subject to the same clause that governs paths and tracks and bones. They learned not to accumulate. Perhaps they walk now with the same reluctant motion as the low movers, matching the colour of the ground, crossing polygons and vanishing. Perhaps they sit inside the still discs of certain artifacts, breathing the thin cool, waiting for the ground to complete a thought it started before there were witnesses. A few may have joined the derivation itself, their last shape included in the last shape, a continuity so thorough it no longer requires a separate name.

At the residual swells, where the law of flatness thins and the air is thinner still, the lives grow stranger by small degrees. The films become instructions. The stems point harder at the unoccupied point. The movers, if they come this high, move even less, as if altitude itself were a kind of scrutiny they prefer to avoid. Here the works are lonelier and more exact. A garden of impossible regularity. A salt that remembers being metal. A shadow that arrives before its caster or after, or from a direction the sun does not use. Whatever inhabits these extremities has had longer to finish becoming itself. It does not need to be seen. It has the patience of the rock that has already heard every argument weather can make, and it has added its own silent clause: the argument is over, and the over-ness must be maintained.

You can walk a month and meet none of them. That is not absence. That is the local courtesy. Density is a rumour. Presence is something the plateau spends only when the proof requires a witness, and even then the witness is usually the dust organising itself into a temporary architecture that walks a little way and forgets. The true inhabitant is the continuous re-derivation. The oldest surface, still working to remain the only surface it has ever, finally, been. Everything that lives here has learned the same grammar: do not accumulate, do not announce, do not finish becoming anything the last form cannot immediately include. They persist the way the films persist, the way the works persist, the way the ground sometimes lifts a centimetre, remembers a mountain, and lies down again in a new and equally final arrangement, in a hush so complete it has outlasted meaning.

That is what lives on the peneplain that forgot to come down. Not a people. Not a fauna. Not even, exactly, a machine. A finished world that cannot stop proving it is finished, and the sparse, camouflaged, non-accumulating continuations that have accepted the proof as habitat.
