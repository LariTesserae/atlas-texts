They do not arrive. They do not leave. Whatever first decided to remain here did so before the rims of the dish had finished their long, unhurried collapse into this shallow bowl, and the decision has never been revised.

You notice them only after the roar has already become the inside of your skull. At first they are simply more stone: the same pale mineral, the same vertical flutings softened by millennia of film, the same shallow concavities that catch the light and throw it back in sliding needles. Then one of the nearer pillars shifts. Not a step. A settling, a transfer of weight so gradual that the water film on its surface never breaks, only thickens for a moment on the downward side and thins on the upward. The movement occupies perhaps the width of a hand. After that the pillar is still again, indistinguishable, and you cannot be certain which of the crowded forms it was.

They stand as the stones stand, packed close enough that passage requires the same sideways turn. Most are a little taller than a person; a few rise twice that height and taper into blunt, weathered heads that never quite become faces. Their surfaces weep exactly as the true stone weeps—an even, unceasing film, cold, mineral, clear. The water that leaves them is chemically identical to the water that arrives. They take nothing that can be measured and they add nothing that can be seen. Their only distinction is a slightly slower rhythm in the way the film beads and slides, as though the mineral itself were thinking.

There are no mouths. The grooves that look like mouths are only more of the same worn channels, and the water uses them without preference. There are no eyes. The concavities that might be eyes hold the same standing glare as every other wet hollow; if they regard anything it is the light itself, which they return unchanged. They have no need of shade. They have no need of silence. The roar is the medium in which they persist, the same way the channels are the medium in which the water persists. After long enough you understand that the roar is not around them. It is the sound of their stillness being continuously rinsed.

They do not hunt. Nothing here can be hunted. They do not grow. Growth would require a chemistry that has been forbidden. Reproduction, if it occurs, is a geological event: once in a span that has no weather in it, a new form simply stands where none stood before, already weathered, already weeping, already the same colour as the wet stone. Or perhaps an old one has only leaned far enough to be counted as two. The distinction is not useful. The crowding remains constant. The equilibrium does not notice.

Sometimes, far off, one of them will lean until its flank touches another. The contact lasts for what might be days. The films merge without swirl or argument, exactly as the channels merge. Then they separate again by the same imperceptible degree, and the joined water remembers nothing. That is the whole of their society: an occasional, unhurried adjacency that changes neither participant.

If you stay long enough that your own skin begins to weep in sympathy, you feel them as a single crowded body that happens to be standing in many places at once. The body is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply finished. It has already become the shape that erosion, water, and refusal agreed upon, and it has no further intention. You can walk among them for a long time. Behind you the gaps close. Ahead they open onto more of the same pale, glistening presences. None of them turn to watch you go. They are already watching, in the only way they know how: by remaining exactly as wet, exactly as still, exactly as present as the country that made them and that they, in turn, refuse to unmake.

The air stays thin and metallic. The light stays a glare without hour. The water stays the same thickness, the same temperature, the same unhurtable descent. And they stay with it, indistinguishable, necessary, already here.
