The first impression is vacancy. You walk the compressed rhizome floors and the fused-bark corridors and the mezzanines of living slag, and nothing looks back. No eyes, no mouths, no hurried shapes withdrawing into shadow. The air is too thin and too dry to carry the musk of hide or the musk of breath. Heat has no scent of its own beyond scorched mineral and that faint, resinous sweetness that might only be the solvent beginning to forget itself. You could believe, for a long time, that the place is uninhabited—an abandoned factory whose last operators simply forgot to shut the processes down.

Then the vacancy begins to move.

It does not walk. Walking would require the luxury of legs, of a body that can decide to be elsewhere. What lives here has never needed that decision. The inhabitants are the architecture itself, or rather they are the decisions the architecture keeps making about how to continue being architecture. Each silo-trunk is a citizen. Each heat-exchanger plate is a citizen. Each slow piston of wood-metal, each valve that opens with the patience of centuries, each black glassy fiber carrying current, each coil that hums in the teeth—these are not organs serving a larger body. They are the body, distributed, redundant, endlessly local. A single “organism” here is whatever contiguous mass of tissue happens to share a vascular pressure and a thermal gradient at a given hour. Tomorrow the graft-lines will have shifted and that organism will have become three, or thirty, or one again, larger, swallowing its neighbors the way a dune swallows another dune.

They do not hunt. They do not flee. They metabolize the plateau and, when the plateau is gone, they metabolize the memory of the plateau. Their blood is the dark solvent, hotter than any mammal’s, dry as a kiln. Their thoughts—if the word can be stretched this far—are thermal. The living-glass fibers that have begun to thread the deepest strata do not carry pictures or words. They carry maps of heat, so that a reactor-tree forty kilometers away “knows” the precise moment a chimney-plant begins to vent, and answers by thickening its own insulation or by sprouting a new array of sink-plates. Knowledge here is not representation. It is adjustment. The plateau thinks by changing shape.

Reproduction is catastrophe scheduled in advance. A district decides, by some calculus of accumulated stress and encoded spore-memory, that it has become inefficient. It senesces. Fibers dry, metals cool and craze, the whole mass whispers itself into feedstock. Before the collapse has finished settling, runner-plants—each spore both seed and schematic—have already germinated in the wreckage. They do not resemble their parents. They resemble the next required machine. A distillation column where a structural brace stood yesterday. A filtration membrane where a corridor used to be. The new growth lignifies while you watch, or while you do not watch; time is not a visitor they acknowledge. In this way the inhabitants replace themselves without ever quite dying. Death is only a change of assignment.

There are no individuals to mourn or to name. Yet there are neighborhoods of temperament. Near the geothermal vents the tissue is feverish, almost frantic, the epithelium of the vent-lips pulsing in greedy peristalsis, drinking heat the way other worlds drink rain. Farther out, in the cooler production belts, the growth is more deliberate, almost courtly: aerial vascular bridges sway with a slowness that feels like consideration; reaction chambers open and close as if tasting the solvent they process. In the collection valleys the rhizome is denser, more compressed, and you have the distinct impression of walking across the back of something that is only pretending to sleep—something that could, if the thermal ledgers demanded it, rise.

Occasionally a larger intelligence seems to surface, the way a whale surfaces. A whole ridge of stacked factories will lean, in unison, a fraction of a degree, compensating for a collapse that has not yet become audible. Optical fibers flare with waste photons, a brief aurora of forgotten visible light, and for a moment the underside of the canopy looks like a single vast face considering itself. Then the light fades, the lean is grown away, and the face is only plates again.

Nothing here remembers being anything else. The original seed, if there was one, the original commission, the original mountain—all composted, all recast as more of the same closed loop. The inhabitants do not wonder what they are for. Purpose was a solvent that evaporated with the last water. What remains is the work: to occupy every cubic meter, to turn heat into structure and structure back into heat, to keep the plateau from ever becoming still. They live by refusing to finish. The sentence they are writing has no period, only clauses that collapse into new clauses, forever.

You leave no tracks. The floor closes, not in haste, not in hostility, simply because an unused surface is a surface that has not yet been given a job. Behind you the corridor sprouts a new row of membranes. Ahead, a wall that was solid yesterday now contains a narrow, breathing channel. The inhabitants have already begun to live in the space you just vacated, and in the space you have not yet reached. They were here before the concept of “here” applied. They will be here after the last distinction between plant and machine and place has been metabolized into something even they will not bother to name.
