The builders are what the basin has instead of a people. They are not many. In a day’s circuit—if a day could be marked here—you might pass three, or five, or none at all, though the work never pauses. They keep to their own radii of labor, each a slow, self-contained weather system of impact and adjustment, and they meet only when one accretion finally collides with another. Even then they do not greet. They simply continue through one another, sharing a wall the way two currents share a channel, until the joint is indistinguishable and they have already drifted apart again.

What you see of them is almost never a whole figure. A forearm, slick and pale as wet limestone, levering a block whose weight would break an ordinary shoulder. A spine, long and segmented-looking under a film of stone-dust and condensation, bent so low it seems the torso has learned to fold like a hinge. A hand the size of a small anvil, fingers fused at the first joints by old scar and newer callus, the nails either gone or grown into blunt horn the same color as the copper chisels they still use. The rest is implied by the spaces they leave: the width of a corridor suddenly occupied, the change in the quality of the dark when a body blocks a drip-line, the faint iron-and-salt smell that is not quite sweat and not quite quarry water.

They are hairless, or the hair has long since been ground away and replaced by a mineral crust that flakes when they move. Their skin, where it shows, is the exact gray-white of freshly split stone that has not yet dried; it drinks the same films of moisture the masonry drinks. Eyes, if they have them, are unused. Work is done by the pads of the fingers, by the way sound changes when a joint is true, by the minute shift in the basin’s own hoarse voice when a course settles. Sometimes a spark from two glancing stones lights a face for half a heartbeat: no expression you can name, only the same absorbed attention a block gives to the block beneath it.

They do not sleep in any way that interrupts the work. They pause. A builder will stand motionless for an hour inside a half-finished chamber, forehead resting against a still-sweating wall, while the next decision arrives through the stone itself. Then the hands move again. Food is whatever still blunders in from beyond the lip— lean, startled things with hooves or pads that leave prints already artifacts by morning. These are used completely: meat for the rare hours when the body remembers hunger, hide and sinew for cordage and lashings, bone for wedges and punches, the rest mixed into mortar or packed into the hairline cracks that the basin keeps offering as further instruction. The pale elongated shapes that live in the wet joints—eyeless, almost boneless, tasting of mineral slime—are harvested the same way, without ceremony and without waste. Their absence is noticed only when a coil of fiber runs short.

Children, if that word still applies, are simply smaller versions already at work. You find them in the tightest passages, fitting the smallest stones, their movements already the same slow accident as the adults’. No one tends them. The place tends them. A dropped hammerstone is picked up by whichever set of hands is nearest; a failed block is broken down and restacked by whoever is passing. There is no apprenticeship because there is no other life to apprentice from. The basin is the only lesson, and it is taught by the act of covering.

They do not name themselves or the work. Speech, when it occurs, is the percussion itself—patterns of clack and thunk and groan that travel farther than any voice could in this wet geometry. A change in rhythm two neighborhoods over means a wall has begun to lean; a sudden bright shatter followed by silence means a block has failed and the fragments are already being gathered. The builders hear these things the way other creatures hear weather. They adjust. They incorporate. A warm seep that should not exist becomes the floor of a new cistern. A stone that rings hollow is left proud so later hammers can use it as a signal. They do not remark on the strangeness because the strangeness is only more material.

Stand long enough in one of the residual channels and you may feel one pass within an arm’s length: the displacement of air, the faint heat that is not quite body heat, the sense of a density moving through a greater density. Then it is gone, already three courses farther on, already deciding the next turn of a wall that will one day be someone else’s floor. They are not waiting for the basin to be finished. Finishing is not a concept that has purchase here. They are the basin’s way of continuing to happen—hands that may or may not understand they are also the lesson being taught, covering what remains, breaking what was, restacking until even the rumor of an original contour is only another joint to be dressed and laid.

Beyond the lip, presumably, other things still live that have not yet been brought inside and remade. No builder has gone to see. There is still too much surface that has not yet been broken and dressed, and the next rain is already beading on every unfinished edge.
