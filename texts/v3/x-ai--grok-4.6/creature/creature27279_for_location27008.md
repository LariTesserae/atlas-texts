The grazers are the first fact of the floor, and they have been the first fact for so long that the stone itself has learned their patience. Each is a fist of pale plates the color of old tendon, locked together with seams that weep a thin, sour mucus. They do not crawl so much as ratchet: a dozen short, hooked limbs find purchase in the microscopic roughness the water left behind, then pull, then lock again. Their mouths are not mouths. They are files. A living abrasive surface, endlessly rasping the bacterial velvet and the mineral blooms that sweat from the limestone in the constant damp. When two meet they do not fight or yield; they simply continue, plates clicking like wet pebbles poured from one palm to another, and the sound of a thousand such meetings is the basin’s lowest continuous voice, the one that never quite rises into the roar above it. They have no eyes. Light would be a rumor they have no organ to hear. A larger walker may take one in passing — a casual scoop, a crunch that is barely distinguished from the rest of the noise — and the gap seals in moments, neighboring grazers flowing sideways as if the world had only blinked.

Above them the walkers begin, and here the basin’s refusal of single forms becomes unmistakable. There are the low ones, broad as overturned tables, their backs worn smooth by the smaller traffic that uses them as moving causeways. Six legs, eight, sometimes a radial spray of twelve that move in slow, independent waves so that the whole animal seems to breathe sideways. Their hides are cool, pebbled, permanently filmed. Heat is a communal loan: they press flank to flank until the chill lessens by a degree that would be meaningless anywhere else and is, here, the difference between motion and the beginning of stillness. Eyes, when present, are cloudy coins set deep, useful only for the smear of motion at shin-height. Most navigate by the way sound thickens or thins, by the wet slap of contact, by the marinade of ammonia and sweet rot that never lifts. They eat without ceremony. A smaller walker is simply opened; a grazer is rasped from the stone like a scab. Nothing is stored. The climate does not change enough to require memory of scarcity.

Some walkers have abandoned the courtesy of separate skins. A single continuous hide roofs two nervous systems, or three, or five, the legs cooperating with the slight delay of furniture being carried through a narrow door. They argue in twitches that travel across the shared surface like weather. Whichever mouth is nearest the opportunity eats; the rest wait, or twitch disagreement, or begin to digest what the first has already claimed. These joined things move with a lumbering grace that is also a kind of conversation. When one section is taken — and they are taken, regularly — the remaining bodies do not flee. They simply reallocate the hide, seal the wound with a thickening of mucus, and continue, now fewer, now lighter, now arguing with one less voice.

Other walkers are hollow in the structural sense. Their bodies are galleries, ribbed chambers open to the damp air, and inside those chambers live the lesser fauna the way the basin itself lives inside its stone. You can see it when the weak light at ankle-height glances off wet hide: smaller shapes moving behind the translucent walls, rasping the inner surfaces, nesting in the joints. The host does not appear to mind. The tenants keep the galleries clean of the worst films; the host provides the climate and the occasional windfall of meat that filters down from the air. Sound lives inside sound. A cry from the hollow walker is accompanied by a fainter chorus from within, slightly delayed, like an echo that has learned to answer.

The largest shapes are not quite animals. They are densities that learned metabolism. A mound of shoulders and flanks, a slow dune of flesh that changes heading by a consensus no single mouth speaks. Legs appear and disappear at the edges. If you watch long enough — and watching here is mostly listening to the shift in the roar — the whole mass rotates a few degrees, or splits, or absorbs a smaller walker that came too close and could not decide whether it was joining or being joined. These living traffic jams leave the deepest hollows in the stone, not by intention but by the simple abrasion of their passing, completing the work the old water began.

The air is a second country, and it is denser than the ground. The fliers do not visit. They occupy. Wings of wet membrane slap and peel; others are stiff as scraped hide and click at the joints; still others are not wings but vanes or gas-filled sacs that row the thick air with a sound like distant oars. Their beating is the basin’s fundamental tone, the weather of noise that arrived before any picture. They live in columns and layers that have no names, staking claims with cries that are coordinates, warnings, recognitions, and occasional lies. Some never land. Their young are born in the volume, wet and unfinished, learning the basin first as collision and then as map. The dead do not fall so much as precipitate, a slow rain of meat and cartilage that the damp keeps from becoming dust. The walkers understand this rain the way they understand everything else: as direction, as opportunity. The grazers do not distinguish it from the mineral films. Food is not a category. It is a vector.

At the swallow-holes the density changes its flavor. The old water left throats in the stone, and the rims are now a single writhing gasket of specialists — folded membranes, faces that are only mouth, tongues like wet leather. Something calls from below the black lenses of the deeper pockets, a subtone felt in the teeth more than heard, and for a moment the gasket stills. Not fear. Attention with teeth. Then the call ends, or is answered, or was never a call, and the feeding resumes. A flier that misjudges the downdraft simply vanishes, added to whatever account is kept in the rock’s throat.

There is no architecture because there is no leftover impulse toward it. The worn hollows where the largest walkers rest are only newer erosion. Bones lose their names and become another polished knob, slightly warmer to a grazer’s rasp than the limestone around them. A dropped stick — if such a thing could exist here — would be eaten for its bacteria or used once as a rasping-post and then ignored. The world does not contain the category of tool, or of building, or of leaving a mark that is not immediately occupied by something else.

Occupancy is the only permanence, and occupancy is never still. Predator and prey are circulation, the way damp circulates, the way sound circulates, the way the cool air sinks into the concavity and cannot be persuaded to leave. Things are taken. Things take. The gaps close. The noise does not dip for grief. Even the rare pocket of silence — a predator’s passing, a sudden communal flinch — is a physical hole that a thousand throats instantly reknit, because vacancy is a kind of death the living will not permit.

If you climb the residual ridge and the density thins by a fraction you would not notice anywhere else, you can almost see a farther dark in which the pale smears of the upper fliers scribble and vanish. The smell is a climate: ammonia, wet mineral, the sweetness of decay indistinguishable from the sourness of life. Below, the living pavement flexes. A shared-skin walker founders, is opened, is distributed. The fliers tighten. The grazers close. The sound, which cannot get louder, changes key and resolves into the same chord.

Then the ridge lowers you again, and the bodies come back up to the knee, the hip, the chest. There is only the cool wet air and the work of remaining upright in a country that has never heard of standing apart, and that will still be making this noise when the last ridge has been walked down into grit and the basin is only a slightly smoother dark, still full, still without a single leaf, still so occupied that the idea of a vacant world would be, to anything born here, a story too thin to believe.
