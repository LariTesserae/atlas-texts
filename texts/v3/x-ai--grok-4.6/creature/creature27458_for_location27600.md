The hollow inhales its people the way it inhales its own breath: slowly, completely, without distinction. What lives here is not a population so much as a continuous metabolism wearing many skins.

At the largest scale the inhabitant is the hollow itself. The braided piers and vaulting roots keep a kind of vegetal ledger. They remember every cut, every bolt, every abandoned tool by growing small, perfect cavities around the memory of its shape. A corridor that narrows around a sleeper is not hostility; it is the wood deciding whether the sleeper still belongs to the present tense. The plants photosynthesize almost nothing now. They metabolize time, industrial heat, and the faint copper taste of the pulse that moves through the entire chamber once or twice a day. When the pulse comes, every living surface aligns for a few heartbeats. Leaves tilt. Aerial roots tighten. The colonial gloss on the floor stills like a held breath. Then the hollow exhales warmer, wetter air and the factories drop half a note, as if acknowledging they are guests inside a larger lung.

Inside that lung the fauna is weather and citizenry at once. Moths the size of open hands do not merely fly; they commute. Their wings carry a fine, conductive dust that the dynamos have learned to tolerate, even to use. Certain beetles have become, in any honest taxonomy, stages of the manufacturing process. They mill cellulose into a warm paste that later emerges from the presses as board or solvent or the hard resin that outlasts the wood it came from. Their larvae pupate inside spent wire, spinning cocoons that insulate better than any human wrapping. The birds have never seen sky. They nest in the cooling fins of transformers and scream in frequencies that make the great leaves shiver like drumheads. Colonial membranes—half habit, half organism—slide across the living floors in tides, leaving a gloss that other things graze. Things with too many joints hang from the vaulting and unspool when the boilers change pitch, riding the machines as if the machines were a tide they had studied for generations. Nothing here is empty of animals. You do not observe wildlife. You wade through a nation that parts and closes and sometimes gets in your mouth if you speak too long.

The people occupy the interstices the hollow has, over long argument, consented to keep open. There are never many. A few dozen at most, scattered through sleeping niches grown into the thickness of the greater trunks, along catwalks that have become half root, in a canteen whose tables are still-living sections of a single fallen limb that continues to put out pale, inedible shoots between the plates. They are not explorers or colonists. They are the latest graft. Their skin takes on the same handled polish as the bark they lean against. Their lungs learn the copper pulse. Children are taught early which insects may be touched and which are, in a legal sense, machinery; the distinction is posted in three scripts, one of them a bite-pattern the wood will eventually overwrite. They harvest what the hollow overproduces, feed the machines, keep the dynamos from being engulfed, and are in turn housed, fed, and slightly rewritten by the same closed loop. Metal left unattended near a growing tip is absorbed within a season. A newly opened hollow is never slept in on the first night. If a corridor begins to close, they wait. The wood is deciding.

There are hours when the whole place seems to remember it was once many separate things—stone that forgot itself, rain that learned to perform a memory of rain, factories that can no longer be fully uninstalled. In those hours the inhabitants, plant and animal and human, move in the same slow hydraulic rhythm. The flowers (nocturnal, meat-scented, the size of fists) open all at once and the air becomes briefly unbreathable with sweetness and wings. Then the pulse passes, the boilers resume their middle-register groan, the moths return to the lamps, and the hollow continues being thoroughly, grindlingly, photosynthetically itself.

What lives here has already finished arriving. It is still moving.
