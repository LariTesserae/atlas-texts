The pale seams come first, because they have nowhere else to be. They thread the joins where basalt meets grown lattice and the runnel keeps its promise of wet, a fibrous felt neither plant nor rot, the colour of bone left too long in mineral water. It does not photosynthesise. It listens to gradients: the faint voltage still leaking from a conduit, the iron that the condensation has borrowed from the rock, the warmth that never quite leaves the lower air. Over decades a seam will thicken until it can hold a bead of water on its own surface, then thin again when the machines’ weather turns dry. Nothing tends it. Nothing needs to. It is the place’s oldest remaining decision about how to stay damp.

Where a leak has been faithful for longer than memory, the straps hang. They are broader, flatter, the same bloodless green-white, and they sweat a film that tastes of fruit and ozone. They do not climb. They simply occupy the one loyal trickle and wait. Sometimes a strap will tear under its own accumulated moisture and fall, slowly, into the runnel; the pale felt below accepts it without ceremony. That is the entire vegetation of the nave: two related hungers, neither ambitious.

The first animals are smaller than the sound they make. In the ducts that still carry a breath of heat you hear the dry scuttle—many-legged, hollow-bodied things the colour of old ceramic. They have no eyes worth the name. Their joints click against the inner walls like cooling metal. They graze the felt, harvest the occasional fallen strap, and nest in the warm housings where the engines’ leftover temperature is most constant. Something uses those housings; something smaller uses the something. The hierarchy is quiet and exact. A larger shape, low and patient, will pause at the edge of a dying amber panel, taste the air with a cluster of pits along its snout, and decide you are neither meal nor rival. It moves on. Its hide is the same subsurface shimmer as the ceramic walkways, as if it grew there or was grown there and then forgot the difference. It does not hunt often. The ecology is a rumour that occasionally proves itself and then returns to rumour.

Higher, in the stacked galleries, the unusual begins to look like habit. The lattice still knits its own hairline fractures while you watch; the new material arrives already the colour of the old, embarrassed to be noticed. Near certain pillars a dropped flake of stone will hesitate, drift a finger’s width sideways, then remember gravity. The creatures have learned these courtesies. The scuttlers time their crossings to the half-beat when sound arrives early; the low shapes rest where the field is kindest. None of it is worshipped. It is simply the weather of the room.

And then there are the ones who still walk the rings.

They are few. They move like people who have been given more space than they can fill and have decided, after a long time, not to fill it. Their bodies are long, the joints a little too precise, the skin holding the same faint subsurface gleam as the walkways they prefer. Eyes are dark and large, but they navigate more by the return of sound and the map of heat against their palms. They do not live in the habitation shells so much as visit them, the way one visits a relative who has stopped speaking. A door that will not quite close is left that way; a basin that still catches almost-sweet water is known, and returned to, and left again. They repair nothing that the lattice has not already begun to repair. They harvest the straps when a season of rain has made them heavy. They listen to the engines the way one listens to an old house settling: not for meaning, only to confirm that the settling continues.

Sometimes two of them will pass on a gallery and pause, forehead to forehead, sharing the low thunder that lives in the rock. They do not stay together long. The emptiness is not a wound to them; it is the correct size of the world. They have names, or they had names, or the walls still remember names that they themselves have let thin into sound. When the interior rain begins they stand under it without hurry, mouths open to the iron and the old electricity, then move on before the heat takes the water back. They know the one stair that still takes weight without complaint. They know the shutter that opens and closes on a schedule written for a crowd that is no longer a variable, and they time their crossings so they are never directly beneath it.

They are not the last. They are the shape the last has taken: a people who have become the city’s remaining occupation, the same way the engines have, the same way the felt has. They keep the routes. They keep the quiet. They keep, along the lowest course, that thin argument of water, and they do not ask it to be more than it is. When one of them finally stops moving, the lattice grows a little closer around the place they chose, matching colour, matching patience, until the distinction between inhabitant and architecture is only another courtesy the room has learned.

The rest is capacity. The nave holds the space it was built to hold. Everything that lives here has simply agreed, for now, that this is enough of a world.
