The first thing that lives here is the mass itself. It is not a collection of species so much as a single occupation that has forgotten how to be many. Roots and rhizomes and the fine white threads of fungi have long since stopped recognizing property lines; they drink from the same black-green well at the plaza and they answer one another through chemistry older than the stone. A frost that grips the upper galleries is felt, minutes later, as a faint contraction in the pith of the lowest tunnels. A sudden melt that floods a corridor is answered by a deliberate thickening of moss on the walls above, a living dam that forms overnight and then, when the water recedes, simply becomes another floor. The intelligence, if that word still applies, is hydraulic and vegetative. It does not plan. It leans. It withholds. It grafts. The chambers exist because growth was discouraged in one vector and invited in another, stem pressed to stem until the join lignified and held. Nothing was ever cut. An unhealed wound would become a drain, and a drain at this altitude becomes a blade of ice that would split the rooms apart. So the mass heals itself the way a body heals, and the rooms remain.

Inside that continuity there are smaller lives that have accepted the terms. Pale moths cling where the drip is slowest, too cold most hours to open their wings; when a rare thaw comes they crawl rather than fly, tasting the slush with coiled tongues before the freeze returns and they become part of the ticking ice-film again. In the deepest, warmest pith—never more than a degree above the rest—something the size of a clenched fist maintains a den that smells of wet wool and old sap. It is never seen. It leaves only the musk on still mornings and the faint, chewed corridors that the moss closes behind it within a day. Birds do not stay; there are no flight-paths that are not already someone else’s leaf. What moves through the galleries besides water is almost always vegetable.

And then there are the figures.

They are rare enough that a traveler could spend a season in the cirque and meet only the hush. They do not announce themselves. They part the living walls the way a hand parts hanging laundry, and the walls close again without scar. Their skin holds the same damp chill as the moss they sleep against; in the dim tea-light it is hard to say where flesh ends and thallus begins. Some are lichen-laminated, their shoulders and the long bones of their arms carrying sheets that harden, slightly, into something like clothing that is never removed because it is never separate. Others are smoother, green-black, their surfaces beaded with the same condensation that drips from every underside. They are not many. A handful, perhaps, or fewer; the mass does not keep census and they have no word for number that is not a measure of drip or of how far a corridor has narrowed since the last wet week.

They sleep in chambers that grow a little smaller each year and they do not mind. When a room becomes too tight they simply stop discouraging growth in a neighboring hollow and begin to lean there instead. The old chamber is overgrown, folded back into substrate; they walk on it later without sentiment. They have no tools because the world arrives already shaped, or shapes itself when leaned upon long enough. A lintel is a pair of stems encouraged to meet and fuse. A bed is a mattress of root and thallus that is cold, faintly springy, always wet. They drink from leaf-cups and from the capillary water that threads the moss; they eat what the mass offers—inner pith, the pale watery new partitions before they darken and bear, the slow sweetness that rises after a freeze when tissues contract and sugars concentrate. They do not speak in the way voices speak. When they need to they touch a stem and wait; the signal travels the way melt travels, and somewhere else in the bowl another figure pauses, turns, begins to move.

They keep the rules because the rules are the only thing keeping the rooms from becoming veins of ice. They do not cut. They do not burn. They do not open a way the mass cannot heal. After a week of rain they climb with everything else as the lower galleries flood to the knee; after a hard clear night they wait until the rime unlocks before they enter the upper fronds, which have become, for a few hours, a single glittering lung too brittle to trust. Weather is the only politics, and they are fluent in it. They know the note a rivulet makes when it finds a new crack in the weeping wall. They know the moment the heart-spring’s pulse changes, the slight lessening of cold above the plaza that means the whole packed city is drinking harder.

At the margins, where the plant-mass has not yet reached the freshest talus, they sometimes stand in the brighter, crueler air and watch the advance. They do not hurry it. The vegetation is not waiting, and neither are they. They have no name for the difference between a city that grew and a city that was built. They have lived here long enough—or the mass has lived them long enough—that the distinction no longer occurs. Soon there will be no outside left inside the bowl. The figures will still be there, parting walls that close, sleeping in rooms that shrink, mistaken for the hush itself, while the slit of sky grows no wider and the drip continues, patient as the cirque’s only clock.
