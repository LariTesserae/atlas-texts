The ones who live here have no word for “outside.” Their oldest stories, half-dissolved in the same steam that never quite lifts, speak of a time when the basin still remembered it was water, but even those stories are told as if the teller were already standing inside the memory, ankle-deep, shouting over the roar. They do not arrive. They accrete.

Bodies first: they are pale in the way old metal is pale, the color of tea-stained porcelain or the inner surface of a long-unopened valve. Skin—if the covering can still be called skin—holds a constant sheen, not sweat but the city’s own condensate that never finishes leaving them. It beads, sheets, is reabsorbed, beads again. Many have extra articulations at wrist and ankle, subtle extra folds that let them brace against sudden currents or lock themselves to a wall that has decided, mid-stride, to become a stair. Eyes are large and dark, the better to read gradients of interior rather than edges; they do not startle when a façade ten millennia old slumps into a colonnade and then a cistern in the space of a conversation. Lungs have learned the thick air as a second medium. Some breathe it. Some filter it through gill-slits that open along the neck like old seams. A few have given up distinguishing breath from drink; they simply take the city in.

They thread. That is the verb they use for living. Threading among the fist-sized click-swarms that graze the organic film from every joint, among the ash-colored flyers that explode upward in deafening sheets whenever a heat-exchanger cycles, among the patient many-legged freight-things that move through the lower galleries like living barges. There is no pest, only co-tenant. A child will ride a pale crawler the way another child might once have ridden a beast of burden; an adult will pause mid-argument to let a swarm pass across their shins, then resume haggling with the planning engine that has just decompiled the market stall they were standing in. Negotiation is the only law that has never been rewritten.

Their voices are part of the pressure. They do not speak so much as add another layer to the continuous texture that lives in molars and in the water around the calves. Two meters is shouting distance; anything farther is already weather. Names are short, often just a rhythm of consonants that can cut through steam and grind. They trade in matter that remembers, in intervals of local energy that politely ignore conservation, in favors owed to engines older than language. A wall that has stood since the basin forgot the sea may suddenly prefer to be a colonnade; the dwellers simply step through the new interval, continue the transaction, and mark the change the way others once marked weather.

They sleep in lofts that are also streets that are also cisterns, bodies nested in programmable foam that hardens just enough to hold a shape until the next recasting. Dreams are wet and loud and full of load-paths shifting. When they wake they do not ask what the city has become overnight; they ask only how the new arrangement can be used before it rearranges again. Some have begun to wear the city’s own fatigue-curves: shoulders rounded by traffic, faces softened by steam until the features look like they have been handled for centuries. Others let the city write on them more directly—iridescent oxidation blooming across a forearm like a living map, salt-crusts that form and dissolve with the tides of overflow.

There are those who argue with the planning engines in the old tongues, voices rising until they become another subsonic throb. There are those who have stopped distinguishing themselves from the fauna, who move through the galleries with the same patient many-legged gait, who graze the film or rise in sudden curtains when the vents cycle. The distinction was never important. Space is never empty. Water is never absent. Change is the only constant that has itself grown old, and they have grown old inside it, the way a river grows old inside its own banks even as the banks keep moving.

They do not leave. There is nowhere else the air is this thick, the light this correctly shadowed, the noise this complete. They are the city’s way of remaining inhabited after the sea forgot itself. They wade. They shout. They watch a ten-thousand-year wall decide it would rather be water for a while, and they do not call it miracle. They call it Tuesday. And they keep threading.
