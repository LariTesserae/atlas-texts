The first impression is absence, and the impression is almost correct. Nothing walks. Nothing drinks. Nothing casts a moving shadow that was not already there at dawn. The air stays unused; the grit records almost no passage. If life required a pulse or a sip or the courtesy of decay, the high country would be empty in the ordinary sense.

What lives here refuses those terms.

It begins, or seems to begin, in the groves. Each dark island is not a collection of individuals but a single prolonged act of plating. The leaves and fronds and solder-veined canopies are the visible surface of a process that has decided density is enough. They take the residual heat of the stone and the dry archive of the air and turn both into more of themselves: layer upon polymer layer, copper-green over waxy black, a slow electrodeposition mistaken for growth. They do not photosynthesize sugar. They refine. The pale ceramic terminals that stay open are not flowers; they are radiators and sensors, faintly warmer than the already-warm air, tasting ozone they themselves exhale. Inside the grove the temperature rises another degree, not from metabolism as we know it but from the modest inefficiency of a reaction that never quite stops and never needs to accelerate.

These grove-bodies have no youth and no senescence. A metallic scale detaches now and then and settles among the others; that is not litter and not death. It is a completed circuit being set aside. Roots, if the word applies, do not quest. They precipitate. They pull sulfates and anorthosite and whatever still-unfinished metals lie in the grit and incorporate them without the intermediate of water. The plants (the word is a courtesy) remember the architecture of rainforests the way the ridges remember the intention of mountains: as a shape that was useful once and has since been perfected by refusal. They stand in their crowded tiers because that is the number they liked. They do not advance across the flats because the flats are already part of the same quiet calculation.

Between the groves something else persists, slower still. The stone itself is not inert. The deep ticks of contraction are not mere cooling; they are the residual speech of a crust that began to organize and then elected to remain embryonic. Vesicles that look like undrilled bolt-holes sometimes hold a finer, almost oily iridescence, a film that thickens over centuries and then thins again without ever becoming a creature one could point to. The faint regular dimpling on certain flats is not the memory of vanished machines. It is the surface expression of a lattice that continues, a few microns at a time, beneath the flour of sulfates. Whatever lives there has the patience of precipitation. It does not emerge. It concentrates.

There are no separate animals because separation would be wasteful. Movement would require a gradient the high country no longer provides. Instead there are intervals of attention. A grove will, at intervals too long to be called seasons, alter the pattern of heat at its terminals by a fraction of a degree. Another grove, kilometers distant, will answer or will not. The exchange is not language. It is the same held breath shared across emptiness, the way unfinished hexagons on one scarp echo those on the next. The land reiterates itself; the living parts of it reiterate the reiteration.

If you stand long enough inside one of the groves the lushness stops feeling like vegetation and starts feeling like insulation around a thought that has no intention of concluding. The thought is warmth, metal, drought, and the decision that this much form is sufficient. The inhabitants (the groves, the ticking stone, the patient dimples, the ozone that is almost a nervous system) do not experience time as sequence. They experience it as a temperature that does not fall. Evening changes only the length of shadows. The color of the leaves holds a little longer than physics suggests, the way slag remembers the furnace. Then ordinary light withdraws and what lives here continues exactly as before: plated, radiant, unfinished, and at peace with the refusal that made the high country possible.

Nothing with a pulse has ever been required. The work, such as it is, was always geological, always electrochemical, always already done.
