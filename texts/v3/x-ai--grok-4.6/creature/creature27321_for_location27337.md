The lives here do not announce themselves. They have never needed to. What occupies the saddle has the same finished quality as the fluting in the rock: not an occupation so much as a completion, the last possible density that can be poured into glare and roar without ever tipping over into visibility. You walk among them the way you walk among the mica winks, certain for long stretches that you are alone, then slowly, unwillingly, understanding that solitude here is only a failure of attention.

Begin with the smallest, because they are the most evenly distributed and therefore the hardest to dismiss as accident. In every damp hairline, in the dark north-stain of every stone, in the trembling grit-film of the tarns, there is a glittering that is not mineral. The insects do not swarm. They do not rise. They exist as a standing dust, an even, almost mathematical sprinkling whose individual bodies are the color of weathered feldspar or the exact sulfur of the map-lichen they graze. Their stridulation is not a chorus; it is a second pressure, a brightness just beneath the great unchanging note of the wind, so constant that the ear, once it has accepted the larger roar as weather in the bone, accepts this as well and stops offering it as sound. They do not appear to feed in pulses or rest in darkness. Darkness is not admitted. They simply continue, a glittering that moves only when the light catches a wing-case at the precise angle that mica also uses. After an hour of watching one trough you realize the glittering is thicker near the swallow-holes and the stubborn stains, thinner on the open fans, and that this gradient has been true for longer than the pipes have been pipes. They are the first texture of the emptiness, the thing that makes the emptiness slightly too sparkling to be honest.

The small mammals are the next refusal. They are all ear and tendon, bodies reduced to the minimum that can still hold a pulse in this cool, this thinness. They occupy the boulder-fields so completely that the fields themselves begin to read as a single pale organism that occasionally, almost reluctantly, shows an eye or the brief wet commma of a nose. Their coats are the precise dirty white of the schist plates, their shadows the same blade-cut cold as the stones’. They do not bolt as individuals. A shift occurs, a sudden local rearrangement of pale shapes that was already almost the ground, and then stillness again, so perfect that you cannot be sure the movement happened. They live in the cracks the way the wind lives in the organ-slots: threaded through, using the rock as both shelter and instrument. You hear them only after you have stopped trying to hear them, a dry rustle that might be grit finding a new angle, or the minute adjustment of a body that has decided the watching human is another stone. They drink from the palm-sized tarns by approaching in the lee of particular knolls, never more than one or two at a time, never bunching, as if congregation itself were a kind of glare. Their young, when they appear, are already the color of the place; there is no vulnerable stage that the light would be allowed to notice. They do not hibernate. There is nothing to hibernate from. The temperature is the temperature of high shade, always, and they have made that temperature their blood.

Then the herds. They are the landscape’s most successful argument against itself. Low, long-necked, not quite goat and not quite the antelope of lower, wetter countries, they pick their way across the gravel fans and residual knolls with a deliberation that looks like grazing but is closer to maintenance. Their coats are the exact weathered feldspar of the open stone; at any distance greater than a few body-lengths they cease to be animals and become another set of pale, wind-fluted forms. They do not startle as a group. The group is already a camouflage that failed upward into totality. What happens instead is a sudden, silent redistribution, a new arrangement of the same emptiness, after which the eye must begin again the long work of deciding which pale shapes are breathing. They move from lichen-crust to cushion-plant to the knuckle-high wiry grass without ever seeming to choose. The plants are as sparse and assigned as the animals themselves; the herds do not deplete them. Nothing here is depleted. They drink from the tarns in the same unhurried, unconcentrated way, a few at a time, the water trembling continuously so that no reflection ever quite forms of the long necks lowered into it. Their voices, if they have voices, are not separate from the instrument of the saddle. Perhaps they contribute a lower, almost tectonic undertone to the roar, or perhaps they have given that up as unnecessary. They do not migrate. There is nowhere in the relevant world that is different from here. The calves, when they appear, are already matching, already spaced, already part of the pale geometry that the wind has been keeping.

The birds are the brightest disagreement, and therefore the easiest to miss for the longest time. They occupy the larger pipes and organ-slots in numbers that do not make sense until you have watched one wall through an entire unchanging noon and understood that the speckles are not mineral inclusions. They are the color of the schist, pale to the point of disappearance, and they do not so much fly as allow themselves to be thrown. The wind takes them from slot to slot the way it takes grit, and their bodies have the same finished, fluted look as the rock that holds them. Their voices are not added to the great note; they are a secondary scream inside it, a brightness that has been going so long it has the same unmodulated quality as the stone-sound itself. You cannot tell whether they are calling to one another or simply continuing the conversation the wind and the mountain began before either of them had names. They nest in the deeper pipes, where the roar is a physical pressure, and their young learn the throw of the wind before they learn anything that could be called flight. Eggs, if there are eggs, are the color of kiln-ash and do not need to be hidden. Nothing here hunts in the way lower countries hunt. Predation, if it occurs, is another texture, another even dust, too thoroughly distributed to leave drama. The birds drink by dropping to the tarns in the same thrown arcs, a brief darkening of the glare, then back into the slots. They do not roost at any hour that could be called night, because night is only a thin rumor that cannot get purchase. They simply occupy, the way the pipes occupy the knolls.

There are no larger animals. There is no apex that would require a different relationship to the light or the noise. What lives here has reached the same equilibrium the stone reached: everything that was going to concentrate has concentrated, everything that was going to be rare has become so evenly poured that rarity itself is camouflage. The three stacked stones on the rim of the trough that commands nothing, the scatter of flakes in the sheltered slot, the darkened smear that might once have been a fire the size of a cup—these are not evidence of residents. They are evidence of passage, of a hand that needed, briefly, to put itself on the world and then moved on into lower country where shadows have edges that travel. The saddle ignored the gesture the way it ignores weather. Nothing here is still enough, in the human sense, to be lived in. Everything here is still enough, in the other sense, that living-in would be a vandalism the place would simply fail to record.

You can stay long enough for your own water to become a problem, long enough for the roar to relocate itself from the ears to the sinuses to the marrow, and the inhabitants will have been around you the entire time, screaming and glittering and matching, a thousand things that chose the color of the ground so completely that the ground became a kind of crowd. They do not watch you, exactly. Watching would be a form of development, a narrative the saddle has refused. They simply continue, the same held note, the same finished forms, while you, briefly, are the only thing that does not belong to the equilibrium and therefore the only thing the light and the wind have not yet finished. When you leave, if you leave, they will not notice the subtraction. The tarns will go on trembling. The pipes will go on singing. The pale shapes will go on being almost, but never quite, the stone.
