The small lives are the first to declare themselves, though they never quite arrive. They are the change in a surface’s wetness, the momentary dulling of a shine that was never quite light. Under every shelf of moss, in the hanging lenses of water, across the constantly renewed slime, they travel as if the world were a single continuous underside. Some are no larger than the memory of a seed, soft as thought and the same color as the bark they pretend to be. They graze the algal film with mouths that are only slower regions of themselves, leaving behind a faint warmer streak that the plateau reclaims before the streak can cool. Others, slightly larger, jointed in ways that suggest they once considered becoming something else, squeeze through the vertical floors as if gravity were a rumor they have chosen not to credit. Their feet, when they possess them, are pads of the same spongiform tissue that makes the galleries; they leave no print, only a brief persuasion that the surface was always that shape.

These are not visitors. They are secretions of the mass, the way a body secretes sweat or a dream. Their abundance is the abundance of a sentence that keeps finding new ways to say the same damp word. Predation among them is neighboring and quiet: an engulfing so gradual it looks like agreement, a dissolving that returns minerals to the dark circulation the roots have already reserved. You become aware of a larger one only as a warmth that is not the plateau’s own, a slow rearrangement in a hollow you had taken for empty. It does not startle. Nothing here startles. The creature—if the word still applies—has been approaching for longer than your attention has existed, built for the architecture of compression, its segments the color of wet slate and old bronze. When it finally occupies the space beside you it is already leaving, or rather the space is already becoming the next room it requires. Wings, on the rare individuals that keep them, open the way a sigh opens: once, to change chambers, then fold back into the body as if embarrassed by the gesture.

Deeper, in the still black waters that are glands rather than lakes, other lives consider themselves. They are not swimmers so much as concentrations, slow vortices of warmer dark that taste of iron and nectar and the ozone of a storm that the plateau decided against. Some hang in the photosynthetic dusk of the lined chambers, turning so gradually that their silhouettes become part of the wall’s memory. Seeds that may not be seeds rotate in pockets of air that have their own private weather; if they ever fall it will be in a century you will not witness, and the falling will be reabsorbed as another darker stripe. The air in these rooms has the patience of compost that has become a climate. What lives here lives at the speed of stain spreading in cloth already wet.

And then there are the ones the marks remember, or that remember the marks. They are easy to miss because they have the same temperature as the walls, the same faint green cast, the same unwillingness to finish a contour. In the hall of foreign stones—true mineral, already being sleeved in moss—they sit or stand or simply persist in attitudes too regular for accident and too tired for intention. Their substance is the plateau’s substance, lignin and cellulose and the mineralized patience of root, but arranged, for a while longer, into suggestions of limb and attention. They do not move so much as reconsider their position over generations of drips. One may occupy the polished depression on the broadest tread, the hollow the size and implication of a lying body; it has been there long enough that the wood has begun to grow around the idea of it, fitting it like a sleeve the way the moss fits the stones. Whatever they were when they first entered the high country—travelers, tenders, offerings, or the plateau’s own earlier experiments in standing apart—they have been composted into a tense the vegetation has agreed, temporarily, not to complete. They are pauses that breathe. Their eyes, when they open (and they open the way hanging water finally lets go), hold the same wet points the evening mist mistakes for stars.

They do not speak. Speech would be a hurry the place has finished with. Communication, if it occurs, happens the way water declares itself: by seepage, by pressure, by the faint adjustment of a fiber somewhere in the living thickness. A gallery may widen a fraction because one of them has leaned, over decades, in a particular direction. A hanging lake may become a waterfall the height of a person because another has waited beneath it long enough to teach the water a new opinion of down. They are used by the plateau as much as they use it; the straight cuts through buttresses, the encouraged steps, the rings of alien stone are less constructions than conversations the mass has chosen to keep open. Time here is horizontal. A single one of them may walk from marsh to climbable thickening and back again in the course of what, elsewhere, would be a lineage. Behind them the rooms close at the speed of thought in wood. Ahead the mist continues to be exhaled.

In the deepest weathers, where directions revert and water sometimes runs up the grooves as if gravity were a local mood, still other lives are possible. They are harder to separate from the air. A brighter smear that you took for sun may resolve, after hours of descent, into a colony of luminous films that have decided to be a ceiling; among them move shapes that are only denser mist, sighing rarely, changing rooms. Or you stand in a doorway of living wood with one hand in two climates and feel, against your palm, a warmth that considers you the way the old rock considers the roots: accurately, without nostalgia, as a condition it has not yet finished with. These may be the plateau dreaming itself in smaller increments. They may be what remains of earlier ranges, planed and ingested and now walking the galleries in the only form left to them. They do not distinguish themselves from the organism. Organism may no longer be the word.

At the further dimming that serves for evening the hanging water holds the last of the broth-light and the whole vast body seems to sweat stars that are not stars. The jointed ones settle into hollows. The film-workers become indistinguishable from the shine they alter. The lingering ones, in their circles and depressions, close whatever they use for eyes, or perhaps they have been closed all along and you only now notice. One droplet finishes its fall. The quiet takes the sound back into the warm, ancient, unfinished dark. What lives here lives as the grain of the place: everything eating the plateau and being the plateau, growth and rot the same color, nothing in a hurry except water, and water’s hurry still a kind of waiting. You may cross a lifetime of its weathers and only ever meet the same slow sentence, rewritten with the same wet words, becoming more itself the longer you remain.
