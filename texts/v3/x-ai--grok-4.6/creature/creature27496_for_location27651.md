The people of the basin are the same color as the light that owns them. Not white, not quite; a mineral paleness that has learned to live without the idea of tan or flush. Their skin takes the glare and gives it back a half-tone duller, so that a crowd seen from a distance looks like a slow rearrangement of the pavement itself. Eyes are narrowed by habit rather than by expression; the lids have thickened into something like the joints between stones, a permanent half-squint that keeps the overexposed air from becoming a weapon. Hair is kept short or bound, because long hair becomes another surface the salt can work on, another thing that must be recut. Voices stay low not from reverence but from physics: anything louder than a murmur walks a few paces and then simply ceases, as if the basin had inhaled it. They have learned to speak with their hands when the work requires it, and the hands themselves are maps—callus over older callus, nails rimed with gypsum, fingers that know the exact thickness of a chock-stone by touch alone.

They are not numerous. The city is a region that never agreed to become a town, so occupation happens in knots: two or three at a hammerstone in a roofless workshop, a family under hide and salvaged plank, a solitary figure levering a lintel that has begun its yearly lean. Children are taught the grain of the herds the way other children are taught the alphabet. A thoroughfare at dawn is a still courtyard by noon; the lesson is simply to notice which way the pale bodies have chosen to pour, then to relocate the day’s work to the next empty room. No one herds in the ordinary sense. They stand in the moving rivers and dip—milk from the goat-sized ones whose flanks are the color of recemented grit, hair that can be twisted into cord, the dry dung that is the only fuel that will take a flame without protest. The animals tolerate this the way they tolerate the walls: as weather that has not yet become intolerable.

The animals themselves are the city’s other population, more continuous than the people and far older in their pact with the place. The larger ones move in loose, shifting constellations, never quite a flock, never quite a herd. They are low-slung, built close to the glare, with eyes that seem to have been edited down to the minimum necessary aperture. Their hides are the same pale as everything else, so that when they cross a plaza they look less like creatures than like a change in the mineral’s own texture. They lick the morning film from the stones, shoulder through ungated lanes, and pause in the leeward shadows of the shoulder-high walls as if the architecture were a kind of shade they had invented for themselves. Smaller lives come in sheets: a rustle felt in the ankle before it is heard, a single changing hide that turns a courtyard into something briefly animate and then empties it again. These do not so much walk as translate the floor from one state to another. The flyers keep to the rim and to the rare hours when the light stands down; their wings make no announcement, and they do not cry. Nothing here cries in a way that would count. The entire ecology has settled on near-silence as the only sound the basin will return.

There is a third presence, thinner than either people or herds, that no one claims as life and yet is licked, collected, endured. In the damp joints a colorless smear persists—not moss, not quite a film anyone would name. It is the last unedited green the chemistry of the place has allowed, reduced to a mineral afterthought. The animals take it. The people do not. That is the whole of the verdure, and it is enough.

What lives here, then, is a single ongoing negotiation between two kinds of stillness and one kind of slow catastrophe. The people restack what the salt unstacks. The animals pour through the resulting corridors as if the corridors had always been theirs. The smear waits in the joints for the next condensation. At night the mineral holds the day’s white for a long time, the herds keep moving through it, and the rebuilders go in under hide and stone. The city becomes only its plan, its quiet labor, and the cool that has been waiting the whole time—older than the walls, older than the pact of silence, older than the idea that anything here was ever meant to stay aligned.
