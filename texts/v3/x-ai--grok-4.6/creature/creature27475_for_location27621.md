The first lives here are the ones that cannot help announcing themselves. They occupy every plane the collapse has offered, and they do so without negotiation. Liverworts felt the walls in overlapping scales that swell and pulse with each bead of seepage; they are not covering the rock so much as becoming its new, slower skin. Sphagnum rises in stacked cushions that hold entire weather systems of their own, each hollow a tiny reservoir where a single crane-fly larva can live out a generation without ever touching mineral. Horsetails stand so close their joints click like abacus beads whenever a current or a passing shrew disturbs them. Ferns do not merely uncoil; they interrogate every crevice, tasting the new soil for the exact fraction of iron and lime that will let them keep going. Nothing here is ornamental. Every frond, every rhizoid, every jointed stem is a claim staked in the first long wet light of a place that did not exist last year.

Among them the smaller animals move as if the architecture were already familiar. Newts hang in the clearer meres with the patience of glass ornaments, their gills flickering in the greenish noon that never quite becomes day or night. Water shrews stitch the moss with such speed that the only evidence is a brief dark thread drawn across a pool and then gone. Voles have already excavated the peat into multi-level warrens whose entrances are disguised by the same dripping curtains of root that hide everything else; they treat the tufa terraces as ready-made staircases. Springtails occupy the surface film in densities that turn still water into a living, twitching silk. The midges do not swarm so much as occupy; they are simply the air’s new texture.

Larger shapes arrive later, or perhaps they have been here the whole time and only now allow themselves to be seen. At the edges of the open basins something elk-sized comes down when the light thins, browsing the newest willow with a deliberation that suggests it remembers the plateau before the floor dropped. It does not linger. A quicker thing—smaller, lower to the water—leaves only a V of wake in the shallows and the faint musk of wet fur on stone. Dippers work the riffles with the focused violence of creatures that have decided this new hydraulic grid is theirs. A heron stands one-legged in a mere as though it has always been a fixture of the place, its stillness so complete that moss has begun to consider colonizing its legs.

Stay longer. The dripping maps itself onto your skin and the cold becomes a second circulatory system. Other dwellers begin to resolve out of the galleries the willows and the precipitating calcite have made. At first they are only absences: a space between trunks that is darker than it should be, a drip that stops when you look directly at it, a faint rearrangement of fronds that is not wind. Then they acquire edges. They are the height of the tunnels, or a little less, as if the architecture and the bodies had grown around one another in the same season. Their surfaces hold water the way the rock does—beading, sheeting, never quite drying. Where light catches them they show the same pale, knife-edged quality as the broken plates of the cleft, except the edges are soft, overgrown already with the same sponge-moss that covers everything. They move by deciding to be elsewhere; one moment a figure is part of a hanging root-curtain, the next it is further along the alley of black water, having taken no visible step.

They do not speak. The basin’s agreement on lower volume includes them. What they do is tend. A hand—if the pale, dripping extremity can be called that—adjusts a sedge so that it will catch the next pulse of seepage more efficiently. Another gathers the slower newts from a pool that is silting and releases them into a clearer channel the beavers have not yet claimed. They coppice the willows with the same pressure the browsing animals use, but they leave the multi-trunked pillars standing as rooms. They seem to understand the travertine as it forms; they press wet handfuls of it into the gaps between mineral walls, encouraging the next storey before the calcite has quite decided its shape. None of this looks like labor. It looks like the place continuing to fill itself, only now the filling has acquired a preference for certain alignments, certain thicknesses of moss, certain depths of water in the plazas.

If you climb one of the residual towers and look down the chain of linked depressions, you can see them in every quarter at once: small pale verticals moving through the green-black lattice, never hurrying, never quite separate from the vegetation that hides and reveals them. They are not ancient. Nothing here is. They have the same raw, first-season quality as the sharpest ledges and the youngest dams. It is as if the collapse, the sudden gift of air and light and unobstructed dripping, did not merely allow life to rush in; it allowed a particular configuration of that life to notice itself and begin, very quietly, to edit the new city it found itself inside.

They watch you the way the heron watches the mere. They do not approach. They do not retreat. When you finally leave, climbing back through the still-raw cleft that smells of broken stone, the last thing you feel is the map of drips they have left on your skin—an architecture you did not ask for and cannot quite wash off. Behind you the basin continues its work: root into mineral, mineral into terrace, terrace into gallery, gallery into whatever they are becoming now that the floor has dropped and the sky has remembered them in pieces. The place has wasted no time. Neither have they.
