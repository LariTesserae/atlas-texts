The high country keeps its living things the way it keeps its weather and its light: as slow recurrences rather than events. Nothing arrives suddenly. What you first take for another slump of ridge or a darker fold in the slate-colored waste will, after hours of walking, resolve into something that has been breathing the entire time you approached. The breath is the first true announcement. It reaches you as a change in the sinuses, a faint metallic warmth that the air immediately begins to unmake, turning it into a glittering rime that settles on your clothes and then vanishes. Only then do the proportions declare themselves. The thing stands as high as the leftover buttes, a single continuous mass whose outline has been planed by the same wind that planed the land. Its hide is not covering so much as continuation: the same wet-slate, old-antler, mussel-shell tones, the same frost feathers in every lee, the same black lichen occupying whatever film of moisture can be held. Distinctions have long since worn away. Perhaps the plateau itself is only the accumulated, discarded skin of earlier generations, ground back into the flour the walkers now ingest.

A step occupies most of a day. The motion is not locomotion in any hurried sense; it is a transfer of weight so gradual that the stone beneath accepts it as another form of settling. Inside, the work proceeds without hurry and without pause. There are no viscera as you would recognize them. There are chambers, nested and reciprocating, lined with cartilage that has the density of cooled slag and with something still denser that moves heat from core to periphery and back again in cycles measured in weeks. The thin air, the mineral dust, the sparse dark stains of lichen are drawn in through pores that open and close with the patience of joints in a glacier. What is refined there leaves in two forms: the brief exhaled mist that freezes downwind into that same edible rime, and the fine black slag extruded along the trailing edge, a trail of industrial dark that the wind and the smaller lives will spend the next century redistributing. No roof, no hall, no imposed structure. The body is the factory and the factory is simply how the body continues. Scaled production has been the only architecture this place has ever needed.

They are never many in any one view. Density here is the density of last stars or of islands in a pale sea. Ten days of walking may show you nothing but the idea of frost and the repeated grammar of inversion and residual spine. Then one occupies the middle distance and the middle distance becomes occupied, heavy, complete. At the rare gatherings that last years they form an archipelago: six, seven dark masses, each the size of a worn mountain, the emptiness between them a positive substance that the wind treats with elaborate courtesy. Whether they gather by intent or by the same slow currents that move the ice-rivers is not a distinction the land bothers to maintain. Birth among them is a geological occasion, a calving or a splitting that occupies seasons and leaves a new butte-shaped mass already weathering into the colors of the plateau. Death, if it occurs, is indistinguishable from further erosion.

In their lee the smaller economies persist. Crab-like forms, silent and low, graze the rime that condenses on cooling flanks during the long dusk; they harvest what the great ones exhale and in doing so keep the flanks from sealing over entirely. Pale kite-shapes ride the weakest thermals the walkers generate, never flapping, never calling, their surfaces the exact gray-blue of tired light; they may be filtering the same thin air or simply remaining aloft because remaining aloft is the only motion the place still permits them. Along the slag trails, burrowers stitch and restitch, turning last year’s black residue into the next century’s faint, metallic soil, the only substrate the finger-high grasses will tolerate. In any single gaze the whole assemblage is sparse to the point of absence. Across the continental reach of the upland it is a continuous, thin film of conversion, appetite stretched to the same scale as the land itself.

Sound does not live here because the great ones produce, as byproduct, a damping. Vibration is swallowed into those slow internal chambers. Your own steps arrive late and already muted. The ice shifts below hearing. The only pressure change that registers as event is the inhalation or exhalation of a walker, and that is weather more than noise. Time behaves the same way: a solvent without urgency. A storm crosses from one worn horizon to the other in a month. The frost-line advances and retreats over years. The walkers themselves keep no seasons you can name; their congregations and dispersals are the closest the high country comes to calendar.

You could walk until every memory of lower, quicker country had been planed away like the rest. The operations would continue unchanged: rock to dust, dust to the refined, walking bodies, those bodies back to rime and slag and, eventually, to rock again. Nothing was built. The only engines are the ones that eat stone and breathe winter, and they have been doing so long enough that creature, factory, and landscape have eroded into a single continuous fact, quiet, established, and privately physical, as if the laws themselves have grown tired and slightly local. What lives here is the place, metabolizing.
