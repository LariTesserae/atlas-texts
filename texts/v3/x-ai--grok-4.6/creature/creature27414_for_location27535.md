The mites come first, because they are the only thing that ever truly occupies the whole of the bowl at once. They lift in glittering veils that are not swarms so much as temporary organs of the air itself—iridescent, faceted, each no larger than a grain of coarse salt yet edged like a cut jewel. Their wings (if they are wings) are plates of the same living gypsum that forms the trunks, so thin the tired violet light passes through them and comes out the other side already fractured into secondary rainbows. They do not fly; they are carried, then choose where to alight. When they settle on a canopy they do not feed in any way a wet world would recognize. They rasp. A faint, continuous whispering of crystal on crystal, a sound that is almost lost inside the grove’s deeper roar. Each mite scrapes a monolayer of mineral from the leaf-plate, incorporates it, and in the same motion sheds an older layer that the wind immediately claims. Their bodies are closed loops of accretion and abrasion. They drink nothing. They remember nothing. They are the grove’s circulating blood, glittering and brief.

Beneath them, and sometimes through them, move the racers. These are the size of a clenched fist or a little larger, many-legged, their shells looking poured rather than secreted—smooth, slightly iridescent, the color of iron-stained chert left too long in a cooling kiln. The legs are jointed like folded lightning, too many of them, and they strike the dust in overlapping rhythms that cancel the wind’s chaos for a few strides at a time. They race the perpetual gale along the open salt-crust, vanishing into the fluted trunks the instant the air shifts against them. Their calls are not voices. They are countersongs: single, perfect notes pitched to drill through the standing drone of the hollow trees. A racer the size of a child’s heart can fill the entire hanging bowl with a tone that seems to originate in the stone itself, a sound so purely mineral it makes the teeth ache and the inner ear ring like struck quartz. They rasp one another when they meet. They rasp the trunks. They rasp the air, somehow, pulling trace metals out of the dry mineral breath and folding them into their poured shells. Their chemistry has no water in it, no sap, no blood. It is a glittering closed circuit of oxidation and reduction, of surfaces becoming interiors becoming surfaces again.

Deeper in the trunks—inside the fluted columns themselves—live the hollow ones. These are seldom seen. They are longer, slower, their bodies segmented like stacked geodes that have learned to walk. They move by expanding and contracting the mineral chambers of their own flesh, a peristalsis of stone that sounds like distant thunder when it happens at all. Their mouths (or what serve as mouths) are rings of vibrating plates that they press against the inner walls of the living gypsum. They drink the resonance. They eat the howl. The grove’s perpetual song is their pasture; they graze the standing waves the way wet-world cattle graze grass, converting frequency into more of themselves. Sometimes a hollow one will extrude a new chamber overnight with the sound of tearing cloth and settling stone, and the mites will swarm the fresh surface in a glittering frenzy before the wind redistributes them. The hollow ones do not compete with the racers. They occupy a different register of the same closed loop.

The plants themselves are not plants. They are the oldest inhabitants that still hold still long enough to be named. Each trunk is a single vast organism of living gypsum and iron-stained chert, its interior a labyrinth of resonant cavities that have been tuned, over uncountable seasons of wind, to the particular frequencies of this hanging bowl. The lace-thin canopies are not leaves; they are arrays of hanging lungs and frozen spray, secondary growths that drink dim light and shear the gale into usable harmonics. They do not photosynthesize. They resonate. Their “growth” is a slow crystallization along lines of standing vibration. A canopy that was intact a moment ago may suddenly shed a rain of glittering plates—not from injury, but because the frequency has shifted and those plates no longer belong to the current song. The mites swarm the falling plates. The racers ignore them. The hollow ones wait for the new note that will replace the old. The plants stand far apart because proximity would detune them. They compete for resonance the way wet-world trees compete for light: the strongest note claims the air, the weaker ones wait, or slowly retune, or die into salt-crust and are scoured away.

And then there are the cairn-makers, or what remains of them.

The stacked plates still show peck-marks. The leaning shelter of interlocked slats still stands, its interior scoured clean, a dune of pale dust built against its lee side by the same wind that has always been here. The ring of darker stones might once have been a fire-pit, though nothing here burns and there has never been anything wet enough to dry. These were made by hands that understood percussion and leverage and the patience of stone. The hands are gone. Or they are not gone. Sometimes, in the long violet-gray that never quite becomes night, a racer will pause on a cairn and rasp its poured shell against the stacked plates in a rhythm that is almost, but not quite, the old pecking pattern. Sometimes a hollow one will press its vibrating mouth-ring to the inner wall of the shelter and produce a note that the grove has never sung before—a note that sounds like memory trying to become mineral. The mites lift from those surfaces in slightly denser clouds, as if the old work still holds a trace charge they can graze.

It is possible they became the glittering things. It is possible the glittering things were always here and the cairn-makers were only a brief, wet interruption that the bowl eventually dried into its own image. The distinction no longer matters. The turbulence is older than either. The wind does not gust; it is a continuous chaotic river stirred by something larger than the mountain, and every living surface here has learned to treat that river as both enemy and instrument. Dust-devils form and collapse in the space of a breath. New branches extrude with the sound of tearing cloth. The landscape is always finishing and always beginning, yet the spacing of the trunks, the worn smoothness of the rim, the exact balance of lavish mineral life and absolute drought—these have not changed in any time a visitor could measure.

What lives here is the agreement itself.

A closed, glittering loop of rasp and resonance, of surfaces becoming song becoming new surfaces, of creatures that have forgotten water so completely they have become the memory of thirst turned inside-out. Stand in the center and the sound is a physical pressure, a living architecture of noise. The mites write temporary scripts in the circulating dust. The racers cut countersongs through it. The hollow ones graze the deepest notes. The great trunks lean and recover, shedding and extruding, always the same forest of standing filigree. Everything is in motion and everything has been doing this, in exactly this balance, for longer than the mountain has had a name.

The bowl holds them the way a ruined nave holds its last echo: not by walls, but by habit. Beyond the lip the mountain continues, higher and emptier, a world of the same dry stone without the grove’s strange abundance. Here, inside the hanging dish, the exception persists. The wind keeps stirring. The mites keep lifting. The notes keep overlapping. Nothing drinks. Nothing freezes. Nothing is still.

And the oldest thing of all—the turbulence that learned how to stay—keeps the whole glittering, howling, mineral cathedral alive.
