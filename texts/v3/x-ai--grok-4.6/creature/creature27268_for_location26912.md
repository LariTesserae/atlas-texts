They come into focus the way the stone itself does: not all at once, not as a finished shape, but as a slow thickening of presence inside the dim. At first you take them for another density of water, a warmer current that has learned to hold a face. Then the face turns and the wet shine on the cheekbone gives them away.

Their skin is the color of the new ledges before the second, harder skin arrives—pale, with a faint gray-green undertone where the light never quite reaches, and always beaded. It never dries. Even the ones who spend the long bruise-hour on the highest spines come back sheened, as if the hanging sea has claimed them by treaty. The beads collect in the hollows of throat and collarbone and stay there, trembling when they speak. You could mistake the beads for sweat or for rain; they are both and neither. The skin itself has a patience. It does not wrinkle further from the wet; it has already accepted the wet as its weather. Fingertips are permanently ridged, the ridges useful, almost too useful, for reading the coming-up by touch. A thumb drawn slowly across a wall can feel the milk waiting just under the surface, the place where a blow would be remembered.

They are not tall. Height here is a negotiation, and the body has learned to stay compact, to fold, to pass through windows that will be doorways next season and wells the season after. Shoulders slope as if permanently prepared to shrug off a hanging frond. The spine has a slight, habitual curve, the curve of someone who has spent years ducking under lintels that rise to meet them. When they stand fully upright—on those rare mornings when a lens of true air opens and they climb to feel their own weight correctly—the posture looks almost ceremonial, a little astonished, as if the bones themselves are surprised to be asked to stack that way.

Hair is kept short or bound in tight, dark plaits that shed water the way the bronze fronds shed spores. Some let a single long strand grow from the temple and weigh it with a small nodule of the reddish metal; it hangs against the jaw like a plumb line, a private measure of which way is down today. Eyes are large, but not luminous. They do not need to gather more light than there is. Instead they have learned to read the wet shine on everything else: the gleam that means a step has risen in the night, the duller gleam that means a chamber has begun to close. Pupils stay wide. In the deeper rooms the irises look almost black, a color that is actually the memory of the hanging sea.

They move as the water moves: without hurry, without waste. A step is also a test. The foot comes down, waits, feels whether the stone is still arriving beneath it, then commits. In the half-drowned streets they are as often swimming as walking; the transition is not marked. One moment the chest is in air that is only thinner water, the next the mouth is closed and the body has remembered how to slip. They do not kick hard. The hanging forests do most of the work, bladders lifting, current shouldering. A hand trails, fingers open, tasting mineral, greeting the fine green hair that nets the light. When they need to speak they stop and come forehead to forehead, the beads of their skins mingling. Words travel that short, wrapped distance and arrive already half-understood. Children are taught this closeness before they are taught not to shout. A shout is a violence against the acoustics of a world still deciding how to ring.

Their clothes are scarcely clothes. Lengths of the black vine, beaten and softened, wrapped and re-wrapped as the body changes or the water rises. Cakes of rendered fat rubbed into the weave so it sheds the worst of the standing rain. In the markets they exchange these wrappings the way they exchange everything: slowly, with the gravity of people who know that dryness is wealth and that a finished corner of hardened stone is an heirloom. A blade of the reddish metal is worn openly, even though it will dull in a week; the wearing is the point, a small brightness against all the green-gold dusk. Most work is done with tooth and bone and the living grain of the wet wood that is not wood. Hands know the difference between a cut that has already browned at the bladder and a cut that will bleed sweetness and invite the rare fauna. They treat the invitation seriously. The rare fauna, when it comes, is not always the size anyone hoped.

There are the gifted ones, born where the older water mixes with the younger in those slow helical stairwells. You cannot see the gift on them. They look the same: beaded, compact, forehead ready. But they walk a half-step differently, as if listening with the soles. They know, without looking, when a wall is about to become floor. They are given the work of sounding the rooms, of singing the low, interval language into the new stone so the stone will remember there is a person here and not merely a pocket of water. They do not live longer. They live *next to*. The coming-up treats them with the same courteous inattention it treats everyone else, but they are never surprised by it. A gifted child will wake in the night and begin to deepen a doorway that no one else has noticed is growing shallower. By morning the family can still pass. The rock, embryonic and indifferent, closed its curve around the need because the need was announced in time.

They share air the way they share bread. In the deeper weeks the inverted basins are tended like gardens. Breath is rationed with opinions attached: whose lungs are wasteful, who has learned to sip rather than gulp, who can stay under longest when the true water climbs to the second storey. There is no cruelty in the accounting, only the practical tenderness of people who have watched a chamber close around a sleeper. You do not sleep in a room that has not been sounded. You do not strike the new stone hard. You cut only what has already browned. These are not laws so much as the habits of a body that has decided to remain.

At what they call night they become a system of remembered routes. Hands find the same handholds they found last season, even if the handholds have risen an inch. Breath is held through the longer duck-unders. Somewhere a signal-stone knocks three slow, then two. The sound travels farther than voices, a language of intervals that means *the market is still passable* or *the new ridge is sweating metal* or simply *I am here, in the warm dark, still arriving*.

They do not think of themselves as waiting for the plateau to finish. The plateau is not a promise. It is a process they live inside, the way a hand lives next to a fever. Some mornings, when the hanging sea tears and a brief lens of astonishing air opens, they climb dripping to the unfinished heights and stand there remembering that elevation was supposed to mean above. They feel their weight correctly for a few breaths. They hear their own voices arrive at their own ears without the wrap of wet. Then the rain occupies them again, and they descend, and the stone offers them a lip, then a step, then a room that was not a room until they needed it.

What lives here is this: a people who have become the courtesy the rock extends to itself. They are lush and quiet and only beginning, the same way everything else is. Their children sleep in hammocks of woven root because the hammock is the only dry-enough place, and even the hammock is beaded. Their old ones tap the reddish nodules from the newest seams, kneeling in a long line that is not quite prayer. Their gifted ones walk next to the coming-up and tell it, forehead to stone, that a person is not a pocket of water. And the town—reef, city, mountain still being born—breathes through them the way it breathes through the hanging forests, in a country of warm dark water that has nowhere lower to go, and so remains.
