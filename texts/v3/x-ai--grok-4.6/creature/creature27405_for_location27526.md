The people of the seams do not call themselves a people. The word would require an edge they refuse to draw. They are the ones who wake with tannin already in the mouth, who measure a day by how the mist decides to hang, who know a gallery by the particular sour-sweet of its rot long before the eyes have finished adjusting. Their skin takes the color of the wood they lean against: not brown, not gray, but that sealed, riverstone gloss the ancient vessels wear where water has run for a thousand seasons. It never quite dries. When they touch one another the contact leaves a faint sheen, like breath on heartwood.

They are small in the way a graft is small — not diminished, only concentrated. Limbs long for the body, fingers padded and slightly tacky so that bark and moss become extensions rather than surfaces. Eyes are large and the color of wet fungus, pupils that open until almost no iris remains; they do not fear the deeper hollows the way a sun-born creature would. Hair is kept short or bound into cords that double as grafting line, because anything longer becomes another hanging garden, another place for the unfallen water to travel. Some let certain locks grow and then cut them at the dark of a moisture-song, offering the length to a new brace. The vine that cinches as it dries is kin; they speak of it as an aunt who never learned to let go.

They do not build. They inhabit the already-growing. A house is a decision the moss has almost made: a pocket stitched with hair and guest-timber, propped with the pale slough of vault-skins, always smelling of sleep and the faint iron of sap. Fires are thumb-sized, fed on the driest of the annual fall, never allowed to remember they could become large. Smoke is discouraged; it travels the old vessels and comes out, days later, as a rumor in another weather. They cook by holding food against the metabolic warmth of a living wall until it yields. Meat is rare. When a herd darkens the upper decks they take only what has already begun to fail — the lame, the lagging, the one whose glitter of wet flank has gone matte. The rest they watch as weather, feeling the faint thunder in the soles rather than hearing it.

Their work is the same work the structure has always done: adjust. They walk the seep-lines with gourds, singing the old chants that are less words than maps of pressure and grain. Water must be encouraged, never commanded. A misplaced sheet of condensation can drown a nursery of filter-ribbons or leave a hanging garden brittle. They prune the guest-plants that would otherwise choke a corridor into a throat. They brace where a gall the size of a hill has begun to slump, using living cords that tighten as they lignify. To cut a great member is not a crime; it is a category error, like trying to argue with a fever. The thought simply fails to form.

They remember nothing of the first growing. The stories they tell are not origin stories. They are weather reports that have learned to walk. One tells of a walker who followed a single beam for a month and found, at what should have been its end, a chamber that was the inside of a seed — warm, wet, the air so thick with unused light that the walker came back speaking in the dialect of moss. Another tells of two galleries that have never met except in a puddle; look down and you see the ceiling of a hall you will not reach for years, or already passed yesterday. Graft-scars open if you do not insist they are scars. The people navigate by the smell of different rots, by the direction the heat prefers, by songs whose intervals are distances of moisture rather than of length. A drawn map is a kind of boast and a kind of blasphemy. Paper pretends the world holds still. They laugh, quietly, the way the architecture swallows vibration, when a child tries to scratch one.

Above them, through them, the other lives continue their own adjustments.

In the marsh-seams the water is never empty. Larval cities turn in slow constellations, each individual no larger than a thumbnail, linked by threads of mucus that taste the brown light and pass the news along. Soft walkers with too many quiet feet graze the nap of the moss, leaving no trail that lasts longer than a breath. Filter-ribbons pulse like forgotten hearts, drinking the dusk and exhaling a finer dusk. Sometimes a ribbon will lift itself entirely out of the water and hang, dripping, as if considering becoming a leaf again.

In the mid-hollows the fliers keep their patience. They have leaves for tails, or tails that have decided they were always leaves. They do not flap so much as adjust the angle of their gliding; a wingbeat arrives as a change in the warmth on your face. They nest in the hanging organs, stitching with silk that is also a kind of sap. When they die they do not fall. They become another curtain, another place for condensation to bead.

The herds are weather that has learned to walk. You know them first as a darkening of the far wall, then as a glitter of wet flanks moving at a distance that makes individuals into a single slow pulse. They cross the upper decks in silence so complete it is a kind of roar felt in the knees. Their hooves, or whatever serves, are padded with the same living cork that swallows sound. Predators exist as the sudden extra quiet: a hanging garden stops dripping for a few breaths. Nothing announces. A shape that was part of the ribbing is suddenly not. Then the drips resume, a little warmer, as if the structure has absorbed the event the way it absorbs everything else.

Deeper still, or higher — direction having long since become decorative — there are lives that have given up the pretense of being separate from the wood. Things that are mostly cambium and decision, that move only when the great members shift their weight. You encounter them as a thickening in the grain under your palm, a place where the heartwood is slightly more awake. They do not hunt. They wait for the structure to dream it is smaller, for a chamber to close with the slow intimacy of a fist, and then they are simply there, filling the mouth-sized space with exhaled warmth and the faint click of adjusting fibers. When the walls remember their scale and recede, these lives recede with them, becoming again the suggestion of a vein.

And the structure itself continues. It is not a god. It is not a ruin. It is the finished object that refused to stop being an organism. Far off, so far it might be another climate, something large changes its weight from one root-mass to another. The entire hollow — if that word still means anything — takes a breath you cannot hear. In the marsh at your feet a line of bubbles rises and does not break. The people of the seams look up, or inward, or along the grain, and go back to their pruning. The dusk continues, being used by the leaves. The heat remains, wet and even, the warmth of a body that has been running for a geological age and has decided this is simply how a body runs.
