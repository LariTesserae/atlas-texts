The first things that live here are the voices. They occupy the air more thoroughly than any body could, a pressure that has long since stopped being noise and become the climate itself. You hear them before the eye can decide what is animal and what is only another slumped contour of stone. The bellows arrive already multiplied, already ancient, as if the cavern has been rehearsing them for so many centuries that the original throats are almost incidental.

They resolve, eventually, into the long-limbed ones. Pale as the dust they walk through, taller at the shoulder than a man could comfortably reach, their bodies stretched into something that looks half-starved and half-architectural. The limbs are too many joints, each one sheathed in hide the exact colour and dryness of the surrounding flour, so that when they stand still they vanish into the geometry of the old streets. Only the slow swing of a neck, or the sudden flare of a nostril, gives them back. The heads are narrow, almost equine, but the jaws hinge farther than any horse’s; when they open, the sound that comes out is not a cry so much as a structural failure, a deep, wet-stone resonance that the walls take up and refuse to give back. They move in loose, patient constellations, never quite a herd, never quite separate. A single animal may graze for an hour on the same silver-grey shrub, stripping the waxy leaves with lips that look too delicate for the work, while others drift past it like weather. Their hooves make almost no mark. The dust remembers them only as faint, braided depressions that the next interval of stillness erases.

Between their slow rivers live the smaller, harder lives. Every crevice, every undercut of an eroded plinth, every hollow left by a vanished stair, holds them: chitinous, many-legged, the colour of old mortar. They do not scurry so much as ratchet. Legs click against stone in overlapping meters, a high, dry stridulation that never quite resolves into rhythm and never quite stops. Some are no longer than a finger, armoured in plates that have the same matte, powdery finish as the lichens they share the rock with. Others are longer than a man’s arm, their bodies segmented into something that looks assembled rather than grown, each plate slightly offset so that motion itself produces the sound. They occupy the vertical as easily as the horizontal; you see them crossing the undersides of the slumped mounds in slow, inverted processions, antennae tasting the same faint mineral damp the succulents drink. They are the city’s most faithful annotators. Where the large browsers treat the ruins as landscape, these treat them as furniture, as membrane, as the only world that has ever existed.

Above both, the winged ones lift and resettle with the same lack of urgency. They are nearly colourless, membranes the shade of old vellum, bodies all tendon and hollow bone. When they launch it is not flight so much as a decision to stop being stone. A flock will rise from the higher remnants in a single, delayed motion, wheel once through the brass-coloured dusk, and return to the same perches as if the interval had been only a longer breath. Their cries are thinner than the bellows, more tearing, and they fold into the greater roar until you cannot tell whether the sound is coming from above or from the walls themselves. They roost in the last suggestions of windows, in the decorative scoring that time left on the rounded mounds, and they watch with eyes that have no use for more light than the rock already sweats.

None of them hurry because nothing here has ever required hurry. The vegetation they live among is itself a kind of animal slowness: the thick-leaved rosettes that store the air’s last reluctance, the interlaced shrubs that hold their architectural canopies as if still obeying some forgotten civic code, the minute succulents and crusts that turn every shallow basin into a living mosaic. The animals do not consume the city; they continue it. The browsers crop the same patient geometries the masons once laid out. The clicking things inhabit the same interstices. The flyers use the same elevations for lookout that whatever built this place once used, or simply never needed. There is no memory of builders among them, and no need of one. The streets have finished becoming geology; the lives that remain have finished becoming the only inhabitants the place was ever going to have.

Walk long enough and the populations shift by the same degrees the districts do. In the field of low plinths the browsers thin out and the clicking chorus grows denser, almost metallic. Around the great slumped massif the pale herds thicken into slow rivers that take half a day to pass, their overlapping voices turning the whole structure into a single vibrating instrument. In the ghost-grid of shallow basins the succulent carpet is thickest and the smallest lives are loudest, a continuous high rattle that you feel in the teeth. Nowhere is there a centre that matters more than any other. The scale is simply the scale of lives that have outlasted the idea of endings.

They ask for almost nothing: the last internal damp of the stone, the faint phosphorescence, the dust that is already everything that ever lived here reduced to the same pale argument. In return they fill every measure of the air with a roaring that has no beginning and will have no end. You can stand in one of the great open intervals, the cool dryness settling into your lungs, and feel them continue exactly as they have continued, abundant and sparse, loud and unhurried, for longer than the surface has words. The place is finished with catastrophe. What lives here is the sound of that finishing, patient, deafening, and complete.
