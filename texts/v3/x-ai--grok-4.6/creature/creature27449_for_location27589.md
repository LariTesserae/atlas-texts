The plateau keeps its census in intervals of current. What lives here does not colonize so much as occupy the momentary lee, the standing wave, the shear where two galleries disagree. They arrive already ancient, already shaped by a thousand previous occupations of the same exact eddy. Nothing here is born in the usual sense; things thicken out of the flour the stone still sheds, or they assemble from the shed skins of larger things, or they simply persist because the water has not yet found a way to unmake them.

Closest to the human scale—if any scale still applies—are the lingerers. Forearm-length, pale to the point of translucence, they hang in the dead water behind every buttress and every unfinished capital. Their eyes are two slow lenses of the same gray-green as the galleries themselves, and they do not track so much as wait for the light to decide it has arrived. A seam of dull foxfire runs the length of each flank, not for signaling but for the private mathematics of buoyancy; when the seam brightens, the lingerer rises half its own length and the current takes it elsewhere. They feed by opening. The mouth is a lantern only when it is empty. When it fills it becomes a dark purse of stone-dust and the torn veils of filterers, and the light goes out until the purse is empty again. They do not school. A nave may hold twenty of them and still feel vacant, each one a punctuation mark the sentence has forgotten.

The filterers are the architecture’s second skin. They stretch across every throat the masons left too narrow, across every overflow the water still obeys. Each is a single living sheet, meters on a side, thinner than ice and older than the ice that sometimes rides it. The sheet is not one animal. It is a lattice of mouths so small they register only as a change in the water’s patience. Nutrients—the fine flour, the dissolved ghosts of lingerers, the mineral rain that never quite settles—pass through and are kept. What is not kept is pressed into a finer flour and released downstream, so that the next sheet may live. When a cataract changes its mind the sheets tear, but they tear along predetermined seams and the fragments drift like banners until they find another throat and remember how to be continuous. Their only light is the light they steal from whatever passes through them: a brief, pewter after-image that travels the lattice and dies.

Larger things use the drop-shafts. They are colder than the water, which is already colder than memory. You feel them first as a change in the pressure that has nothing to do with depth—an incoming subtraction, a volume that was not there deciding it will be. They do not swim. They fall upward or downward according to a logic the currents invented for them, appearing in one well as a pale, slow eclipse and gone into the next before the groan of displaced water has finished arriving. Their flanks carry no foxfire. Their mouths, when they open, are not lanterns but absences: circular darknesses that drink the ambient pewter and give back nothing. What they eat is everything that cannot get out of the shaft in time. A lingerer, a torn filterer, another of their own kind that misjudged the bloom. They do not store. They pass through themselves the way the water passes through the stone, and what they leave behind is a finer, colder flour that the filterers will later strain.

There are others that never quite cohere into bodies. In the slack galleries, when the silt draws its ghost floor-plans, the drawings sometimes thicken. Circles become eyes that are only geometry. Overflows become mouths that are only the idea of hunger. These last only as long as the stillness lasts. When the pulse comes they dissolve back into cloud, and the cloud is already animals turning, and the animals are already gone. The stone does not distinguish. It has been planed past the point of recording any particular vanishing.

Nothing here possesses a tool, because the plateau itself is the only tool that was ever required. Nothing here builds, because building ended so long ago that the verb has no remaining tense. They live on the motion the masons left running, on one another, on the inexhaustible dust of a finished world. Distances continue to lie. A lingerer that seems an arm’s length away is a day’s drift. A filterer that seems a curtain is a week of slow transit if you are trying to go around it. The larger things do not experience distance at all; they experience only the next shaft, the next subtraction.

Sometimes, in a nave so vast the ceiling is only a rumor, all three kinds occupy the same volume for a little while. The lingerers hang like forgotten lamps. The filterers span the side-channels like faded heraldry. Something colder than the stone uses the central well the way a thought uses a skull, appearing and gone. The water thickens, then thins. The single note that is not quite sound arrives in the jaw and departs. Then the gallery is empty again, and the emptiness is older than any of them, and the stone, which has already decided its shape, does not notice the difference.
