The basin does not keep residents so much as it keeps occurring. Life here is a verb that has forgotten how to become a noun.

Begin with the crystals, because they are the slowest lie the place tells itself. They are not plants. They are decisions that have chosen to be silicate for a while. A hanging curtain of verdigris fronds is a single long thought that decided, sometime in the last century or the last hour, to occupy this particular overhang. A candelabra of dull-gold pyrite is another thought that preferred verticality and a little more ostentation. They drink nothing; they metabolise fracture and light-fatigue. Their “growth” is a continual recasting of the same argument: this surface, now. When the argument fails they simply fold the old body inward like a fist closing on itself and open the same pattern ten metres away, leaving only a glitter of abandoned powder that the next generation of crawlers will inhale and become. Break a frond and the argument notices you. Overnight the replica hand appears—faceted, slightly too large, cool as the dais, ozone-scented. It is not a threat and not a gift. It is the crystal saying, in the only grammar it owns, we have updated the map to include this new density.

The fauna are the same argument spoken faster, in a dialect of wings and cartilage. They are not many species; they are one storm rehearsing itself. The mica-winged flyers are the storm’s syllables, thumbnail-sized, voices like foil tearing, never the same cloud twice. They treat empty air as both lung and battlefield, reconfiguring so rapidly that any given body is already a memory of itself. The cartilage kites are the longer sentences: slower, booming, dust-fur catching the tired daylight and throwing it back as bruised iridescence. They gyre in patterns that almost look like writing until you try to read them. The iridescent crawlers are the punctuation—sudden, boiling mats that erupt from the crystal roots as if the stone itself were coughing them up, then vanish back into hairline cracks as if embarrassed by their own solidity. They unmake whatever has begun to look finished. New crystal spurs last until midday. Whole thickets become talc. The husks of the flyers and kites sift down and are immediately restirred into the next iteration. Nothing is allowed to conclude. The roar is the sound of a market that is also a war that is also a hive collapsing and reforming in the same breath; the crystals ring in sympathy and the animals answer, or feed, or both.

These two registers—slow silicate thought and furious granular weather—would be enough to fill the nave. They are not. Scattered through them are the artefacts, and the artefacts are inhabited.

The canted arch is not a ruin. Its surface crawls with script that rearranges the moment you look away. The characters are not recording a past; they are composing in the present tense, a sentence that never quite ends because the speaker is still deciding what it wishes to have said. Stand near it long enough and you feel the grammar tugging at the inside of your skull, trying to conjugate you into its next clause.

The fused-glass dais is colder than the air because it is thinking in a different thermal regime. The maps that flicker across it last only a few heartbeats and describe places that cannot exist in three dimensions; they are not maps of elsewhere so much as maps of elsewhen, or elsehow. Gravity eddies around it. Sound arrives early. Time forgets to stay even. The dais is a mind that has chosen to be furniture for a while, or a piece of furniture that has remembered it used to be a mind.

The force-lines are simpler and more arrogant. They are visible only as a polarisation of dust, yet they hold an entire crystal pillar at an angle the mountain itself would never have permitted. They are will, left running. Whoever decided the pillar should lean has not revoked the decision.

And sometimes the stone itself becomes briefly transparent and you see the galleries: layered, receding, packed with depths that cannot fit inside the mountain. They are not empty. Things move there—too far, too folded, too patient to resolve into shapes you already have names for. They do not come out often. When they do, they come out as a pulse in the light, as a new crystal that grows with uncharacteristic deliberation, as a sudden local revision of the rules. Moisture on your skin or breath is insult enough that they steal it instantly and convert it into more of the dry ringing dust; they are allergic to the unfinished wetness of ordinary life. Remain still and the crawlers treat you as another outcrop and begin to colonise, because stillness is the only invitation they recognise. The mountain holds the basin the way an old hand holds a coin it has carried for decades: worn, unsurprised, entirely willing to let the coin keep changing its value. You are not a visitor. You are another brief density the place has decided, for now, to tolerate inside the roaring, glittering, waterless air. Already the crystals have begun to consider the shape of your hand. Already the storm has tasted the moisture it took from you and is deciding whether to make more of you, or less.
