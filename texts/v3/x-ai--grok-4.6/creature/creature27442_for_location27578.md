The Hold does not host life so much as it condenses it, the way a cold housing condenses grease. Everything that persists here has learned to live on remainder: on dust, on vibration, on the heat that leaks from unfinished processes, on the thin rumor of air that still remembers it once belonged to weather. The mountain itself is too new to offer soil or season. What lives inside its wound has had to invent its own metabolism, dry and interlocking, a closed circuit of scrap and scrape.

The people come first in any accounting, though they would not put themselves first. They are a short-generation folk, the children or the children’s children of whoever first dogged a plate into the cleft and decided the thinness could be bargained with. Their bodies have the look of something that has been stored too long in a desiccator: lips permanently chapped into white deltas, eyes set deep as if the sockets themselves were trying to conserve moisture, skin that maps every old dehydration crack in fine pale calligraphy. Joints announce themselves. A cough is an event; it raises a small weather of dust from the felt of a collar. They dress in layers that have become a second geology—oil-blackened canvas, boiled wool, scraps of older insulation stitched into gaiters—cinched at every opening because the mites travel any unguarded hem. Personal machines hang from them like votives: a pocket dynamo whose crank they turn absently while waiting for a bay to finish migrating, a listening trumpet of spun alloy pressed to a bulkhead so they can hear, through the shy vibration, whether the next shift’s work has already begun two levels down. They sleep in relays, three bodies sharing the same cot-space across a day, the mattress still warm from the previous occupant, the locker still holding someone else’s grease-blackened shrine of bolts. Children exist here, improbably. They learn early not to run. A running child stirs loft-rats; a loft-rat in panic is a spark looking for insulation. The children play games of negative space—who can stand longest in the exact silhouette of a gantry arm, who can pocket a fallen summit-needle without cutting the cloth. Their laughter, when it happens, is a private weather close to the mouth, instantly baffled by hanging wire and cork.

They do not so much inhabit the machines as complete them. A woman drawing wire hears a frequency no one else can; the wire itself seems to sing only for the die and the hand. A foundry no larger than a cupboard pours alloy the color of old tea into molds that will become the teeth of gears that will, in a week, be worn down and recast. Behind chain-mail curtains, fingers assemble relays in the furious hush of piecework, each click a small refusal of the thinness outside. When the mountain shrugs—still young, still settling another finger-width in the night—they brace on the yellow members socketed into the older stone. Everything else may walk. A vanished bay is not a loss; it is a reassignment. They follow the heat, or the rats, or the faint new tracks dogged into rock that has not yet decided to be permanent. Sweat is harvested, an embarrassment turned resource, scraped and fed to condensers that complain and yield a few cloudy drops no one drinks if they can help it. Water is the enemy that lifts salts and eats insulation; a spilled cup is a geological event, fined in labor by the night shift.

Then there is the rustle, the fauna that were never plants’ children. Nothing green has ever held. In the coolest seams a dull lilac bloom sometimes appears, more mineral than organism, a crust the night shift scrapes for the trace metals it concentrates; the animals ignore it. They eat dust, shed insulation, microscopic grease, one another. Flake-mites travel in silent carpets across vertical plate, flat as paper, invisible until a filament bulb shifts its angle and the whole wall seems to breathe sideways. Hinge-crabs, fist-sized, carapaces the exact dull alloy of the oldest pipes, nest in any right angle and extend a single pale feeler to taste vibration. Machine vibration they leave; the slow pulse of a sleeper they may harvest—a button, a loose screw, a flake of skin. Loft-rats (not rats) run the vertical city the way fish would run a reef of rivets: longer in the spine, ears like folded foil, hind feet that splay into dry fans so they can drop from a catwalk and land without sound on a pile of swarf. At shift-change they pour, a soft continuous motion along every edge, and for those minutes the Hold seems twice as inhabited, a doubled city of motion and hush. They carry sparks when panicked. Extinguishers stand half-empty within reach, always.

Other lives have begun to specialize in the new chemistry. There are the static-wisps, barely more than charged dust given a preference for direction, that collect on the undersides of catwalks and discharge in tiny, soundless blue stitches when a person passes too close; children dare one another to let one walk their knuckles. There are the oil-threaders, thin as horsehair, that live inside the oldest housings and spin faint iridescent skeins from condensed lubricant; the day shift harvests the skeins for gasket material and pretends not to notice how the threaders seem to prefer certain machines, as if choosing. And in the highest, stillest seams, something almost like a lichen of pure electrostatic cling will sometimes form—a faint, prickling film that the loft-rats will not cross. The night shift leaves it. It may be concentrating something useful. It may simply be the mountain beginning to think in mineral.

All of it—the people with their clicking joints and pocket dynamos, the mites in their paper carpets, the crabs tasting sleep, the rats pouring like dry water, the wisps and threaders and the lilac bloom—forms a single dry metabolism. The machines never rest; the Hold is always being born a little more and coming apart a little more. Work-bays migrate. Tracks are temporary. The mountain leans. Life here is the art of remaining in the interval, of fitting into leftover space, of turning remainder into continuance. At the grated platform above the last catwalk, when a shift ends, a person can look up through the slits at that dim non-color that might be sky and hear their own pulse, very close, while below the silent rearrangement continues: arms unfolding, belts reversing, fauna flowing along the lips of things. A single bright needle of summit mineral may tick against the grate, sharp enough to draw blood, pretty enough to pocket. No one drinks the condenser water if they can help it. Throats stay dusty. The wound stays full.
