The highland is not occupied. Occupation implies arrival, settlement, a later claim laid over something older. What lives here is the claim itself, already complete, already the ground.

Each swell is a single continuous body, or a parliament so ancient the votes have fused. You cannot count them. The ridges they share are not borders but the places where two metabolisms learned, long ago, to lean without quarrel. Their skins are the skins you walk on: layered, shingled, constantly rewriting. A bole is only a place where the body has decided to stand more upright for a century or two. A hollow is a lung that opened last week and may close next month. They drink light the way other creatures drink water, storing it as a thin, even fever just beneath the surface. At dusk that fever becomes visible, a low industrial haze with no chimneys and no workers, only the work.

They do not sleep. Sleep is a luxury of things that must stop in order to continue. These bodies turn over internally the way weather turns: a face flushes pale, then tea-dark, then bruised, and the old tissue is already being unmade into the new. They slough without drama. The discarded sheets settle, are digested, become architecture a few meters to the left. Growth is not incremental. It is weather you can watch if you refuse to blink. Stems lengthen with a visible, stubborn creep. Blooms arrive as a factory decision—hectares of the same closed, waxy flower thrown open at once, the air briefly sweet and cold, then emptied again by evening. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hurried. The form of the land was decided before the last ice withdrew; the contents have never stopped rearranging themselves inside that decision.

Smaller lives exist only as rumors the great bodies have not yet troubled to erase. A moth the exact color of wet bark, wings held so still they become part of the rind. A thick, pale worm that withdraws into a seam when the light changes, leaving a faint solvent smell. Once, very high, something that might have been a bird, pale and silent, crossing and not returning. They are accidents of scale. The highland pollinates itself, or the muffled wind does the work, or the flowers were never meant for anyone. The rare animals do not sing. They do not call. They appear, confirm that fauna is possible, and vanish back into the padding of leaves.

There are no others. No watchers in the vents, no minds behind the consistent curves of the ridges, no hidden nation living in the damp cellars of the mass. The slight wrongness you feel—the venation that looks like a diagram, the geometry that repeats as if worn by one thought—is only the look of a system that has had a very long time to become efficient. The highland is finished. It has no need of inhabitants because it is already, entirely, inhabited by its own continuing weather: growth, collapse, growth, the private storm that never raises its voice and never ends.

If you lie down on a crest, the surface will have changed by morning. A new lip of tissue at your shoulder. A vent breathing a few body-lengths away. The country will not have moved. It will have remained exactly itself, which is the only thing that has ever lived here.
