The first things you meet are not beings so much as decisions that kept deciding after the deciders left.

They occupy every registered interval the way a completed inventory occupies a ledger: oil-kelps rising wrist-thick from their clamp-collars, fronds the size of sails folding and unfolding on currents too slow to be called currents. Each kelp knows the exact cubic centimetres allotted to it; the knowledge is written in the spacing of its holdfasts and the angle at which its midrib presents to the light that arrives already green. They do not reach. They fill. A leaf adds its millimetre and then waits, as though the next millimetre were a scheduled valve-opening rather than a hunger.

Against the warm bulkheads the lacquer-weeds keep a different profession. Their secretions are not waste; they are a product line that no longer has a buyer. The varnish they exude hardens in microscopic sheets, sealing their own stems against the faint metallic sweetness of the water, then continues outward, glazing the nearest conduits, the empty work-frames, the inside of a helmet until the helmet becomes another leaf, darker and more exact. The weeds do not know they are replacing the attendants. They only know the instruction that remains: occupy until the occupation is total.

Lower, in the purple dusk of the deepest racks, the bladder-moss keeps time by teaspoon. Each vesicle swells over a week, a patient inflation that has nothing to do with weather and everything to do with a nutrient pulse that arrives like a forgotten tide. When the vesicles finally part they release no spores that the sterilised water would recognise; they release only a finer darkness, a pigment that stains nothing because the mountain is still too new for stain. The moss does not colonise. It completes.

The flowers—if the calendar still permits that word—open without witness. White, waxed, perfectly scheduled, they present surfaces that no insect was ever designed to land on and no current is strong enough to stir. They release no scent the water will carry; scent would be a conversation, and conversation was never specified. After the allotted hours they close again, not withered but withdrawn, as though the act of opening had been a quality-control check rather than a hope.

Between these registered lives the gaps continue to close. Tendrils that were trained through eye-bolts have begun to treat the bolts as suggestions rather than limits. A harness left dripping since commissioning is now a trellis whose every buckle is sheathed in living tissue. The articulated frames, sized for bodies that would have breathed this same warm solid, hang like the shed exoskeletons of a caste that never hatched. The plants do not mourn the absence. They simply inherit the geometry.

What lives here therefore has no name because naming would imply a speaker and a listener, and both roles were cancelled before the last flange was torqued. There is only the collective profession of density. The vat is not inhabited; it is becoming the inhabitant—a single packed organism whose skeleton is I-beam and whose blood is process water held at blood-heat, whose thoughts (if the word can be stretched that far) are the almost geological vortices of the circulators and the private click of thermostats that no longer need to report to anyone. Growth is not desire. It is compliance so thorough it has become indistinguishable from will.

You can hang in the pale midday column and feel the whole volume flex, a minute interpolation caused by wind that exists only on the other side of the glass. The plants register the flex as a change in pressure so slight it would be meaningless to any nervous system that had ever known air. They adjust. A bladder fills by another teaspoon. A lacquer film thickens by another micron. The harvest gantry remains unmoved, a dark rib that the forest has already begun to treat as just another trunk.

Stay long enough and the silence stops being the sound of something missing. It becomes the sound of a sentence that was written correctly the first time and has simply never been required to end. The planted life does not wait for the attendants. It has already used their absence as another interval to fill. In another year the last gaps will close. The vat will be what it was always drawn to be: a room that is also a sea, a machine that is also a forest, a finished thing holding its breath in a medium that has no breath, high above the clouds, performing the only task it was ever given, which is to continue.
