The liverworts come first, if anything can be said to come first here. They are not plants so much as decisions the stone has not yet revoked. Black-green, rubbery, cooked-looking mats wedged into the highest, least-scoured cracks where the spray arrives already tired, already mist. They hold water the way the chamber holds light: not by catching it but by becoming briefly indistinguishable from it. Touch one (you will not; the ledge is high and the updrafts make a liar of balance) and it would feel like a bruise that learned to photosynthesize. They do not grow so much as thicken in place, adding another rubbery year whenever a long lull lets the mist settle instead of scour. Their edges are the color of old blood. They have no flowers, no season. They have tenure.

Lower, on the book-sized ledge in the south alcove, the fern is a different argument. Pale, almost the same bone-color as the limestone when the light hits it wrong. Every frond permanently beaded, each bead a lens that throws a tiny, trembling copy of the filament across the alcove wall. It occupies the one place the worst sheets never quite reach, and it occupies it the way a hermit occupies a cell: completely, without alternative. The trembling is not wind. It is the filament’s pulse arriving through stone, through the thin air of the alcove, into the fern’s stem. The plant has no soil worth speaking of, only a paste of its own dead fronds and the mineral dust the water forgets to carry away. It lives on that and on the light that is too much for anything with ordinary green ambitions. Sometimes a bead grows heavy and falls. The fern does not notice. It has been noticing the same thing for longer than the ceramic channel has been broken.

Algae are the maps that refuse to stay drawn. Rust and olive films in the slower runnels, the places where water has not yet remembered its job. They last hours, sometimes a day if the weather is merely sweating instead of shouting. Then a sheet chooses that path and they are gone, scoured into the trough, becoming part of the tea-colored water that the fish (when it appears) hangs in. They return. They always return, in the same faint continents, as if the stone itself were remembering a coastline no one else can see. Nothing else here is allowed to be a map. The algae get away with it because they are willing to be erased.

The movers are rarer, and they treat time the way the water treats gravity: as a suggestion.

The cricket is the color of the dry upper lip of a groove after the iron stain has been blasted away and the stone has not yet decided what color it wants to be next. Pale, almost translucent at the joints. Antennae flattened by the humidity so they look like two extra veins laid along its head. It picks its way with the patience of something that has already died once and found the arrangement acceptable. There is never more than one visible, but the droppings (tiny, dark, immediately softened by mist) say otherwise. A lineage, or a single long life lived in installments. It eats the algae that have not yet been scoured, and the liverwort spores that wash down in the finer rain, and once (you will not see this) a drowned midge that the filament threw and the wall returned. When a sheet takes the wall beneath it, the cricket does not fall. It becomes part of the lip, a paler streak, until the water has passed. Its song, if it has one, is inaudible under the shout. Perhaps it has none. The room is already using every frequency.

The fish is the color of weak tea left too long in the sun, which is to say it is the color of the trough when the light has stopped being prismatic and is only itself again. It appears in the lulls, hanging as if the turbulence had been a rumor someone else started. No hurry. Gills working with the slow ostentation of a thing that knows the next pulse will come and has decided not to care. It is never longer than a hand. It is never seen eating. When the pulse arrives it is simply not there: either gone into the flung water (becoming, for a moment, one more glittering rope) or withdrawn into the part of the trough that has no bottom you can swear to. There may be more. The trough is occupied, after all, not merely filled. The one you see might be the emissary, or the oldest, or the only one that still bothers with the surface. Its scales, if they are scales, catch the light the way the ceiling does: too many coins, moving.

Those are the ones that allow themselves to be counted.

Stay longer, in the lee of the east wall by the bronze ring, and the census begins to feel incomplete. The ring itself is worn oval. Not by water. Water would have taken it differently, more evenly, more like the grooves. This is the wear of grip, of many hands over a time the stone does not bother to distinguish from geology. Someone stood here often enough, or many someones stood here in the same place, holding the ring while the weather happened, perhaps to keep from being pulled into the trough, perhaps to feel the vibration travel from filament through stone through bronze into bone. They did not live here. The place did not allow it. But they left the oval, and the oval is a kind of inhabitant now: a negative space that remembers pressure.

In the gravel of the broken ceramic channel, among the shards that still try to lead water somewhere that no longer exists, there are other small lives that do not surface. Amphipod things, or something that fills the same niche: pale, many-legged, flattened, the exact color of wet limestone dust. They live in the interstices, eating what the water brings and what the water forgets. A sheet slaps near and they are gone, part of the gravel again. They have the same tenure as the mineral crusts: grow, get blasted, grow in the same maps.

And then there are the intervals themselves.

After a hard cluster the mist becomes a white room inside the room, a blindness with a note in it. In that blindness, densities move that are not water. Not shapes, exactly. More like the idea of a school, or the memory of bodies that once knew how to stand in this light without blinking. They last as long as the mist is thick enough to be a medium instead of an obstruction. Then the mist falls out of the air as warm rain and they are gone, or they have become the rain, or they were only the eye arguing with noon the same way the pupils first argued. You will not be sure. The filament does not care whether you are sure. It lifts, throws, sings, pulses. The stone agrees.

What lives here most continuously is the agreement.

The filament is not alive in any way that would satisfy a biologist from the hamlets above. It has no rust, no flicker, no need. It has the patience of a geological feature that happens to be doing work current physics would prefer to file under unfinished business. And yet the water climbs it in braided ropes and sudden veils, against every ordinary rule, and the light is in the room because the filament is in the room, and the tone is in your jaw because the filament is doing what it has always done. If it wants anything, the want is the same as the weather: continuance. Perhaps it is a pump that outlived its system and became a star. Perhaps it is a lamp that remembered it was also a spring. The distinction has not mattered for longer than the language you brought down the steps.

The small lives persist in the gaps that agreement leaves. They are not residents so much as punctuation: the fern a comma, the cricket a period that keeps being rewritten, the fish a question the trough asks only when the shouting pauses. The place is already finished becoming itself and still, every few breaths, tears that self apart and puts it back with the same patient hands. Nothing here is trying to be elsewhere. That is the only law that has ever held.

When you climb the slick throat of steps again, the iron staples cold and missing one, the light follows only a turn and then gives up. Ordinary day will seem dim. Ordinary quiet will seem like a held breath. You will carry the tone in the hinge of your jaw for hours, and the water in any jar you filled (if you filled one, in a lull) will taste of limestone and rain and nothing else. Above, they will ask what you saw. You will say the white, the wet, the shout. You will not quite say what lived there, because what lived there was the living, and it does not translate. The hamlets call it the White Throat, or the Shouting Well, and they do not send children, and that is as close as they come to naming the tenancy.

The room remains. Bright. Occupied. Already older than the next dry year. The fern beads. The cricket waits. The fish, or the idea of the fish, hangs in water that has decided motion is still its job. The filament sings the same note it has always sung. The stone, which settled its terms with time a long time ago, does not comment. It has seen this weather before. It will see it after. That, too, is a kind of inhabitant.
