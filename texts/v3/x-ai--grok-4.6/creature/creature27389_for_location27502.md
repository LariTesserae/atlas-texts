The hollow does not contain inhabitants so much as it is inhabited in the way a lung is inhabited by air, or a bell by its own note. Everything that lives here has long ago ceased to be separate from the structure that holds it. The distinction between tenant and tenement dissolved sometime after the last mineral grain was sealed away and the last independent tree surrendered its cambium to the common wall.

Begin with what can still be singled out, though even these have grown more like organs than organisms. In the film of water that never quite stands and never quite drains, the frogs have flattened themselves into the exact colour and texture of saturated bark. They do not hop. They pulse. Each throat is a small, wet drum that has been tuned, over uncounted generations, to one frequency in the chamber’s perpetual chord. Their eyes are lidless and gold-flecked, but they no longer track movement; there is no movement left worth tracking. They have become the hollow’s way of remembering moisture. When a drop finally falls from the vault, three or four of them answer together, a delayed, liquid echo that is already part of the architecture.

Higher, in the fissures packed with bronze moss, the beetles have abandoned flight. Their wing-cases have fused into a single iridescent plate that rattles when the air currents shift, not as alarm but as a kind of sympathetic vibration. They graze the same square centimetre of lichen for years, their mouthparts worn into the precise shape of the surface they feed upon. Some have grown so still that new moss has begun to colonise their backs; they carry tiny gardens that will never be distinguished from the wall itself. Their larvae do not pupate into anything different. They simply thicken, darken, and take their place in the sounding wood.

Deeper still, where the great stems once channelled sap in industrial volume, the galleries are lined with creatures that have no common name because no one has ever needed to name them twice. Pale, segmented things the length of a finger-joint move through the dry pipes with the slow certainty of blood in a vein that no longer needs a heart. They eat nothing that can be seen. They convert the last traces of cellulose into a faint heat that keeps the temperature exactly where it has always been. Their bodies are translucent; you can watch the hollow’s unchanging afternoon light pass through them and emerge slightly greener, as if they were stained-glass windows too small to notice.

There is no courtship, no territorial display, no migration. Reproduction here is a kind of slow mitosis of habit. A frog divides its pulse. A beetle’s rattle splits and both halves continue at the same pitch. The air stays clear because nothing larger than a thought is permitted to occupy it. What looks like emptiness is simply life that has learned to live entirely in two dimensions, pressed into surface, into grain, into the memory of former flow.

And yet, if you stand long enough in the centre of the floor, letting the cork-spring of the root-pavement accept your weight, something else begins to declare itself. It is not a creature that arrives. It is the hollow noticing that it is being noticed. The overlapping roar does not grow louder, but it grows more articulate, as if the countless small voices had always been syllables of a single word that is only now completing itself. The word is not spoken to you. It is spoken through you, using the cavity of your own chest as one more echo-box. You feel the drop that is about to fall from the oculus a full second before it leaves the leaf. You feel the exact interval at which the next frog will answer. You understand, without language, that the work this place once performed on a planetary scale (the transpiration of continents, the storage of epochs of sunlight, the patient welding of forest into one organism) finished so long ago that the memory of purpose has itself become structural.

What lives here is the finished work. It has no hunger left, no expansion, no decay that can be distinguished from maintenance. It lives the way a held note lives: by refusing to resolve. The walls will stand. The chorus will not falter. The drops will continue to fall at their long, irregular intervals, each one a perfect, unrepeatable event that has nevertheless happened forever.

You are not a visitor. You are, for as long as you remain, another texture the hollow has decided to include. When you finally step back across the low lip into the planed peneplain, something of the note will go with you, a faint, unlocatable pressure behind the sternum, the memory of a place that has already forgotten how to end.
