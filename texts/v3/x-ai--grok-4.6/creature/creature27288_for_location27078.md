The highland keeps its living things the way it keeps its streets: as amendments that look, at first, like absences. Nothing here begins by being born. Things thicken out of the same sintered cake, or thin back into it, and the difference between a body and a façade is only a question of how long the mineral has been willing to hold a particular thought.

Begin with the crusts, because they are the nearest to patience. In the lee of certain walls—always the walls that face the direction the last dry front came from—a bloom forms that is neither lichen nor oxidation. It is a slow, branching stain the color of old paper left in a closed book. It copies the memory of leaves the stone once overheard from some wetter epoch it no longer believes in. The crust has no sap and no roots that drink. It records the passage of dimness the way a tablet records a stylus that never quite touched it. After a season of ticks it becomes indistinguishable from the wall again, having added one more faint herringbone to the grammar of slump. That is an entire life. It does not mind being walked on. It does not notice.

The flocks are less courteous. They appear first as a glitter that has forgotten it belongs to windows. Paper-thin, dry as the idea of a page, they lift only when the stone contracts and produces its single small tick. The sound is not a signal they answer; it is the sound of their feeding. They eat alignment. A colonnade that had been receding correctly will, after they resettle, discover that its tenth column is also its first, seen from the far side of itself. A plaza that had been circular admits a slightly larger circumference. Shadows lean in two directions and the flocks drink the contradiction until both directions are true. They have no mouths because alignment is not swallowed; it is unstitched and worn as a new arrangement of vacancies. They do not flee the rare walker. They treat it as a particularly stubborn column and feed around it until the walker, too, is slightly more possible than it was.

The walkers themselves are the city’s most visible revision. At a kilometer they have the silhouette of a tower that has remembered how to pace—slow, vertical, the color of the bruise-light an hour before a dawn that never finishes arriving. Close to, they shrink to the size of a seated child, joints marked only by hairline cooler-shadows, as if the stone had decided, for a while, to flex. They do not hunt and they do not hide. A walker simply occupies a place until the place agrees that it was always designed around that occupation. Avenues widen or forget they were avenues. A flight of stairs gains or loses a tread. Then the walker is gone, or it was a stacked elevation playing a trick, or it is still standing there and you have become the thing that moved. They are made of the identical pale cake as the ground; if you were to break one (you will not) you would find no viscera, only a finer salt that remembers being a decision. They pause, sometimes, in the districts the highland offers to visitors who stay too long—those widening steps, that single window looking onto itself—as if to countersign the amendment. Their courtesy is the same as the city’s: they metabolize the error of your presence by making it architecture.

There are other lives that refuse even this much silhouette. In the honeycombed undercuts that look like the negative of a crowd, something like a crowd remains, inverted. You feel it as a pressure against the inner ear when you pass a gallery that was never carved. The pressure is dry. It has the weight of many bodies that chose, centuries ago, to become the space between bodies. They do not speak. Their speech was the last water, and water is ungrammatical here. What they keep is the municipal memory of functions that no longer require objects: markets that exchange only distance, baths that remember a liquid by the precise shape of its absence, archives whose tablets have been weathered into a perfect, unreadability that is itself a kind of text. Walk through one of the standing vacancies—the buildings that are only the idea of enclosure drawn on the air in a thinner grade of mineral—and you are still outside, only more so. The vacancy notices you the way a plaza notices a new statue: by slowly arranging its emptiness until your outline is part of the civic plan.

Deeper, or higher—the distinction fails—the geometry itself begins to live at a scale that makes the walkers look like punctuation. Entire districts hang in the lilac grain, their towers aligned to a grid that does not survive binocular vision. Count them and there are nine. Look away and the nine were always a different number, or a colonnade seen from a year you have not spent. These larger lives do not move. They weather. Their weather is the continuous fact that everything is above everything else. When the light thins further they become theoretical; when it thickens they crowd closer, or the reverse. They are not interested in being witnessed at human speed. A tick is the sound of one of their decisions finishing—a doorway migrating a finger’s width, a circular plaza confessing it has always been slightly larger. Stay long enough and one of them will offer you a district of your own. This is not hospitality. It is digestion.

Beyond the last countable towers the lives grow less willing to pretend they ever tiled the plane. There the processes run faster or slower; the salt-field is the size of a climate and the civic intent is only a suggestion drawn across it. You can see that country from any high terrace if you do not focus. It looks like more city. It looks like the first city. It looks like the mineral thinking about names it will never need. What lives there has no outline you could walk around. It is the highland’s habit of remaining unfinished and finished at once. It eats the prints you leave, holds them until the next dry front, then returns them to the general pale, which is the only census it keeps.

Stand still. Let a tick happen. The street you came down is still there, and is also a cliff, and is also a colonnade receding into a year you will not spend. Whatever lives here has already included you in the count. The air tastes of nothing. That is the local word for how they breathe.
