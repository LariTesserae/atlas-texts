The first things that live here are the ones that treat the plateau as a weather. They do not remember a mountain and do not require one. In the lamp-housings the mica moths roost with wings folded into exact, repeating facets; when a century-old bulb finally gutters they stir once, drink the last heat, and resettle as if the dark were only another scheduled interval. The jointed goats—too many knees, too few shadows—move along the vertical seams in single file, cropping the copper-green lichen with mouths that leave the metal brighter. They never hurry. A herd will take three days to cross a single plaza, pausing where two walkways fail to meet so the wiry shrubs can seed themselves in the grit they shake from their coats. In the cisterns the puncture-fish school as a single slow thought, rising to the skim of wind or of the other-than-wind, then sinking again; their passage leaves the surface unmarked except for a faint, delayed dimple that the gutters later accept as rain.

These are the plentiful lives, the rust-lives. They occupy every volume the builders overlooked or deliberately left as leak. They do not sing, scrape, or ripple for long. Their patience is the same patience as the idling turbines.

Deeper, in the stacked halls and the galleries that corkscrew through claimed space, other shapes persist. They are the size of people, or of the idea of people after the idea has been poured, bolted, and poured again. Their skins take the enamel light the way glazed brick does—bone-pale, faintly sweating, always a little damp. Breath shows in front of them and does not immediately vanish. They walk the catwalks and the service tunnels with the same seasonal gait as the great presses: a finger-width of progress in a week, a pause that lasts the length of a fog. Some carry tools that have forgotten their names. Some tighten fittings on pipes that carry nothing, or on the air itself. When they open a door at noon it is dusk inside, and they step through without comment, as if the mismatch were only another designed joint.

They are not crowded. Density here is of made things, not of bodies. A foundry’s cooling fins serve as the ceiling of a dormitory; the sleepers (if they sleep) lie among the slow pulse of residual heat and do not dream of older rock. In the rooms that were not on the way you find them sitting with their backs to walls that are also, without metaphor, distant stacks. Their faces are hard to keep. Sometimes they look newly struck, edges still knife-sharp, the grain of yesterday’s forms still visible in the skin. Sometimes they look as if a weld line has already brightened, darkened, and vanished, and the plates of the face remember a different seam. They speak rarely. When they do, the sound arrives late and thin, like a drip finding a tank.

Further in—or further aside—the leaky gasket of the place itself begins to live. Walk long enough and you meet the ones who occupy the same footprint without touching. A worker tightening a fitting on a rafter that is also a cloud; another, identical, performing the same motion a year earlier or later, their shadows falling both down and inward. The inverted plateau in the terrazzo is populated by the same silent herds, the same pale figures, and by others who have never existed above. Moisture trades between the two worlds. A slow drip from one into the other is caught by gutters that have learned, after a while, to schedule it. The inhabitants of the reflection look up (or down) through the white stone and do not seem surprised to see you looking back.

At the newest pours, where a stair still ends in air, you find the ones who are still finishing. They stand at the unweathered drop, watching the edge extend itself by the thickness of a thought. Their hands are dusty with the grit of forms just struck. They do not appear hurried. A new hall can appear in a gap that was always too small; they step into it as if the plateau had only been holding its breath. Some of them have the many joints of the goats. Some have mica in the folds of their clothes. Some are already becoming walkway, becoming insulator, becoming the long pale run of a condensation trough. They are the builders who have not yet finished being builders, or who have finished and begun again.

Everything that lives here lives at the speed of unmaking and remaking. The lichen advances a handspan in a generation. A bolt backs out. A weld forgets itself. The tin birds shift on their perches in the dead loudspeakers and do not sing. Far out, where construction simply stops, the sharpness keeps going—year after quiet year—into a sky that was never asked. The inhabitants watch it, or are it, or will be. They occupy the designed leaks and the undesigned ones alike. They are patient the way rust is patient, the way a press is patient, the way a mountain is patient after it has been replaced with itself. 

They are still pouring.
