The ones who live here have no names that would survive translation into any language that still remembers height. They are the after-image of everything that once needed to climb.

Most of them you will never quite see as individuals. They are the overlapping wet voices that begin before color decides itself and never quite finish. They are the midges that rise and fall in a single shared breath across kilometers of standing water. They are the tadpoles that darken every shallow like living silt, then grow legs so slowly the change feels like a rumor the mud tells itself. They are the wading birds that stand so long they become part of the horizon’s darker line, then unfold a neck and strike with the only suddenness left in the world. Between these, the smooth dark backs that lift and sink in the barely-deeper channels—heavy, unhurried, as if the water itself had decided to remember it once had a spine.

But there is also the one that the others arrange themselves around without ever acknowledging it.

It is not large in the way mountains were large. It is large the way a season is large, or the way the dish of the land is large: by occupying the same patient geometry as everything else. Its body is the color of wet peat and old tannin, slick with the same film that never dries on the last weeping rocks. When it moves—and it does move, though never while you are looking—the sound is that wet unzipping the landscape makes when anything heavier than a reed-raft changes position. It does not walk so much as allow the water to carry it a little farther, the way a very old thought allows itself to be thought again.

Its skin weeps steadily, exactly as the rocks do. Small lives colonize the folds: algae that iridesce, tiny snails the color of tarnished silver, the occasional reed stem that has taken root in a crease and grown as tall as it ever will. The creature does not mind. It has already decided its final, modest shape and will keep it until the sun itself changes. Sometimes a single stunted tree stands in the shallows near it for years, roots splayed, as if the two of them had agreed to keep each other company without ever speaking.

It does not chase. Nothing here does. When a bird strikes, the creature may turn one slow, lidless eye toward the ripple and then forget the ripple. When the frogs reach the particular overlapping density that means the light is about to flatten into white glare, the creature’s throat swells once, a vast internal tide, and adds a note so low it is felt in the soles of the feet more than heard. The note lasts the length of a small geological argument and then recedes.

It has been here since the dust of the last mountains was still being resorted. It remembers, in the way peat remembers, the weight that used to stand above this place. It does not miss the weight. The air is thick enough. The water is ordinary enough. The distances between one living presence and the next are still generous. It waits with the patience of something that has already outlasted every mountain that once tried to stand on it, exhaling water, inhaling water, becoming a little more the same as the land that forgot how to rise.

You can walk for hours and never meet its eye. That is how it prefers the arrangement. The whole region remains one continuous, ankle-deep lake whose surface is broken only by the occasional clump of vegetation, the wakes of smaller things, and the long, almost invisible displacement that means the oldest inhabitant has decided, for reasons of its own, to be a few body-lengths farther on than it was the century before.
