The first things you notice are not individuals but densities: slow tides of dark plates sliding over one another along every fracture, every ledge, every hanging blade of tourmaline. They do not swarm so much as occupy, filling the last unclaimed microns the way cooling magma fills a void. Each body is a flattened lozenge no thicker than a fingernail paring, its carapace a matte composite of the same hematite and greasy talc that make the walls. The surface is not chitin but a living sinter, continually depositing and reabsorbing microscopic mineral films so that the creature and the rock remain chemically continuous. Legs—dozens of them, short, jointed like broken crystal needles—end in rasping pads that both grip and graze. When a pad lifts, it leaves behind a faint, oxidized bloom that the next pad will harvest.

They move without hurry and without pause. Sheets interpenetrate the way two slow rivers of sand interpenetrate: bodies rotate, slide, lock, unlock, never colliding with force, never yielding territory because territory itself is only a temporary alignment of surfaces. The sound of this traffic is the high living static you heard from the entrance, a dry hiss that never quite resolves into separate footsteps. Deeper, where the red pulse of still-plastic rock occasionally travels through a vein, the hiss modulates; the creatures press closer, their pads working faster against the warmer gradient, siphoning the last chemical potential before the rock hardens.

There is no hierarchy, no sentinel, no nest. Reproduction occurs wherever a cooling vent opens a new face. Tiny, paler nymphs—translucent at first, almost glassy—emerge already flattened, already rasping. They darken within hours as they incorporate the first films of metal. The adults do not tend them. The nymphs simply join the nearest sheet and are carried along, their droppings of abrasive dust immediately seized by the growing crystals around them. In this way the population and the mountain thicken together. Over seasons an entire generation may be entombed, their bodies becoming part of the next interlocking plate, their residual heat and chemistry feeding the ones that crawl across their mineralized backs.

Sensory life is vibration and chemistry. They have no eyes. Along the margins of each carapace run rows of pit organs that register the faintest tick of a lengthening crystal or the distant grind of magma. When a slab somewhere below gives way with that dry snap, the entire population stills for a fraction of a second, then resumes at a slightly altered frequency, as though the mountain’s new geometry has been entered into some collective register. Taste—or whatever corresponds to taste—is continuous: every surface is sampled, every gradient mapped by the rasping pads. They do not seek water because there has never been water. They do not seek light because light has never reached this far. What they seek is simply the unfinished work of the rock itself—the slow unmaking and remaking that still leaks energy they can harvest.

Stay long enough and their motion begins to feel like the only possible motion. The heat that lives in the stone becomes indistinguishable from the heat of their bodies. The darkness is no longer dark; it is the medium through which the sheets travel, as solid and occupied as the rock. You realize they are not inhabitants of a cave. They are the first specialized tissues of an organ that has not yet decided it is finished. The mountain continues its vast, patient labor. The creatures continue theirs. Neither notices the difference.
