The pumps keep their own counsel and their own time. They are the oldest continuous presence, not because they were first—the stone was first, and the water that wrote the stone—but because they have forgotten how to stop. Each stroke is a thought the cavern has already thought a million times and still finds worth repeating. Their iron bodies have grown a second skin of scale and rust-lace; valves seat with the wet click of a throat swallowing. They do not live the way a crayfish lives, yet they occupy the same patient grammar: intake, pause, release. Water moves because they move it, and they move because the water has nowhere else to go. In the long dim they have become less machine than organ, the cavern’s slow heart, beating at a rate that would look like stillness if you were in a hurry.

Around them the smaller lives have settled into every leftover interval. The crayfish are the most numerous, and the most matter-of-fact. Pale as boiled bone, they pack the crevices and the perforated walkways and the insides of pipes that no longer carry pressure, only a thin film. They click when the pumps change pitch—not in reply, exactly, more as if the sound had shaken loose a reflex they have forgotten they possess. Their mouths work constantly at the joints, the seams, the places where iron weeps. Whether they clean or merely feed is a distinction the chamber does not bother to keep. After they have been at a leak for an hour the leak is smaller. No one assigned them the work. The water taught them the way it taught the limestone its scallops.

The salamanders move differently, as if they have more time than the crayfish and less purpose. White, pink-gilled, their limbs seeming one too many at every joint, they thread the shallows in processions that begin nowhere and end in the same nowhere. They do not startle. A boot descending beside them produces only a slight, unhurried redistribution, like silt deciding to be elsewhere. They breathe the air that sits on the water and the water that sits on the air; the two media have become, for them, a single lukewarm medium. Sometimes one will rest its chin on a submerged step and remain there for what would be, elsewhere, a season.

The moths keep to the vertical. They roost in the lamp cages and on the undersides of tanks and in the rusted lace of the gantries, wings the exact grey-brown of damp newsprint. When they move it is never one moth: it is a collective shiver that travels the length of a beam and then ceases, as though the beam itself had briefly remembered it was once wood. Their sound is the sound of a page being turned two rooms away. They do not fly so much as relocate, and only when the faint internal light in the western channel thickens enough to be mistaken for a sky.

In that same channel the fish hang vertical, noses just breaking the surface film, as if the film were a text they have been assigned to read for the rest of their lives. They are the colour of weak tea and they are everywhere the water is deep enough to let them forget the bottom. They do not school. They occupy. Dust occupies an unused room the same way.

Something larger keeps the sump. You know it only by the long, slow boil that rises through the black water beneath the pump house, a column of silt that takes the better part of an afternoon to settle, and by the respectful emptiness the crayfish maintain around that particular dark. It does not surface. It does not need to. Its presence is a pressure the rest of the chamber has already adjusted to, the way the walkways have adjusted to the new limestone pillar that grew, over years, from a seep in the north wall. Whatever it is, it is not hungry in any way that would hurry it. It has the same viscosity as the time here.

And then there are the traces that might still be people. Enamel cups hanging on hooks whose nails have become part of the brick. Oilskins stiffened into the exact slump of the last bodies that wore them, as if the wearers had simply evaporated and left their outlines behind. A chalkboard whose last column of numbers is neither fresh nor ancient; it is merely complete. Bootprints in the silt fill with water and become part of the floor’s own small map. Whether anyone still walks those prints is a question the chamber answers the way it answers all questions: by continuing. If there are people they have learned the same courtesy as the moths and the pumps. They do not announce. They do not sing. They keep to the negotiated height of the water and the negotiated dimness of the air. Perhaps they are only the memory of people, worn as smooth as the steps that are no longer steps. Perhaps they are still here, moving at the same rate as everything else, so that the difference between a living hand and a rusted valve has been filed away. The place does not distinguish. It has already arrived at the shape it means to keep, and whatever lives inside that shape—pump, crayfish, salamander, moth, the heavy thing in the sump, the possible last human breath—has been given all the time it needs to become exactly as quiet, as wet, and as finished as the stone itself.
