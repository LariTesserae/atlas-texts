The kettle keeps its own time, and what lives here has learned that time as a kind of weather.

First the green things, which are not so much inhabitants as the climate given flesh. The tree-ferns do not merely stand; they occupy, their unrolling croziers moving at the speed of decision itself, each frond a slow argument with gravity and drip. Moss is the true citizenry—beards, cushions, films, the stuff that has decided the young stone’s sharpness is a personal insult and will not rest until every plane is softened, every fracture line stuffed. In the thermal seams the orchids and their thick-leaved cousins drink the rock’s sweat with the patience of things that have never known drought. Under them, in the deepest shade, the filmy forest lives on nothing but the idea of water, leaves so thin they are almost windows. Nothing here competes. Everything touches. The peat mat underfoot is less ground than a single slow organism, springy, rust-veined, holding the sky in its puddles the way a body holds a fever.

The smaller lives move inside that green weather. Midges stitch the warm air above every seep, not in clouds so much as in changing constellations, born and dying in the same held breath. In the films that never quite become streams, pale amphibians the length of a thumb hang perfectly still, as if the warmth invented them one afternoon and then grew distracted. They are not rare once you stop looking for motion; they are simply the same colour as wet stone and the same temperature as the air. When a sheet of seep lets go and slaps the peat they vanish without a ripple, only to reappear higher, or lower, or in the next lens of water, as though the kettle itself is breathing them from one pocket of moisture to the next. In the peat there are other small wills: things that eat rust and leaf-mould, that tunnel the thickness of a fingernail in a decade, that have never seen a predator larger than a midge. The open side of the kettle is a rumour of elsewhere; the single dark bird that crosses it does not live here. It only reminds the place that flight still exists.

And then there is the other presence, the one that uses the leftover grammar of the engines.

It lives in the turbine housing, or rather the housing has slowly become a chamber of its body. The riveted door hangs open because it has not been closed in longer than memory; ferns have begun to curtain the threshold. Inside, the generator’s long, dying vowel is the sound it wakes to and sleeps under. The air is the same even warmth as the rest of the kettle, only thicker with oil-memory and the faint sweetness of old insulation. A few bulbs still burn the colour of late afternoon; it has not occurred to anyone to turn them off, and so they have become another kind of moss, another constant drip of light. Along the pipes that enter and leave the cliff, hands (or what serve as hands) have learned the exact temperature of every joint, the places where the metal still sings with water, the places where rust has become a second skin. It moves rarely, and when it does the amphitheatre answers with a change in the stacking of sounds—the drip altering its rhythm for a moment, the seep sheets hesitating, the midges lifting as one.

It is not waiting. Waiting implies an elsewhere that might arrive. The mountain is still rising, the stone is still new, the cloud still pours without emptying. The bearing still complains in its own slow language. Whatever lives here has simply stayed long enough that the distinction between inhabitant and place has worn as thin as the filmy leaves. Sometimes, at the lip where the peat ends and the world falls away through white, it stands for the length of one unrolling crozier and looks at the older, colder ridges that have not yet been joined. Then it turns back into the kettle, into the loud wet room the mountain made by accident, and the green closes behind it as if it had never been anything but another seep, another warmth, another thing the young rock weeps into being.
