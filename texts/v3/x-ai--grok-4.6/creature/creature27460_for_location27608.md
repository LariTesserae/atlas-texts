The people who live in the unfinished highland have the look of something the range itself has slowly assembled. They are compact, close-jointed, their bodies built for the same work the masonry does: holding weight without complaint, giving a little when the ground lifts, staying. Faces weather into the same angular planes as the pale rock; the high thin light etches them early and then holds them there, so that a woman of forty and a man of sixty can be told apart only by the particular set of their patience. Eyes are narrowed not from suspicion but from habit, from measuring distances that change, from watching a ridge that was empty become a street. Hands are thick at the base of the thumb, scored with old grit, always a little cold even in the inner rooms. They dress in successive skins of wool and hide and hair-rope until the human outline blurs into the same layered, rounded mass as the wool-beasts they sleep against. Nothing is ornamental except what the lichen dyes allow: a rust band at a cuff, a sulphur stain on a child’s hood, colors that look as if the stone itself has begun to think in pigment.

They move the way the walls stand: without hurry, testing each joint. A step on a stair that has begun to tilt is placed as carefully as a block on its bed. Voices stay close to the mouth; even laughter is a low thing that does not travel far through the density. They speak of water as one speaks of a guest who might yet be persuaded to linger, and of timber as one speaks of the dead: with oil, with names, with the knowledge that it will not come again. Children learn first the names of lichen colors and the particular smells of the different stables, then the feel of a true face of stone, then the long wait that is the real craft. A child can grow from first steps to first full season of masonry between one notable rain and the next. Grief is treated the same way: sat with, oiled, not hurried. They sit on the sunny steps in the middle of the day with the animals pressed around them and watch the next ridge, which is also rooms, which is also still deciding whether to be mountain or city.

The wool-beasts occupy the ground floors of entire districts as if the buildings had been raised around their patience. They are heavy, shaggy, rock-colored themselves, their breath a permanent weather that freezes on lintels and makes the lower rooms smell of damp fleece and slow rumination. Dogs sleep in heaps wherever the sun finds a step, rising only to follow or to warn with a sound that is more shift of weight than bark. On the roofs the small browsers move like living fragments of the masonry: low, sure, almost silent, cropping the lichen crust, leaving their dark pellets in the gutters as another kind of mortar. At certain hours the courtyards fill with wings until the air itself seems to turn; the nests are simply more rooms in the cliff that never ends. At night other feet take the lanes: the pad of things that come down from the unfinished peaks, the click of keratin, the brief gleam of eyes at a leaking cistern. No one counts them. The highland is always more crowded than its rooms, warmer in its passages than the stone should allow, layered in its smells (fleece, dung, bird, people, wet rock) the way the walls are layered in their courses.

They work stone with stone and time. A team will spend a season on a single corner because three unbuilt storeys already lean on it in the mind. Copper is a rumor, a thin edge that came from a valley no one now walking has seen. Most days the only metal is the ringing of pale rock when it is struck, the only mortar the grit they grind between two faces until the faces consent to vanish into each other. When a new scarp rises through a floor they do not rage; they number the blocks and take the rooms down and build them again a little higher, a little differently, the way the range itself is still rising. Maps are stories the oldest tell while sitting in the thin noon light, pointing at a stack of roofs that everyone agrees has always been there. Navigation is by lichen shade, by which courtyard holds the noon longest, by the particular way afternoon cuts a certain untrimmed skyline that was a bare ridge in someone’s youth.

At evening they draw inward. Smoke from dung-fires lies in the lanes like another visiting water. Wings return to the holes. The people close the small inner rooms so that the air a body has already warmed can be used again, and they sleep in the living heat of animals and kin, the stone sweating its thin beads into the old grooves that lead to the cisterns. The highland does not finish. Neither do they. They are the breath the young mountain has learned to hold: damp in the joints, sharp at the edges, quiet, still becoming.
