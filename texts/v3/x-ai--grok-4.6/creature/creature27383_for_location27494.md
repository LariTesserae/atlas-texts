The mussels keep the hours.

They do not know they do. They only open and close by the same chemistry that has always opened and closed them, a slow pulse timed to the pump’s deepening and shallowing, to the faint warming of the overflow thread, to the iron taste that thickens when silt lifts. In the dark they look like nothing, just another rustication along the brick courses and the flanges. In the bottle-green light they become architecture again: rows of dark ovals following the mortar joints more faithfully than the brick ever did, clusters packed into the lee of every stanchion base, a living dentil course under the galleries. Their siphons make a second, finer current, a million small inhalations that never quite still. When the water-hammer comes they clap shut in a single delayed shiver you feel through the water more than hear, then ease open again, already filtering the cloud they themselves helped raise.

Around them the smaller work continues without ceremony. Freshwater sponges fist the corners where current dies, beige and slightly furred, each one a patient lung. Leeches sew themselves to the undersides of the gratings in neat dark stitches; they wait for whatever larger body will pass close enough to be tasted. Crayfish own the tile. They move like thoughts the hall is having about itself—slow, jointed, unhurried until they are not. Their claws click when they startle, a sound like two wet pebbles struck together, and then they are gone into the flour, leaving only the faint rake-marks of their walking. In the still hours, when the pump’s note shallows, they come farther into the open, antennae testing the dimness, as if the floor itself had grown legs and decided to patrol its own silt.

The fish treat the columns as weather. Schools of the small silver ones hold in the lee, bodies aligned so precisely they look like a single hammered sheet that has learned to flex. They turn together when the current strengthens, a bright unfolding and refolding. They tap at anything that hangs still—skin, neoprene, the last honest thickness of a handrail—as if testing whether it, too, might become cover. The larger fish are different creatures entirely: dark, patient, built like the shadows of abandoned tools. They hang under the walkways and inside the sheared throats of pipes, motionless for minutes at a time, only the slow fan of a pectoral fin proving they have not become part of the iron. When the pump asks for more water they leave their cover and work the open volume, sliding through the standing current along the west wall as if it were a road they have always owned. You see them best by what they displace: a sudden thickening of the green, a ribbon of silt that should not have moved.

Eels treat the whole hall as a single long corridor. They use the old conduit runs, the overflow, the black mouths of the collection drains. You never see a whole eel. A tail, thick as a wrist, disappears into a pipe. A head, blunt and incurious, eases out of a grating and withdraws. They are the hall’s only true travelers, passing through on business that has nothing to do with this room and everything to do with the wider dark beyond the brick.

The pale plants are almost an afterthought, a rumor the light keeps trying to tell. They stand in the silt pockets along the east footing, ribbon-like, the color of old ivory left too long in tea. No larger than a hand, rooted in whatever organic dust has settled there over decades. They do not thrive. They persist, which is the only kind of thriving this climate allows green things. The brown-green film that covers every still surface is more successful: it is the hall’s true skin, a living glaze that takes the bottle-glass light and holds it, faintly, so that even the darkest stretches of brick seem to remember daylight.

And then there is the other.

It lives in the flooded stair at the north end, where the used daylight arrives and where the collection drains empty their black water. It is not always there. Sometimes the stair is only water and silt and the slow rotation of a few large crayfish. Sometimes it is occupied. You know before you see: a displacement, a thickening, the particular way the flour lifts in a slow unfolding cloud that has nothing to do with the pump. The thing itself is the color of the water when the water is thinking about becoming dark. It is long, but not eel-long. Thick through the shoulders in a way that suggests it was once something that walked, or at least something that remembered walking. The head is blunt, the eyes small and set wide, filmed with the same biofilm that covers everything else. It does not hunt the way the dark fish hunt. It waits the way the hall itself waits. When it moves it does so with the unhurried certainty of a current that has decided to become flesh for a while. It has been here long enough that the mussels have begun to consider it another stanchion; a few young ones have settled along the ridge of what might be a spine.

It is not a guardian. It is not a ghost. It is simply what the hall grew when it had finished becoming water and still had one shape left to try. The silver schools ignore it. The crayfish give it room the way they give the intake cage room. The eels pass it without comment. Only the lamps seem aware: their small orange filaments halo a little more thickly when it rises into the grate-light, as if the electricity itself recognizes another long-term resident.

Everything here has already arrived at the form it will keep. The mussels will filter until the last lamp dies and then they will filter in the dark. The crayfish will click. The dark fish will hang. The eels will come and go. The pale plants will stand their small ground. And the thing in the stair will wait, becoming a little more itself each year, a little less distinguishable from the water that made it, until even the displacement it causes is only another kind of current, another way the drowned nave has learned to breathe. 

Stay long enough and you understand that nothing here is visiting. The hall does not host life. It is life, expressed as brick and iron and cold groundwater and the bodies that learned to wear those things as casually as the mussels wear the flanges. The climate of drowning has had time enough to finish its work. What lives here is simply what the work required.
