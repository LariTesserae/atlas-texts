The water holds them the way a last breath holds the shape of the lungs that no longer need it. They do not swim so much as they are conducted, bodies tuned to the same slow directional pulse that has forgotten destinations. Pale at first, then not pale at all once the dark has finished claiming them: the small ones move in densities that would choke any other sea, lantern-jaws hinged like unfinished sentences, each mandible a long, translucent scoop that opens and closes without hunger, only the old vascular courtesy of taking what the current has already decided to offer. Their bodies are flattened just enough to slip between the hanging curtains of root, and when a school turns the entire interval between two structures becomes a single breathing lung of flesh, a living weather front that never breaks. Faint constellations flicker along their flanks—bioluminescent punctuation, never a sentence—then vanish before they can be read. They do not see. They listen to the standing roar that the older hollows have been singing since the last leaf forgot how to be a leaf, and they answer in click-trains so rapid they become texture rather than sound, a second current running through the first.

Larger forms occupy the plains of the flattened crowns, those almost-level timber seas the size of vanished cities. They are plated in overlapping shields the color of old ironwood left in brine, each plate worn smooth by the handshake of pressure until they resemble hulls that chose, long ago, to rest keel-up and never leave. Their mouths are not mouths. They are rasps, broad and courteous, that travel the living walls in paths so ancient the wood has grown grooves to receive them. Nothing is torn. The structures have already agreed, in the last metabolic contract the world ever signed, how much of themselves they will yield; the grazers take exactly that and no more, leaving the chambers and the ribbed passageways intact, cathedral and larder in the same slow breath. They move at the speed of memory. When two of them pass, their plates rasp against each other in a low-frequency conversation that travels kilometers, mountain speaking to mountain through the dark. Between them the smaller traffic continues: filter-feeders like living sieves anchored in the hanging stipes, their bodies chambered so the water itself becomes their blood; things that live inside the hollows, long and segmented, the exact color of the compressed xylem, pulsing in time with the roar that has no beginning. These inner ones never emerge. They have become part of the architecture’s own circulation, feeding on the faint warmth that still rises from the peat floor far below, that ancestral tissue still honoring agreements no one remembers making.

Deeper still, where the structures’ roots braid into the compressed peat, there are the ones that do not travel at all. They are neither plant nor fauna anymore. They occupy the exact volume they have always occupied, bodies that grew into the only shapes the water would allow after the light failed. Some are vast, sessile, their surfaces pitted with the same polite rasping scars the grazers leave higher up. Others are colonies of something smaller, a living weather that has chosen to stay, their collective noise adding a harmonic to the standing roar so that the entire place hums in a single held note. Sight is budgeted nowhere. Pressure tells them everything: the approach of a school, the slow passage of a plated hull, the distant rumble of another structure answering across the interval. Temperature never argues. The cool is the same in every chamber, every hollow, every body. Occasional glows appear—along a feeding trail, on the trailing edge of a fluke, in the brief opening of a lantern-jaw—and are swallowed at once, local rumors the dark does not keep.

They do not arrive and they do not leave. Reproduction, if it still occurs, is only the replacement of what was already there, a closed loop so perfect it has forgotten it is a loop. No larva drifts toward a surface that does not exist. No elder sinks to a sediment that was never anything but ancestral tissue. The fauna treat the architecture as both cathedral and larder because those are the only two words left that still fit: reverence without gods, consumption without loss. The currents move through them the way they move through everything else, slow, directional, never arriving anywhere new. The roar continues. The dark continues. The cool continues. What lives here is the final grammar of persistence, held open and sparse and complete, each body a clause in a sentence the world finished speaking long ago and has never needed to revise.
