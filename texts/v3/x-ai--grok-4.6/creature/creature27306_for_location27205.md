The basin keeps its living things the way it keeps its water: in layers that refuse to choose a single register, some decided, some still thinking, all of them occupying the same low country without quite excluding one another. What first presents as emptiness is only the slowness of their metabolism and the patience of their occupancy. They have had the same geologic interval the ridgelines have forgotten, and they have used it to become continuous with the climate rather than merely resident in it.

Begin with the ones that do not move, or that move on cycles longer than a walker’s attention. The mosses are not cover. They are a distributed animal of surfaces, a single thought the stone has been having for so long that it grew chlorophyll and learned to drink vapor before it falls. Each dark cushion on the weeping outcrops is a mouth and a lung at once; it thickens when the unweather sound swells, drinking the extra pressure as if it were a nutrient the air had finally decided to share. The hanging beards are older still. They remember directions the compass never included, and they lean into those memories when the dimness deepens, rearranging themselves by fractions so that the next condensation front will unzip along their lengths in a pattern that is almost, but not quite, the same as last time. They do not compete. They saturate. In the runnels the drowned greens have become a second soil, a living peat that keeps versions of footprints the way the black earth does, except the versions they keep are warmer and slightly offset, as though the step had occurred twice, a breath apart.

The ferns are more argumentative. They shoulder in the dim because they have not yet finished deciding whether they are one organism or a crowd. Their fronds are varnished not by any patient hand but by the constant downgrade of their own exhalations; they breathe out a film that is cooler than the air and slightly more viscous, so that insects (the few that persist) walk on them as on a skin that has not quite agreed to be walked on. When the hanging curtains of water form, the ferns nearest them darken and drink from both sides of the impossibility, their roots extravagant even by the standards of this place, tasting the double-pools as if tasting a thought and its correction at the same instant. They do not die back. They only become more themselves, blacker, wetter, until a whole shoulder of them looks like a single muscle that has been holding a posture since before the last lake finished leaving.

Higher, but never high, the short trees have chosen a different bargain. Their trunks are rumors of height that the basin politely declined. What they possess instead is reach: roots that do not merely anchor but converse with the second ground, sliding through lenses of water that have not yet chosen to be surface or subterranean. The lichens on their bark are not decoration. They are the trees’ way of remaining visible in a light that arrives already used; pale, cleanly so, they store a dim phosphorescence that is only the memory of clearer days, released in tiny increments when the many-throated pressure rises. Stand long enough near one and you will feel the bark’s map shift, very slightly, as if the tree were adjusting its correspondence with a copse that is both an hour off and immediately to your left. The trees do not mind the contradiction. They have lichened over it.

Then there are the ones that travel, though travel here is a negotiation with volumes that occupy the same space twice. In the deeper channels, the pale backs appear and are gone before the eye has finished deciding they were there. They are extensive rather than large, smooth the way wet stone is smooth after centuries of the same drip, and they are mostly an event of displacement. The water they move through remembers them longer than they remain; the interference rings they leave behind do not originate from any thrown stone because they are not caused, exactly. They are the creature agreeing, for a few body-lengths, to be both the swimmer and the swam-through. You will not see a head or a tail that commits to being either. What you see is the suggestion of an animal that has found it cheaper to be continuous with the hydrology than to maintain the usual boundaries. Their rare tracks in the peat are depressions that fill from below as well as above, two skins of wetness slightly out of register, and the peat keeps both versions with equal courtesy.

In the meres, when the sound is at its lowest, the gelatinous clusters assemble. They are the water’s recanted thoughts given temporary bodies: small, lens-like, briefly coherent, the color of the inside of the shell the light already resembles. They do not swim so much as reconsider their position. A cluster will occupy a volume, then occupy it again a hand’s breadth to the side, the two occupations overlapping until the mere cannot decide which skin is the present one. They last only as long as the quiet; when the swell comes they dissolve back into the unfinished thought they came from, leaving the water a fraction more decided than it was. The basin does not mourn them. It has weather for this.

At the loudest part of the cycle, when the moss leans and the standing sheets take on geometries that hold for a few breaths, the peat itself lifts. Not everything that rises from it becomes a shape you can keep. Some of what uses the extra room the thinness provides is only the memory of a gait, a pressure that walks without weight and leaves tracks that persist longer than any creature should. Others are more committed. They have the outline of things that once needed walls or pylons and then discovered they no longer did; they move through the interleaved mist as through rooms whose doors have been forgotten on purpose. You will not see faces that admit a single expression. You will see the suggestion of attention, a darkening of the already-dark air that is not shadow but occupancy, and then the occupancy will be using a different set of distances, the copse that is both far and near becoming, for them, simply near. They do not hunt. They do not flee. They attend the swell the way the rocks attend their weeping: as a geology they have agreed to remain inside.

None of them are new. That is the quietest fact, quieter even than the individual beads releasing after the front has passed. The basin is not hosting survivors of a breakage. It is a completed climate, and what lives in it has finished becoming the sort of life that climate can sustain: cool, dim, crowded with absences that have learned to weigh as much as presence. They drink the used light. They speak in the regional plumbing and in the applause-through-a-wall and in the low pressure that forgot the moon. Between events they resume the version of fullness they can keep—moss saturating, ferns shouldering, pale backs displacing, clusters recanting, peat remembering. The land remains low enough to hold every one of them. The air remains cool enough to make each of them briefly visible, a film on the next thing it touches, before the film becomes landscape again.

If you stay, you will begin to feel counted among them. The condensation will treat your skin as another weeping stone. The second ground will answer your steps as confirmation rather than echo. And when the next swell arrives you may find that some small part of you—some unfinished thought you brought in from clearer country—has decided to stay and grow lichens. The basin will not object. It has been finishing itself long enough to know how to include whatever arrives already used.
