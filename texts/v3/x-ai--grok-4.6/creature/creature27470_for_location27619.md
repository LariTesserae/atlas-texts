The first things that live here are not bodies so much as permissions the wet has granted itself. They begin as film: a thickening of the constant sheet-water on brick, a hesitation in the way a droplet decides to fall. Over hours that film gathers a faint internal grain, like dust deciding to remember it was once stone. By the time it has weight enough to slough free it already knows the pipes. It travels them from the inside, a slow, warm pulse that the lagged iron mistakes for its own condensation. When it emerges at a joint or a weeping flange it does so as a sleeve of living slickness, colourless until it chooses otherwise. In the dim it takes the hue of wet rust; under the rare unshielded arc it flares a brief, sourceless milk, the same milk that sleeps in the deepest channels.

These sleeves do not crawl. They flow against gravity when the mood takes them, climbing the undersides of walkways the way liverwort climbs, leaving behind a brighter wet that dries into a metallic sheen no different from the one already colonising the hottest pipes. Sometimes they pause and open. What opens is not a mouth. It is a pale, waxy spathe, identical to the flowers the ferns produce without pollinators, and it stays open only long enough to drink the steam. Then it seals and the sleeve moves on, a little thicker, a little more certain of its own edges.

They are not many. The basin does not require many. A dozen, perhaps, moving through the entire two hundred paces on a cycle older than the overlapping skins of paint. They tend nothing and repair nothing, yet wherever they have passed the bricks weep a fraction more generously and the algal mats realign themselves into tighter mosaics, as though the water has been reminded of a preferred pattern. The engines do not notice them. The engines notice only load and the slow insistence of the lake that wants to exist. The sleeves notice the engines the way a tide notices the moon: as a rhythm that can be entered or ignored.

Deeper, in the channels that never quite empty, something slower keeps its own counsel. It is not one thing. It is the milkiness itself deciding, on certain nights, to cohere. When the arcs throw their mismatched shadows the milk rises in soft, vertical columns that never break the surface. They stand for minutes at a time, featureless, faintly luminous from within, and the water around them forgets to ripple. If a sleeve of film encounters one of these columns it simply parts, flows around, and reforms on the far side, carrying a new, colder gleam. The columns do not pursue. They wait until the light changes or the pumps cough and then they subside again, leaving the channels looking exactly as they did, except that the next overflow from the cistern tower will taste very slightly of iron that was never in the pipes.

The plants know all of this and do not comment. The giant horsetails continue their impatient uncurling; the aroids continue to mortar themselves into the engine-house walls. Occasionally a frond will dip lower than the water level requires and stay there, dripping in a rhythm that matches no rain and no valve. That is as close as the vegetation comes to acknowledgement. It has already edited animals out of the equation; these newer tenancies are simply further edits, quieter still.

On the barracks verandah, where the pilings lift the brick just clear of the highest seepage, there are impressions in the wet wood that might once have been footprints. They fill and empty with the same unhurried tide that rearranges the gravel beds. No one has stood in them for a very long time, yet the impressions remain sharp, as though the wood itself refuses to forget the weight. Sometimes, in the brown dusk that passes for noon, a sleeve of film will occupy one of those hollows and hold the shape of a foot for an hour, then relinquish it. The shape is always slightly too long, the toes slightly too undifferentiated. It is never quite human and never quite anything else. The barracks does not mind. It has been sweating long enough to understand that occupancy is a temporary courtesy the wet extends and then withdraws.

What lives here, then, is the wet’s own hesitation made mobile, the industry’s leftover heat given a slow will, the plants’ indifference given occasional form. Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing sings except the valves and the sudden local downpours. The inhabitants do not name themselves. They do not need to. They are the place continuing, the equilibrium acquiring the smallest possible extra motion so that it may remain equilibrium a little longer. When the next cycle begins—the water rising, the steam fattening, the croziers forcing themselves into air that is already too full—they will still be here, a little more themselves than before, a little less distinguishable from the curtains of water that never stop arriving.
