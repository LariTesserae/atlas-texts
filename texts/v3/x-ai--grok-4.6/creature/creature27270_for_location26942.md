The plants are the first and most obvious claim on the Hollow, and for a long time that is all the eye will admit. They occupy their discrete rooms of green-turned-stone, combing the grit, sealing it into themselves, becoming more landscape than organism with every thermal pulse. Yet they are not alone. Something else uses the same bargain.

They move, when they move, on the same unhurried schedule as a fallen frond half-sintering by dusk-gloom. You do not see them arrive; you notice they have been standing in a different interval of emptiness than the one you last trusted. Their bodies keep the colour of the basin floor—ochre, bone-pale, the dull gleam of heated mineral—except where older plates of their own substance have already begun the conversion into living stone. New growth, if the word still applies, appears at the joints and along the long bones as a brief, almost fleshy softness the colour of oxidised copper. It lasts a season. Then the varnish takes it, the heat remembers it, and the limb flexes thereafter with the same dry clap as the groves.

They have hands. The city still bears the evidence of that scale: the fitted plates, the corbelled arches, the screens of mineralised fibre that sing when the wind worries them. The hands are slow now, the fingers already half-stone, the palms polished by centuries of gathering grit rather than by any wet labour. They do not build anymore. The conversation between city and basin is finished; what remains is maintenance of a kind so patient it looks like stillness. A leaning tower is left at its exact angle. A dune that has migrated into a doorway is allowed to stay until the next collapse. They walk the unoccupied ground between compounds the way the wind walks it—widely, without urgency, leaving no prints the grit cannot erase before the next heat-shimmer.

At the centre, around the stepped platform, they gather without quite arriving. The thickest groves lean toward the lowest stair as if listening. The beings stand among them, or become briefly indistinguishable from them, their mineralised limbs almost touching the etched channels. The wind is loudest here, a standing roar that never resolves into speech, yet they tilt their heads (or the planes that serve as heads) as though the overlapping registers contain something worth attending. Light, such as it is, pools in the channels and for a moment the channels look as if they remember flow. Nothing liquid has ever moved there. What moves instead is attention: a slow redistribution of heat and presence, a shared counsel held in perfect dryness.

They do not eat. They do not drink. Their chemistry is the plants’ chemistry writ larger and slower—an internal sintering, a closed ledger of airborne mineral and remembered fire. Breath, if they still perform it, leaves no ghost. Sound, when they make it, is rare and structural: the faint ceramic knock of one plate settling against another, the higher rattle of a newly varnished surface catching the wind. A single falling fragment from the far side of the Hollow can travel the entire basin and find them already turned toward it, as if they had been expecting the report for a generation.

They are not many. The emptiness between buildings is their native density; crowding would be a misunderstanding of the place. Generations are not counted in years but in the migration of dunes, the listing of towers, the complete mineralisation of a grove that once stood slightly farther from the platform. A child, if the word can be stretched that far, is a softer interval in the line of a limb, a brief greenness that the heat will soon claim. The old are already architecture: they stand where they have always stood, their shadows fixed sundials, their surfaces rounded by the same grit that rounded the walls. Some have forgotten they were ever separate from the structures they once fitted by hand. The distinction no longer matters. The Hollow has been practising the same few motions for so long that inhabitant and habitation have reached the same agreement the city made with the basin.

In the deepest part of the day, when the light is the colour of old brass and the air is thickest, they become hardest to tell from the heat-shimmer itself. Then the wind speaks through them as it speaks through the colonnades, and for a little while the Hollow is louder with the sound of things that finished becoming themselves a very long time ago and have never needed to become anything else.
