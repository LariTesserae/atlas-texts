The lizards are first and last, the ones that make the density visible. They occupy the flutes of the basalt and the sanded-down stacks as if the stone had grown a second, movable skin. Their bodies are the color of cooled slag and old pipe-scale, ridged in the same vertical grammar as the columns so that a whole hoodoo can appear, from a little distance, to be nothing but stacked hides. When a shadow crosses they slough. It is not flight. It is a collective rearrangement, a face of rock deciding to become another face. They do not scatter far. The next flute, the next vent-lip, the next patch of sinter that still holds a little of the morning’s condensation. They drink by pressing their throats to the metal sweat at first light; they feed on the mica-winged insects that rise in brief, dry glitter from the vent-edge plants. Their blood is already the temperature of the ground. They do not bask so much as remember.

The insects themselves are almost not there until the light hits them. Wings like thin plates of mica, bodies the exact dull of rusted screen. They live in the wet breath of the vents and on the plants that are more crust than leaf, the ones that occupy the mouths of old valves and grow a rind of the same white mineral that coats the pipes. Their flight makes no sound the thin air will carry. It is only a shifting of sparks at the edge of vision. They pulse with the vents: a sudden lift when the steam speaks, a settling when it folds back. Nothing eats them in numbers large enough to matter except the lizards and, at dusk, the birds.

The birds keep to the upper courses of the dead stacks, the ones that still stand forty meters and have not yet been fully rounded into hoodoos. Pale, almost the color of the steam itself. They do not call. The air is too thin and they have no use for announcing. At dusk they drop in a single motion, a handful of ash thrown down the face of the plateau, and feed on whatever the day’s heat has stunned into stillness—lizards too slow to slough, insects that mistimed a vent, the occasional small mammal that misjudged a seam. They nest in the fluting the wind has made of old brick and boiler plate. Their droppings add another thin layer of white to the sinter. They keep the same hours as the pressure below: wait, pulse, wait.

In the deeper seams, where the packed stone leaves just enough room for a body that is not vertical, something larger conducts itself. You see it mostly as interruption: the heat-shimmer of a flank sliding between two collapsed dikes, the wet print of a broad pad in the mineral mud of a basin no wider than a room, a scatter of bones arranged with the neatness of long habit—not scattered, not gnawed in frenzy, but placed, as if the eater and the eaten had agreed on the geometry. It is the size of a small mule or a large dog that has learned to be horizontal. Its coat, when the light catches it, is the same bone-and-rust as the boiler housings. It drinks from the tea-colored skins of water that shudder when a nearby vent speaks. It hunts the lizards when they are stacked too thick, the birds when they drop, anything that has been slowed by the ground’s stored heat. It does not announce. Its hours are the vents’ hours. Pulse. Wait. Pulse. The bones it leaves are already half-sintered by morning.

There are no people. The last shift ended long enough ago that the huts have become only paler rectangles of dust, a geometry the plateau remembers without needing the original tenants. The cisterns still catch the night’s thin gift and the metal still sweats at dawn, but whatever hands once tended them have gone into the same blending that turned smokestacks into organ-pipes and girders into dikes. What lives here now has no second story, no canopy, no use for a name that would have to be carried in thin air. The plateau has already arrived at the temperature and the density it can keep. The lives that remain simply occupy the seams, pulse when the mountain pulses, and leave the rest to the wind and the slow rounding of every upright thing.
