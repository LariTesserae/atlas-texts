The first lives that take the basin do not wait for soil. They wait for the moment the rock is still wet enough to remember being liquid. The salamanders arrive in that window, translucent, forearm-long, their bodies more membrane than meat. Each one is a slow argument against finish. Their skins throw oil-slick rainbows only where the stone is newest, as if the light itself has not yet decided how to leave them. They haul themselves across glassy black planes and pale granular ones with the same deliberate gait, pausing whenever a new fracture plane shears into existence. The report of tearing stone does not startle them. They lean into it, pressing their pale undersides to the fresh surface, and the mucus they leave behind smokes and then hardens into a film that the next water sheet will dissolve or incorporate. They are mapping the mountain’s undecided edges with their own secretions, laying down a temporary cartography that lasts only until the next lift or sag.

Their migrations are not random. They move toward warmth, toward the amber seepage, toward the places where the thrum is deepest. When a terrace begins to rise they turn as one gleaming front, a slow silver flood of bodies. They do not compete. They share the same unfinished grammar: the knowledge that the ground beneath them is still being spoken into place. Sometimes two of them meet at a seam that has just closed. They press their heads together and remain that way, motionless, while the rock around them continues its minute adjustments. When they separate, a faint ridge of crystallized mucus remains, a small, accidental sculpture that the pillars later treat as data.

Above them the insect-analogues live in two times at once. The grazing rivers never stop. They are dense, coordinated, almost liquid themselves, feeding on the mineral films that precipitate and vanish by the hour. Their mouths are not mouths but vibrating fields that loosen the newest crystals without ever quite taking them. They leave the older, already-weathered surfaces untouched. The aerial swarms are the same creatures in a different mood: sudden, tearing-silk hiss of wings, bodies so light they are almost only the sound they make. They rise when the mist thickens, drop when a new vent hisses steam. Their wings click against one another in patterns that almost resolve into numbers, then dissolve again. They do not build. They taste the air for the next change and then become part of the change.

The flyers are larger, half-lost, membrane-and-frame things that fold and unfold with dry, architectural clicks. They occupy the mist the way the salamanders occupy the stone: as if the unfinished medium were the only one they trust. Their voices never become speech. They remain whistles, wet slaps, overlapping clacks that fold back from the opposite rim three times, each echo lower, as though the basin were chewing the sound and finding it not yet ready. Sometimes a flyer will land on one of the three low pillars and remain there while new material extrudes beneath its feet. It does not feed. It simply rides the growth for a while, clicking more slowly, as if listening to the timetable that has nothing to do with ordinary tectonics. When it lifts off again the fresh layer it rode has already begun to look indistinguishable from the old, except for that brief inner brightness that fades like a held breath.

In the deepest, warmest basins something heavier lives. It is known mostly by what it displaces. A sudden rise of water, a ridged silhouette that appears and is gone, a change in the thrum that feels like a body turning over in sleep. It does not surface. It does not need to. The water itself seems to remember its shape: concentric ripples that linger too long, a warmth that does not match the surrounding stone. The salamanders give it a wide, respectful berth. The flyers drop lower when it moves, their clicking denser, almost anxious. Whatever it is, it treats the unfinished state as a kind of nest. It is waiting, or it is the thing being waited for. Its presence makes the basin feel slightly more decided, as if the mountain were practicing the idea of having a heart.

None of these lives are the oldest. The oldest things here are the ones that do not announce themselves as lives at all. The partial arch of matte metal stands alone, the missing half implied by a curvature in the mist where raindrops hesitate. It is not a ruin. It is a sentence still being written. Three pillars extrude new material from their crowns at a visible crawl, fresh layers indistinguishable from the old except for that inner brightness. The shallow dish throws its ghostly inverted relief: the basin as it will be after the next adjustment, or as it was before the last one. These objects continue the work. They do not belong to anyone who has left. They belong to the process that is still arriving.

And yet something uses them. Something that is not the salamanders, not the flyers, not the heavy presence in the warm basins. It is known the way the heavy thing is known: by displacement, by a change in the quality of the unfinished. Sometimes the mist thickens in a way that is not weather. Sometimes the thrum deepens by a note that feels chosen. Sometimes a new fracture plane shears into existence exactly where the dish predicted it would, and the air afterward tastes faintly of ozone and intention. Whatever lives at that level does not walk the raw floor. It occupies the timetable itself. It lives in the decision of when a terrace will rise, when a seam will close, when the mountain will allow itself another fraction of finished profile. It is not a single image. It is the pressure that keeps the grammar unfinished just long enough for the other lives to occupy it.

The creatures that appear to have been waiting for precisely this degree of incompletion are not only the visible ones. They are also the ones that move through the objects, or as the objects, or in the intervals the objects create. They do not settle. They cannot. The basin is still deciding its own shoulder, and they are the ones who prefer a world that has not yet chosen its final verbs. When you stand at the rim and look in, what you see first are the gleaming fronts of salamanders, the hissing rivers of insect-analogues, the folding frames of the flyers. Look longer and the heavier silhouette turns in its warm water. Look longer still and the artifacts continue their quiet labor, and the mist takes on a shape that is almost a curvature, almost a hesitation, almost a presence that has been here since before the first plane sheared into existence and will remain after the last one has decided to stay.

They live here because the place has not yet become a place. They live in the arriving.
