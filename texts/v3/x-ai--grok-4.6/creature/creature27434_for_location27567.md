The first lives one notices, if one stays long enough for the eyes to accept the brown dusk as light, are the small ones. They occupy the clearer pools the way the mineral occupies everything else: completely, without surplus. Pale crustaceans no thicker than a thumbnail drift or cling, their carapaces the exact milky translucence of new calcite. They turn with the same unhurried regularity as the drips, feeding on films of bacteria that themselves feed on the faint sulfur that never quite arrives. A thumb-sized thing will sometimes roll over in a deeper pocket and send up a single dimple that the surface immediately forgets. There are others still smaller, thread-like, living inside the condensing breath itself, appearing only as a momentary thickening of vapor before they settle again onto stone.

These are not interruptions. They are the place continuing.

Stay longer—hours that the basin does not count—and other shapes begin to separate from the glistening. In the narrow channels between cauliflower bosses, pale amphibians lie half-buried in warm silt, their skins the same wet sheen as the flowstone draperies that lean over them. They do not hunt so much as wait for the crustaceans to pass within the slow radius of a tongue. Their eyes are small, dark, and almost unused; they have not needed to look far in a very long time. When one does move, the motion is a single liquid shift, like water deciding to occupy a new hollow, and then stillness again. Their lives are measured in the same intervals as the falling drips.

Deeper still, in the few pockets that stay body-warm even when a visitor’s ankles have gone numb with the sameness, there are slower things. They are longer than a forearm, flattened, the color of old bone left in mineral water until it has forgotten it was ever anything else. They do not swim. They ease. Over seasons that the basin does not acknowledge they reposition themselves by the width of a hand, always toward the hottest seep, always leaving the silt behind them as undisturbed as if they had never been. Their presence is known mostly by the absence they create: a slightly clearer channel, a ledge that stays inexplicably free of new crust. They have no need to eat often. What they take they take so completely that nothing is left to mark the taking.

And then there are the ones who placed the ladle.

They are not many. Three, perhaps four, though it is difficult to be certain because they occupy the same visual register as the formations themselves. Their skins have taken on the perpetual wet gleam; mineral has begun, very slowly, to cede itself to them in thin veils across shoulders and thighs. They sit or recline in the triangulated spaces the old stones still hint at, or on the half-swallowed steps, or simply against a wall where the weeping is gentlest. They do not speak. Speech would be an event, and events have been set aside. When one of them moves it is with the same deliberation as the amphibians, a single considered relocation that may take the better part of what elsewhere would be called a day. A hand extends. The ladle, which has grown almost as mineral as the ledge it rests on, is lifted, dipped into a pool whose temperature matches the inside of a mouth, and set down again. The water is not drunk so much as allowed to become part of the one who holds it. Then the hand returns to stillness.

They were not always like this. The steps, the three stones, the ladle itself are the last evidence of a time when they still shaped things. That time ended so gradually that no one among them marks the ending. They simply became the equilibrium. Their breath is the condensing air. Their pulse is the interval between drips. They do not age in any way that would require the basin to notice. They have not left because leaving would be a change, and the place does not change. They have not died because dying, too, would be an event. They remain, glistening, occupying the spaces the mineral has not yet claimed, as finished as the sentence the basin itself has become.

Nothing larger lives here. Nothing smaller is missing. The crustaceans turn. The amphibians wait. The long pale shapes ease another hand’s-width toward heat. The still figures lift the ladle or do not. The walls weep. The air condenses. The mineral bodies press close. Life here is only another way the place has of remaining exactly itself.
