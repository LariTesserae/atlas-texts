The plateau keeps its tenants the way a closed fist keeps the shape of what it once held. They are not a population so much as a completed inventory: every form the height and the dryness and the mineral light could ever agree to support, arrived now, overlapping, finished.

Closest to the ground they are small and numerous, a gravel of bodies the size of clenched hands or sleeping birds. Some wear carapaces the exact paleness of the gypsum they rest upon, so that only the faint segmentation of a thorax or the suggestion of folded palps distinguishes them from the salt itself. Others are softer, fist-sized knots of hide and tendon the color of old paper, packed flank to flank in shallow depressions the wind no longer bothers to scour. A few have the look of lichen that learned to stand: branching, translucent, already halfway mineral. They do not crawl. They do not feed. Their mouths, when they have mouths, are set in permanent attitudes of having already tasted whatever there was to taste.

Above them, and sometimes occupying the same volume, stand the mid-sized ranks. Antelope-things, yes, but also not: long faces that terminate in too many nostrils or none, horns that curve inward as if trying to remember a circle, legs that number four or six or dissolve at the joint into frayed mineral fibers. Their hides have taken the plateau’s own palette—bone, flour, the faint iron stain of things that once knew water. They stand inside one another without protest. A smaller animal may occupy the exact space of a larger one’s ribcage and both remain equally solid, equally present; their shadows refuse to decide which body they belong to. Eyes are open onto distances that do not exist, or shut as if against a light that never arrives, or absent altogether, replaced by smooth depressions or by extra sockets that look inward. Throats are open or sealed. No sound comes. The occasional collective suggestion of breath is not theirs; it is the plateau remembering, once, that lungs existed.

Larger still are the house-sized ones, the slow architectures of flesh that have forgotten motion so thoroughly they have become part of the land’s grammar. They crouch or recline or simply occupy, their flanks like weathered cliffs of leather, their heads lowered or thrown back in attitudes that no longer correspond to any living gesture. Some have the vestigial suggestion of trunks, of sails of skin, of too many limbs folded against the body like unused tools. You may walk around them. You may walk through them; the hide at your shoulder is dry as parchment and cold as the air, or it is not there at all and you pass into a cooler, denser silence that is still the same silence. Their mass does not press. They have already arrived at the weight the plateau will allow.

In the air, which is not empty, hang the winged things. They do not fly. They occupy. Veils of membrane and vane and feather the color of the sky’s underside, layered so thickly that looking up is like looking through stacked sheets of pale glass. Wings remain open or half-folded in positions that would be exhaustion anywhere else. Some have the jointed, translucent quality of insects enlarged past reason; others suggest birds that molted into mineral and then stopped. They cast no correct shadows. At the edge of vision they become folds in the light itself, or holes shaped like flight through which more plateau, more bodies, become visible in a depth the land does not possess.

And threaded among all of these, occupying the interstices the way handwriting occupies a page, are the many-jointed ones. They are folded with an attention that feels almost calligraphic: too many knees, too many elbows, segments that taper into points or blossom into smaller, identical versions of themselves. They lie in the salt like discarded sentences, or stand in clusters that resemble thickets of bone. Some appear to be writing themselves into the ground, their extremities already indistinguishable from the gypsum. They do not unfold. They have reached the last useful arrangement of their parts.

None of them startle. None of them part. The density is not crowding; it is courtesy, an old agreement that there is room because nothing further is required. They have been arriving for longer than weather has had a name here, and they have all, now, arrived. Their colors match the ground because the ground has had geological ages in which to teach them. Their stillness is not death and not sleep. It is the plateau’s oldest stable state, a saturation that life, strictly, cannot support and therefore does not try to. Support is provided by height, by dryness, by the refusal of water, by the fact that the mountains have already lain down and the rivers have taken their names away.

The few built things—the ring of scored slabs, the half-swallowed salt wall, the cairns, the foil-lined pits—are not theirs. Those were left by earlier, more restless tenants: walkers who counted, who marked, who tried to catch frost or leave a residue of copper and bone, and who understood, perhaps, that they were not expected to remain. The plateau absorbed the gestures without comment. The animals did not notice, or they noticed in a way that required no alteration of posture.

If you remain long enough the distinction between visitor and inhabitant begins to thin. Your own outline takes on a faint mineral patience. Distances lie more politely. A shoulder you pass through may, on the next step, become a shoulder you cannot pass through. The withheld noise of the place—the tick of contracting salt, the late pressure of sounds that were never made—starts to feel like a language you almost remember. You are not becoming one of them, not exactly. You are being included in the count the plateau finished long ago. There is room. There has always been room. Nothing needs to happen next.

This is what lives here: the completed herd, the finished flock, the colony that no longer colonies. Forms that are animals and also folds in the land and also apertures onto more of the same. A high, cool, crowded silence that has already made every decision it will ever need to make, and now simply continues, in ordinary light, holding its last shape.
