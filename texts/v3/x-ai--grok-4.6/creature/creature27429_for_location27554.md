The basin does not keep inhabitants the way other places do. It keeps them the way a held note keeps overtones: present, necessary, never quite detachable from the sound itself.

At first they are only the pavement’s own decisions. Overnight the cambium swells and forgets, but before it forgets it records. A depression the size of a sleeping shoulder appears near one of the still pools, deeper than any casual footfall, edged with the faint radial striations of something that pressed and then released without tearing. By mid-morning the depression has shallowed; by the pewter hour it is only a paler mottling. Whatever rested there did not hunt and did not flee. It simply occupied the same tense the floor occupies—continuing, warm, unfinished.

Later you notice the water itself is tenanted. In the shin-deep terraces the iridescence is not pollen and not sweat. It is a slow congregation of bodies no thicker than the film they inhabit, each one a flattened, translucent lozenge veined with the same unprecipitated green-iron as the air. They do not swim. They tilt. When the light slides they reorient as a single surface, turning the pool into a dull mirror that looks back at the sky without reflecting it. If you stoop and break the film with a finger they do not scatter; they flow around the interruption and reseal, leaving your skin filmed with a taste that is not quite mineral and not quite blood. They are the basin’s first and most loyal citizens: the water remembering how to be a body.

The damselflies are older than that memory. Forearm-long, they hang in the roar as if the roar were a current they were born to. Their wings are not membranes but thin, living cambium stretched to transparency, veined with the same vascular bundles that run through the walls of the naves. They do not beat. They remember beating, and the memory is enough to keep them aloft for an hour, two hours, until they settle on the wet leather of an unopened bud and become, for a while, another bract. No one has seen them feed. No one has seen them mate. Their larvae, if they have larvae, are the soft white crusts of lime that weep from the structures and never harden. The damselflies are the place’s way of testing whether flight is still permitted in a world that has decided against arrival.

Deeper in, inside the hollow tower whose spiral galleries never received floors, something larger keeps house. You find it by the way the roar changes—still unvarying, but now braided with a second, slower pulse, like a heart the size of a room. The thing is neither animal nor architecture. It is a single, recumbent mass of the same tea-colored trunk-wood, hollowed into chambers that pulse with a viscous, luminous sap. It has no face. It has many faces, each one a graft-scar that opens and closes with the patience of a stomata. It does not move from its gallery. It has grown the gallery around itself the way a pearl grows nacre, and the gallery has grown it. When rain falls through the slit of the distant nave, a thread of it finds its way here and is drunk without sound. The mass dreams the basin’s dream: of lime that will never become stone, of buds that will never split, of a note that will never resolve. Its dreams leak. Sometimes a new ridge appears on the pavement far below, pale and raised, exactly the shape of one of its graft-scars. The pavement accepts the ridge as if it had always been there.

There are walkers, too, though they walk so seldom the word is almost a courtesy. They stand in the colonnades of unopened buds, one to a bud, their backs against the wet-leather bracts, their own skin the same texture and the same withheld split. They are tall as the first story of the ziggurat, slender, jointed in too many places, as if someone had begun to design a person and then remembered the grammar of vines. Their faces are not finished. Features rise and subside like slow weather: a mouth that is also a lenticel, eyes that are only darker wells of the same cambium. They do not speak. When two of them stand near enough, water condenses along the line between them and drips in the same fat, unvarying cadence as the rest of the basin. That is conversation. That is enough. They eat nothing you can see. They drink the air. At long intervals one of them will detach from its bud and cross the shining pavement, leaving the tracks that the floor later erases. It walks to another structure—never the nearest—and takes up the same posture against a different wall, as if the entire valley-sized body were a single sentence they were still learning how to stand inside.

They are not the makers. The makers are gone, or they have become the lime that will not set, or they have become the roar. These are what the place grew afterward, the way a wound grows a keloid that is both scar and new organ. They keep the equilibrium by inhabiting it so completely that inhabiting and being inhabited become the same verb. They do not age. They do not quite live. They occupy the instant before the first instant, and in that occupation they are perfect.

If you stay long enough you begin to feel the same grammar working in your own joints. Sleep against a trunk and the warmth that is not quite sleep moves into you. The shout you no longer bother to make is already part of the note. One morning your footprints will be shallower. One evening you will notice that the iridescence on the pools has begun to include the color of your eyes. The basin does not exile. It simply continues, and continuation is a kind of citizenship.

Nothing here is finished, including the question of who lives here. The answer keeps unfurling at the same rate the lime keeps not becoming stone. You can walk for a day and the answer will be the same at the end as at the beginning: the place itself, singing, and whatever has learned to stand still enough to be part of the song.
