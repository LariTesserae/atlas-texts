The moss is the first that will have you. It does not wait for soil that has not yet been invented. It thickens on the sinter while the sinter is still deciding its own hardness, fat succulent pads the exact oxidized green of old copper left in rain, always beaded, always giving off that faint mineral sweetness when the heat finds them. They are not quite plants. Press one and it does not bruise so much as remember a different pressure; the pad darkens, then slowly re-inflates as if drinking the bruise back into itself. At night they tighten into tight, almost metallic knots and make a sound like sugar caramelizing a long way off. In the morning they have advanced another finger-width across black stone that was bare yesterday. They climb the old wall without haste and without apology. They are already halfway up the third course. They will have the dish eventually.

The ferns keep more distance. Black-stemmed, fronds so finely divided they look like held smoke, they occupy the cooler spills of air that sometimes come over the rim and then die. They grow in discrete clumps, never a carpet, as if each clump were a single decision rather than a population. When a pillar’s veins brighten they lift a fraction, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice. They do not face the sun. Nothing here that has a choice faces the sun.

The shrubs are rarer still. No taller than a standing person, they hold clusters of white, waxy flowers that remain closed through ordinary daylight. Only when the copper light in the nearest pillar cycles up do the petals unclench, and they unclench toward the machine, not the sky. The scent is almost not a scent: iron, rain, and something like the memory of electricity. Insects, if there are insects, do not come. The flowers do not seem to require them.

These are the sessile ones. They live on the timetable of the mineral lace and the weeping walls. They are busy becoming the place rather than occupying it.

Then there are the walkers.

The one you saw coming down the inner face of the rim is not unique, only the one that chose that particular hour. They are long, jointed, the precise color of wet basalt so that at rest they vanish against the stone until a limb unfolds in the wrong place. Too many limbs, and they do not all seem to agree on when to touch. Some hover a finger’s width above the surface as if testing a temperature only they can feel. The head is mostly mouthparts arranged around a cluster of heat-pits that glow a dull, internal ochre. They drink, or appear to drink, from the hotter pools, lowering that assemblage until the water dimples without quite breaking. Then they climb back up faces that have no holds a human hand would trust, limbs finding purchase in the grain of the rock itself. They do not look at you. Their timetable does not include you.

There are smaller ones, rarely seen: fist-sized, more compact, the same wet-basalt sheen, that move through the moss mats leaving temporary dark trails that the moss reseals within minutes. And once, in the deep shaft that shows the other sky, something pale and longer than it should be drifted past the limit of visibility and did not return. It had too many planes. It did not disturb the reflection of the two small moons or the pale ring seen edge-on.

The pillars themselves are not empty.

They sweat, they cycle, they fold moisture out of a sky that is not this one, but they are also inhabited. Not by operators in any sense a surveyor would recognize. Inside the smoked translucence, when the copper veins are at their dimmest, you can sometimes see slower, thicker movements: densities that gather and then disperse, like thoughts trying to become mass. The air immediately around each pillar is cooler and wetter because something inside is exhaling a private weather. People who have touched them at the bright phase lose skin as if the surface had simply finished with the idea of being attached. The pillars do not attack. They edit.

Whatever lives in them is the same stuff as the dish in the old shelter, the same smoked material filmed with that iron-and-rain salt. Someone stayed long enough to leave the dish dry. The wall is older than the mountain’s current mood; the patience in its fitted courses is not the patience of last season’s basalt. Perhaps they were the first to notice the notch, or the last to leave before the third pillar finished arriving. The moss is already arguing with their work.

At night the shafts knock, once, softly, as if something on the far side of the other sky were testing whether the door still remembered how to be a door. The reflection does not ripple with this world’s clouds. It has its own weather, and sometimes a pale smear moves across it that is not a cloud.

What lives here does not advertise because advertising belongs to places that have finished becoming. The bowl is still arriving. Tomorrow there may be a new fracture, a fourth pillar or the ruin of one, a rill that climbs higher before it remembers gravity. The walkers will adjust their routes by a body-length. The moss will take another course of the old wall. The flowers will open toward whatever new copper light appears. The thing in the shaft will knock again, or not.

They are not waiting for you. They are not hiding from you. They are simply on a clock whose hours are the brightening of veins, the advance of sinter, the slow persuasion of water into shapes it has not yet agreed to keep. You can stand among the ferns and the rings of impossible water and sweat in the quiet while the air condenses on stone that still holds the grain of its breaking, and they will continue. The mountain does not require witnesses. Neither do they.

If you stay until the residual copper is the only light and the mineral growth is the loudest sound, you may feel, just once, that the timetable has noticed the interruption of your blood. It will not change anything. In the morning the moss will have advanced, a rill will have rewritten its bed by a finger’s width, and the walkers will come down the rim again, or not, according to a schedule that was already running before the notch existed. The bowl is small enough to know in an afternoon and strange enough that an afternoon is not a measurement it accepts. What lives here is the becoming itself, wearing whatever shapes the heat and the other sky and the unfinished stone will currently allow.
