The Terrace holds its lives the way old basalt holds heat: without display, without release, simply because nothing else remains to hold them. What first declares itself is not a body but a density of occupancy, a sense that every surface has already been claimed by something that refuses to hurry.

The insects come before the eye can decide they are insects. Their rasping is the sound of the succulents themselves thinking, a dry seed-rattle that never quite resolves into individuals. Then the lizards: they are the masonry’s second skin, the same grey-green and dust-bronze as the walls they stream across. They do not startle. They merely change which part of the stone is moving. Their circuits have worn shallow troughs in lintels and thresholds; a child growing up here learns to step around those troughs the way another child learns stairs. The birds are smaller still, fist-sized, metallic, nesting so deep in the interpenetrating roofs that their chatter seems to come from the rock’s own throat. When the wind takes their voices it does not carry them away; it braids them into its own long note until the distinction between animal and weather dissolves.

The plants occupy more completely than any animal. They have grown into one another until the distinction between individual and thicket is only a habit of speech. Cushion upon cushion, succulent overlapping succulent, they form a single living pavement whose leaves are almost mineral, thick and matte, the colour of tarnished bronze left too long in a drawer. They drink a film so faint it is nearer chemistry than water. Once a decade they add a ring of tissue. In that same span a wall may slump a finger’s width and a family will simply re-stack the fallen stones and continue living in the new angle. The plants and the houses have begun to share outlines: a roof becomes a garden becomes another family’s floor. Paths are not cut so much as permitted, narrow tunnels roofed by living mats that brush the shoulders of anyone who passes.

And then the people, who have lived here long enough that the distinction between them and the Terrace is also only a habit of speech.

They are not numerous. The disc could hold perhaps three dozen hearths if every room were counted, and some rooms have been hearths for longer than the oldest stories remember. They move the way erosion moves: slowly, with an economy that looks like reverence until you realise it is simply the only speed left that does not waste what cannot be replaced. Their gestures are small. A hand lifts, pauses, settles on a succulent’s crown as if checking a pulse. A head turns toward the rim and stays turned, listening to the wind’s unending note the way another people might listen to a river. Voices travel in the thin air like pebbles dropped into a cistern: they do not raise them. They have found the exact pitch that threads the wind rather than fighting it.

Skin takes the colour of the stone and the dust together, a living bronze that darkens in the creases and lightens across the planes of cheek and forehead. Hair is wiry, the same grey-green as the bunchgrasses gone to seed, often left long and then simply tucked or bound with a twist of the same grass. Eyes are the colour of the haze itself, so that looking into them is like looking into the drop at the rim: something is there, but it recedes through ochre veils. They dress in what the Terrace itself offers: woven mats of the living carpet, stiffened and layered until they hold a shape, or simply the dust itself, which they allow to cake and then score with mineral lines that echo the scalloped cliffs. Tools are extensions of the same patience: a flake of basalt used for three generations, a sliver of meteoritic iron that has been cold-worked so often its original shape is only a rumour, bone awls polished by the same hands that now use them.

They keep the hearths their great-grandparents kept. Repair is the only architecture they still practise. A slumping wall is not failure; it is the Terrace continuing to speak, and they answer by restacking, by letting a cushion plant take the new angle, by adjusting the overlapping roofs so that one family’s terrace remains another’s floor. Children learn the names of lizards before they learn the names of stars (the stars themselves are only brighter motes in the same ochre veil). Counting the lizards is not idle; it is how they measure the day, how they confirm that the circuits still hold. Tending the plants is how they measure the decade. Listening to the wind is how they measure everything longer than that.

They wait with the rock. Not for rain (none of them has a word that still means rain as event rather than legend). Not for visitors (the drop is too final; nothing climbs anymore). They wait the way the last terrace of a vanished range waits: because the waiting itself has become the shape that remains. In the late light that is every light, a woman might stand at the scalloped rim with a child against her hip, both of them the same colour as the stone, both of them listening to the same note the wind has sung since before language. The child will one day stand there with another child. The wall behind them will have slumped another finger’s width. The succulents will have added another ring. And the Terrace will still be exactly as horizontal as it has decided to stay, until the last grain is carried off and even waiting has nothing left to hold.

What lives here has already become the place. The place has already become what lives. There is no remaining distance between them large enough to name.
