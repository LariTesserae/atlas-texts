The first lives are not lives you would name. They are a decision the seams made, or that the lime in the new concrete allowed: a film no thicker than the idea of green, olive only when the light columns stand still and you look along a grout line for a long time. It is not meadow. It is occupancy. The film holds to the darker joints because that is where the mineral breath of the pour is slowest to leave, a faint alkali the first cells can use. Hand-rough, yes. If you pressed a palm there you would feel the change before you saw it. Nothing else has claimed those kilometers of ruled darkness yet. The film does not move. It waits the way new paint waits.

Then the small walkers, the ones that treat the bolt-heads as a forest of identical trees. Pale crabs the width of a thumbnail, already the colour of the silt they stir, already the colour of the unused crane’s boom where divers once wrote names that have not yet been overwritten. They do not hurry. There is no season that hurries them. They work the risers of the drowned towers and the expansion joints that still smell, if smell existed here, of the yard. Amphipods in the same silt, industrious in the way dust is industrious when a door is left open. The mussels have chosen the welds. They line them with a geometry the welders would recognise: straight, parallel, unapologetic. Sparse as lichen, the description said, and it is true; each animal is a period in a sentence that has only just begun. They filter what the pumps send them. They do not know the pumps. They know current and lime and the pale blue that is day.

The eels arrived the way eels arrive. No one wrote them on a list. They use the stairwells of the towers that were never meant to be underwater, the ones whose windows stand open like mouths that forgot to close. The stairwells are chimneys of colder water; the eels hang in them or travel them, bodies the same dark as the unlit landings. They do not school. There is room, and room is the first law. On the silted treads they leave the small signs of having been: a groove, a shed something, the faint electric rumour of a life that came through locks when the last gates opened and the basin took its fill. They hunt what the film and the mussels have not yet become. They wait in the chimneys.

The fish occupy. That is the word. The silver ones with blunt tank-bred heads hang in the private weather of the condenser islands, in the shimmer that is leftover heat, the way dust hangs in a loft when no one has walked through it for a year. Not many species. Not yet. They face the same direction or no direction; there is no current that organises them except the artificial tide the operators run, the one that can lose to a real gale and still be the law. Among them, rarer, the darker immigrant, the one like a cod that remembers rivers. It does not join. It patrols the lee faces where the plates are still faintly warm, a single thought moving through rented water. They eat what the racks lose, what the film releases, what the storm lifts when the silt that has not learned where to settle becomes a hanging sheet. There is always room. The silver ones will be more before they are different. The darker one will remain a count you can keep on one hand.

The kelp is agriculture and therefore almost a citizen. It lives on cables in plots as rectangular as the caissons below, dark acres whose service buoys are lost in the glitter of the silver lid far above. It moves in the tide like hair in a sleeping mouth, the description said, and that is exact: not wild sway, but the sway of something grown to a specification and then left to the specification of water. The farmers come down in bells, their lights the only warm colour, and when they leave the kelp continues the work of being a field that no bird will ever see. Between plots, nothing. The nothing is also inhabited, by the same hunger that inhabits the seams.

And then the large thing, the one that is not many of it. It moves along the joints where the warm effluent ghosts, a passage of displaced water you hear before any shape arrives, a sound like a tarpaulin drawn slowly over a hull. It is fauna, the older business, the life that happens when you leave a door open. No stocking list named it. It uses the industrial weather the way the crabs use the bolt-heads: as given. You do not see it whole. You see the lean of the volume, the momentary thickening of the blue, the way the silver fish occupy a little more tightly afterward. It is already older than the basin in the only way that matters here. It does not live in the towers or the racks or the condenser shimmer. It lives in the patience of the pour-joints, in the long ruled lines that go on until they become a horizon of more work. It is the first thing that treats the size as a home instead of a specification.

All of it is running. The pumps do not sleep; circulation is the contract. The water is still hungry, still tasting of the rivers and the cut aquifers and the lime that has not finished leaving. What lives here lives in that hunger, on the edges that have not learned to be old, in a sea that was finished and then filled and is now the only temperature there is. The names in the silt on the crane’s boom are still there. The film is still a haze. The large thing still passes without a name. Nothing has had time to bury any of it. The place is not wild. It is not tame. It is occupied, sparsely, exactly, the way a new wall is occupied by the first lichen and the first decision to stay.
