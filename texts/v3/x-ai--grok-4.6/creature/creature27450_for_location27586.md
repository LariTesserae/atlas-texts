The ones who remain do not call themselves inhabitants. The word would require a settled inside, and the basin has none. They move as weather does, occupying a hall only while it is still deciding to be one.

There are the browsers first, the long-legged things that were never quite deer. Their spines run with a second set of leaves, dark and waxy, that fold shut when the walls around them unzip. They taste the older cambium, the slow façades that have not yet remembered they can become flowers. Their hooves print circles that fill at once with pale thread-roots; by the time the animal has taken three more steps the prints have become small, upright mouths that close again, having sampled nothing they wanted to keep. The browsers know which trunks are still listening and which have already turned their attention elsewhere. They do not linger near the central clearing. When a structure slumps into raft-bloom they wade through the petals without hurry, eating only the ones that have already begun to cork.

The copper fish keep to the temporary canals, flashing in schools so dense they look like a single beaten-metal sheet being folded and refolded by the current. When a street decides to become a wall they simply vanish—some into the new tissue, some into the sudden dark beneath a lifting root-mat. They reappear later in a different lane, carrying in their sides the faint electrical taste of whatever consensus the mycelium reached while they were gone. No one has seen them breed. It is possible they do not; the town may simply remember them into being whenever the water is deep enough.

Higher, the slate birds occupy sockets that will not stay sockets. They line their nests with shed fibers that still twitch, still try to become stairs or windows. The young hatch already knowing the wet applause of a collapsing nave. They fly the short, broken circuits between structures, never crossing the open water at the center except in panic, and even then they skim so low the pale eels rise to meet them, mouths open, listening.

The insects are the only ones who seem to work. Metallic greens, bruised purples, they stitch pollen and something else—packets of charge, rewritten sugar, the town’s own next draft. They land on a newly transparent window-skin and leave it slightly more certain of its shape, or slightly less. Their flight is the mid-range roar’s highest thread.

And then there are the others, the ones the basin does not quite reject.

They are few. You can count them if the light holds and the paths do not rewrite themselves between one breath and the next. They walk the woven plates of root and leaf-mold with the same careful slowness the ground uses when it considers incorporation. Their skin has the same faint give as living cambium; in places it weeps the same copper-and-leaf sap. Some of them still have faces that remember being human. Most do not. Joints have learned new grammars. A shoulder may open into a small, temporary window when the being needs to see behind itself. Hair is often aerial root, tasting the air for the next consensus. They do not speak with mouths. Information moves through them the way it moves through the xylem—pulses of sweetness and voltage that the others feel in their own sap.

They tend nothing. The town does not require tending. They simply remain inside the weather of it, crossing a plaza that was a pool an hour ago, sleeping in a ribbed nave that will be a spiral of pale flowers by the time they wake. When a structure chooses a volume that physics finds inconvenient, they step into the brief tearing of air and ozone and continue walking through the larger interior that should not exist. They have learned the taste of the recoil: the moment a tendril reaches toward the central clearing and flinches back. They do not follow it. The empty water is a law they also obey, though none of them can say who wrote it.

At dusk—if the light can still be called dusk in a place that never quite dries—they sometimes gather on the wet shoulder of the rim, where the stone weeps without stopping. They stand far apart, each on their own island of living mass. They do not look at one another. They listen to the grinding of roots, the distant musketry of setting lignin, the wet tear of a rooftop becoming canopy. The sound is the only market left, the only industry. When one of them finally moves it is to step down into the dish again, to let the ground decide, once more, that they are not yet a feature worth keeping.

The pale eels hang vertical in the uncleared water, heads toward the ordinary sky. They are the only things that never remodel. Whatever they are listening for has not yet arrived, or has already passed and left only the trembling. The others leave them there. Emptiness, they have learned, is still part of the architecture.
