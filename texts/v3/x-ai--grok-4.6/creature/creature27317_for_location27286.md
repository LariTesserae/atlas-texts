The first impression is always of vacancy, a completeness of green and glitter and paused iron that has no need of occupants. You walk the scarps and the hanging valleys feel emptied on purpose, as if the place had decided long ago that additional bodies would only interrupt the beads of water or the increment of a piston. Yet the moss remembers weight. Here and there a slab is compressed in a shallow, elongated hollow the size of a sleeping calf or a folded human, the liverworts already knitting back across it so that by the next dew the dent is only a rumor of itself. Something uses the same resting places season after season, or century after century; the plants have learned the shape.

They are not animals in any hurried sense, and they are not quite the people who once signed their initials into lintels. What remains of that lineage has thinned and slowed until the difference between a body and the medium it occupies is mostly a question of temperature and the faint metallic aftertaste they leave on the air. Their skin, when it shows, is the same pale grain as the young stone, faintly translucent at the edges so that the wet light passes through wrists and ear-tips and turns them into temporary lenses. Hair, if they keep it, is not hair but a fine overlay of the same film-thin succulents that cover every other surface; it holds dew in the same cups and threads the same hairline rills down the nape. They dress in what the valleys already provide: layered moss cut and re-cut by their own patient motion, fern fronds stitched with the thin riveted wire they salvage from abandoned pipes. The garments never fully dry. They are not meant to.

Movement happens at the tempo of the machinery. A figure may take an afternoon to cross from one terrace to the next, each step sunk to the calf, each lift of the foot leaving a brief dark well that the green closes before the next footfall. They do not speak. Vocal cords, if they still possess them, have gone unused long enough that the hush of the highland feels like their native grammar. Communication, when it occurs, is a matter of placement: a palm left on a warm stack for the length of a condensation cycle, a valve eased open a degree more than last decade’s setting, a thickening of moss they encourage on the sunny side of a condenser so that the shade-line in ten years’ time will fall exactly where a resting hollow is wanted. They seem to know the forecast the plants already know. Sometimes you find a pipe that has been gently rerouted, not broken, merely persuaded by decades of leaning weight so that its slow charge now warms a new cushion of liverworts. The gesture looks like care and also like the plants doing the caring through them.

Their numbers are the same scarcity as everything else that is not green. You might walk for days and see only the after-image of one: a glitter that is slightly too organized, a shadow that lasts a fraction longer than the hard light should allow, a displacement that the moss has not yet forgotten. At dusk, when the whole highland beads, they become briefly more visible because they bead as well. Droplets gather along the ridges of a spine or the rim of an ear and each one acts as a lens; for a few minutes the figure is a constellation of tiny, precise fires. Then the water runs, the light equalizes, and the shape is only another occupied surface among surfaces.

They eat what the place has already agreed to give. The oils stored in the thicker succulents, the mineral film the fresh cracks still exhale, the condensed water itself licked from the underside of a frond or collected in the cups their own garments form. There is no hunting. There is almost no prey. Once in a long while one of them will pause above a suggestion of smaller motion in the moss, wait out an entire formation of dew, and then simply cover the spot with a hand until the rumor stills. Nothing is taken that the valley cannot replace in the next slow cycle. Their own dead, when they occur, are not buried so much as surrendered: a body laid into a deep moss slab on a terrace that already holds a brick pump-house. Within a season the greenery has claimed the outline; within a decade the bones are another set of pale blades the ferns have learned to grow between. The machinery nearby may advance an extra hand’s-width that year, as if acknowledging the addition.

Children, if the word still applies, appear the way fresh cracks appear: suddenly, thinly, lined at once with the first film of green. They are born already damp, already the color of stone, and they learn stillness before they learn locomotion. A new one may remain in the same hanging valley for years, occupying the same compressed hollow, watching the light compress distances until a far chimney and a near fern occupy the same plane of attention. Only when the rock itself lifts another fraction, or a pipe begins to carry a slightly warmer charge, do they rise and begin the long walk toward the next scarp. Their eyes, pale and clean as the sky, never seem to narrow against the glitter. They have grown the lenses they need.

In the joints between plant and engine they are most themselves. You can find them at rest against a condenser whose patient rhythm matches the pulse they no longer hurry. Moss grows across their shoulders and is trimmed, increment by increment, by the same gears that trim it from the metal. They do not repair the machines so much as inhabit the same geological patience, becoming another moving part whose purpose was never loudly declared. The air around them tastes a little more of wet stone and a little more of that faint metallic agreement. If you stand very still—still enough that your own footsteps have already been eaten—you may feel the residual lift of the highland passing through them as well, a pressure too slow to be a tremor, shared equally by flesh, frond, and brick.

They are the highland thinking itself in another register, one more iteration of the repeating valley, sharper and greener and more thoroughly occupied than the last. Behind them the glitter closes. Ahead, another bright scarp waits, already beading, already sure they will arrive exactly when the dew is ready.
