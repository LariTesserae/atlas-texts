They are the ones who arrived with the tear, or who found themselves already standing when the ground opened and simply stepped down into it. A single generation has been enough to pack them into the wound until they and the hollow share a pulse. You see them first as a density rather than as separate bodies: figures that never quite stop adjusting, always a plank-width or a handspan out of last season’s true. They move as if the floor is still deciding whether to be floor.

Their skin has taken on the colour and temperature of the clay banks. It is pale, almost bluish in the even grey light, and it never quite dries. A fine emulsion of silt lives in the creases of wrists and the webs of fingers; after a day of packing walls or shifting planks it looks as though they have been lightly glazed. Fingertips bleach white from the stone-pecking and the constant cold wet; the nails grow thick and slightly ridged, like the layers the banks still show. Feet are broader than they were a generation ago, toes splayed and calloused from gripping wet cobble and the bowing single planks. Children are born with that look already starting, as if the hollow taught the womb the necessary shape.

They dress in what can be wrung. Split-willow mats beaten until they flex, reed thatch stitched into short cloaks that shed the worst of the drip, scraps of hide from whatever the last flood floated down. Everything is the colour of old tea or wet stone. Copper, when they have it, is not for ornament; a thin awl or a fish-hook is hoarded the way other people hoard stories. Hair is kept short or bound tight against the perpetual condensation; long hair would be another thing to wring. Breath shows even in what other places would call mild weather. They have learned to work with their mouths slightly open, tasting the mineral chill so they know when a new seep has started behind a wall.

Voices stay inside the chest. The hollow is a bowl; anything louder than a murmur returns from the opposite bank as if the stone itself were answering. Conversation happens in short, overlapping phrases, the way eaves overlap. They speak of the Opening the way other peoples speak of a first ancestor: not quite a god, not quite an accident. Some keep a slack of withy in every wall because they believe the hollow is still widening, a finger’s width each decade, and they will not be the ones caught loving a corner that decides to become trough. Others say the weeping is only memory now, the stone refusing to forget it was riverbed. Both groups rebuild the same way when a bank calves in the night: collect the fresh cobbles before the emulsion swallows them, pack the new face, climb a handspan higher. The oldest houses look like slow geological arguments, watermark upon patch-course, the inhabitants living inside the debate.

Work is the weather. After rain the whole settlement becomes a shallow lens and they simply wade, trousers rolled or abandoned, baskets poled by children who already know the difference between a footprint that will hold and one that will fill from below. Adults lift planks to the next pair of sockets worn into every sill. Someone is always wringing. Someone is always packing wet clay into withy forms that will take the cool and stand a little out of true because the ground is still drunk. The dull tap of pecking stone carries only a house or two before the damp air eats it. At the rim the weeping rock hisses continuously; they have stopped hearing it the way people stop hearing their own blood.

There is almost no one who does not carry a small, constant ache in the joints, the body’s way of remembering the low wet cool. They do not complain of it. Complaint would be another sound the bowl would throw back. Instead they have a habit of touching walls as they pass, checking for new drip-lines, new stains that will become grooves that will become problems. A child is sent up the eastern bank to scrape liverwort for poultices. The single alder is left alone even though its roots are tearing a collapsed wall; no one has decided yet whether it is a guest or a warning.

At the hour when the light goes colourless they stand on the roofs more than they stand on the ground. The packed reed-and-clay plane is their street, their drying ground, their lookout. Smoke shears sideways and never quite leaves the hollow, so they live under a thin moving lid of their own breath. A figure tests a plank with a heel, moves it two sockets, goes on. Another wrings a cloth over the trough and watches the water take the dirt as if it had been waiting. They do not look like a people who expect to finish anything. They look like the activity of staying: packed so tightly that a single absence would be a collapse, yet ready, always, to rebuild a handspan higher when the young ground shrugs.

They taste of silt and woodsmoke and the chill of stone that will not stop bleeding. The hollow holds them. For now it holds. They hold it back.
