The first bodies are not the ones that move. They are the ones that have already decided the stone is a kind of sky.

They hang in the upper third of the bowl in overlapping eaves, each eave a living shingle. What you take at first for wet rock is a stacked parish of folded membranes: long, slightly translucent, veined the way old ice is veined, the color of tea left too long in a cup. When they hang they are almost quiet. When they unfold they make the sound the prompt already gave you—wet paper remembering it was once a tree. They do not fly so much as pour themselves into the next available emptiness. A terrace of them will lift together only after a long, shared hesitation, as if the decision had to soak from body to body the way drip soaks from basin to basin. In the air they are clumsy and complete. They do not hunt so much as collide gently with whatever else is using the same volume, and then they hang again, a little lower or a little higher, and the hanging is the real life.

They lick. That is the work. Their faces are mostly tongue and the fine, combing bristles around it. They work the mineral skins on the water, the algal whispers too thin to be called green, the salt-ghosts left when a basin evaporates a finger’s width and then is refilled. They also lick one another, not from affection you would recognize, but because a neighbor’s membrane is another surface, and every surface here is in use. Young of theirs are not born so much as unhooked: small, damp commas that cling to the parent’s underside until the underside becomes too crowded, at which point the comma drops a ledge or two and becomes, immediately, another shingle. They have been doing this since the rock was still deciding whether to be a mouth.

Lower, and in the wetter seams, the jointed things keep the vertical. They are not one kind. That is the point the bowl has been making for longer than taxonomy. Some are long as a forearm and thin as a thought, all femur and patience, picking their way along hairline cracks with a sound like someone slowly counting beads in the next room. Others are blunt, armored in overlapping plates the color of old spoons, and these ones farm the drip-paths: they arrange their bodies so that water must cross them, and they sieve what the water has already sieved. A few are almost all leg, a radial argument of joints, and these ones do not walk so much as test. They tap, they withdraw, they tap again. When they find a pock that already has a tenant they do not fight. They wait at the lip until the tenant, for reasons of its own metabolism, shifts half a body-length, and then they take the vacancy as if it had been held for them. Nothing here is owned. Everything is occupied.

The air between the hangers and the walkers is the third density. It is not empty enough to be called air. Midges the color of dust live entire lives in the warm column and never touch stone. Larger fliers—leathery, short-winged, with faces like clenched fists—work the column in slow gyres, mouths open, taking whatever the column offers: the midges, the shed scales of the paperwings, the continuous fine rain of skin and fragment that is the colony’s other weather. These fist-faces churr. The churr is not a call. It is a way of occupying frequency the way a body occupies a ledge. When many churr at once the note drops into that low register you felt in your teeth, the shared heat-voice, and for a while the bowl seems to be speaking with one badly shared throat.

In the basins themselves there is a slower nation. The water is never still and never deep. What lives in it is pale and unfinished-looking, as if the dark had refused to let them choose a final shape. Some are all mouth and ribbon, rasping the mineral skin from below. Some are clustered like wet seeds, budding rather than breeding, so that a font that held a handful last season now holds a palmful, and the overflow carries the surplus down the next thread to start another font. A few larger shapes rest on the bottoms like discarded gloves. They do not swim. They pulse. When a paperwing or a jointed thing dies and is received by a ledge too near a basin, these pulsing ones extend a slow, unspecialized reach and take what the warmth has not yet finished. They are the reason rot cannot complete its sentence. They keep the sweetness mid-word.

There are also the ones you only know by the gaps they leave. Something heavy enough to make a terrace launch in panic now and then, though you never see it whole. A pressure in the darkest undercuts. A smell more coin-like, more ferrous, as if a large old tool were being slowly licked clean. The colony does not flee this presence so much as rearrange around it, the way drip rearranges around a new flake of stone. Perhaps it is only several of the armored walkers acting in a temporary agreement. Perhaps it is the mountain’s own leftover heat given a temporary hide. The bowl does not distinguish. Fit is the only law, and the law is thick.

At the lip, in the shrub that will not die, a last, thinner population keeps a different faith. These are small, dry, almost ordinary—finch-shaped, or mouse-shaped, or something that borrowed both and then forgot which. They know wind. They leave the bowl for whole hours, which here is a kind of blasphemy, and return with seeds that will not grow and insects that do not belong and, once in a long while, a bright scrap of something manufactured that the rest of the colony will strip for minerals before the scrap understands it has arrived. The shrub-dwellers are loud in a different key, a thin scolding, as if they still believed the world beyond the slit was the real one. The rest of the bowl ignores them the way a climate ignores a rumor.

No one here is rare. Rarity would be a kind of waste, and the mouth does not waste. What looks like a single species at first glance is always, if you wait, a gradient: the hangers grade into smaller hangers that never unfold, the walkers grade into things that have given up walking and become furniture that happens to metabolize, the basin-lives grade into the films they eat until you cannot say where the water ends and the appetite begins. Young appear the way grit appears—continuously, without headlines. The fallen are a cuisine before they are a tragedy. Heat is the tablecloth. Dark is the room. Drip is the only clock, and it has never learned another hour.

If there is a who, it is this thickness answering to itself. If there is a what, it is the argument the mountain has been winning since the rock first learned to hold its breath and the sky agreed to pass over. They are not visitors. They are the weather that learned how to stay. When you leave, what you carry out is not a specimen but a new way your own pulse has of mistaking itself for water ticking in the dark.
