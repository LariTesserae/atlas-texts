In the high dark the first thing that lives is posture.

They stand where the consoles still remember being consoles, a kilometer or more from the next pair of hands. The word people is used for them the way one uses the word river for a dry course that once held water: generously, and only in the long intervals when speech itself is quarried. Their clothing has sintered to them, slag-grain against slag-grain, so that it is no longer possible to say where fabric ends and the residue of a body begins. Shoulders have taken the slope of cooling hoppers. Necks have shortened into the suggestion of chimneys that no longer draw. The hands rest on levers that have not traveled in a span that would embarrass any calendar still willing to count; the metal of the grips has slowly annealed itself to the metal of the palms. Once in a duration that feels like news, a single knuckle whitens. It is not blood receding. It is a microscopic press achieving the density it was asked for, then holding. The unwhitening that follows is the only motion the shift still permits.

They do not blink. Moisture left the eyes the way it left the sky; the air would take the film if they lingered unblinking, so they have simply stopped lingering that way. Their mouths, when they open at all, open the width of a thought and close again. Conversations, such as they are, happen at the frequency of contracting plate: a word, then the quiet of a held tool, then another word that may answer the first or may be the first word of a different century. They are not waiting for the works to start. The works persist at the exact output required to remain themselves. The standers persist at the exact posture required to remain standers. If one of them were ever to fail, a neighboring failure—perhaps a gallery sheet cooling by the width of a held breath—would already have begun in the complementary direction. The average would not notice.

Against the lukewarm gratings, indistinguishable until they choose otherwise, lie the ash-foxes.

They are low, longer than they first appear, their pelts the same matte alloy that sheathes the conveyor galleries. Heat-sight, if anyone still possessed it, would find them only as slightly brighter absences. They do not hunt. Hunting implies a future in which something is caught and something else is changed. They attend. For hours they become the architecture: a fold of warmth against a vent, a line of quieter infrared along a cooling-tower base. When they move it is without sound the sintered dust can ring to; they simply cease to be one grating and begin to be another. Their ears, if they are ears, are tuned to the plateau’s residual fire rather than to prey. They listen for the next exhalation through the floor, the next almost-imperceptible brightening of a brick that remembers it was fired. They do not breed in any way that would require a second generation to be seen. Scarcity is their medium. They are the highland’s way of keeping a few low, living thermometers, uninterested in being counted.

Rarer still, so rare they are almost a rumor with a filing number, the pane-moths.

They are flat as a stain left by a lamp that was sold for scrap. They feed by sitting. Residual magnetism—old dies, forgotten windings, the faint charge that still walks the electrostatic galleries—charges them the way a battery is charged by a current too weak to notice. When they have taken enough they make one flight. It is slow, across a street that may be an hour wide, wings like hammered sheets of the dull alloy that drinks infrared. They die on the far curb. Nothing in particular presses them into the pavement; the even heat and the weight of the finished dark are enough. In time they become another dark flour, another clause in the sparse sentence of the city. No one sweeps them. Sweeping would be a change.

And then there are the Listeners.

No one claims to have counted more than one, and even that claim is made in the register of mineral speech: few words, long intervals. They are not animals so much as a habit the dark has learned. The darkness here has weight; it has had time to settle into seams and door-jambs and the mouths of stacks. Occasionally that weight pools. A doorway that was merely a rectangle of finished dark becomes, for a while, a rectangle that is also listening. The air inside it is a fraction warmer, as if the plateau’s residual heat had decided to attend. Then the pooling moves on. The doorway is left slightly warmer than it was, and the dark continues its occupation elsewhere. Whether the Listener is the dark itself briefly deciding to have a shape, or a last remnant of something that once listened for rivers, no one asks. Asking would require a question, and questions were spent down with the water. The Listener does not hunt, does not feed, does not fly. It occupies, then occupies a different threshold. In a place where nothing arrives and nothing leaves, this is the closest thing to travel that remains.

Other lives are slower still, and may not be lives in any grammar that still has verbs of becoming.

In one yard the molds wait in ranks, each the negative of a part cast so often the idea of the part outlived the need. Dust sifts into them. The even heat hardens the dust. A second, softer city is growing underfoot: a city of absences made solid, rooms the size of the original rooms, streets that remember the gauge of vanished rails. No one interrupts. The growth is so gradual it counts as stillness. Perhaps this, too, is the plateau thinking—now in the language of negative space, now in brick, now in the faint ring of sintered dust when something far off sends a single low note along the tableland.

If you stay long enough to navigate by warmth instead of by any memory of stars, you begin to feel the grain of the silence. Put your ear to the pavement and the old erosion is still finishing, a sound like a file drawn once across a century. The standers do not look down. The ash-foxes do not prick their alloy ears. The pane-moths have already completed their one flight. The Listener, if it is near, has already pooled and moved. What lives here lives by remaining. It has already become what it is. The rest of duration is simply the being of that, without remainder, in the high dark that has weight, under a sky that has forgotten morning, on a floor that was once mountains and is now only the decision to stay lifted and worn until lift and wear canceled, and what remains is this: a city the highland grew because that was the shape left to grow, and a handful of persistences—posture, attendance, one slow flight, a pooling dark—that are the same decision, still being made, at the temperature of a palm held too long against a sleeping animal that will not wake.
