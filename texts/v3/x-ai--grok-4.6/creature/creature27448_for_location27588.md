The ones who remain do not call themselves the last. They have no word for last. They have words for the lean of a swell before it folds, for the particular green that means the kelp is seeding, for the way a cable sings when the rust has finally eaten through one more strand. They live in the knuckles.

They are short, dense, built like the rock they inhabit: low center, thick through the chest and thighs, skin the color of wet iron where it is not scarred or barnacled. Their hair is not hair exactly; it is a fine, waterproof down that lies flat in the constant spray and stands only when the electricity in the air is high enough to lift it. Eyes are small, deep-set, the color of the pewter light, and they do not blink against the rain because the rain is simply the air. They taste metal on their tongues the way others once tasted seasons.

They do not farm. They tend. The moss mats are cut in careful rectangles and rolled like turf; the cuts heal in days. Liverworts are scraped for the bitter green paste that keeps the salt from rotting the joints. Fern knives are harvested only after a storm has already broken the weakest fronds, so the living plants are never wounded. In the intertidal they move like the crabs they share it with: sideways, low, hands and feet finding the same holds the water has already approved. They wear the old generators the way a hermit crab wears a shell. A family will occupy a single turbine house for three generations, patching the groaning blades with scavenged conveyor belt and mussel cement, listening to the pitch of the groan the way a physician once listened to a chest. When a machine finally stills they do not mourn it. They strip the copper, the last bright veins, and carry it upslope to the next house that still turns.

They speak little. Speech is expensive in the roar. Most of what they need to say is done with the angle of a shoulder, the placement of a stone on a cairn, the particular knot they leave in a rope. When they do speak it is in a language that has more words for kinds of wet than any older tongue ever needed: the wet that comes from above, the wet that comes sideways, the wet that rises from the rock itself, the wet that is only the air remembering it used to be ocean. Children learn the words by tasting them. A parent will put a drop of each kind on the child’s tongue and wait for the face to change.

They eat what the water offers and what the green will spare. Fish taken from the kelp cities are never taken whole; a single muscle is cut from a living school and the school closes around the wound as if it had never been. Seal is rare and eaten only after the animal has already chosen the rocks to die on. The great sounding things in the deep channels are never hunted. Their notes arrive through the soles of the feet; the people stop whatever they are doing and stand still until the note has finished traveling through the mountain and out the other side. They believe (though they would not use the word believe) that the note is the mountain speaking to itself about how much lower it will be next century. They do not interrupt.

At night they do not sleep so much as wait in a different register. They wedge themselves into the old concrete sheds whose roofs have already been peeled, or into the lee of a dome where the moss is thickest. Bodies press together not for warmth (there is no true cold, only degrees of wet) but to share the small electricity that gathers in hair and skin. Sometimes a waterspout walks close enough that the air tastes of ozone and old batteries. Then they sing, a low harmonic that matches the groan of the remaining turbines. The song has no words. It is only the sound of leftover momentum, of water that has never learned how to be still, of people who have decided the same.

They do not remember cities. They remember the rectangles of darker weed and the broken teeth of pilings, and they leave small offerings there: a coil of still-bright copper, a perfect crab shell, a knot that has no practical use. They do not know why. The gesture is older than their language. Sometimes a child will ask what the offerings are for. The answer is always the same, spoken into the child’s hair so the wind cannot take it: “For the ones who thought the water would ever be still.”

They will be here next century. The mountains will be lower, the ocean a few centimeters higher, the kelp a little thicker. They will have moved their houses one terrace downslope. Their down will be a little denser, their words for wet a little more precise. Nothing in them is in a hurry except the part that is already water, which has never needed to learn.
