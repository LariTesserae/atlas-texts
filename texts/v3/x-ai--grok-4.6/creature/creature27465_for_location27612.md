The hollow does not keep a single tenant. It keeps a tempo, and whatever can live inside that tempo claims a shelf or a well or a pause between knocks and calls the claim enough.

First the water itself occupies, not as element but as resident metabolism. It arrives already working: melt-skin that thickens and thins without ever choosing a state, threads that invent new pitches on every fresh fracture, the upward rain that forgets gravity for a few seconds and then remembers with a sound like someone changing their mind. In the two or three warmer wells the water is almost a body—slightly solvent, iron-sweet, never quite freezing. Amphipods stitch those wells the way fingers worry a rosary. They are dark, compressed, all motion and no spectacle. When the great valve seats itself somewhere under the marsh they vanish into the ochre flakes as if they had never been more than a trick of the light. The midges lift only in the silence after, a twenty-second summer that dies the instant the next knock arrives. They are not hopeful. They are punctual.

The frogs have learned the same clock so thoroughly they have almost become peat. Wet-bark bodies, eyes that do not blink until the roar has passed. They sit on the slumping moss shelves exactly where a person would rest a hand if a person were foolish enough to stay. When they move it is a single tasteless blurt, more disappearance than locomotion. Something smaller and mole-shaped works the walls from inside the peat. You never see more than a dimple, a brief snout the color of old tea, then a new bead-line of water finding the hole and changing the chord the hollow sings. The wall heals around the memory of the hole as if the ground were practicing being older than it is.

A mustelid uses the far cut at the hours that are always dusk. It is longer than the thought of it, dripping, listening with its whole spine to the same valve-knock you have begun to wait for. It does not hunt during the roar. Nothing hunts during the roar. The roar is the one honest law. When it fades the mustelid is already gone or already feeding; the distinction is not one the hollow bothers to keep. Birds skim the lip and refuse the mouth. They know the center is reserved.

Yet the true occupancy is slower and less animal than any of these. It lives in the rind, in the gas coins that smell of struck flint, in the way a fresh peat shelf will lean out overnight as though offering itself and then sag by afternoon into a ramp the water accepts without comment. It is the mineral enamel that weeps and crazes and peels, the ice needles that grow, smash, grow again. It has no face you can point to and therefore no expression you can misread. It is the hollow deciding, hour by hour, what kind of room it intends to be. Heat is granted only at the wells and revoked everywhere else; that contract is older than the opening. Sound is a tide that the plants and the small lives have already signed. The empty middle is not empty out of fear. It is empty the way a lung is empty between breaths—so that the next swallow of the world can be taken exactly.

Stay past the second roar and something else begins to assemble itself out of the available materials: cold so clean it hurts the teeth, the sweet solvent note, the iron, the constant bead. It is not a creature that arrived. It is the condensate of the place learning, very literally, how to have a body. Pale mineral films on the newest rock, moss cushions that hold too much water and therefore slump into almost-limbs, the occasional warmer upwelling that never quite becomes a spring. It does not walk the floor. It occupies the walls and the skin of the water and the brief columns of midges and the listening pause of the mustelid. When the discharge comes it does not flee; it becomes the roar’s inner surface, the shaped loudness itself, then lets the level drop a finger’s width and starts the dripping over, slightly rearranged, as if practicing a name it has not been given yet.

Nothing made lasts the night because the hollow is still too young for metaphor and will not tolerate souvenirs. A twig, a print, a scent that does not already belong is filmed, sunk, or grown over until it is only more peat. The inhabitant—call it that for now—has the same intolerance. It is local, contractual, and exact. It inhales the spent gray light and the industrial weather that has no factory. It exhales beads on the undersides of leaves. It keeps the center clear because the center is how the under-country swallows. And every time the throat of stone takes another mouthful, the hollow answers in the only voice it has so far invented: loud, edged, and perfectly timed, as if it had been waiting for this particular work and had never been built for anything else. 

What lives here is still becoming the answer. It has not finished choosing its edges. It weeps because everything here weeps. It listens because the knocking is the only clock that matters. It leaves room because the place itself is the main material, and the lives that keep to the skin of it are only the first, wet draft of whatever will eventually claim the whole pocket as a single, dripping, rhythmic body.
