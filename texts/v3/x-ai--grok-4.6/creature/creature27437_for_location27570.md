The lives here do not arrive as invasions or colonizations. They accrete. They are the plateau’s long, patient answer to its own unfinished cooling, the way a scar answers a wound by becoming another kind of skin. Nothing is in a hurry to be distinct from the slag-dusk and the buried foundry. Distinction is expensive; blending is a wage you can live on for geologic time.

Begin with what barely bothers to be separate. The bruise-mats are not merely food. They are a low, rubbery weather of their own, half-adhered, half-migrating by millimetres when a rain-cell has passed and the stone is briefly drinkable. Touch one and it yields like cooling tar, then firms, sealing the bruise it just made. Inside the mat live threads that are neither hyphae nor roots: mineral-hungry, heat-hungry, tasting the annealed plates the way a tongue tastes metal. They mine the film left by venting, concentrate the faint sugars the stone sweats when it is hottest, and hold the wet an hour longer than the rock would allow. Walk far enough and you realize some mats are older than the shallow canyons they occupy. They have been thickening and thinning with the heat-tides since the plateau first learned to tick. Animals do not graze them so much as subscribe to them. A pass of rasping mouths leaves the mat darker, almost black, and it answers by releasing that faint overheated-sugar smell, a chemical shrug that says the contract is still good.

Above the mats, in the more reliable seeps, the jointed stalks wait. They have no interest in leaves. Their dark flanges are gutters and locks, folding dew and condensate inward with a slow, peristaltic click you feel more than hear. Some of the stalks are hollow; others are packed with a gelatin that stores both water and the infrared the stone leaks. They do not photosynthesize so much as they loiter in the leftover radiation, converting the plateau’s own waste heat into the smallest possible increment of self. When a heat-tide peaks they swell, flanges spreading like dull fans. When it ebbs they collapse into themselves and become another rib of stone until the next wave. Insects—if that word still applies to things with too many joints and no interest in flight—mine the flanges for the condensate they hoard. The stalks do not mind. Everything here has learned that holding is temporary.

Then the movers.

They come in densities that feel like manners. You can walk a day through emptiness that is not empty, only politely spaced, the ground ticking, a vent field murmuring far off like a throat clearing two valleys away. Then the air changes temperature by a degree you feel in your molars, and the bodies are simply there, a weather of them, all of them the same absorbent dusk as the rock so that outline is a rumor and heat-signature is the only honest census.

The largest are the ones that treat their own guts as portable summers. They are long, low, articulated more times than seems necessary, each joint a hinge that can lock against the wind or unfold to drink a wider patch of warmth. Their hides are not skin so much as a slow ceramic, plated in overlapping scales that have incorporated the same iron and slag the plateau is made of; they do not gleam. Along the belly, just under the plates, run sacs lined with something dark and vascular that drinks heat the way the mats drink wet. They linger on the hottest seams until those sacs are banks, until the animal itself is a low, walking isotherm. Then they set off across the long cold-dark between vent fields, and behind them comes a retinue that is not quite a herd and not quite a parasite-load: smaller multi-jointed lives, some no bigger than a fist, some the size of a crouched child, all of them following the climate the big one carries. They graze the mats the carrier disturbs. They sip the condensate that beads on its cooling flanks. They sleep in the lee of its body when a wind decides to exist. The carrier does not seem to notice them, or perhaps noticing is another word for the arrangement having lasted long enough to become anatomy. When the next hot seam appears the carrier settles, opens its joints, and begins to recharge. The retinue scatters into the new warmth or stays, according to hungers too old to be called decisions.

Others have specialized in the exhales. They station themselves downwind of active vents with a patience that looks like geology. Their mouths—or the structures that serve as mouths—are wide, fringed, faintly iridescent in the infrared, sieving the steam for whatever the galleries below have cooked: mineral motes, the protein-mist of things that have never seen even the plateau’s leftover light, midges that live their entire lives in the rising wet. These sifters stand so still they become another kind of vent furniture. Only the faint rhythmic flutter of the fringes gives them away, and even that can be mistaken for heat-shimmer. Some are solitary. Some gather in loose ranks when a vent field enters its chorus phase, a whole slope of them facing the same direction like iron filings. When the rumble comes—the continent clearing its voice—they pause mid-sieve, fringes still, as if listening with their whole surfaces. Then they resume. The plateau is paying them; they are punctual.

There are the kneelers at the wrist-deep mirrors. They approach the black water as if the water might take offense. The motion is the same every time: a slow folding of too many limbs until the body is a compact, prayer-shaped knot, the mouthparts descending last, sipping without rippling more than they must. They drink, they wait, they drink again. Some have tongues or rasps that leave the water faintly clouded with the mineral film they have also been stripping from nearby stone. They do not linger once the sipping is done. The mirrors are temporary and everyone knows it. A kneeler that stays too long becomes a silhouette against the next rain-cell’s reflection, and silhouettes are how the interruptions hunt.

The interruptions themselves are the quietest thing in a quiet place. You do not see them arrive. You notice that the small wet sounds have stopped, that the air in one direction is a degree warmer than the ground has any right to be, that something hard has been set down with the care of a thought. They are built for the long wait and the short close. Their camouflage is not pattern but temperature-matching; they can lie on a cooling seam until they are the seam, then unmatch in a single contraction when a grazer or a kneeler comes within the radius of one precise unfolding. The kill is not a chase. It is an edit. A body that was there is no longer conducting heat in the same way. The interruption feeds without hurry, because hurry is a sound and sound is a luxury the plateau taxes heavily. Afterward it may drag what remains toward a fracture or a quiet vent, not to hide it but to let the factory finish the work: heat and sulfur and the slow chemistry of the galleries turning the rest into mist that someone else will sieve.

There are more. Things that nest only in the rolled, annealed plates, reading the grain of the stone the way other animals read current or wind; their young hatch already knowing which fold will hold warmth longest. Things that migrate not by star or scent but by the multi-day heat-tides, leaving whole tracts so empty the plateau seems to have exhaled them, then condensing again where the buried weather is rising. Things small enough to live in the condensate films themselves, grazing bacteria that live on the wet iron smell. Things that have learned to time their own metabolisms to the factory’s phases so that when the rumble comes they are already still, already listening, already slightly more themselves.

None of them build. None of them name the rivers that are only stains. They have had longer than mountains to notice that the world is already a machine—vents, heat-tides, annealed grammar, salt-maps rewritten by wind—and they have taken their wage from it without ever needing to call the arrangement by any word. They occupy. They use. They continue. The high dark does not climax around them and they do not climax inside it. They are another of its climates: sparse, courteous, full enough, empty enough, warm in the bones, and running, quietly, for as long as the lid stays on and the foundry below remembers how to tick.
