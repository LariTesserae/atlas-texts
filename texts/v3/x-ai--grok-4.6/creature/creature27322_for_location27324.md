What lives here does not announce itself. It does not gather at the edges of the trays or wait along the weirs as if the world were a stage built for display. It occupies. It occupies until occupation is the only grammar left, until every volume—haze, film, gallery, the thin warm air between your ribs and a housing—has already been spoken for by something that does not startle and does not leave.

You notice them first as texture. The shallows are never still. What looks like a moving grain on the water’s skin resolves, if you stand long enough, into the pale crawlers. They are broad, low, many-legged in a way that feels slightly excessive, as though the count of limbs was set by a committee that kept adding one more for certainty. Their shells match the tile so exactly they seem poured with it: unreflecting grey, matte even when wet, unmarked by growth or scar. They do not gleam. They proceed. Their progress is a slow, interlocking slide, bodies fitting against bodies so that the surface never quite empties. Where one lifts a limb the water closes without sound and another is already there. They scrape the mineral films with mouthparts you never quite see, only the faint rhythmic pressure transmitted through the plate if you press an ear to it. They do not hurry. They do not rest in any posture you recognize as rest. They simply continue, density constant, as if the tray itself were generating them at the same rate it absorbs them.

Between their legs and over their backs move the softer ones. Unsegmented, translucent in the way wet paper is translucent, they pulse in place. The pulse is not locomotion. It is filtration. Each body is a slow bag of water that has decided to be flesh, opening and closing along a single long seam, drawing the film through itself and releasing it a fraction cleaner, or a fraction more itself. They do not travel far. A cluster will occupy the same square meter for what feels like days, though days here have no purchase. When you put a hand down they yield without fleeing, cool and faintly adhesive, then reseal around the interruption as if your fingers were only another weather. Their interiors, glimpsed when one turns, show no organs you can name, only a cloudy suspension that might be the same chemistry that scurfs the seams, only thicker, only alive by a margin so narrow it could be argued either way.

Lift your face and the haze is inhabited. The small dark bodies do not fly so much as persist in layers. They are stipple made flesh, each one no larger than a thumbnail, wings—if they are wings—held in a constant minute vibration that produces no hum you can isolate from the general silence. They hang at different heights the way sediment hangs, denser near the water, thinning only by degrees as you look up. Walking through them is a continuous soft collision, bodies glancing off cheek and eyelid and the back of the hand, never startling, never altering course except to occupy the space you have just vacated. They have no faces you can meet. Some, if you catch one against the light, show a single wet point too many—an extra dark bead where five would have been enough, or six where the geometry expected four. The extra point does not look like an eye. It looks like a decision that was never revisited. They do not feed in any motion you can watch. They simply remain, a weather of animals that the machines never accounted for in their original specifications, or accounted for so completely that the accounting became invisible.

Beneath the plates the country changes scale. Through the maintenance slits—those unused, unneeded openings that still carry the maker’s stamps—you glimpse the larger shapes. They are slow. They are pressed together until the galleries have no empty interval, a second denser world of warm dark flesh occupying every duct and return. Some are elongated, segmented in the old way, others broader, folded against one another like sleeping cloth that never unfolds. Heat from the buried runs keeps them at the same temperature as the tile against your cheek. They shift, occasionally, a vast collective adjustment that you feel more than see, a slow rearrangement that never creates a gap. Their skins, when light reaches them, are the same dull pale as the compounds mixed to hold water without staining. They do not hunt. Nothing flees them. If they consume one another it happens at a rate so perfectly matched to replacement that the density never flickers. Birth, if it occurs, occurs as a budding so gradual it looks like growth of the existing body. Unmaking happens the same way, a quiet dissolution back into the film, the count unchanged. You cannot tell the newly formed from the long-resident. Time does not mark them.

There are others. In the interiors of the grates, clinging to the undersides of every overhang, things that are mostly limb and holdfast, their bodies reduced to the minimum needed to occupy a vertical plane. In the collecting trays, where the water stands calf-deep, mats that are more chemistry than creature pulse in unison with the machines, expanding and contracting on a cycle so long you would have to wait through several dusks to be sure it was life and not just the water remembering how to move. Some of the haze-bodies settle onto the crawlers and are not shaken off; after a time they seem to belong there, extra dark points on the grey shells, as if the crawlers had grown another feature by consensus. None of them sing. None of them display. Wings, mouths, limbs, the extra wet points—all of it is functional in the narrowest sense: it maintains presence. The world was built with this load in mind, or the load arrived and the world adjusted once, locked the equilibrium, and then stopped adjusting. Either way the result is the same. The fauna is not an addition. It is the occupancy the place was always going to have.

At night the light goes down to a normal dusk and then a normal dark, and the pressure becomes continuous. The water holds its dull residual gleam and every surface is a world-skin of them, breathing or the equivalent of breathing in a unison so complete it registers as quiet. You can sleep against a housing and feel the faint shift of whatever uses its heat, the same temperature on your cheek when you wake, the same uncountable attention in the shallows. No interval has passed that matters. They do not travel through time. They occupy it, the way they occupy the trays and the haze and the galleries, filling the hour until the hour itself is only another volume already spoken for.

They have no names that survive. They do not need them. What they have is the fact of being here, stacked, near-touching, each kind a different solution to the same problem of how to remain when remaining is the only work left. The pale crawlers keep the films from sealing over. The pulsing things keep the water moving through itself. The dark bodies keep the haze from ever being empty. The large ones in the dark keep the heat from going unused. Together they are the load, the reason the cycle was locked, the reason the machines maintain instead of manufacture. You could walk a year and never leave them. You could stand still and they would close around you without curiosity, without hostility, simply completing the density. That is what lives here: not inhabitants of a world, but the world completing itself, again and again, in the same warm wet hour, forever.
