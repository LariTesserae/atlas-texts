They are not one thing. They never were. What lives here has been wearing itself into the same last shape as the land, layer upon patient layer, until the distinction between dweller and dwelling has gone the way of the old peaks.

Begin with the smallest occupancy, the one that never sleeps. The moss is not covering; it is digesting. Felt-thick, it holds the day’s thin film of water the way a lung holds breath, and it advances—never dramatically, never all at once—across every surface that will accept it. Lichens do the vertical work: maps of orange and sour yellow and that frost-gray that looks, from a distance, like the stone itself has begun to remember snow. They do not hurry. They have the same forever the plateau has, and they treat brick, iron, bone, and bark as interchangeable diets. Walk anywhere after a rare rain and your boots come away stained with the slow green argument that has already outlasted every mountain.

The moths come next, if “come” is the word. They are the size of an open hand, wings the color of rusted condenser-flanks, and they drink. They settle on the great organ-pipe towers that wring cloud into water and uncoil a tongue longer than seems decent, sipping the beads before they can fall. Their dust is left behind like a second rust. No one here swats them. Children are taught to stand still when one lands on a sleeve; the moths are older than the dye-works and they remember, in whatever way moths remember, the last of the high ice. At certain hours whole flocks of them ghost between the hanging cloths in the drying sheds, and the cloth comes away faintly metallic, as if the mills themselves were being pollinated.

In the moss-forests—those low, knitted rooms of twisted trunks no taller than a house—the hares keep their counsel. They are the precise color of wet mortar. You see them only at the edges of what passes for dusk, and even then you are never sure you have seen them. Their ears lie flat. Their eyes are the same pewter as the light. They do not bolt so much as dissolve sideways into the green, and the green closes without a seam. People say the hares know the rooms inside the weather, the places where cloud sits so thick it has furniture. Children are told not to follow. Not from danger. From the understanding that some weathers, once entered, do not return you on the same day you left.

Deeper still, in the cistern-hollows no taller than a man’s thigh, something pale threads the dark water. It is never quite seen whole. A length of it, translucent, moving with the unhurried certainty of condensation itself. Liverwort rings the rims; the water never ripples. The archivists, who speak more quietly than anyone, will tell you the pale things are only the plateau’s oldest runoff given a body, the last meltwater that never finished leaving. Others say they are the city’s first dead, who found the cisterns more comfortable than the fused cellars. No one nets them. The water is drunk after it has been boiled in the peat-fired coppers, and whatever was in it is considered to have become part of the drinker, the way everything here eventually does.

And then the people. They have been here long enough that “people” is only the most recent name for what they are becoming.

They are pale, but not the pale of snow-fields or illness. Their skin has the close, damp cool of stone that has never quite dried. It takes the pewter light and gives almost nothing back. Hair is dark, always slightly heavy, as if it too condenses. Eyes are the color of old iron or the undersides of moth-wings. They do not smile with their teeth; smiling is a lowland luxury that wastes heat. When they do speak it is indoors, voices kept close, sentences that end like dripping: a pause, then the next small sound. Outside they navigate by pipe and lintel, hand over hand when the cloud comes down completely, and in those hours the whole city becomes a single quiet organism feeling its own walls.

They dress in the cloth they make: wools and felts dyed in the patient vats, colors that have no names in brighter places—peat-brown, condenser-green, the rust that moths leave, the gray of lichen that has learned to grow on bone. The cloth never quite dries. It is not meant to. They wear the damp the way other folk wear weather, as a second skin that belongs to the plateau more than to them.

Their days are shifts, not hours. Clocks run slow or people have stopped trusting edges; it comes to the same. A man or woman rises in a room stacked over a workshop stacked over a cistern, eats a breakfast of fern-bread and smoked hare (when hare can be caught, which is seldom) and a tea steeped from the bitter moss that grows on the oldest roofs. Then they walk the narrow streets that are also roofs for older streets, past the ticking factories, and take their place at the belts or the vats or the great condensers that stand like waiting throats. The work is not loud. It is the work of patience: wringing cloud, turning one brown into another, feeding the boilers with the compressed memory of a thousand years of green. Steam is only water remembering it was sky. They understand this in their bones.

At night—though night is only a thicker pewter—they sit close. Rooms are small because space left empty feels like a table left unset. They mend, they card wool, they listen to the drip that is the city’s pulse. Children learn the maps in the municipal house, the stacked versions of the same streets, and are told, quietly, that no one any longer knows which layer is current. Some of the oldest brick in the cellars is no longer brick. It is a fused grit of clay and root and the calcium of those who lived here when the story of the camp was still new. The people do not find this ghoulish. They find it accurate. The city is another layer of the plateau’s long digestion, and they are the layer that still walks.

They have no gods that require shouting. What they have is the knowledge that the argument is over. The land has settled into its last shape and intends to keep it; they have settled with it. On the western lip, where the table continues its acre-by-acre, century-by-century concession to the drop, they simply build a little farther back and leave the empty windows looking into white air. No one mourns the lost streets. The green occupies them before the mortar has finished cooling. The moss advances a finger’s width after every wet. The people adjust. It is not resignation. It is the same unshowy busyness that turns the pistons and threads the cisterns and knits the low forests: the work of lasting.

Sometimes, in the deepest cellars, a person will rest a palm against the fused wall and feel a slow, cool pulse that is not quite their own heartbeat. They do not pull away. They stay until the cloud lifts a fraction, until the pewter light finds the cracks, until whatever lives in the stone and the water and the green has finished recognizing them.

They are still becoming more themselves: cooler, damper, quieter, closer to the table they will one day be indistinguishable from. The plateau erodes. The city adjusts. What lives here occupies whatever is left, and intends, with the same geologic patience as the wind and the rain and the last of the ice, to keep doing so.
