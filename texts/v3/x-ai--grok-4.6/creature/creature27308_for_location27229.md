The first things that live here are not the ones that walk upright and speak of addresses. They are the pale lengths that tile every upright face, forearm-long and overlapping, their undersides working the mineral smear with mouths that never quite close. They are a second skin the city grows for itself, a living clapboard that thickens wherever the films run richest. Strike one and the whole wall ripples an alarm too fine for ears; the flyers take it up at once, and the air becomes a single wound of sound that lasts until the offender is gone or has learned. They eat what the darkness permits: the chemosynthetic glaze, the shed flakes of one another, the faint wrong-placed warmth that leaks from the walls a half-step late. They do not die so much as slough and re-adhere. In the oldest courts their bodies have become the mortar.

Through them, always, the flyers. Soft-winged, faces all ear and membrane, they do not fly so much as occupy the volume in a shouting that never rests. The shout is map and name and warning braided so tightly that a listener who stays long enough begins to hear grammar in it: this ledge is claimed, that drip-line is thinning, a larger shape is shouldering through the understreet two courts over. They pause only long enough to drink the same films the clingers work, or to snatch a jointed thing that has climbed too high. Then the air is full again. Their droppings are another architecture, phosphorescent for a breath, then just more mineral for the pale ones to farm. Children learn to walk with their mouths open so the shout can sit on the tongue and tell them where the next socket-room begins.

In the standing water the jointed ones think their fever-thoughts. Too many elbows, too many slow decisions. They rearrange the passable lanes over seasons, a rustling migration that elders track the way other cities track stars. Larger shapes use the same drowned streets as roads: shouldered, blind, their backs stretched with drum-skins that turn every drip into a note the city can feel in its teeth. You meet them first as a change in pitch, then as cold sliding past your shins. They are patient. They have been using these routes since the routes were still dry stair-cuts. They eat the films, the flyers that misjudge a descent, the heat that arrives through the wrong door. Sometimes they eat a person who has forgotten the rule about dryness. The person is not mourned as lost so much as reclassified: another kind of film, another slow circulation.

The people live among all of this the way the water lives in the sponge. They are not separate. Their rooms socket into one another and into the fauna until the distinction between habitation and hide becomes a courtesy, like “street.” They navigate by the wet heat of other bodies, by the temperature that appears a moment after the valve is shut, by the iron taste that means a new seep is opening under last year’s floor. Sight is a habit they have mostly retired; the lid gives them nothing to see with. They listen for the shape of a room. They taste the air. They dream in drip-rhythms until the shouting outside and the shouting inside agree, and then they are no longer newcomers.

They speak of the Growers the way other people speak of weather or gods: never seen working, only the result, which is always slightly more city fitted into slightly more highland. No one finishes a sentence about permanence. A door becomes a window over the course of a childhood; a neighborhood lifts a man’s height and the old sluice is remembered as stairs by those who are no longer believed. The people adjust the way the animals adjust, which is to say they do not hurry. Children are taught tendencies: this court will pack with flyers when the eastern ridge shoulders higher; that basement will become a tank before you are old enough to drink from it. Elders keep the longer memory, the one that says the central basin was once a single street, and they are correct, and it does not matter.

At the raw lip, where the stone is newest and the water comes off it in visible plumes, the people wear their breath like personal weather and the fauna thicken to soup. Here the saturation is almost complete. A person standing still long enough will find the pale ones beginning to tile their boots, the flyers pausing on their shoulders as if the shoulders were just another ledge. The jointed things investigate the ankles. It is not predation. It is recognition. The highland is still arriving, and everything that lives here is part of the arriving: the clingers, the shouters, the fever-jointed, the blind-shouldered, the ones who used to call themselves citizens. They eat the films. They eat the shed heat. They eat one another in the slow ways the place permits. They are the other infrastructure, the living mortar, the continuous unspooling of drops on hide and on the drum-skins and on the thin ice that flexes when the air shifts.

What lives here is the saturation itself. It has no single face. It has the overlapping pale lengths, the ear-faced shouting, the too-many-joints, the patient larger dark, and the upright walkers who have learned not to finish sentences and not to trust a dry patch and not to try to see. It grows the way the city grows, the way the ridges lift: in patient increments, a knuckle of new life showing through the older skin every few decades. You feel it in the teeth, in the wet cold that occupies rather than bites, in the dream that continues after you wake. It has always obtained. It is still arriving.
