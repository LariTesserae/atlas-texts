The people who remain are few, and they have been few for longer than any of them can count. They are not a tribe with a name they still use aloud. They are the ones who did not leave when the last usable pass silted shut or when the chord changed and the old caves began to scream instead of sing. Their bodies have taken the plateau’s shape the way the stone took the wind’s: low to the ground, joints that fold small, skin that does not argue with sun or grit. Hair is cropped or braided tight and oiled with the wax the carpet-plants yield when you crush them just so. Eyes are narrowed not from habit but from the light, which at this height has no mercy and no shade except the ones you carry with you.

They live inside the instrument. A good flute-cave is chosen the way a lowlander might choose a well: by pitch, by the way condensation beads on its inner flutes, by how the hanging mats (beaten bark, gut, the fibrous inner layer of the carpet) can be layered to baffle without silencing. Families are small because space is small and because a bad season of weather can empty a hollow for a generation. Children learn the hand-signs before they learn to walk far. They learn the stone-sequences: a line of marked pebbles in the lee of a wall can say “fog harvest good, stay,” or “chord rising, the deep hole tonight,” or simply the name of someone who will not be coming back. Forehead to forehead is for the rare still pockets, when the wind forgets itself for minutes and the silence is so complete it feels like being born again without lungs. They do not waste breath on stories that cannot be told in weather. The oldest among them sometimes still remember that the wind was once spoken of as a who, but they have mostly stopped; speech is expensive, and the who is obvious.

They eat what does not fight the air. The carpet is peeled in thin sheets, the understory wet and faintly sweet, full of the tiny work of holding mist. In the hanging gardens they string whatever fiber will last: more bark, more gut, the pale aerial roots that feel along the rock like blind fingers. Those gardens drip. A family can live on what one well-chosen hollow intercepts if the weather does not change its mind. Meat is almost never. When the calms come — the sudden, violent stop of the instrument, ears ringing in a new kind of noise — something moves on the scoured flats. Tracks begin and end in the middle of bare stone. Bones are found in the lee of things, scoured light as paper. Whatever leaves them is gone when the wind remembers. The people do not hunt it. They wait, and sometimes they find what it leaves, and sometimes they do not.

The plants themselves are not merely vegetation. Out on the pavement they are a pelt, aerodynamic, waxed, pressed flat by centuries of the same argument. Kneel and it is a jungle viewed from above: layered, busy, holding water that arrived as mist and will leave as mist. In the pockets they lose all shame. They hang. They stack. They drip. Curtains thicker than a body, mosses that have become furniture, air-drinkers with no interest in soil. In those syrupy, green-smelling rooms the air is a different country. The plants move when the wind cannot reach them. Not much. A rearrangement. A turning of faces toward the last drip, or away from a hand that has taken too much. People who stay too long begin to notice that the hanging gardens remember who was generous and who was not. Offerings appear in the smaller cavities — fiber, a tool, a tooth, a twist of dried leaf — put there as thanks or as a way of asking the instrument to keep its present pitch a little longer. The people do not say the plants are listening. They simply leave the offerings and repair the mats.

There are other lives that do not stay. Pale kites with bodies like slivers ride the standing waves of air above the flutes, visible as glints and then not. They do not land often. Birds from below the rim almost never cross. In the mist, when it is thick enough to be a room for a few minutes, it sometimes holds the impression of the last thing that walked through it, a few paces behind, fading. People who have walked too far in that mist say the impression is not always their own. They do not say what else it might be. The stone under the palm is warm and, if you wait, not entirely still. There is a pulse that is probably only blood learning the chord. Probably.

The people repair. That is the main technology. Listening is the main science. They know the difference between the roar that means go and the roar that means get in the hole now. They know that silence is not safety; it is the plateau inhaling. They travel in the early warmth, sleep in whatever hollow has a pitch they can tolerate, and they do not assume a future in which the wind will be reasonable. A person here is optional. The oldest surface and the youngest weather do not require witnesses. The few who remain are the ones who agreed, long ago, to be worn. They are still being worn. The chord continues. The green secrets continue in their pockets. The long, warm, deafening vacancy continues, and inside it a handful of upright mistakes keep listening, keep repairing, keep setting their small marked stones in the lee of walls that are already returning to the grammar of the plateau.
