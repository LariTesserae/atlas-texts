The lives here did not arrive. They were already the argument the plateau was having with itself when the last mountain-shadows flattened into habit. Distinction is something the chord still performs—kite from grazer, hunter from column, town from nerve—but it is a courtesy of scale, not of kind. Everything that moves or waits or remembers does so as a local thickening of the same dry metabolism, a temporary insistence that one chamber or one joint or one gaze should be counted separately for a while.

Begin in the smallest registers, the ones the wind declines to use. Inside the nested hollows of the floor and the flared bases of the columns live the clickers and the rasp-singers. They are not insects; they have never needed the wet vocabulary of chitin and hemolymph. Their bodies are faceted lattices of smoked glass and compacted pollen-analogue, each facet a tiny resonator. They move by shifting charge across their surfaces, a dry creep that sounds like two old coins deciding whether to touch. Their songs are private weather: sequences of ticks and scrapes that modulate the standing chord only in the nearest chamber, a brightening no larger than a held breath. They feed on the shed dust that the living ground continually offers and reclaims, grazing the particulate the way a tongue might remember salt. If you lie still long enough against the ivory-and-iron cambium you feel them as a faint percussion in the bone, a reminder that even the quietest rooms are occupied. The towns have old laws about opening them. The laws are not moral so much as topological: to breach one of these lives is to introduce a new cavity, and new cavities change the music for everyone.

Higher, and louder, the kite-flocks. They are paper only in the way a reef is coral: thin, many-jointed, edges ringing with a sound like cooled metal drawn across itself. Each body is a folding of the same silicified tissue that makes the floor, stretched and articulated until it can ride the thin air without ever quite flying. They do not flap. They adjust angles, a slow origami of lift, grazing the dust-weather with wing-margins that taste charge. A flock two swells away registers first as a shimmer in the chord, a brightening that the columns take up and return so that the open country briefly knows itself as one glittering circuit. They have no faces. Where a head might be there is only a denser clustering of joints, a knot of decision that turns the whole kite toward the next patch of shed skin. In spore-season they become almost conductive themselves, their rings rising until the air between them carries messages the towns do not need wires to hear. Children of the columns are taught their names last, after etiquette, because the kites are weather more than animals, and weather is not addressed, only answered.

The grazers move in bands that look, from a distance, like the memory of a riverbed deciding to walk. Cattle-sized, yes, but the size is incidental; what matters is the texture. They are weathered basketry, or the idea of basketry after it has been left in dry wind for a geological afternoon: interwoven straps of living cambium, pale where they have cropped themselves thinner, iron-dark at the joints. They do not chew. They press the living floor with broad, faintly giving pads and drink the nap that grows back, a slow rasping that sounds exactly like rain on a roof no one living has heard from the sky. Their interiors are resin-warm, structures like stacked pollen and smoked glass, lymph that never learned to be blood. They are abundant the way stars are abundant: not heaped, but distributed across the faint residual swells so that the eye, wanting mist, finds instead these slow constellations of hunger. The floor does not mind. Cropping is one of the seasons it has always known.

The hunters are the plateau’s louder facts. Long, jointed, almost ceremonial, they run with a dry clatter like a handful of thrown sticks deciding to become a single animal. Their bodies echo the columns they sometimes pause beside: shoulders rounded by the same abrasion of time, limbs fluted and then softened, bases that seem poured into the act of standing. They do not stalk so much as conduct a falling scale. The hunting call begins in one throat and the nearest column takes it, then the next, so that a single pursuit becomes an afternoon-long sentence spoken by a mouth too large to see. Prey and predator have been the same conversation for so long that their bodies look designed for one another; the designing simply stopped. If you opened one (the laws again, almost geological) you would find the same dry honesty: no solvent, only resin and glass and the compacted memory of a chemistry that photosynthesizes without green. They are not cruel. Cruelty requires a future in which something might be otherwise. Here the hunt is clockwork at biological scale, a migration of pressure the floor conducts into the chord and does not distinguish from any other season.

And then the towns, the ganglia, the places where the dry nervous system decided to thicken into streets.

It is not always clear that “people” is the first category that applies. The column-dwellers are grown as much as born, or perhaps the distinction finished when habitation and growth could no longer be sequenced. Their skin is the color of the cambium itself: old ivory in the high chambers, rusted iron near the flared bases where the floor rises to meet them. It is cool, faintly giving, like very dense cork or the interior of a horn. They photosynthesize a little, a private lattice-work that needs no green and almost no water; the rest of their energy they take from the towns’ harmonic engines, which pump temperature, charge, and sound the way other worlds pump sap. Internally they are chambered, light, their lymph a warm resin that never learned thirst. Eyes like dark resin, seeing in the permanent dim. Limbs long, jointed in echoes of the hunters but slower, ceremonial in a different key. They do not sweat. They do not salivate. Speech, when it happens at all, is often hummed into the chord or conducted by touch; during spore-season, when the air becomes a glittering circuit, conversation is almost entirely tactile, a closing of distances that the etiquette of interiors has already taught them to tax carefully.

They practice an engineering that does not distinguish horticulture from architecture from applied topology. Rooms are larger inside than their skins because the skins remember being vascular and have never stopped. A stair that follows the old helix will, if walked with the right attention, deliver you not only upward but later, or into a chamber that exists primarily as a standing wave in the column’s song. They harvest the slow reefs—waist-high thickenings of the floor, worn as everything is worn—cutting living plugs and setting them into the columns, where they take without fuss, knitting dry to dry. Repair is not construction; it is remembering. A room in the third town can be, for an hour, a room in the seventh: not a door, but a negotiated identity of interiors, held by the same horticulture that makes the stairs misbehave. They use it sparingly, the way other cultures use roads, because the holding costs something in the chord, a brief flattening every living thing feels as weather. Children learn this etiquette before they learn the names of the flocks. Identity is a local custom here, precious because the alternative is the finishedness that already sits under everything.

They have no word that means thirst. They have a word that means the memory of a solvent, and it is liturgical, used the way some peoples use the names of dead gods: carefully, and not in the house. In the sudden awful quiet of the attenuations—those unmarked places where the living ground thins over older stone and the chord drops out—they sometimes stand for a long time, listening to the mineral the organism once ate and then became too complete to need. It is not pilgrimage. It is maintenance of a distinction the plateau itself no longer bothers to make.

Nothing here is young. Nothing is finished becoming, because it finished so long ago that becoming is no longer a local custom. And still it is not still. A kite turns on its many joints. A grazer band crops a swell so ancient it is almost only an idea of height. A hunter’s falling scale is taken up by the columns and returned, transfigured, until the open country is one sentence. In the towns the hum continues: part machine, part metabolism, harmonic engines seated in what wood became when it learned to do without water. A spore-season is coming; the air already has that faint metallic taste. The pale coin slides a hand’s width. The floor, which is the forest, which is the body, which is the oldest city, conducts every footstep, every clatter, every ring, every hummed word into the chord and does not distinguish one weight from any other season of pressure it has known.

The plants did not mind. They became the ground, and the ground became a throat, and the throat has not stopped sounding, and what lives in the sounding has never needed to be anything else.
