The first lives you meet here are the ones already walking the benches when the light is still only a bruise. They are not goats, though the word will do for a while. Their bodies are built low and heavy, a compact engine of muscle and hollow-fiber thatch the color of wet iron and rusted fracture. The thatch is not fur; each strand is a sealed tube that holds a thimble of the thin air against the skin, so that when they move they seem to carry their own small climate with them. Their gait is a series of deliberate tests: a hoof—broad, cleft, edged with the same pale mineral the vents lay down—touches, pauses, feels for the newest joint or the slick of melt, then commits. It looks mechanical only because the ground itself is still deciding how to be ground. They travel in those loose, breathing constellations, never quite a herd, never quite separate, the spaces between them filled with the same cold air they use the way other animals use cover. Breath is language here. They shape the steam of it into hanging signs that last just long enough for the next body to read before the wind hisses them apart. Horns, if that is the word, are paired spirals of keratin grown around cores of deposited sinter; they lower them to the rock and stand locked, listening to the plate’s slow shove the way you might listen to weather. They do not startle when the deep note comes. They have been practicing the same patience the land is practicing.

They graze little. The wiry grass is more scaffolding than food. What they scrape and rasp are the lichens, iron-orange and sage, and what they wade for in the standing skins of water are the thumbnail crustaceans that turn the silt with industrial calm. A single animal may spend an hour in water no deeper than its hock, the ice forming and unforming around its locked legs, while its tongue works the bottom like a slow dredge. They treat time as another kind of elevation: something you wait inside until it changes.

Above them the clickers work the updrafts that peel off rock still warm from below. They are dark to the point of oil-sheen, compact, wings more vane than feather, riding the shreds of cloud that catch and tear on the rising land. They do not sing. The sound they make is a rapid internal click, pebbles in a dry throat, used to map the bruise-light and the heat-roads that rise from the vent ranks. Their numbers seem wrong until you understand they are not feeding on the land so much as on its weather: the midges that hatch in the seeps, the larvae that dimple the black shallows, the parasites that live in the thatch of the walkers. They nest in the newest cracks, lining them with moss and a regurgitated cement that hardens almost as fast as the mineral terraces grow. A nest may be used for a season and then abandoned when the joint opens wider or the vent finds a new throat a meter to the left. They treat the unfinished the way the walkers do—as ordinary.

In the delays themselves—the calf-deep, algae-dark waters that have not yet been given permission to become lakes—life is denser and more secret. The larvae are translucent, segmented, pulsing bags that rise and fall in clouds, filtering mineral and the faint iron taste of old snow. They dimple the surface in patterns that look almost deliberate, as if they too are trying to invent a current. Beneath them the crustaceans work with the patience of a foundry that has no deadline. Pale as the underside of ice, they turn the silt not randomly but in slow, repeating gyres that carve micro-channels; over days those channels deepen a finger’s width, then another, so that when the land finally tilts a fraction more the water will know which way to run. You hear them only if you kneel, and even then the sound is more felt than heard: a soft, continuous rearrangement, like gravel being sorted in the dark. The cold comes up through the knees too fast; most things here do not kneel.

Other lives emerge only if you stay still long enough for the place to continue without you. Along the vent ranks, where steam breathes in low, wool-muffled rhythm and the terraces lengthen while you watch, there are the heat-scuttlers: small, many-legged, their bodies glazed with the same sinter they eat. They convert fresh mineral into a kind of living slag, packing it into the growing lips of the terraces, speeding the work the water has already begun. They do not mind the temperature that would scald; their blood, if it is blood, runs as hot as the issue from the earth. They appear and disappear with the shifting throats, as mid-process as everything else.

Rarer still are the ones that use the emptiness the way the others use the air or the water. They stand. At first they look like stones the size of a person, or like the land’s own unfinished statuary, lichen-covered, moss-bearded, their outlines blurred by the light that has no edges. They are tall and thin, wrapped in layers they cultivate on themselves—the same iron-orange and black-green that cling to the newest fractures. They stand for days at the lip of a hollow or along a fault that has not decided whether it is ridge or wound, listening with their whole length. When they move it is only as far as the next seep or the next rank of vents, and they move the way the herds move: testing, locking, waiting. Their faces, when the cloud knits just so, are indistinct, more mineral gleam than feature. They do not build because they have never needed to; the cold is shelter enough when you wear the mountain’s own skin, and the standing waters are both drink and delay. Sometimes they wade, sometimes they simply stand in the steam until the mineral begins to crust them too. They are not shepherds, not quite. They are another punctuation, another way the emptiness is used. The walkers pass them without startle; the clickers ride the thermals they create simply by existing, tall and still, in a place that has no mid-distance furniture.

All of them treat the deep working—the pressure in the molars, the single felt note—as weather. They have been here long enough that uplift is just another change in the light, another reason the ice forms and unforms in the same hour. Nothing they do looks finished. Their trails across the gravel are faint and quickly rewritten by melt and wind. Their young, when they have them, are born looking even more provisional: the walkers’ calves already thatched but still learning the lock of the joints, the clickers’ fledglings more vane than wing, the scuttlers already glazed but still deciding which terrace to join. Even the still-standers seem mid-growth, as if they might yet decide to become part of the next ridge.

Stand among them long enough and you begin to feel the larger life that is not any one of them. It is the place itself still arriving, the slow industrial shove that has not been switched off. The animals, the larvae, the vent-things, the tall stillnesses—they are not inhabitants so much as the first sentences the land is speaking while it lifts. They punctuate. They delay. They turn silt and scrape lichen and ride the unfinished air. They live in the spaces between one presence and the next, and those spaces are still being made. The cold feels permanent only because the altitude has not yet been revised. When the table of rock finishes rising, or when it stops, they will still be here, or they will have become the next thing the mountain tries. Nothing is old. Everything is still being lifted.
