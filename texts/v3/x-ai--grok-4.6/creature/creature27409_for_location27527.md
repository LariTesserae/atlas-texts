The chamber does not keep pets. What occupies it has the quality of a secretion that learned, over an uncounted interval, to secrete itself. The first impression is of density without hierarchy: bodies fitted into every unused millimeter the way condensate fits the underside of a rib. Nothing claims a territory because the room itself is the only territory, and it was finished before any of them arrived, or condensed, or were poured.

Begin with the water-dwellers, because the water is the oldest motion. The grebe-analogue is not a bird that forgot how to fly; it is a neck that remembered a different gravity. The cervical column has one extra hinge, set just behind the skull, so that the head can remain perfectly level while the body yaws a few degrees off true. It paddles the same two meters because those two meters are the only length the shallows were ever given. The wake it leaves is not a disturbance but a measurement: each stroke recalibrates the surface tension so the next bead from the nearest pore will strike at the designed angle. Its eyes are the color of wet enamel and do not blink. When the light (such as it is) catches them, they show a nictitating membrane that has grown cloudy from never needing to clear. It does not fish. It does not preen. Once in a great while a pale crustacean will climb onto its back; the grebe-thing neither notices nor dislodges. The passenger is simply another bead that chose a warmer trajectory.

The crustaceans themselves are a tiling, not a population. They are the color of skim milk left in a copper bowl. Their carapaces have lost the last suggestion of segmentation and become a single, flexible plaque that can lock edge-to-edge with its neighbors, forming temporary pavements across the gratings. They feed by rasping the biofilm with mouthparts that look like the scoring on the fabricated cornice outside—too regular, too intentional. When one is consumed by another the act is silent and exact: the eater does not grow, the eaten is not missed. The ratio holds. Beneath them, in the dark between the two gratings, something longer and thinner works the same arithmetic on a slower clock. You never see more than a suggestion of jointed shadow, but the thrum you feel through the soles of your boots is their feeding, or their breathing, or the two made indistinguishable by cold.

In the air the traffic is older than flight. The membranous ones are not moths and not bats; they are the chamber’s way of circulating the condensing layer without ever allowing a draft. Their wings are veined with the same copper-green as the sweating pipes, and each vein is a capillary that drinks the beads directly from the atmosphere. They do not hunt. Their mouths work because the mouths are part of the pump. They follow paths so precisely overlapping that the air itself has worn grooves: if you stand still long enough you can feel the faint, regular pressure of a wingtip passing through the space your cheek will occupy in another three seconds. They have no individual deaths. A torn membrane is simply reabsorbed by the next passer, the way a rill reabsorbs a splash. Their eyes—when the dimness lets you find them—are clusters of unlit glass, the same glass as the blocks set into the walls. It is possible they are the interior faces of those blocks, extruded into the room and given just enough motility to keep the fog from settling into a permanent blindness.

Under the false ledges the heaps breathe. They are not amphibians; the word implies a life that could choose land. These have too many joints because each joint is a valve. Their skins are the exact temperature of the water that weeps across them, so that the boundary between body and drip has dissolved. They pile not for warmth (there is none to share) but because the pile is the only shape that lets every throat reach the air at the designed interval. The chorus they make is not communication. It is the audible portion of the compressors’ exhaust, pitched a quarter-tone off so the two frequencies beat against each other and produce the low, wet throb that you first mistook for silence. Their throats go in sequence, never overlapping, never pausing. If you watch long enough you see that the sequence is the same as the sequence of beads detaching from the pores above them. Cause and effect have been soldered together and then forgotten.

The long body in the gutter is the only one that looks as if it might once have had a name. Furred, yes, but the fur is a pelt of mineralized algae, each hair a hollow tube that wicks moisture inward. It occupies the curl of gutter the way a tongue occupies a mouth: not resting, not waiting, simply completing the circuit. Its eyes (there are more than two) track you without the head moving. The tracking is not curiosity. It is the room acknowledging a gradient and beginning, instantly, to flatten it. While you watch, a film of water closes over the place your boot compressed the marsh. The long body does not shift. It was already accounting for you.

There are others you will never isolate. Eyes that are not on the outside of the glass because the glass is a membrane and something on the far side of the membrane is using the fog as a cornea. A sweetness under the metal that is not decay but the collected exhalation of every throat, recycled so many times it has acquired a flavor like wet iron and old paper. Occasional pale fronds that are not the planted ferns but mobile, drifting, attaching for a few cycles to a rib or a crustacean’s back before dissolving back into the film. The ferns themselves occupy rather than grow because occupation is the only verb the room permits. Their white tips are not frost; they are the precise point at which chlorophyll was told it had come far enough.

None of them startle because startling would be a change in rate, and the chamber has no unused rates. Their motions were poured at the same time as the rills and the false-cirque. They are the fauna of an hour that was built to last, the occupancy of a wound the mountain (which is not a mountain) refuses to close. If they were stocked, the stocking was done by the same hands that scored the bedding planes too evenly. If they condensed, they are a second distillate of the same pores that weep the water. Either way, they are not visitors. They are the reason the volume feels heavier than its furnishings, the living weather that makes the manufactured weather possible.

You are the only thing that was not anticipated, and therefore the only thing the room must work to erase. A wing occupies the space your shoulder vacated. A crustacean tile clicks back into place over your footprint. The grebe-thing’s wake remains identical. Stay, and the cold behind your eyes becomes another pressure the compressors have already budgeted for. Leave, and the seam forgets you with the same efficiency it once forgot how to be a door. Behind you the chamber continues its single, unfinished sentence: wet, dim, crowded past reason, the first moment of a geology that will never be allowed to become itself, and the lives inside it—whatever they are, however they began—hold that moment open like a mouth that has forgotten it was ever meant to close.
