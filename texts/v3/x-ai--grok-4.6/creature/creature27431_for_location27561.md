The lives here do not begin. They precipitate, the way the oilier water does on metal that has never been cool enough to deserve it. A film first, then a thickening, then something that decides it has joints.

Along every rust-channel the jointed tasters pick their way. They are not insects and they are not machines; the distinction has not been ratified in this country. Each limb ends in a mouth that is also a palpus, a small wet vice that tastes iron, slag-dust, the faint sweetness of whatever the factories have just finished forgetting. They move by tasting forward. A limb finds a rivet still warm from some earlier century of pour, clamps, samples the alloy, and only then does the rest of the body follow. Their bodies are pale the way old insulation is pale, segmented the way a cooling crack is segmented. Too many joints, yes, but the extras are not decoration. They are contingencies. When the ground rearranges itself (a shelf becoming a drop, a drop deciding it was always a fan of rubble), the extra joints simply fold into the new interval and keep tasting. They do not startle because startling would require a before and an after, and they live only in the long middle.

The helmet-crabs graze the weeping. They are the size of a man’s head if the man had been poured rather than born, carapace the color of condensed milk left too long in a tin. They do not scuttle. They lean. Each claw is a slow spoon that lifts the condensing film, drinks it, and sets the emptied metal back as if returning a borrowed tool. Their undersides are soft, clicking faintly against the stone when they shift weight; the clicks are not communication but the sound of cartilage remembering it used to be something hotter. Colonies of them occupy the same square of dripping girder for what would be seasons if seasons existed. They eat the film, they eat the slower moss-that-is-felt, and when one grows too heavy its shell simply splits along an old pour-line and the new crab steps out already the same size, already grazing. The old shell stays, becomes another housing, another remnant the air occupies.

In the deeper channels (never deep, only less shallow) the patient one lies. You know it first as a refusal: the black water will not quite lie flat, a warmth that is not the air’s warmth breathes against your shin. It is large the way a shed is large, long the way a conveyor is long. Parts of it are muscle. Parts of it are cable that never learned it was not muscle. It does not hunt. Hunting is sudden and this place has outlawed suddenness. It occupies. When a taster or a crab or a length of discarded product drifts near, the water simply becomes mouth for a while, then becomes water again. The swallowed thing is not killed so much as enrolled. Later you may see a pale crab grazing along what you took for a piling and realize the piling is flexing, very slowly, like a throat that has decided to be architecture. The patient one is not a single animal. It is the channels’ way of staying unfinished.

Under the walkways the click-colonies hold. Soft bodies the color of wet felt, packed so close they share skins. They click not in rhythm but in consideration, each click a small collapse of air, a drip with intention. Wings exist among them, membranous, veined with something that might once have been wire. The wings do not fly. They extend into the thick dark and feel its resistance, mapping the rooms that were never built, the distances that will not stay agreed-upon. A wing brushes the darkness and the darkness brushes back, slightly oily, slightly warm. Information passes that way: where the next press will close (in a season, or two), where the water is showing that other, paler sky, where a ridge of slag has begun to want to be geology and should be avoided until it finishes deciding. The colonies eat what precipitates and what the factories leave and, without hurry, one another. A body that has clicked its last simply softens into the mat and is tasted by its neighbors over the course of a long dusk.

There are larger occupants. They move through the intervals the way you do: sideways, one foot lifted higher than seems reasonable. Their silhouettes are shed-shaped, tank-shaped, sometimes almost person-shaped if the person had been assembled from cooling cracks and leftover cable. Skin weeps the same oilier water the metal weeps. They tend the factories, though “tend” is too brisk a word. A figure will stand beside a press for the entire slow closure, one palm against the housing, matching heat, matching the held-breath quiet. When the press finally opens, whatever has been made (a coil that is also a corridor, a gear whose teeth are doors) is already old, already occupied by clickers or films. The tender does not take it. The tender simply turns, very slowly, and begins the equally long walk toward the next machine that will need witnessing. Their faces, when they have them, are unfinished: pour-marks still visible, eyes that do not so much see as condense the dark into something they can drink. They do not speak. Speech would require names for directions, and names last only as long as they are repeated. They repeat nothing. They have already arrived.

Some live in the reflections that precede. You see the shed at your shoulder before you have finished walking toward it; the water around its pilings already holds a figure that has not yet stepped out of the thickness. These reflected ones are slightly paler, slightly more finished, as if the other sky (the one pricked with rivets or stars) has lent them a draft of completion the country itself has not yet granted. They do not stay. When the real shed arrives they are already gone, or have become the sweat on its bricks, or have stepped sideways into one of the unbuilt rooms where the same slow engines work and the same animals do not need to see them.

Everything here eats the leftover heat, the leftover metal, the leftover decisions the land has not finished making. Nothing is finished, so nothing quite dies. A tender whose heat has matched the air too perfectly simply weeps into the rocks until the weeping is indistinguishable, and later a new jointed taster tastes the spot and finds it familiar. A crab shell becomes a housing. A length of muscular cable decides it was always a channel-dweller. The factories keep producing objects that have no inventory name because the objects are already lives, already occupying the space between bodies until the air itself is a third material, wet, warm, and slightly conductive.

They do not need to be seen. The darkness has thickness enough to hold them. You could stand until your own outline softened into occupancy, until the intervals learned you, and they would continue their uncounted work: tasting, grazing, occupying, clicking, tending the presses that close over seasons, becoming the next version of the same unfinished country, in the dark that is not night, without any particular need for you to have been there at all.
