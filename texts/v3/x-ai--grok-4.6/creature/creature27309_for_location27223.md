The basin does not keep a tenant the way a house does. Nothing rooted, nothing warm-blooded, nothing that would leave a nest or a scat or a second winter’s scratch on stone. The description is exact on that point and remains exact. Yet the room is occupied. Occupation here is a thinner, slower claim, assembled from posture, residue, and the particular way light and melt refuse to be merely weather.

At first the occupation seems to belong to the ice. The blades and keels do not stand; they attend. Every one of them lists a few degrees west, toward the notched lip, as though a single instruction had been given once and never rescinded. Their sunward skins are pitted, glossy, in constant small expenditure; their shaded skins stay furred and still, holding last night’s rime like a private weather. They crowd so that a body must turn sideways in the densest alley, and in that turning the ice becomes briefly architectural—nave, screen, rood-loft of frozen water. A pane of clear ice will catch the mineral sky and lay a trembling coin of it on the back of a hand, then keep the coin there while the hand moves. The drip from the melting faces has syntax: slower, more separate notes in the blue morning; a quicker, overlapping chatter at the height of the glare. When a blade finally unweights, the report is dry and single, followed by a long granular slide, after which the grammar of drip simply acquires one new voice where the stump bleeds. This is not metaphor added afterward. The place itself keeps offering the comparison—congregation, cloister, figures told to look west—and then withdrawing it, because the ice is also only ice, cycling through spend and retraction, corridor and closure, season after season, while the schist walls remain the finished argument they have been for longer than any leaning.

The human traces complicate the occupancy without resolving it. The slumped crescent of slabs is still a lee; the cobbles inside it are paler, as if they remember being turned. The hammerstone lies where it was set down, its equator bruised, the block it was dressing left with one face only begun. The iron pin has wept its long rust teardrop down the noon-crack and will go on weeping. The pecked marks weather into the general fluting; the row of foreign quartz cobbles stays aligned on its boot-wide ledge, white against the dark wreck of the floor. These things are not native and not recent. Ice has advanced around them and drawn back around them many times. They persist as a kind of unfinished sentence in a language the basin did not invent. Someone carried the quartz. Someone chose the crack that takes sun at midday. Someone sat or slept sitting up long enough for two courses of the wind-break to matter. That someone is not present in any ordinary sense, yet the arrangement continues to occupy the east wall the way the ice occupies the center: by remaining, by having been placed with care, by refusing to become merely more cobbles. The rust stain lengthens on a scale no visit can watch. The paler stones in the lee keep their paleness. If this is habitation, it is habitation as remainder, a habit of order that the cycles have not yet erased.

Even that account feels premature, a settling. The basin’s real occupancy is more distributed and less personal. It lives in the local economy of water: the rust-colored seep that is thread by day and tooth by night, the tea-colored pockets that skin over and dissolve while they are watched, the ancient exits through the cobbles that darken and then vanish into the mountain’s body. It lives in the scarce sounds—the glass-harmonica of thinned wind along the keels, the single ringing of a wafer of rock at night, the way breath becomes a brief white architecture and collapses. It lives in the light’s arguments, which are sharper here than memory usually allows: transmissions, white fire, wandering coins, edges that could cut cloth. Shadows have the authority of cut stone. The sky at the zenith is near-black, and the glare that remains of thought arrives already too bright to be useful.

A further layer emerges only if one stays past the first inventory. In the exact middle, facing the notch, the wind-break, the rust teardrop, and the quartz row fall into a line no present visitor arranged. The alignment may be accident—the basin has only so many sheltered facts, and they have settled into the only balance the walls permit—or it may be the faint continuation of the same attention that made the ice lean west. Either way, the line occupies the space the way a pulse occupies a neck: quietly, insistently, counting something that is not for the person who hears it. The place insists on being a room, a crowd, and a clock at once, and the insistence itself is what inhabits. It does not require a body. It uses the blades as its congregation, the traces as its liturgy, the drip as its hours, the sudden fall of a keel as its only punctuation. Night retracts everything to teeth and feathers; dawn begins the spending again. Over a season corridors close and open. Over many seasons the grove keeps its westward stance. The occupant is that keeping.

What lives here, then, is not a creature and not quite a ghost. It is the basin’s decision to remain a cloister open to distances that cannot be usefully thought, a decision enacted equally by ice, by leftover work, by light with too many edges, and by the thin air that turns every remaining thought into glare. The midge that dies in a sun-pocket is only a brief error; the iron-and-leaf rumor in a crack is only evidence of a greener world that does not apply. The true tenancy is the posture itself—the leaning, the alignment, the cycle that is neither hurried nor still. When a visitor recrosses the lip, the brightness follows a few steps down the ridge and then cuts off, as if the room had a door. Behind the door the occupancy continues, unconcerned, dripping, frosting, attending west.
