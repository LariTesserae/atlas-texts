The first lives are the ones that do not move. In the wet seams a green wire uncoils, a fern reduced to its own vascular afterthought, rooting only where condensation never quite stops. Beside it the tea-colored moss holds the stone the way a bruise holds skin. Higher, on the cooler north faces, the succulent is trained by patient hands into a slow calligraphy of pale pads; it drinks the film that beads on iron and gives back almost nothing. Three clay pots sit under a drip that has not failed in living memory. The bitter leaf they raise is harvested leaf by leaf, never enough to be a crop, only enough to stain a cup and remind the tongue that something still chooses to grow here. These are not decorations. They are the settlement’s only admitted wilderness, stains that happen to photosynthesize, clinging to architecture the way rumor clings to a new wall.

Everything else that lives here walks, or once walked, or will walk again after the next shift.

They come into focus the way the light does: from below, from the side, never quite from above. Faces take the furnace-glow first, then the caged filaments, then the dull green coins of surface-noon that arrive already used. Skin is the color of the air they breathe, a warm tea that never quite clears. Sweat is constant and unremarked; it leaves a fine mineral map at hairline and collarbone, a glittering that is not jewelry. Clothes are few and thin, chosen for the minutes they take to dry. They smell of gypsum and old steam even when freshly washed. Soft soles are the civic law; the stone still has edges, and the custom of quiet has soaked into leather and cloth the way salt soaks into brick.

They move the way heat teaches movement: slowly, with the body’s full attention on which railing can be trusted, which pipe is only warm, which patch of floor still remembers the last rain. Children learn the map before they learn letters. A child can tell you, without looking, where the condensation will start this hour and which gallery will stay dry enough to strike a match. They do not run. Running is a surface habit, something that happens twenty steps up and is left there, like the paler dust they sometimes bring back on their shoulders.

There are no old people in the way other towns have old people. Age here is a thickening, a slower blink, a deeper knowledge of which surfaces blister. The oldest still work the wire-draw or the bearing shop because the machines prefer familiar hands and the rock has not yet decided to forget them. They sit in the almost-cool hour before the fires are stirred, when a finer rain falls and the whole chamber eases by a single degree that everyone feels in the lungs. Conversations then are longer, but still pitched for the size of the room. No one calls from gallery to gallery. Sound travels too well; courtesy has become structural.

Families occupy the stacked rooms the way cargo occupies a hold: efficiently, with affection measured in the space it does not take. A dwelling may have one brick wall and three of living rock, the corners still sharp enough to catch cloth, the joints already blooming their first white frost of salt. Laundry disappears in minutes and returns tasting of the ceiling. Meals are eaten close, because the alternative is to shout across a heat that already lives in the mouth. Children sleep in the inner rooms where the stone is thickest and the ticking of expansion is a lullaby. At night (a word that only means the glass coins have gone out) the settlement contracts into itself. Arguments stay behind doors. Public space is for crossing, for the brief markets when a delivery comes down the stair: sacks, ingots, a crate of the leaf, news from a country that no longer feels like theirs.

They are proud of the newness. A threshold that still has its factory edge is a small boast. Fresh rooms open in the eastern wall by patient cutting, never blasting; the rubble is crushed and fired into more of the brick that stacks them higher. The settlement eats its own wound and grows. People pause to watch a new face of stone come into the dim light, as if watching a relative arrive. Time feels thick here; a wall is stained before it is finished, a machine is already being repaired by the next shift while it still runs. Sequential things overlap, and the people have learned to live in the overlap without surprise.

They go up. They come back down. The surface is another climate, another set of rules about wind and animals and distances that do not end in rock. They do not linger in the mouth of the stair. The heat below has become the condition of home; the lungs expect it, the eyes have already adjusted, the quiet has already closed around them like a courtesy the town extends to itself.

Stand in the central floor when the furnaces open their mouths and the air turns the color of weak tea. You will see them all at once, which is both privilege and sentence: the slow figures crossing on catwalks, the hands that know every valve, the children mapping railings with their palms, the old ones sitting in the almost-cool, the bitter leaf drying in its three pots, the green wires in their seams. Nothing else lives here. The absence of every other creature has a weight of its own; it makes every human sound arrive over-clear, a cough or a laugh or the set-down of a cup spoken into a held breath. The rock is still learning the shape it was given. The people have already learned it. They inhale with the boilers. A brief rain starts, locally, as if a cloud had been born at arm’s reach, and then it stops, and they go on, in the dim, in the heat, in a quiet so complete it has become the way they love the place that holds them.
