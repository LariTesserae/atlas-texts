The groves are the first and last inhabitants, though they do not think of themselves that way. Each stand is a single slow metabolism distributed through hundreds of pale trunks and the overlapping shields of their foliage. They drink almost nothing. What moisture they keep is cycled internally, recaptured from their own exhalations, perhaps even coaxed in trace from the unrelenting light itself through reactions no other plant has ever needed. Their roots fuse into the bedrock and do not wander. A grove expands or contracts by a few meters across a human lifetime, following pulses too faint and too deep for any surface observer to name. Inside, the air is still and a degree less cold; the light is filtered to a green twilight that never quite warms. Sound dies there. A leaf may reorient with an almost inaudible tick. That is the grove speaking, if it speaks at all.

Between the groves the land is not empty. It is occupied by things that treat distance as a kind of weather. The largest of them is the color of the dust itself, low-slung and broad, its hide matte so that even at noon it throws almost no highlight. It walks for weeks without hurry, crossing the pale gravel and the yardangs with a gait so even it seems mechanical until you notice how it pauses, now and then, to taste the air with a pair of blunt, branching organs that fold back against its skull. It does not run. Nothing here runs. When it reaches a grove it feeds with methodical patience on fallen fronds and the lower cushions, grinding them slowly, then lies for a day or two in the filtered dimness before it leaves again. Its kind is so rare that two of them meeting is an event that might not occur in a century. When they do meet they stand near one another for a long time, flanks almost touching, and then part without haste. No one has seen them born or die. Their tracks last only until the next wind.

Smaller lives appear as afterthoughts. In the brief hours when the sun is low enough that the light turns the varnish on the rocks to a dull bronze, pale moth-like forms lift from the undersides of the largest leaves. Their wings are dry paper, their bodies almost weightless. They do not feed in any ordinary sense; they drift, they settle, they vanish again when the light hardens. Beneath the gravel, in the hairline fractures the groves themselves follow, there are burrowers the size of a clenched fist. They surface only after the night’s frost has already gone, leaving tiny, perfectly round openings that the wind erases before midday. Their eyes are vestigial. They taste mineral and the faintest chemical traces the groves leak downward.

There are others that never quite resolve into individuals. In the long aerial rivers that the highland generates, thin veils of something living sometimes ride the dust for days: filaments so fine they catch the light like the iridescence at the edge of vision. They do not land. They may be related to the moth-forms, or they may be something older that learned to stay aloft when the surface first dried. In the coldest hollows, after the deepest nights, a glitter that is not quite frost sometimes lingers an hour longer than it should. It is not ice. It is a film of organisms that wake only then, convert the last of the night’s cold into a brief chemical flush, and are gone by the time a traveler’s breath has stopped steaming.

Nothing here claims the groves as home in the way a forest creature would. They are way-stations, or lungs, or simply the only places where the light is not a blade. Predation, when it happens, is an event measured in seasons: a shadow that is not a cloud passing over a grazing form, a brief struggle too distant for sound, then the pale dust settling again. The winner does not linger. There is no chorus, no constant small motion. Densities are so low that an entire lifetime can pass without one living thing seeing another of its kind.

And yet the place is inhabited more thoroughly than it first appears. The groves themselves are colonies of more than trees. In the twilight understory there are cushion plants whose surfaces bloom with the same reflective powder, and among their fronds move things no larger than grains of the finest dust: translucent, almost mineral, feeding on the microscopic films the night frost leaves. They are the true recapturers, the ones that close the last loops the larger plants cannot. A fallen trunk may lie for centuries, becoming sculpture, and still these smallest lives work it, molecule by molecule, until it is dust again. Time here is not empty. It is occupied by metabolisms too patient to notice interruption.

No one has left a path. No one has stacked a cairn. If there are minds among these lives they do not mark the stone. They have had longer than any traveler’s history to decide that marking is unnecessary. The alignments some groves show—the shared lean of trunks, the openings that face the same compass point—are only wind and light acting across ages. The inhabitants have reached the same equilibrium as the land itself. They live by refusing hurry, by recapturing what little is given, by treating the enormous brightness and the thin cold as simply the conditions of being. A single life, however long, remains a brief interruption in the glare. The highland does not notice. It continues, brightly, coolly, and almost silently, and everything that lives there has learned to continue with it.
