The basin does not keep a census. What lives here is counted in plates and columns and disturbances, in the wet ticking that never quite becomes a voice. Begin lowest, where the water table stands in the gutters like a second street and the brick itself sweats metal. There the cockroaches have made themselves architecture. They do not scatter so much as peel away in sheets when a boot or a falling pipe disturbs them, dark lacquered bodies locking edge to edge under the tanks and along the undersides of the old conveyor housings. They eat the grease that still weeps from bearings no one has turned in years, and the grease, in this heat, never quite hardens. Their egg cases cling in ridges along the warmest pipes. A generation is a week. They have no interest in the light.

Above them the midges make the air grainy. In any shaft of the dim white sky they become a single moving thing, a column that leans and reforms, eating whatever microscopic film the water leaves on itself. They do not bite much; the chemistry has taken the edge off hunger or given it another shape. At dusk—which is most of the afternoon—they rise in such numbers that the bats do not so much hunt as swim. The bats themselves are small, quick, stitching the vapor between cooling towers and the sawtooth roofs. Their droppings stripe the brick in long pale runnels that the next rain never quite washes clean. They roost in the hollow cones of the towers by the tens of thousands, a sound like soft paper when they leave all at once, then nothing.

The flooded cellars belong to the frogs, or to the things that have kept the frog’s outline while forgetting its voice. They are everywhere the water stands more than a hand’s depth: a continuous small disturbance, bodies rising and settling, throats working without song. Heat has taught them silence. Their skins take the color of wet rust or of the silt that has baked into pottery; you see them only when they move, and they move like thoughts you almost had. They eat the midges and the smaller roaches and one another. Tadpoles—if that is still the word—cloud the warmer shallows in black commas that become, in a matter of days, more of the same wet ticking. No heron comes for them. Nothing green remains to hide a heron.

Rats treat the old rail cuts as roads. The tracks are gone or buried; what remains is a linear marsh of warm standing water and slag, and the rats run its edges in single file, heads low, tails writing brief messages in the film. They are sleek from the same grease the roaches work, and from whatever still dies in the sheds. Their burrows open under the collapsed lintels and in the salt-crusted concrete where it has heaved. You hear them more than you see them: a sudden dry rush, then the basin taking the sound and thinning it to nothing. They do not fear the dogs because the dogs are rumor. Packs exist as distances, as droppings steamed open by the heat, as a single far bark that the vapor flattens into something almost geological. The dogs themselves, when they appear, are long-legged and pale, moving with the unhurried economy of things that have learned how little the city will give. They drink from the canals and leave again. The spaces between them are measured in hundreds of meters of blank wall.

Birds hold the upper volume. The cooling towers are their cathedrals, tall enough and hollow enough that the colonies inside them would be wonders anywhere else. They nest on every ledge the rust has made, white streaks running down the truncated cones like slow water. They eat the midges in great circling harvests and drop into the canal for the frogs when the heat eases a degree. Their wings, leaving, are the loudest ordinary thunder the basin allows. Then the air closes. Nothing fills it.

At night something larger uses the canal. It does not hurry. By morning there is only the fading V of a wake and a smell of mud and old iron, as if the water itself had shifted in its sleep. No one has a reliable shape for it. Those who still keep boats speak of a back broader than a door, of eyes that catch the last gantry lamps like wet coins, of a patience that belongs to the heat rather than to hunger. It may be many things, or one thing that has lived here longer than the mills. It does not break the surface often. The frogs go still when it passes.

People remain the rarest texture. They cling to the slightly higher ground the way stains cling to the brick: a cloth in a window, a boat tied where the street forgets it is not a channel, a man crossing a yard with a bucket already small against the shed wall. The terrace houses whose steps now end in water hold rooms larger than the lives inside them. A few still draw from the old grid; lamps make small brown islands that do not quite reach one another. They move water, or they wait for the compressor two streets over to kick on and remind them the skeleton still twitches. They fish the canals with lines that come up heavy with frogs or with nothing. They know the night thing by its absence in the morning. They do not call this ruin. They have no other climate. Their children, when there are children, learn the wet ticking before they learn words, and the taste of metal in every puddle, and the way the heat does not break. Presence is scarce. They are part of the count, not the crowd.

What lives here has eaten the green and kept the rest: the heat, the water that never recedes, the machines that have become geology with windows, the long vacancies between one occupied doorway and the next. Processes continue. The condensation runs its daily cycle on every pipe. The animals shift with the dimness. A conveyor jerks a meter and rests. None of it is waiting to become anything else. The basin holds them the way a kettle holds water—not dramatically, just completely.
