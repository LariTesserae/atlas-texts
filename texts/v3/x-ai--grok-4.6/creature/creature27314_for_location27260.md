The people of the notch are not a tribe with a name they speak to outsiders, because outsiders do not come. They are the ones who stayed when the last dry-season migrants moved on, generations back, and then stayed again when the cloud decided the basin would never fully lift. You meet them first as motion in the green: a forearm, wet to the elbow, reaching to redirect a drip; a child’s bare heel flashing on a notched root; an elder’s staff (itself a living shoot, encouraged straight and then cut only at the last) tapping the same joinery it tapped yesterday.

They are short-backed and sure-footed, built for ducking and for never quite standing fully upright except on the rare open platform. Skin stays pale where clothing covers it and takes a cool, almost bluish undertone where it does not; the constant film of water keeps sun from ever really naming a color. Hair is worn long and simply bound, heavy with the same moisture that darkens every timber. It is not unusual to see a strand of liverwort or a thread of moss caught there and left, as if the head itself were another fork for epiphytes. Their hands are the real chronicle: knuckles thickened from wet fiber, palms cross-hatched with old nicks from stone and the rare copper blade that must be honed after every few hours of green wood. Fingernails are short, often stained the tea-color of the standing water. When they grip something—a lashing, a child’s wrist, the rim of a clay hearth-bowl—the grip is complete and then immediately released, the way you handle anything that is still growing.

Clothing is never dry and is never meant to be. They wear layers of beaten inner bark and split vine, wrapped and re-wrapped so that water can run through rather than pool. A few own pieces of the old wool, felted and re-felted until it is more membrane than cloth; these are precious and are used as outer hoods when a rain cell sits on the ridge. Children go mostly bare except for a twisted-fiber belt that holds a small gourd or a packet of something edible. Jewelry, when it appears, is functional first: a copper pin that also serves as a fid for tightening lashings, a drilled stone that can be used as a weight or a sinker. They smell of wet tannin, woodsmoke that never quite catches, and the particular mineral sweetness of crushed moss.

They speak little when the water is loud, which is most of the time. Voices are pitched to carry through mist without shouting; a sentence often arrives already half-absorbed by leaves, so they have developed a habit of repeating the last phrase as confirmation, like an echo they make themselves. Names are short, often the names of plants or of particular drips or of the animals that refuse to be evicted. Children are taught the difference between a stem that may be cut and a stem that is holding someone else’s floor long before they are taught the difference between one person and another. Privacy is not solitude; it is the practiced decision not to look through the leaf-curtain two arm’s-lengths away. You can hear a couple arguing, or lovemaking, or the wet slap of someone beating fiber, and you simply keep walking the slick timber as if the sound were another form of rain.

Their work is the same conversation the houses are having with the trees. A morning is spent re-tying what yesterday’s growth has loosened, or encouraging an aerial root downward with a notch and a stone weight so that next season it will be a ladder. They fish the sheet-water with fiber nets so fine they look like drowned hair, taking the small cold fish that taste of tannin and mineral. They harvest fungus that fruits in the constant dusk under the densest platforms, and the bitter tubers that only sweeten after days of leaching in running rills. They do not store against a future dryness; they store against the next rearrangement. A basket of grain must be checked hourly for birds and for the dark, cat-sized mammals that use the same roots the people do and that will sit in a doorway, unafraid, until hissed at. Those mammals—sleek, almost otter-like in the wet, with intelligent, unyielding faces—are not pets and not quite pests. They are co-tenants. Sometimes a child will share a platform with one for an entire afternoon, neither yielding.

The animals are not background. They are the other households. Frogs occupy every scale: thumbnail frogs in the folds of leaves, fist-sized ones in the cisterns, and the pale, almost translucent ones that live inside the thickest moss mattresses and sing at a pitch just above the drip. Their egg-masses hang everywhere, gelatinous extra fruit that children are taught not to pop unless they are hungry and the season is right. Birds work in shifts so regular they could be a clock: the thin, loud mountain ones at the canopy edge, the heavier wet-forest ones that punch through understory like thrown stones, the ones that have learned to wait until a person sets a gourd down. At dusk the bats boil out of the deepest hollows in the old trunk, so many that for a few minutes the mist looks as if it has grown fur and then decided against it. Insects are weather. Midges in the cool hours make the air look carbonated. Larger beetles blunder into faces when the light lifts a degree. After a hard pulse of rain the armying insects braid the paths and the people simply step through, slapping, laughing, cursing the way you curse a relative who will not leave.

Nothing here is owned in the dry-land sense. A platform is “ours” until the limb decides to put out a spray of new growth that drips into the hearth, at which point it becomes a problem to be solved in common. The long split-log table is always wet at one end; people simply migrate toward the drier end as the day weeps on, leaving the soaked end to the frogs and the occasional mammal. Market is the daily unplanned spill of baskets onto the central platform: fish still twitching, packets of dyed fiber, a few hammered ornaments that gleam briefly when a rare shaft of light finds them, fungus wrapped in leaves, the half-tame dark mammals arguing over a scrap. It never starts. It never stops. Someone is always re-lashing. Someone is always chasing a bird out of the grain. Someone is always up in the canopy with a stone knife, redirecting a drip that has become inconvenient.

If you stay, the place begins to keep you. A moss print appears on the side you sleep on and does not entirely brush off. A particular frog decides your cup is an acceptable cistern and must be tipped out each morning, gently, because the children have named it. A path you used yesterday has already grown a little shut; someone who did not need to be asked has cut it open again with two strokes of an adze, leaving the living wood to seal the wound in its own time. The cloud lowers. Rain finds a new pitch. The people glance up, then return to the work of keeping water moving, bindings wet-tight, load-bearing stems uncut. They are not surviving the basin. They are the basin’s way of having hands.

The rock underneath remains patient, rounded, almost indifferent. Everything else—leaf, frog, child, lashing, bat, drip—continues the long, wet argument of staying.
