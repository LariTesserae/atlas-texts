The highland lives first. It is not host but inhabitant, not setting but the single avid presence that has mistaken itself for a world. Every ridge is a thought it has not finished having. Every vault is a lung it grew because the idea of breathing arrived before the idea of an outside. The mass does not contain life so much as it continually secretes the conditions under which other lives become possible, then reabsorbs them when their usefulness as commentary is spent. You feel this before you meet anything that could be called a creature: the walls tick, the floors lift by fractions of a finger-width while you rest, the air itself is exhaled from stomata the size of grottos and tastes of iron and unripe tannin, as though the highland were always on the verge of speaking and had chosen, for now, the older language of sap and frost.

Smaller lives appear as afterthoughts the mass has not yet decided to cancel. On the colder faces, films of luminescence crawl with the patience of bruises forming. They are not plants and not quite animals; they are the highland’s own chemistry remembering an older sun and leaking the memory as color. A patch will stain a hanging curtain of wood the dull purple of old blood, then fade, then reappear meters away, having crossed the intervening darkness by some slow, adhesive logic. They feed on the same sugars the larger grazers scrape, yet they ask so little that the highland rarely bothers to close over them. Their light is not for seeing. It is a confession, a faint admission that illumination once existed as a possibility and has since been filed under weather.

The grazers come next, or perhaps they were always there, flattened into the grain so thoroughly that distinguishing them from the living wood requires the patience of years. They have rasping mouths and bodies like compressed pith, limbs reduced to hooks or suckers or nothing at all. They drink the slow, viscous sap before it can glass, leaving faint, shiny trails that the cold soon hides. Some grow so still that the cambium rises around them, incorporating their bodies as living knots, galls that still pulse faintly with an independent hunger. Others migrate on a scale of seasons, following the rare condensation events, arriving at a mere just as the black water appears and leaving when it is drunk back into the walls. They are the highland’s most intimate conversation with itself: eat, be eaten, become another ring.

Then the singers. Their bodies are instruments first and animals second. Resonant chambers occupy most of their volume—coiled, branching, or simply vast hollows framed by thin living struts. They hang from the undersides of vaults or lodge themselves in fresh splits, inflating slowly, releasing notes so low the air seems to thicken around them. A single voice can last from one false dusk to the next; a chorus moving through a chain of chambers becomes indistinguishable from weather, a front of sound that the wind itself sometimes steals and plays back, changed. Their songs are maps, claims, warnings, courtships, all the same drone. Distance means little; a singer in one drowned valley can answer another ten kilometers of void away, the intervening membranes transmitting the vibration the way bone transmits a whisper. They do not fill the spaces. They ornament them, the way frost ornaments a blade of grass: briefly, completely, then gone when the temperature shifts.

Swarms move as coherent darknesses. They are not clouds so much as decisions the air has made to become briefly solid. Millions of small bodies—winged, drifting, or simply falling with purpose—harvest the continual slow snow of shed scales, pollen, and microscopic fragments the highland sloughs from itself. They click or hum in frequencies that the singers sometimes answer, sometimes ignore. A swarm passing through a greater hollow can last hours, a rustling night inside the night, and when it is gone the air tastes cleaner, as though something necessary had been performed. Predators treat them as weather too: a resource that arrives, is taken, and will arrive again.

The predators themselves are mostly geometry interrupted by stillness. They wait as part of the architecture. A fold in the wall is not a fold. A hanging root is not a root. A ceiling is not, for the moment, a ceiling. When the moment comes there is no lunge so much as a sudden, correct rearrangement of limbs, an unfolding that looks, in the rare patches of glow, like a theorem proving itself. They drop, or reach, or simply occupy the space a grazer or a stray singer had been using. Everything they eat has already eaten the highland, so the meal is only a delayed conversation. Afterward they become architecture again. Some remain so long in one attitude that the plant-mass begins to grow around them, and they allow it, becoming living corbels or temporary vault-ribs until hunger returns.

And then there are the ones who cut.

They are rare enough that each encounter feels like punctuation. Pale, long-limbed, their skin taking the tannin-stain until they are the same dark as the walls they move along. Eyes are ornamental; they navigate by the changing texture of air on skin, by the ticks and flexes of the living surfaces, by the way sound returns to them altered. Their hands (or the structures that serve as hands) know the difference between a stem that will lash and a stem that will heal the lashing into itself. They do not fight the growth. They join it. Their platforms of still-living wood become galls that think. Their rings of hardened exudate are calendars, windbreaks, or simply the first thought after hunger given lasting shape. Blades of split wood left in niches are already half-swallowed, the plant trying, as always, to file the tool under weather. Fiber ropes hang slack and furred with rime until they, too, are grown over.

These cutters endure rather than dwell. They move along the grain of the highland the way the highland itself moves: slowly, completely, without asking permission. They harvest sap knowing it will freeze, knowing the next mere may not appear for a local year, knowing the walls will not be where they left them. Their voices, when they use them, join the singers’ choruses so seamlessly that after a time the distinction collapses. Perhaps they have always been among the singing things. Perhaps the highland, being young and busy with its own becoming, has begun to think with their thoughts, using their cut-and-bind logic as one more internal pressure that shoves new ridges upward. Or perhaps they are only passing through, and the artifacts they leave are the highland’s way of remembering that something once distinguished itself long enough to make a mark.

Nothing here is finished, least of all the lives. The plant-mass breathes on a scale of years; a wall advances, a new vault swells like a bruise in reverse, a singer’s body is slowly walled into a living pipe that still, occasionally, sounds. Condensation events sweep through and the meres refill and the grazers arrive and the cutters, if they are near, pause to drink and then move on. The air remembers every sound and returns it changed. You do not inhabit this place. You are permitted, for a while, to move along its grain, another brief commentary the highland will eventually absorb, or grow around, or teach to sing. The emptiness itself feels like another organ, and everything that lives here—film, grazer, singer, swarm, predator, cutter—is only the highland practicing the difficult art of being more than one thing at once, forever in the act of finishing itself and never quite finishing.
