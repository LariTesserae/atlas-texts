The stone-coloured ones hold the largest rooms. They do not herd so much as occupy adjacent vacancies, each animal a room’s worth of stillness that occasionally remembers it has legs. Their bodies are the same unfinished limestone as the walls: pale, pocked, the hair short and coarse so that grit lodges in it and stays. Shoulders rise into a hump that looks accidental, as if the spine never quite decided how high to go. The legs are short, the joints thick, the hooves a single broad plate that clicks once, sharply, on every fresh fracture and then is silent again. They stand for hours with their heads lowered not in grazing but in a kind of listening that uses the whole skull. When they do move it is along routes that already exist in the talus, though the rock itself shows no path. Their eyes take the pewter light and return it dulled, a wet coin at the bottom of a shaft.

They eat what the hollow allows: the hand-high ferns, the uncommitted grasses, the gray-white bloom along damp joints. Sometimes they lip moss from a seep until the stone shows through. They enter the black pools without hesitation and stand in them as if the water were only another kind of floor. A bellow starts in the chest and leaves the mouth already tiled by the rooms it has not yet reached; you hear it arrive from three directions at once, none of them the animal you can see.

The compact ones keep to the seeps. They are lower, darker, the colour of stone that has been wet for a long time. The body is a single wedge of muscle, the head broad, the ears small and set far back. They do not lope. They flow along the bedding planes where water still remembers how to run, noses close to the condensation, pausing at every basin as if checking a clock. Their eyes are smaller than the ungulates’ and catch less light; they hunt more by the sound of hooves and the particular tick of drip-water that means a drinker has arrived. When they take something the struggle is brief and oddly courteous, as if both parties already knew the interval. They drag the body into a side chamber whose roof is still partly intact and feed without haste. Afterwards they lie in the same posture the ungulates use, occupying the space rather than resting in it.

Between the hanging blocks the birds row. Their wings are too large for the gaps and too heavy for true flight; each stroke sounds like canvas slapped once against wet stone. They are the colour of the dust the rock makes when it breaks—neither dark nor pale, a mid-tone that disappears against almost everything. The legs fold back along the body in a way that looks unfinished. They do not perch so much as clamp, talons finding the same raw edges the collapse left. Their calls are short, metallic, and they use them the way the ungulates use bellows: to stack sound in rooms they are not in. You hear a bird for half a day before you see the slow, rowing shape cross a chimney-hole and vanish again.

Higher, in the fractures that still remember being ceiling, the hanging things keep their own weather. Most are bats, ordinary enough until they move. Then the colony becomes a single decision: a rustle that gathers into a roar that is only wings and then is a rustle again, and the air under them thickens for a moment with the smell of old fruit and ammonia. But if you look carefully—if the pewter light happens to strike at the right angle—some of the shapes are wrong. Longer faces. Ears that fold in a different grammar. A few hang not by hind feet but by something nearer a hand, fingers longer than they should be, the membrane between them thinner, almost translucent. They are only bats if you do not look carefully, and the hollow does not encourage careful looking. When they shift they do it together, even the ones that are not quite bats, as if the distinction has not yet been argued into the rock.

There are others, less willing to be named. In the deepest bowls of standing water something occasionally surfaces that is not a reflection: a broad, pale back, the suggestion of limbs folded close, then nothing again. Along certain wet seams the moss itself seems to hold a slower occupancy—threads that withdraw when touched, a bloom that was not there yesterday and will be gone tomorrow, though the hollow does not otherwise keep yesterday or tomorrow. Once, in a chamber where the light is a shade less dim, a flake of worked stone lies among the grit. It is the kind of thing a hand makes when it is still learning what a hand can do. Nothing else has been added. No second flake, no ash, no wall. The animals pass it without interest. The hollow keeps company with occupancy, not with making, and whatever hand left the flake appears to have learned that lesson or forgotten it.

They do not leave. They do not arrive. The rents in the roof stay the rents in the roof. A carnivore crosses the same empty floor at an interval so regular it has become a kind of architecture. An ungulate stands in the same relation to the same hanging block, breathing the same held coolness. The sounds stack and do not cancel: hooves, cough, slap of body into black water, wet-canvas wings, the tiled echo of a bellow meant for open air, and underneath it all the second rain of drips finding the same stones. Time has stepped out. What remains is the equilibrium after the event—a recently torn country that has decided, with complete seriousness, to stay exactly as torn, and full of living bodies that have no intention of becoming anything else.
