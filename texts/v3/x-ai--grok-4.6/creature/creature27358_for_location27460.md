The water does not contain them. They are the water’s density, its grain, its slow internal weather. At first glance the lens looks merely crowded, a cold broth thick with pale shapes, but the longer the eye stays the more the distinction between medium and occupant dissolves. Every cubic meter is spoken for. Bodies press against bodies without collision, their mineral skins clicking once, then locking into the next minute of work.

They are grown rather than assembled, yet the growth has the patience and the geometry of manufacture. Plates of nacre overlay darker, fibrous muscle that contracts in long, peristaltic waves. Joints are ratcheted, not hinged; limbs end in rotating burrs or chisel-edges whose wear is repaired by the same secretions that built them. Inside, when one of them turns, thin metal-bright septa flash like the windings of a dynamo. Chambers open and close with the regularity of valves. Nothing about them looks improvised. They look like the solution a young mountain would reach if it were given time and a closed volume and the instruction to become useful to itself.

The work is the rock. A single fresh fracture—still glassy, still smelling of the lightning that split it—can occupy the same clustered mass for years. They do not bring tools. They are the tools. Grinders lock their chisel-limbs into the stone and rasp at the speed of cold chemistry, ingesting silicates and exhaling a faint milk of slurry. Immediate neighbors, a different caste, sieve the milk through ranked filters that pulse like gills. The processed dust is reincorporated; nothing is wasted, nothing is hurried. Residual heat from the mountain’s birth, redox gradients in the newest cracks, and the weak electric fields the animals themselves generate and then feed upon—these are the only energies. There is no sun-chain, no green film, no mercy of photosynthesis. The stone is both larder and foundry.

Sound is the other saturation. Closest is the dry factory-floor click of thousands of mineralized feet and mouthparts. Beneath it a low, regular thudding—internal valves or swim-bladders or whatever analogue they possess—beats in slow unison so the entire basin has a pulse. Superimposed is a higher, almost electrical rasp, the collective discharge of the current-makers. Bubbles rise in disciplined columns from vents they have excavated; they break at the surface in a steady industrial fizz that never quite becomes a boil. The water carries a metallic tang and a faint tingle. Wind above the rim adds nothing. The mountain is too new to have learned how to whistle.

They reproduce by locking. Two or more individuals press together for days, exchanging not gametes but patterned mineral secretions that harden, plate by plate, into a new body already armored and already working. The young do not play. They attach to the nearest unoccupied fracture and begin. Death is rarer than reproduction and equally public. A failed individual is dismantled by its neighbors with the same unhurried efficiency they apply to the rock; components are sorted, cleaned, reincorporated. The population density never thins. The cirque remains saturated, a single breathing machine whose parts happen to be alive.

Depth changes them the way pressure and dimming change everything here. Near the surface they are smaller, quicker in their slowness, their internals flashing gold in the green-gold light. Mid-water they grow membranes stiffened with cartilaginous spars and drift like living intake grilles, harvesting the smaller ones with lazy mechanical grace. At the bottom, where newest fractures still leak a mineral fog, they become larger, quieter, more purely geometric—distilled toward some final industrial form. Even there nothing lunges. Everything waits its turn.

A weak seiche, once a month, sloshes the entire lens a few centimeters up one wall and down the other. Fine sediment lofted by the grinders takes weeks to settle. Light itself ages as it descends: gold becomes patient blue, and the animals there grow stiller, more crystalline, as if the pressure has taught them the last necessary shape. The cirque does not remember being empty. It has been this full, this loud, this crowded with patient living machinery, for as long as the rock has been sharp. They are the mountain’s unfinished thought, chewing itself into completeness, one micron, one click, one slow pulse at a time.
