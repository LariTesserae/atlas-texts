They are the ones who never learned to stand apart. Density is their first language, the press of shoulder and flank and hanging basket the grammar they were born inside. You would not call them a people so much as a packing that learned to speak, to sing against itself, to keep count of water and of the dead goats that must never be left to swell in the well. Their faces take the stain of the place the way the plaster takes soot: ochre along the cheekbones, a permanent dusk in the creases, lime-white half-moons under the nails from the endless scraping and re-smoothing. Eyes accustomed to light that is always a little wrong—milky, yellowed, never quite the color of day—so that when they do ascend to the lid they squint as if the upper glare were a wound.

They move as current, always touching. A hand finds the shine on a corner that every generation has grabbed; another steadies a child who is running the galleries in the old game whose only rule is to set every hanging thing swinging. Nearness is not intimacy and not inconvenience; it is simply the condition, like warmth, like the low roar the well makes of every bleat and laugh and mallet-strike. To be alone they would have to climb into a light-well and hang there like the pigeons, and even then the sound would find them. They sleep in stacked rings, bodies fitted into niches that were once rooms and are now something between bunk and cupboard, the timber above them black with the breath of those who slept there before. In the deep night the place still ticks and they tick with it: a quern turning because the grain will not wait, an animal shifting, the slow drip of condensation they will wipe into jars at first light.

Their craft is the same short list repeated until it became law. Earth, fiber, fire, time. They form bricks in the upper yard and lower them on slings, still, as if the first bowl might one day need another skin. They split reed far from here and weave it into screens that are more lung than wall, so the galleries breathe and the argument of the goats travels unhindered. Wood is only ever reused; they read the scars of old joinery the way others read weather, knowing which beam will fail slowly and which has already begun to smile downward like the corbels. Metal they name and inherit. A copper knife is a lineage. Stone they batter into usefulness and then forget, except for the one fitted channel that still feels like someone else’s work, the only masonry in a world of modeling. Repair is Tuesday and also worship. They speak of the first bowl as a person who lay down and became useful, and when they press new plaster over old they are tucking a blanket around that sleeper. There is no “behind” the walls, only more of themselves, compacted, sacrificed rooms turned to rubble fill. They thicken; they do not break the shell.

The animals are not kept so much as co-occupants, the second architecture. Goats folded into their low ring near the trough, heat a second hearth, voices the drone the whole bowl rides upon. Their restlessness can set the plaster humming. Pigeons own the highest joists and the white throats of the light-wells; their droppings are gathered with the same grave attention as the seep, because nothing that falls is wasted. Chickens stitch the lime floor into a living script. Cats who belong to no one in particular flow along the rails like spilled shadow, hunting the moths that live in the wool and the beetles that live in the grain. Lizards the exact color of the walls wait, still as thumb-prints, for the insects that wait for the stores. At night the many-legged things tick across the dish of the well when the lamps dip, and no one bothers to name them. The people move through this the way the people move through themselves: current, packed, touching. A sick goat is walked. A child is scolded for startling the pigeons into the light-well, because the light-wells are common and must stay clear. An animal that dies below is a public problem, announced, hauled, the spot lime-washed again.

They count water the way other folk count kin. The seep is led in and voiced into the trough that never quite fills; they watch it the way one watches a sleeping child. After rain—rare, percussive, the light-wells suddenly drums—they run with pots, shouting, the air briefly tasting of wet plaster before the place drinks it all back. Damp lives in the lowest courses and in the undersides of the hanging stores; they press palms to the walls at knee-height to feel the cool and know the season. Almost nothing green is allowed to volunteer. The potted leaves are dragged into the noon disk and dragged out again before they scorch; they are tended the way one tends an invalid, portable, temporary. The moss that mapped itself across one neglected gallery is left, because it tells them the turn of the year more honestly than any mark they could scratch.

By evening the lime holds the day’s heat and the lamps multiply—clay saucers, fat, floating wicks—until the galleries are lined with low, soot-colored stars. Condensation begins its quiet work on the north faces. Someone is always still plastering, because the skin sheds. Someone sings, not over the noise but into it, and the note finds the plaster and travels the long way around, answered from a far niche. They lean on rails that have been leaned on since the wood was a different tree. The air is goat and fat-smoke and wet lime and the faint green exhale of the plants finally allowed to rest. They do not go outside; they ascend, and even the upper flats are only the lid, not a land. The earth above is the thickness of a roof. All the life is down here, touching itself, making more of itself out of clay and noise and the little water it can keep. They have worn the place and been worn by it until the distinction is a polite lie they tell the children: that there was once a first bowl, and it lay down, and they are what it dreamed.
