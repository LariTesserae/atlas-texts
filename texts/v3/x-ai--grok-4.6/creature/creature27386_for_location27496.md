The first lives you meet are not creatures so much as weather given teeth. The midges do not merely persist in the sleet; they are a second precipitation that has learned to feed. Their bodies are little more than paired blades of chitin sheathed in a film of supercooled water that never quite freezes. When a gust slams them against an ice-glazed rush they shatter, yet the fragments keep twitching, keep biting, until the next flash of lightning shows them already reassembled into a new cloud. They do not die so much as change scale. After an hour among them your exposed skin is a map of tiny, perfectly circular welts that weep a fluid slightly warmer than blood; the midges drink that, too.

Beneath their hiss the rodents run in braided rivers of their own. They are the colour of the clay banks—blue-grey, wet, always on the point of slumping. Fur the thickness of a clenched fist stands out in stiff, ice-laden spikes that click together when they turn. Their teeth never stop. They strip the alder bark in long, spiralling ribbons that freeze into brittle corkscrews before they hit the moss. Sometimes a whole pack will pause, heads cocked, and you realise they are listening to the ice forming on their own backs, deciding whether to chew it off or let it become another layer of armour. They have no burrows that last. A nest excavated at dusk is a black pond by the next tearing of lightning. They simply move the nest with them, carrying pups in their mouths like extra tongues.

The waterfowl occupy every lead that stays open longer than a minute. Their plumage is not feathers but overlapping plates of something like horn soaked in oil, iridescent the way an oil-slick is iridescent, never quite the same colour twice. They do not fly. They do not need to. When a channel they occupy is suddenly abandoned by its water they simply stand, packed so tightly their plates lock, and wait for the next torrent to lift them. Their calls never resolve into individuals; they are a single hoarse chord that rises and falls with the wind, as if the flock itself were one throat being slowly torn. Occasionally one will tip forward and vanish, leaving only a slick of that same oil on the surface. Something below has taken it. The others do not pause.

In the deeper braids those larger shapes turn. You never see more than a suggestion: a ridge of hide the colour of wet slate, a slow uncoiling that makes the sleet above it hesitate. They are cold-blooded in a way that feels like a choice rather than a limitation. Their blood, when one of the gravel bars slashes them open, is black and does not steam. It beads on the clay and is swallowed by the moss within seconds, as if the ground itself were tasting them. They surface only to breathe, and even then only as a momentary slick, a place where the rain forgets to fall for the space of a heartbeat. Whatever they eat, it is already part of the water.

Yet these are only the obvious tenants, the ones the storm has not yet finished inventing. Other things move at the edges of the flashes.

There are walkers whose legs are jointed the wrong number of times, who pick their way across the saturated flats without sinking because each foot is a splayed, membranous fan that spreads on contact and then folds away like a closing eye. They leave no print that lasts; the mud remembers them only as a brief, warmer wetness. Their heads are featureless except for a vertical seam that opens when they feed, revealing a ring of rasping plates that strip the ice-sheath from a willow in one long, ringing peel. They do not travel in groups. Each seems to be the only one of its kind, or perhaps they are all the same one, appearing wherever the lightning next tears.

In the groaning canopies something nests that is not quite a bird and not quite a growth. It begins as a thickening in the ice on a branch, a place where the glaze is milkier, more opaque. Over hours it swells, pulses once, and unfolds into a creature of translucent membrane and fine, black veins. It hangs, listening to the trunks split, then drops. What it hunts is the sound itself. It wraps around a failing limb and drinks the vibration until the wood is silent and the creature is opaque, heavy, ready to freeze again into the next canopy.

And then there are the ones that have begun to use the artefacts.

A few of the pale rodents have learned the ring where rain falls upward. They sit just outside the still air, fur dripping, and wait. When a sheet of water lifts they dart in, snatch whatever small thing has been caught in the reversal—midges, a vole pup, a scrap of bark—and dart out again before the ice can decide they are not allowed. They do this in perfect silence, as if the stillness inside the ring were contagious.

Near the warm glass pylon a different thing has taken up residence. It is tall, thin, the colour of the storm itself, and it never quite stops moving. Its skin is a slow crawl of nested geometries that match the pylon’s, though they never quite repeat the same pattern. It does not eat in any way you can see. It simply stands, one long-fingered hand resting on the pylon, and the ice that should armour it sloughs away in perfect, ringing sheets. The plants will not grow within two metres of it. The midges veer aside. Sometimes, in the brief dark between lightnings, you think you see it lean its featureless head against the glass and listen, as if the pylon were telling it what the basin has not yet decided to become.

None of them stay. A nest, a hunting ground, a favoured scarp—all of it is provisional. The land calves, the channels rewrite themselves, the ice shears, and whatever lived there simply continues a few metres farther on, or a few metres down, or a few metres into whatever the next flash reveals. They are not surviving the basin. They are the basin’s way of remaining unfinished, of keeping the wound open long enough to see what else might still crawl out.

Stand still and they will begin to live on you as well. The ice armours your coat, the midges find the gaps, a rodent pauses to taste the salt at your wrist. The storm does not distinguish. Everything here is still deciding whether it is inhabitant or landscape.
