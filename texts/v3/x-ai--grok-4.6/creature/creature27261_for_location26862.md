The hollow does not host life so much as it hosts the finished idea of life, a closed circuitry of motion that never quite becomes event. What dwells there has already arrived at its own last shape and now simply occupies that shape with the patience of lichen.

At first you notice only the animals, because they are the easiest to mistake for ordinary creatures that have wandered in and forgotten the way out. The pale voles are not quite voles. Their fur is the exact grey-green of the damp earth they never quite disturb; they pass through moss as if the moss had already calculated the exact volume they would occupy and left it vacant in advance. Their eyes are the colour of wet stone and do not track movement so much as confirm that movement is unnecessary. The white hares sit in doorways the way a thought sits in a mind that has stopped thinking: perfectly placed, already complete. Their winter coats never moult because winter here is not a season but a decision the hollow made once and then refused to revise. When they do shift, it is only to occupy the next inevitable posture, the one the light has already prepared.

The deer-things in the square are smaller than memory insists deer should be, as though the hollow has gently edited them down to the scale of its streets. Their velvet never peels; the antlers remain in that soft, blood-warm state forever, neither growing nor shedding, simply remaining the colour of old copper left in rain. They stand with the absolute economy of statues that have decided, after long consideration, that statues are enough. Their hooves press the living carpet and the moss rises behind them like a tide that has been waiting for permission to be a tide. No print lasts longer than the time it takes the air to remember its own dampness.

Birds occupy the thatch the way condensation occupies a leaf: completely, without remainder. Their throats work, a tiny peristalsis of almost-song, but the silence that lives in the hollow is denser than any sound they might produce. The silence is not empty; it has weight and temperature. It presses against the possibility of noise the way the turf roofs press against the possibility of weather. Insects exist only as a continuous, glittering punctuation in the water-films, walking on surfaces that refuse to break. They do not fly. Flight would require a gradient of air, and the hollow has none.

Then there are the ones who still resemble people, though the resemblance has grown thin as old cloth. They are few. They move through the interleaved streets and alleys with the same unhurried grammar as the hares. Their clothing is the colour of the walls, of the moss, of the packed till itself: greys that have learned green, greens that have forgotten how to be anything but grey. The cloth (if it is still cloth) is worn to the same soft authority as every lintel and every basin. You cannot tell where garment ends and skin begins; both have been handled by the same unchanging damp for so long that the distinction has become a courtesy rather than a fact.

They do not speak. Speech is a slope, a falling-away from one moment into the next, and the hollow has levelled every slope. Their mouths exist. Sometimes the mouths open, as if to taste the air that already tastes of wet iron and crushed chlorophyll, but nothing emerges that the silence does not immediately reabsorb. Their tools are the last remaining arguments against stillness: bone needles, antler adzes, fire-hardened stakes, scraps of copper recast so many times the metal has forgotten it was ever ore and now remembers only the shape of being useful. They use these tools to repair what has already reached its final, perfect wear. A loose turf is lifted and settled back into the identical sag it had before. A crack in rammed earth is packed with the same earth. Leaves are harvested that have already begun to replace themselves before the hand has finished closing. The work is not labour. It is maintenance of an agreement that was signed so long ago the signatories have become the ink.

Their faces, when you can see them beneath the same fine hoar that covers every other surface, are neither young nor old. Age is a gradient. They have the particular stillness of people who have finished becoming themselves and now simply continue, the way the well continues to hold exactly the same water. Eyes the colour of river pebbles held to a light that has no source. Hands mapped with the same rounded authority as the stone they touch. They drink from the well with the animals, the same unhurried motion, the same absence of thirst that is being quenched. Around them the paving stones hold their perfect circles of damp as if the idea of an imperfect circle had been gently, permanently forgotten.

What lives in them is not quite blood, not quite sap. Their chemistry is the same slow, bright metabolism as the ferns and the horn-leaved shrubs: a process that does not require thaw because it has never needed warmth in the first place. Breath would show in this cold, yet they do not breathe hard enough for breath to become visible. They do not need to. The air of the hollow already contains everything they require, held in suspension the way the light is held, the way the water is held in every film and every basin.

Descend far enough, stay long enough, and you begin to understand that the distinction between inhabitant and inhabited has also been levelled. The people are the town’s way of remembering it was once built by hands. The animals are the town’s way of remembering it was once walked through. The plants are the town’s way of remembering it was once open to weather. All of them together are the hollow’s way of remaining finished. They do not live in time; they live in the held breath that time became when it had nowhere further to erode.

Sometimes, in the permanent noon that is not noon, one of them will sit on the split-stone bench whose moss has already taken the exact shape of a sitting body. They sit. The air tastes of iron and green. Nothing happens. That nothing is not emptiness. It is the entire, completed event of being here, of having always been here, of needing nothing else to occur. The hare remains in its doorway. The fern remains uncurled to its millimetre. The well remains full to its line. And whatever lives in the hollow continues, not by enduring, but by having already arrived at the only place it was ever going to be.
