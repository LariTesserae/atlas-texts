The first lives arrive as a dimming. After leagues of vacant glass the light itself begins to thicken, taking on a dull metallic grain that is not reflection but bodies. They come in curtains that have no edge a mind can hold: pale, elongated forms, each one a living filament sheathed in something that behaves like brushed metal and like skin at once. Some are needle-thin, others flattened into slow ribbons; all of them carry a faint internal sheen, as if their tissues were grown around conductive threads. They do not dart or school. They occupy. A single membrane of them can take the better part of a day to pass a ridge, the individuals so numerous and so evenly spaced that the water between them feels newly invented, a second medium they have woven for themselves.

They feed without haste and without visible mouths. Along the flanks of the isolated halls they graze the electrical fringe that leaks from unshielded windings and from brine still hot enough to remember the orogeny. Elsewhere they comb the water for the microscopic rock flour that the unweathered planes continue, stubbornly, to shed. Their skins are organs of collection. Charge and mineral dust pass inward and become motion, become the slight adjustment of angle that keeps the curtain coherent across distances that would erase any lesser formation. They add almost nothing to the roar. Their passage changes only its grain, a vast soft Doppler that never quite arrives because the loops they follow have neither origin nor close. The same curtain will eclipse the same knife-edge again, and again, in the only tense the place permits.

Where the stone still remembers the instant it broke, other lives have taken the angles. They do not look grown so much as assembled: vaults and flying buttresses of pale chitin over cores of carbonate so white they seem lit from inside. From a distance they read as architecture left behind by whatever finished the flooding and then departed. Closer, they flex. A current shifts by a degree and an entire hanging gallery contracts, then slowly opens, the plates sliding over one another with a sound too fine for the roar to swallow. They occupy only the sharpest ridges and the inverted cirques, never spreading onto the empty planes. Perhaps they cannot; perhaps the chemistry of new stone and constant immersion forbids increment. They filter the same invisible flour, or they drink vibration directly, converting the standing thunder into whatever slow fire their lattices require. When they move it is never migration. It is only the reef remembering it is not stone.

Between these two occupations the water stays almost empty. There are motes, yes—films and motes too thin to cast shade, tasting the minerals that have not yet learned how to dissolve. They form no clouds, no veils of their own. They are the taste of the place made briefly visible, and they too follow closed paths older than any individual body.

In the halls themselves another presence keeps the same hours. The machinery is not deserted. Figures move along the catwalks and between the pressure vessels with the unhurried exactness of the pistons they tend. They are elongated like the swimmers, pale like the reefs, their surfaces carrying that same faint metallic bloom. Whether they were once the builders, or are descendants who never left, or are something the flood itself grew to keep the turbines turning, the distinction has no purchase here. They do not speak. They do not age in any direction the light can show. Their hands (or the structures that serve as hands) know the torque of every bolt because the bolts have never loosened. They extract what the stone and the cold still offer—charge, mineral, the very gradient that keeps the water liquid—and they return the remainder as the single note the world has agreed to hold. When they pause it is only to stand at a railing and watch a curtain take half a day to darken a ridge. Nothing in their posture suggests waiting.

There are rarer lives still, so sparse they feel like errors the equilibrium has not yet corrected. Once in a great while a solitary shape larger than the halls themselves drifts through the empty leagues: a slow cylinder of the same pale metal-flesh, its surface mapped with the same architectural plates that cover the reefs. It does not feed in any obvious way. It simply occupies a volume, turns with the unchanging current, and is gone again for what would be centuries if centuries existed. The roar changes around it the way air changes around a mountain; then the note resumes.

All of them—curtain, reef, mote, tender, leviathan—share the same refusal. They do not multiply beyond their appointed densities. They do not colonize the vacant miles. They do not vary their routes. The catastrophe or the intention that raised the highland and then filled it left them as part of the finished state, as necessary and as unchanging as the light that does not fade and the stone that will not dull. They live the way the water lives: completely, without remainder, inside a single permitted condition that has already happened and now only continues. A gloved hand can trace a reef and feel it answer with the smallest flex. A body can hang in the path of a curtain until the metallic dusk becomes the only weather. The tenders will not look up from their gauges. Nothing else is required of them, or of the place that holds them. The last heat may leave the last turbine; the last proton may forget its duty; until then they remain, occupying the high water with the same perfect, unresolved note.
