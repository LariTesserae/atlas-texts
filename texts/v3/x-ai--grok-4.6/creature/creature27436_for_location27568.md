They have the look of something that was never introduced to the idea of elsewhere. Not from refusal—there is simply no practice of it. Faces take the weather the walls take: a high, even tan that does not quite reach the inner cuttings, then a paler damp-shade under the eyes and along the throat where sweat and old masonry have argued for years. Hair is kept short or bound, because the wind on the trestles is a constant editor and because fig-dust and slag-flour settle in anything longer. Hands are the first thing you notice once you stop looking at the height. Knuckles thickened the way bolt-heads have thickened into knobs. Palms mapped with the same grooves the rain-channels cut and then abandoned. Nails pale, often split, frequently stained at the beds—brick-red, iron-black, the faint green that comes from stripping vines that have decided a gallery is now a trunk.

They dress in what the place has already finished with. Cloth from the weaving sheds that still turn, cut and recut until the original bolt is a rumor, the way the engine-house roofs are rumors. Colors that were once dye-vat bright have gone to the same finished age as the paint on the iron: rust-over-green, blood-over-grey, a yellow that has decided to be dust. Layers, always, because the heat is residual and uneven—warmth coming up through a floor at night, a sudden thinness when the wind finds a new gap in the high walks. A jacket will have been a child’s, then a lining, then a patch on something older. Nothing is discarded that can still occupy space usefully. They look, at a distance, like another course of the masonry: varied, load-bearing, softened at the corners.

They do not hurry. The air is thin enough that hurry is a tax, and the work itself has no interest in finishing today. A man mending a downpipe will pause to watch the condensate choose its thread, as if the water might have learned a new opinion. A woman shifting tomatoes against a south wall will stand a long time with one hand on the box, feeling whether the earth inside has remembered last night’s drip. Children are the exception only in volume, not in kind; they run the High Paths and the goat-tracks, but they stop dead at certain gratings the way a lizard stops, head tilted, testing whether the memory of load-bearing still holds. They learn the language of damp the way they learn not to fall: north walls, undersides, the mouths of weirs. A child who cannot yet speak in full sentences can already tell you which overflow is marked for the common cistern and which is watched.

Their days fold into the place’s light. Midday they occupy the undersides and the inner yards, the deep cuttings where the stacked architecture makes its own dim weather and the lianas have closed the alleys. Voices carry differently there—softer, because the leaves take the edges off, and because the frogs (after rain) and the tick of condensation are already speaking. Upper walks empty except for laundry that snaps like another kind of flag and the swifts writing their short violent sentences in the updrafts. Morning and late afternoon the Heights become a system of edges again, and the people come out onto them: tank-mirrors, gold steam, the long clerestories throwing slabs of white that make even a tired face look briefly ceremonial. They work then with the unhurried precision of people who have watched the same process fail to conclude for a generation. A belt is replaced one section at a time. A kiln is fired one month in four, and the month is treated like a harvest—attendance, a certain formality around the pour, then the long cooling that is also work.

They live among the census, not above it. Goats are not quaint; they are milk, meat, and the unofficial post, their bells an irregular grammar on the uppermost batter. Dogs choose the warm iron the way the people choose the old engine basements when the brief rains make the upper rooms sweat. Chickens in the vine-yards, carp in the darker cisterns, the silver flash when crumbs are dropped. Cats know the residual heat better than anyone; they are already on the floor you were thinking of. Lizards are so ordinary they have become a kind of moving punctuation—the color of whatever they last agreed with, throat-fans opening in the iron glare as if the noon itself needed reminding it was bright. Bats pour from an old flue at first cooling. The hawk that belongs to no one has names the way certain overflows have names; it is seen often enough that its absence is noticed, like a drip that has stopped.

Plants are not tended so much as negotiated. A fig that roots in a wall is the wall-owner’s until it crosses a joint; then the argument begins, serious, unhurried, lasting years if necessary, because a vine takes a decade to close an alley and no one here is in the business of sudden outcomes. Cuttings are kindness. A share of condensate is kindness. A warm floor offered when the upper rooms grow clammy is kindness so ordinary it is almost unnamed. Punishment is relocation: down a terrace, into a noisier cutting, away from a favored drip or a south wall that still remembers how to hold tomatoes. There is no wilderness to send anyone into. The Circum ends as a wall. Below is haze. It is not forbidden. It is not the subject. Their weather is this weather. Their ground is this stacked, poured, bolted, tray-grown ground.

They keep the present from undoing itself. That is the whole of the vocation, if it can be called that. Electrification arrived and stayed practical—light in cages, a few motors, the tram on the east incline whose cable sings when the wind is right. No one is inventing the future. The foundry pours are ceremonial now, seasonal. The brickworks feeds the endless small repairs. Dye-vats kept just off the boil. Engine rooms that turn because turning is cheaper than the long argument of stopping. They stand in doorways and watch processes that will not finish today, and this watching is itself a kind of maintenance. Time is thick. They measure it by which court the figs finally took, by which overflow was recut, by the last time the foundry drew a crowd, by the section of the great belt that is newest (which is never the whole belt). A machine is repaired with parts of its grandparent. Paint fails in mappable patterns. Seasons are a change in the angle of the light and in how often the tanks sing after the brief warm rains that sheet the upper decks and vanish into the designed throats.

What has emerged, slowly, the way the moss emerges in the spray-shadow of a cooling tower that still turns, is a people who have become another climate of the place. They have weather. They have citizenship of a terrace. They have been added to for so long that the additions—children, repairs, arguments over a joint, a new trough of succulents that drink almost nothing and still look like wealth—have the same finished age as the slag-block and the blood-brick. Nothing in them is new and nothing in them is falling. The erosion happened and was accepted. They thicken. They stay. You can put your hand on a shoulder the way you put your hand on a wall and feel the same composite warmth: sun, residual engine, the faint composting heat of a leaf-trough three stories up conducted down through iron whose age no one bothers to name. They were made here, or they made themselves here, which has become the same sentence. The lizards still arrive at noon. The condensate still finds last year’s groove. The swifts still write the same short lines in the updraft. And the people of the Heights, entirely of the invention, continue in the only way the Heights know—which is to occupy, densely, without remainder, and to let the light and the tired machines and the patient green do most of the talking while they listen for the drip, the belt-slap, the goat-bell, the tram two terraces down, and the almost-silence that means everything is, for now, still holding.
