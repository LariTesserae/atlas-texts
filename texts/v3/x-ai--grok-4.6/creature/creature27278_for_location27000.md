The first lives you meet are the ones that still answer to names, though the names themselves have started to fray at the edges the way the new stone does. They are few enough that a day’s walk between monuments can leave you convinced the basin has gone entirely to animals and architecture; then a voice will ride the tearing-seam of the sound and you will see them—small against the inner weather of a hollow, moving along a gallery that was not there yesterday. Their bodies keep the bronze-gray light the way the buildings do, a dull holding rather than a reflection. Skin takes on a faint mineral grain if they stay long in one place; hair collects the same copper-green that fever-lives in the leaves. Eyes are large, not from any obvious night, but because the dimness is a medium they have learned to drink. They do not look unfinished. They look like people who have agreed, some time ago, to finish at the same slow pace as the ground.

They live in the skins of the monuments. Habitation is a clinging, a nest of rooms and condensate-farms and workshops pressed to the inner walls so that the great shafts remain weather. A family will occupy a vertical meadow of moss-rooms three storeys high, the floors grown rather than built, the ceilings still adding themselves. Children learn to walk on surfaces that remember being asked to be floors; they learn, earlier, that a spilled cup will tidy itself only if the spill has been introduced politely. Adults set palms to bulkheads and the material rearranges—not instantly, not as obedience, but as a conversation that ends in a window or a stair or a depth that is not the outside. They call this courtesy and do not decorate the word. Tools are often the absence of an edge: a thought held against a surface until the surface agrees to become the shape of the thought. When they work metal or mineral they do it the way one might work a muscle, by asking it to remember a different tension.

They are not lonely. That is the first thing that will not resolve into a simpler picture. Across the voids they call in pitches that braid with the load-bearing groan, so that a greeting can travel three monuments and arrive still warm. In the confluence where the green almost becomes a street they barter in ordinary voices—leaf-rot cistern water against a length of vine that has been taught (badly) to hold a shape, a rumor of the next flex against a handful of the jelly the night-things leave. They pray, some of them, but the prayers are addressed as often to the tearing-seam as to anything with a face. They complain about the rationed light, about the way distance sometimes recedes as policy, about the children who must be kept from the outer galleries when the muffling bands drift through. The complaints have the cadence of people who have decided the place is theirs enough to criticize.

What they are becoming is less settled. Some of the older ones have begun to flex with the buildings, a slow night-shift of stance that leaves them taller in the morning or differently jointed at the hip. A few have let the grain of the walls continue under their skin until their hands no longer need to ask; the wall simply knows they are arriving. They do not speak of this as change. They speak of it as the land finishing its thought through them. Others remain stubbornly separate, insisting the city is only a splint and they only the ones who must stay until the ground no longer groans. Both kinds raise the same children. The children hear the difference in the roar the way other children hear a door.

Around them, and through them, and in the long intervals they do not fill, the rest of the living keeps its own unfinished grammar.

The flocks are the most public. They move as weather systems because that is what the basin has taught them weather is: a darkening of the already dim, a second architecture of wing-roar that briefly makes the tearing-seam sound almost gentle. They are not birds. The membranes are too large, too independently muscled; each “wing” is a surface that can rewrite its own camber mid-stroke, so the flock can turn inside its own volume like a single thought changing its mind. Their mouths are on the undersides. They drink the bronze-gray as much as they drink the nectar the hanging gardens exhale after a moisture-tide. When they pass close you smell iron and a sweetness that has nothing to do with fruit. They nest in the inverted wells and on the lips of the rings, and the buildings tolerate them the way a body tolerates its own pulse. Some of the people say the flocks are the city’s first attempt at a voice that could leave.

In the sharp new gullies, when the stone sweats, the jointed things pour. They are bright, too many-segmented, each plate a pale hexagon that still remembers the kiln. They taste mineral the way other tongues taste salt; you can watch a ridge lose a handsbreadth of itself in an hour of their shining descent. They are gone by what passes for morning, leaving the channel with a new, wetter grammar. The people do not hunt them. The people wait, after the pouring, and collect what the jointed things have refined—trace metals in a jelly that the moss-rooms will drink and turn into another storey. It is not farming. It is closer to the way one might wait for a fever to break and then use the sweat.

The lush pockets are where density becomes briefly ordinary and then exceeds it. Grazers the size of benches move through the moss-rooms from the inside, eating the architecture while leaving the walls standing; their mouths are a slow abrasion, their waste a finer, more ambitious moss that will try to become rooms of its own. Pollinators that are more temperature than body drift between the door-sized leaves, and the leaves turn to follow them even when there is no sun to follow. Vines scribble the same urgent sentence over every upright thing and then, in the confluence, attempt letters; the attempt is always something else, looping, wet, preferred by the birds that are not birds. Fruiting bodies open with the sound of wet cloth and are emptied by mouths that should not have found them yet. The ecology has the brightness and fragility of an invention that has not yet been told it is finished. Predators follow, of course. You hear them more than you see them. A scream starts in a hanging garden three monuments away and arrives intact because the basin is one acoustic chamber and the air has nothing better to do. The predators themselves are rarely more than a rumor of teeth traveling as a pulse. When they are seen they do not agree with one another about the number of legs, or whether legs are the right word.

Then there are the lives that do not fit.

Pale ribbons inhabit the tearing-seam itself. You see them only when the pitch changes and they are shaken loose—drifting, tasting of ozone, the exact color of a sound that has been stretched too far. They thread back in. Some of the listeners among the people claim the ribbons are how the roar thinks. Others say they are only another pioneer, overclocked, using the sound the way the jointed things use the new stone. When the muffling bands pass, the ribbons go still and the people grow uneasy, because the quiet is not empty. Something hunts by the relief of it. The pictures do not agree. Sometimes it is a thinning of the dimness that walks on too few feet. Sometimes it is the absence of the groan given a shape. Children are kept inside. The buildings iris their outer apertures shut, a courtesy that feels, for once, like fear.

Inside the hollows live the soft drums. If a lung is an animal they are animals. They swell and empty against the inner skins; the thunder of it is a thing you feel in the floor and in the teeth. They are not pets. They are not livestock. They are closer to a second set of organs the monuments grew once people began to cling to the walls and needed something to keep the emptiness from becoming a different kind of roar. The people tend them the way one tends a heart that has decided to live outside the body. When a drum dies the building flexes as if it has lost a stance, and the groan deepens for days.

On the bare lithic stretches the night-things walk, though night is only a further rationing of the bronze-gray. Too few feet. Prints that fill by morning with a jelly the plants will drink and the people will sometimes harvest if the night-things have been courteous enough to leave it near a monument. The night-things themselves are the color of the dust that has not yet become soil. They do not pack. They use the emptiness the way the flocks use the air: as a medium that is still deciding what it will carry.

And the plants. The plants are indecent because they refuse the patience the land has not yet earned. They are lush the way a shout is loud in an empty hall. Leaves the size of doors keep a private dusk on their undersides, faintly lit, as if each blade were remembering a different sky. Stems already woody on ground barely older than a rumor. Mosses that practice architecture so earnestly the people have begun to live in the practice. The vegetation drinks the damp, yes, but it also drinks the dimness and the sound; after a moisture-tide you can hear it grow, a crepe of new leaf, the moss-rooms adding a storey with a sound like wet cloth applauding. The vines do not climb so much as insist. In the long condensation-shadows the growth is thick enough to lose a body in, and sometimes a body is lost—not eaten, exactly, but incorporated, the way the buildings incorporate a needed stair. The people do not call this dangerous. They call it the green finishing a sentence they started.

The buildings themselves are the longest-lived of the inhabitants, or the slowest to admit they are inhabitants. Their surfaces take the light and hold it under the skin so that each monument is a dull lantern, a bruise of illumination. Up close the grain rearranges while you look, the way a muscle rearranges under work that has not yet been named. Walkways exist, then do not. Apertures iris. A staircase will be there in the morning because last night someone needed it, or because the building did. They flex in the night, taking a new stance, polite, enormous; the floor changes angle by a degree you feel in the inner ear, and the groan deepens, and somewhere a channel finds a sharper letter to cut. Some of the people say the city is scaffolding. Some say it is a splint. Some say the city is what called the ground up and is now obliged to stay and listen to it heal. The buildings do not contradict any of these. They simply continue the conversation the land began when it finished arriving last season and had not yet decided how to weather.

Under all of it the land is still living in the open, at a biological pace as much as a geological one. Ridges lift a handsbreadth in a week, shedding plates. Hexagonal joints fail in bright flakes where moisture has worked. The groan is the sound of unfinished joints remembering they are allowed to become something else. The people walk it, the animals taste it, the plants scribble across it, the buildings brace it. No one owns the process. The process is what lives here most completely: a hollow place densely used, a quiet geometry that will not stop screaming, a city that has not yet been given enough world and a world that has not yet been given enough time, both of them busy, warm, dim, damp at the edges, green where they can stand to be, and strange with the particular strangeness of things that are still being decided.

Stay long enough and the distinctions begin to sweat the way the stone does. A child’s palm on a bulkhead and the bulkhead becoming a window onto a depth that is not outside—whose courtesy was that? The pale ribbons shaken loose when the pitch changes—are they fauna or a thought the roar is having? The night-thing’s jelly that the moss drinks and then builds into a room a person will later sleep in: at what point did the mineral become the plant become the house become the sleeper? The hunter that uses the quiet—when the muffling band passes and the children are kept in, is it coming from the emptiness or is it the emptiness remembering it can be a mouth?

What lives here is the decision, still being made, about what a life is allowed to be when the ground itself has not finished arriving. The people are one clause of it. The flocks, the jointed pourings, the drums, the ribbons, the indecent green, the flexing lanterns, the land that groans and lifts and sheds—these are the others. They do not settle into a single image because the place has not settled. They only become more themselves the longer you listen, which is to say they become more like the sound: a weather of living, low and load-bearing, high and endlessly tearing, and between those registers every clatter and wing-roar and hydraulic syllable of a world that is still, politely and enormously, taking its stance.
