The people here are the ones who wipe the tiles. That is the first thing you notice if you stay long enough to be invited indoors: the constant, quiet circuit of a cloth along glazed surfaces that never quite lose their chill. They keep the rooms high so the damp can pool somewhere above the head, and they keep the windows sealed because a sill left open for an hour becomes a thoroughfare. At night the stairwells tick and rustle; everyone knows this is only the smaller tenants testing the pipes. A household that hears nothing checks the drains before it checks the locks.

They eat what the vats give them—dense yeast cakes that taste of the warm broth of the markets, a pale rendering oil that never quite loses the memory of whatever was melted down to make it. No one keeps a pot of soil. The official plaques still say the plateau was stripped for foundations and the earth never came back, but the unofficial knowledge is older and simpler: the stone sweats, and what sweats will not be rooted. Children learn the streets by the mineral fans that streak every façade, pale calcite and rust named like cousins. They can tell you which weeping marks the oldest foundry quarter and which marks the dye-house alleys where the air thickens with peppery musk.

Those alleys are never empty of other bodies. The gutters run occupied: pale crustaceans the length of a thumb moving in continuous, unhurried traffic; eel-things that have memorized the sluice-gates the way the people have memorized the bells; something jointed too many times that clicks along the undersides of the grates and is, by long custom, simply municipal. No one owns it. No one evicts it. It is part of the plumbing the way the bronze bells are part of the air.

Above the streets the sky between the stacks is a moving mesh of industrial wings. Soot-hawks labor past with faces like leather bellows, loft-rats that prefer gliding to flight, the great slow kites that ride the heat off the smelter roofs and drop only to drink from the cistern lips. Their shadows braid at dusk until the streetlamps come on through living dusk. In the long covered markets the air itself is occupancy—breath, wet fur, the musk of whatever nests in the rafters and will not be dislodged. The density of bodies is the other weather. A tannery district will sit for weeks inside its own low yellow cloud; two days’ walk east the foundry quarter breathes a clearer, hotter drizzle that smells of wet coke and the sweet rot of grease-traps.

Once or twice in a generation the larger herds move. The big gray browsers, shoulder-high and rain-slick, take a month to cross a single borough, grazing the bacterial dark that grows in every unattended trough. Traffic parts around them. The tramlines were laid with that parting already understood. No one hurries them. Nothing here is in a hurry to become something else. A chimney that began to lean in one century finishes leaning in the next and is then shored, not replaced. Paint is allowed to finish its conversion into stain. The water finds the same paths. The animals inherit the same ledges.

Stand on any roof—there is always a higher one—and the stacks recede into a darker band of weather. Between them the wings turn and turn. Below, the weeping never quite stops. The people live inside that sound the way they live inside the one continuous noise of the city: water in pipes, the slap of drive-belts two centuries old, the layered shouting of animals that have claims on certain alleys and will not be moved. After an hour you stop hearing the separate parts. You live inside the single acoustic body, a continent of wet courtyards and open sheds, still condensing, still being walked on by more lives than it was ever designed to hold.

They have always lived here. The stone sweats, the bells go slightly flat, and the city drinks from both the sky and itself. What lives here is simply what has learned to occupy the condensate.
