The first lives do not travel. They arrive as stain and stay as architecture. The felt is one organism stretched across every plane that holds still longer than a season: sphagnum, liverwort, the blue-green algal nap that tastes of metal when you press your tongue to it. It lives by remaining, by condensing the half-water air into itself, by advancing a millimeter whenever a new fracture weeps. It is the highland’s true surface. The stone is only what the felt has not yet finished remembering.

From that continuous pelt the columns rise, each a solitary decision. They are not trees. A column is a single fused thought of frond and cistern, thirty or forty meters of wet jade, hollow, fluted, cool inside as marrow. The bole holds water the way a lung holds breath; the rotating parasol is both collector and gesture. It turns according to a private arithmetic of turgor and glare, shedding curtains that feed the felt below or simply complete the column’s own weather. Between one column and the next lie hundreds of meters of glittering vacancy because that is the distance each requires in order to remain itself. They add a frond, lose a frond, rebalance. A decade spent watching one will show you almost nothing and everything.

In the shallows and the menisci live the smaller, quieter persistences. The pale amphibian, thumb-sized, the exact color of the algal nap, does not so much inhabit a pool as wait inside its surface tension. Its skin is another felt. It watches with eyes that are only denser patches of the same camouflage. Generations turn over in a single tarn without ever needing to announce themselves; the water and the wind and the long cool lukewarm do the rest. Higher, rarer, the adjusters appear on days when the sky tears open into that hard white-blue. They do not flap. Their long membranes make minute corrections against the updrafts and the exploding highlights, dark hyphens that are seen long before they can be heard. What they are—pollen given flight, the columns’ own dreams made briefly visible, something older that has learned to ride the glare—remains a rumor the light itself keeps.

Then there are the incidents that still remember they were made. In the turbine halls, already wearing the same moss pelt as the cliffs, the keepers move through oil rainbows and the fruit-interior glow of sodium lamps. They are few. They belong more to the scream of the generators than to the highland’s slower roar. Speech is a mouth pressed to an ear and still uncertain; more often they write in grease or simply listen with their teeth to both frequencies at once. Their clothes have become a second skin of wet felt. Their eyes stay narrowed against the perpetual plated glare. They maintain what they no longer name—the purpose of the pumping houses has slipped, the tramway cables are only a hanging garden now—yet someone still adds oil, still replaces a bearing, still lets the nearer stations keep their thin electric note beneath the general deafness. At the brief bright dimming they call night they sit inside the concrete and feel the highland trying to tell them something they once knew how to answer.

Farther out, where the geology is still steaming and the living skin has not yet arrived, nothing lives. The light is already this bright. The roar already occupies the middle of hearing. Whatever comes will have to learn the same saturation, the same refusal of quiet, the same patience that is almost indistinguishable from standing still. There is no hurry. The felt will reach it. The columns will rise. The keepers, or whatever the keepers become, will follow the pipes like roots that forgot they were metal. The highland does not yet know it is finished, and neither do they.
