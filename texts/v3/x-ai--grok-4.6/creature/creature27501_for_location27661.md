They are few, and they have learned to occupy the intervals.

You meet them first as interruptions in the geometry: a figure paused on a mid-level catwalk while the turbine above it completes another nearly geological revolution, or a pair of silhouettes moving through a sudden sheet of reflected noon, their outlines erased and then restored as the glass field below them shifts the light. They do not hurry. The air is too thin for haste and the stone too certain of its own unfinished business. Their bodies have taken the altitude into themselves; ribs show, lungs work with a shallow persistence, and the skin—where it is visible at the throat or the wrists—holds a dull metallic sheen, as though the same slow chemistry that skins a forgotten wrench has begun, very gently, to claim them.

They dress for layers of temperature rather than weather. Lowest, in the foundries cut into living rock, they wear almost nothing that can melt or cling; the heat rises through the soles and they step as if the floor itself were a living thing that must not be startled. Higher, they add wraps of a dull, heat-tempered cloth that can be shed in the humid alleys where the condensation pipes bloom. Boots are scraped each morning. Overnight the land adds a film of black glass, vernix-thin, that rings like porcelain when it is chipped away. Some of the older ones no longer bother with the finer cracks in their palms; the glass there has become a second set of lines, dark and painless, and they read them the way other people once read fortunes.

Their work is attendance more than labor. The stone is already the furnace; they merely guide what it offers. They watch metals sweat from new basalt, they quarter the glass sheets while the hexagonal flags still remember the sound they made when the wind last struck them, they listen to the turbines whose pressure is felt first in the breastbone. Conversation, when it happens, happens close, in the damp pockets where droplets gather and run and gather again. Voices do not travel. They have learned a language of pauses and small gestures that fits the patience of the place. A hand held near a pipe means the film inside has thickened. A glance toward the newest fissure means another tongue of bright rock has arrived, halted, and is listening.

They live in the stacked mid-levels, in rooms whose walls sometimes show the same flow-lines as the extruded streets, as if the rock exhaled them ready-made. Furniture is simple, often grown rather than built: a bench that began as a cooling flow and was only later given a back. Clocks hang on every wall and all of them lose the same fraction of a second; no one corrects them anymore. Time here is still finding its elevation, and the people have adjusted their inner measure to match. Meals are taken when the light on the glass fields reaches a certain angle, or when the condensation gutters have filled enough to be worth collecting. What they eat is whatever the mountain and the pipes will yield: mineral-rich condensates, the scraped films (iridescent, faintly sweet, neither quite alive nor quite stone), stores brought up from lower, older slopes, and, very rarely, something left by the pale walkers.

Those others appear at the edges of vision, usually at the hour when the sodium lights begin to compete with the last flare of day. Long-legged, narrow, the color of cooled ash or of bone that has never seen the sun. They pick their way across the unbroken pahoehoe as if it were a skin of ice, testing each step. No one has decided what they are. The people leave out shallow pans of water or coils of still-warm metal; sometimes the offerings are gone by morning, sometimes they have only been rearranged into patterns that look almost like writing. The walkers do not stay. They cross, they pause as if hearing the mountain consider its next millimeter, and they recede into the raw horizon. Children—there are children, though they are uncommon—are taught not to follow.

The films in the pipes are another matter. Maintenance crews scrape them out on a schedule that is more ritual than necessity. The residue is not bacterial, not mineral, something that occupies the hyphen between. Left alone it thickens into veils that shimmer with a slow internal weather. Some crews have begun to leave patches of it, claiming the water runs clearer afterward, or that the sound of the turbines changes by a note too low to name. They do not discuss this in the open halls. They only look at one another with the same unhurried recognition they give the newest buildings, the ones that seem less constructed than exhaled.

Over years the distinction between inhabitant and place continues to thin. An elder’s hair takes on the dark luster of the glass fields. A tool left too long beside a sleeping worker will sometimes wake with the same thin black skin that now maps the worker’s own knuckles. They chip it away or they do not. The land is still adding itself; they have stopped pretending they are only visitors. At night, when the rock holds its heat like a banked kiln and the few industrial lights do not travel far, you can stand on a high gantry and hear them breathing in their scattered rooms, a sound no thinner than the wind, no louder than the stone deciding whether to rise another fraction. They are not waiting for the city to finish, or the mountain. They have understood that neither will, and that this is the condition under which they themselves are allowed to remain.

Far below, a new flow emerges, bright and brief, then halts. Someone will go down in the morning to see whether it has become a street, a wall, or only another listening. They will take their time.
