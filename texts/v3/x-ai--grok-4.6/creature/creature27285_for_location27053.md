The dwellers occupy the intervals exactly as the ferns do: one presence, or at most a pair, allotted to each rectangle of wet stone, never closer, never farther. They are tall and attenuated, their bodies having lengthened in the same slow vertical that still lifts the plateau itself. Limbs are few and decisive—two for standing, two for the rare, necessary gesture—jointed like the bedding planes of the granite, so that when they fold they produce the same clean, unweathered angles as the lintels of their houses. Skin is neither hide nor bark but a living continuation of the rock’s own surface: pale where the granite is pale, darker where basalt shows through, always filmed with the same thin, unshed water. It does not wrinkle or scar. Droplets form along the clavicles and the long line of the sternum at the same regular interval as they form on the cliffs, hang for the same measured instant, and are replaced. In the even light the dwellers appear glazed, permanently wet, their color deepened exactly as the stone is deepened.

They do not walk often. When they do, it is along the raised slabs, each foot placed with the precision of a mason setting the next course, never splashing, never leaving a print that lasts longer than the film of water can erase. Most of the time they stand in the doorways or at the edges of the basins, as still as the ferns, each unfurled to the same degree. Their faces are long and planed, features reduced to the simplest necessary planes: a low triangle of nose, rectangles of closed or half-closed eyes that take in the regional view without focusing on any single distance. They have no hair. Moisture beads on the scalp and runs in the same silent curtains that sheet the roofs.

They do not speak. The mist would swallow any syllable before it formed, and in any case there is nothing that requires saying. Communication, if the word still applies, occurs through the shared exudation: a slight quickening of the film on one skin is answered, half a mile away, by an identical quickening on another. The message is never more than presence, the confirmation that the interval is still occupied, that the measure has not been broken. They eat nothing. The closed loop of plant, water and stone admits no consumption. At long, irregular intervals a dweller will lower itself to a moss cushion and rest a palm against the saturated chartreuse; the moss neither yields nor grows, yet something passes—enough, apparently, to maintain the equilibrium. They do not sleep. The constant warmth and the absence of night make the distinction meaningless. When they enter the dwellings it is only to stand inside for a time, as if to remind the stacked blocks that they, too, belong to the same finished arrangement.

Children are not born. The dwellers arrived—or condensed—with the plateau itself, each one already the height and degree of unfurling it would always be. There are no elders. Age is a current they were never required to enter. If one of them were to cease, the interval would simply remain empty, a ceremonial gap among the others, and the town would continue exactly as before, neither expanding nor crumbling. Tools of stone and dark, water-swollen wood lean unused in the corners of store-houses that store nothing. The tools are as sharp as the day they were flaked, because they have never been asked to cut.

In the middle distances the mist silvering their edges makes them look like further outcrops of the weeping rock, or like the ferns given an extra, vertical permission. They watch the far escarpment where the plateau has not yet decided to stop rising. They do not wait for it to finish. Waiting would imply a future different from the present, and the present is already complete. They simply continue, open and quiet, for as far as the even light will let the eye travel.
