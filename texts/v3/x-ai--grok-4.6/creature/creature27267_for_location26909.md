They are still there.

Not as memory or residue, not as the faint pressure a vanished crowd leaves in the air, but as the city itself is still there: precisely placed, damp-skinned, unaged. You find them first in the open sward, standing among the short-horned herds they once kept. Their coats are heavier than the climate quite requires, woven from the same pale fiber as the trees’ inner bark, beaded with the same unmoving moisture. Some have one hand resting on an animal’s flank; the contact has lasted so long that moss has begun, in places, to creep across both knuckle and hide as though they were a single outcrop. Their faces are turned in every direction the basin offers—toward a neighbor, toward a distant hall, toward the faceted ridges that have never receded. None of the expressions have finished. A half-smile remains half. A frown remains the first tightening of brow, never quite arriving at anger or sorrow. The eyes, pale or dark, hold the same unchanging west-of-noon light that the tarns hold.

Inside the low houses the stillness is denser, almost architectural. A woman sits at a stone table with a copper bowl she will never lift again; the last drop of whatever it contained still hangs, spherical, from the rim. A man stands in a doorway with one shoulder against the jamb, the posture of someone who has just decided to go out or has just decided to stay. Children occupy the packed-earth courts in the exact geometries of interrupted play: one knee raised, one arm flung, a carved wooden bird still leaving the hand but never quite airborne. Their hair, wherever it falls, has been arranged by a wind that no longer exists. In the taller halls, under the slit windows, figures sit or stand in the permanent half-light as though the gathering had only just quieted. Hands rest on knees or on neighboring shoulders. Someone’s mouth is slightly open, the beginning of a word that the air has agreed never to carry.

They are not statues. The distinction matters. Stone would have been simpler; these bodies keep the temperature of living flesh, the faint give of muscle that has simply forgotten how to continue the next contraction. Blood, if you could see it, would still be the same blood, circulating through the same unwearying loop it has used since the instant everything stopped. They do not hunger. They do not sleep. The dampness that beads on moss and leaf beads also on their lashes, on the inner wrists, on the hollows of throats, and stays. When the pale sun (always the same height, always a little west) strikes a cheekbone or the curve of a collarbone, the light is returned exactly as it was returned the first time, and the time after that, and every time since.

There are more of them than the streets suggest. They occupy the groves as well, sitting or reclining among the succulent leaves that never fall. A few have climbed—or were climbing—the lower slopes of the unfinished mountains; you can still see them as pale marks against the quarried planes, one foot higher than the other, fingers spread against rock that has never known a second season of ice. They look neither triumphant nor lost. They simply remain at the height they reached.

What they were doing when the equilibrium arrived was ordinary. That is the quiet terror and the quiet mercy of it. No one was in the middle of a great crime or a great revelation. They were mending, watching, speaking, waiting for the next thing that would have been the next thing. The process that lifted the range and settled the basin and then withdrew simply included them in the final arrangement, the way it included the ferns in their attitude of having just uncurled. They were not asked. They have not been released. They have not decayed. The same hush that keeps the water from running keeps their lungs from needing another breath, their hearts from needing another reason.

If you walk among them—and the packed earth will take your weight as it took theirs—you will hear your own footsteps with shocking clarity, because nothing else answers. Their eyes do not follow. Their heads do not turn. Yet the longer you remain, the more it seems they are not unaware. The stillness is too complete to be mere absence. It has the quality of a held note, or of attention that has nowhere left to go and so has become the place itself. They live here the only way left to live: as the unchanging part of an unchanging afternoon, the city’s true masonry, more lasting than the fitted stone.

And they are not alone even in this. The lizards the color of wet rock occupy the same crevices they occupied; the amber insects still cling to the undersides of leaves. The birds remain on every cornice. All of it—people, herds, moss, tarn, mountain—belongs to the same arrested breath. Whatever once moved through them has stepped aside and left the shapes standing, perfectly themselves, forever not quite the next moment.
