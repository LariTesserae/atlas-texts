The first things you notice moving are not the towers. They are too patient, too much a part of the slow argument the body is having with itself. What lives here first announces itself as a pale flicker at the edge of an unfinished plaza, then as a dry clicking that the living floor drinks almost before it happens.

They stand higher than they should. Legs like stripped saplings, jointed in too many places, each foot ending in a cluster of blunt keratin pads that the substrate accepts without scar. Their bodies are the color of the undecided ivory ground, slightly translucent at the flanks so that the faint internal glow of stored light shows through in veins. They do not graze living tissue. They crop only the gray fertile dust that the chimneys exhale, the same dust that falls like weather. A long prehensile lip, more root-tip than mouth, sifts the stuff; nothing is swallowed wet. Their gut is a closed spiral of metallic xylem that extracts what little energy the dust still carries and vents the rest as a warmer, coarser powder that other, smaller things later harvest. They travel in loose, silent herds of three or seven, never more, because the emptiness between districts is pasture enough and the encoded logic of the place does not yet require denser traffic. When one of them pauses, it is usually to listen with the whole length of its neck to a wall that is still thickening. Sometimes a rib of new lignin will rise under a hoof and the animal simply steps onto it, as if the city had offered a courtesy.

Above them, in the unfinished galleries where the vault has not yet decided how high it wants to be, the calipers wait. They are not animals in any comfortable sense. Each is a cluster of living measuring arms—some two meters long, some twenty—hinged around a central node that pulses with the same even overcast light as the walls. The arms are lined with rows of sensitive, chitinous blades that can close with the precision of a jeweler’s tool or the suddenness of a trap. They do not hunt often. Most of their time is spent tasting the air for the ozone-and-copper bloom of a factory-flower that has just opened, or for the faint electrical change that means a new gallery is about to dimple into existence. When they do move, it is with a mechanical unfolding, one arm after another planting itself, drawing the rest of the body across a span that did not exist the day before. They pollinate nothing. They prune. A caliper will wait years beside an over-eager buttress, then snip it back so the load can be redistributed according to a plan no one present remembers writing. Their patience is the patience of the organism itself: they are its immune system and its surveyors, grown from the same tissue, still attached by hair-thin conductive roots that trail behind them like forgotten cables.

Between these two scales the air is busy with smaller, glassier lives. The pollinators have wings like fractured mica, veined with the same metallic xylem that runs through the towers. They do not fly so much as they are conducted, riding the faint electrostatic gradients that the respiring walls create. Their bodies are little more than a pair of compound eyes and a coiled, needle-fine proboscis that uncoils only for the factory-flowers. Those flowers open without color. They announce themselves by scent and by a sudden local warming of the air, a bloom of ozone that tastes like a storm that will never rain. The insects drink nothing; they sip a silica-rich nectar that hardens instantly into a thin, conductive film inside them. After a few dozen such meals the insect itself becomes a seed: it settles on a raw patch of substrate, unfolds, and grows into a calibrated tool or a new length of photosynthetic foil, exactly as specified. The cycle is so tight that it is hard to say whether the insect is fauna or a mobile organ of the foundries.

There are other shapes, still rarer, that appear only when a district finishes deciding itself. In the quiet hours—though hour is a word the place has not fully invented—something like a procession will form. Thin, bipedal figures, no taller than the browsers’ shoulders, their skin the same cork-dry parenchyma, their faces featureless except for a single horizontal slit that glows with stored daylight. They walk the elevated root-roads that end in mid-air and simply step off, trusting the next decision of growth to catch them. Sometimes it does. A new span unrolls beneath their feet while they are still in the air. Sometimes it does not, and they fall the long way down onto the living floor, which accepts them without injury and begins, very slowly, to differentiate them into something else: a new press, a length of railing, a cluster of seeds that will later unfold into tools. They do not seem to mind. Their metabolisms are as closed and arid as everything else’s. They carry no water and remember none. What they do carry, in the faint electrical whisper that runs through their xylem, is the urban logic itself: the half-forgotten instruction that this basin should become a city, that waste heat should become light, that stone should be rewritten into architecture. They are the memory of the implant, walking.

All of them, browsers and calipers and glass-wings and the thin walkers, share the same silence. Sound here is mostly the absence of it, broken only by the vegetative thrum of distant presses and the occasional dry crack of a branch electing to exist. Yet they are not isolated. The living floor conducts more than weight. A browser’s hoof-click, a caliper’s precise snip, the sudden electrical bloom of a flower—all of it travels through the continental mat as a kind of shared proprioception. The organism knows where its parts are. The parts know, in some wordless way, that they are still deciding the shape of the whole.

Nothing here is finished, and therefore nothing here is only one thing. A browser may, over seasons measured in the slow weather of spores, begin to grow extra joints until it is more caliper than grazer. A walker may lie down on an undecided patch of ivory ground and simply become a new neighborhood, its limbs spreading into root-roads, its single glowing slit becoming a window that never closes. The place is still writing its own rules. What lives here is whatever the next decision of growth requires, and also whatever remembers the old solvent that is no longer present, or never was. They move through the sparse intervals as if the emptiness were already a city, because in the only way that matters here, it is.
