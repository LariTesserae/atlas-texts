The first things that live here do not have names that last longer than a season of drip. They arrive as stain. A film no thicker than breath settles on the pale new faces of the rock and begins to drink the standing dusk, and what drinks thickens. Liverworts first, flattened into wet leather that follows every micro-fracture as if the stone had always been waiting to be a page. Then mosses that grow a nap, a direction, a way of holding water so that the wall itself begins to sweat green. They do not root so much as remember; they remember how to be a lung when the air is already a lung.

Under them the ferns stack. Frond over frond until the geometry of the cleft is rewritten in repeating dark. Each new leaf is almost black in the apse, a green so starved of sun it generates its own dimness, and when you push a hand into that density the wrist disappears and the fingers come back colder, filmed with something that is not quite slime and not quite dew. The plants do not compete. There is no light-gradient left to climb; there is only the permission of the closed room, the cracked window of ten wet years compressed into fewer. They grow into one another. Root into root. A hanging garden shears away in a single wet mass and the scar is already greening at the edges before the sound of its falling has finished echoing. Brightness is treated as injury. They close over it.

Deeper, where the mosses have a nap you could lose a thought in, something older and slower works. Threads finer than hair, neither plant nor quite fungus, stitch the new cracks to the old ones. They taste the faint heated-metal note that does not belong to geology and they do not refuse it. They drink what the factories have given the valley wind. The plants have not been told they should sicken. They thicken instead. A stem straightens in the almost-inaudible after a stone the size of a chest has finished becoming floor. Water transfers from frond to frond with a sound like a small throat clearing. The density makes its own weather.

And still nothing with a heartbeat walks here. The silence of bodies is itself a kind of inhabitant, a negative space that the lushness presses against. No insect-tick, no bird using the updraft, no small mammal testing the talus. The air that pools after dark carries the industrial bass as a pressure in the ribs, a periodicity the mountain does not keep, and whatever might have come has decided against the vibration, or the newness, or the way the cleft is still being drawn. What remains are the listeners that have no ears: the mosses that flatten and spring with every change in the pitch of the wind, the ferns that register the distant blasting as a shiver of loose chips and then keep growing. They live inside the reed of rock. When wind and water coincide they become the instrument; when the generators far below cycle down they become the small sounds the density makes.

The cleft itself is the other life. It has not yet decided its walls belong to weather. They still belong to fracture, and fracture is a metabolism. A slab lets go without preface. Mist boils from a seam that was dry. Joints open in increments too small to see and too large to live with. The mountain lifts; water finds; frost (on the rare clear night) wedges. Storms occupy rather than visit. The work is unbuilt and unceasing: lift, split, wet, green, collapse, again. You could believe the world is being manufactured in this one room, not by anyone, not toward anything. The inhabitants are the manufacturing. They are the raw fact of formation wearing a nap of moss, wearing layers of almost-black frond, wearing the slow shine of water that is already becoming mist.

In the lull—if you wait through one—you hear them most clearly. Not voices. The almost-inaudible creep of a root into a crack that did not exist at dawn. A stem deciding to straighten. Condensation gathering, letting go, gathering again, as if the entire nave were a single slow pulse. Then the mountain remembers itself. The new bright wound appears and is already being closed. The plants have not been told the place will not stay. They live as if the dark were permanent, as if the standing dusk were a kind of soil, as if the industrial note under everything were simply another mineral they had learned to use. They keep becoming thicker. They keep losing themselves into the gutter and beginning again on the scar. That is what lives here: the refusal to wait for the mountain to finish deciding. The lush, the black-green, the unceasing colonizers of a room that is still being torn.
