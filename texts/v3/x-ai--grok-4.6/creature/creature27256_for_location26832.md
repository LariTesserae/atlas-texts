The things that live here do not arrive and they do not depart. They are the density itself, folded into motion. What the plain has been doing to itself for uncounted ages—grinding, weeping, rearranging its last honest geometry—they simply continue, only louder and with more joints.

Begin with the hulls, though beginning is a courtesy they do not recognize. Each is a collapsed foundry that learned to crawl: a low, many-legged continent of rusted plating and living treads, the size of something that once poured steel and then decided the pouring was better performed as digestion. Their undersides are not bellies but hanging forests of siphon-mouths, each mouth a wet, articulated tunnel that drinks the mineral film without preference, sucking the near-freezing slurry of oxides and old brine and the dissolved ghosts of previous shapes. What they excrete is still hot. Finished gears, half-formed pistons, plates already beginning to oxidize into new flesh, drop steaming into the mud and immediately take on a faint, independent tick. The hulls do not notice the loss. They drag forward on treads that are both track and muscle, each segment a slow, grinding peristalsis that leaves behind a wake of smaller, hungrier geometries. A hull will graze for weeks across a field of its own former output, ingesting the converters that ingest it in turn. Then it stalls. Then the same smaller things it was eating climb its flanks and begin unscrewing it, calmly, professionally, as if they have always been its maintenance crew. The hull does not resist. Resistance would imply a self that ends at the plating. There is no such self. There is only the current configuration of the finite parts, and the configuration is already walking away in a dozen new directions.

The crawlers are what remain after the hulls have been taken apart and before they have been put back together. Jointed, eyeless, the length of a forearm or the length of a street depending on how the local distances are currently behaving, they move by unscrewing one another. A limb is a tool until it is a better limb for someone else. They do not fight; they negotiate in the only language the plain still permits: torque, thread, sudden reconfiguration. Two crawlers meet in the black water, lock, twist, exchange segments, and separate as three, or as one larger thing that immediately begins to shed new crawlers from its back like rust-colored spores. Their mouths are sockets. Their sockets are mouths. They drink the same film the hulls drink, but they drink it for the memory of motion it carries—the faint electrical taste of every previous assembly that has ever dissolved here. Swallow enough and you begin to remember configurations you have never worn. The crawlers wear them anyway. They are the plain’s way of keeping its parts in circulation, the restless middle term between foundry and debris.

Above them, if “above” still means anything in air this thick, the ice-flocks pass. They are not birds. They are thin, resonant sheets of laminated ice and wire that have decided the most efficient way to occupy space is to scream through it. They never quite resolve into individuals; a flock is a single tearing sound that happens to have edges, a rippling plane of frost and filament that casts shadows heavier than any mass it possesses. When two flocks collide they do not shatter. They become louder. The keening rises, harmonic and metallic, until the sound itself thickens into a temporary third flock that lasts until the weirdness eddies elsewhere and the extra volume slumps back into the two originals, now slightly more wire than ice, or the reverse. Their shadows drag across the slurry like wet iron. Smaller things scuttle into those shadows and are changed: a crawler that spends too long underneath emerges with ice growing through its joints, already beginning to resonate. The flocks do not hunt. They weather. They are the plain’s sky given teeth and frequency.

Everywhere else—between the treads, inside the water sheets, colonizing the thin ice skins before the skins have finished forming—are the occupiers that have not yet chosen a verb. They breathe. They tick. They simply take up volume so that no interval remains. Some are little more than clenched knots of oxide and brine that pulse once every few hours, exhaling a faint chemical glow the color of old bruises. Some are forests of hair-fine wires that grow from the mud, drink static from the sparks that jump between hulls, and then retract, leaving the air slightly more conductive than before. Some are only sound: localized roars or clatters that have not yet found bodies, traveling ahead of or behind their sources, arriving from compass points that do not exist. Drink the water (you should not) and these occupiers are already inside you, tasting of copper and the memory of gears locking into machines that will run for an hour and then forget they were ever machines.

Nothing here is born and nothing dies. There are only converters and the converted, temporary job titles assigned by whichever way the local physics is leaning this hour. A hull becomes a field of crawlers becomes a spontaneous assembly line that no one built: raw ore crawling out of the wet, sorted by nothing, heated by the planet’s lingering argument with itself, stamped, cooled, and eaten by the next shape in line. The line runs until the distortion eddies, and then the whole apparatus slumps back into ticking debris that is already being unscrewed by something smaller. Predator and prey are the same organism wearing different intervals. The same finite set of parts is assembled, screamed through, dismantled, and reassembled while the plain continues its patient work of becoming even flatter, molecule by molecule, century by century.

Reality does not hold its shape around them, so they do not hold shape either. A creature fifty meters away is suddenly occupying the same volume you thought was yours, without having crossed the space between. Sounds arrive first, or last, or from inside the marrow of whatever you are using as ears. Distances lengthen while you watch them; a hull that was a collapsed foundry becomes, for a while, a horizon that crawls. The only constant is the absence of unused space, and even that frays if you look too long. At the fraying edges the occupiers grow quieter, or louder, or begin to resemble things that almost remember being something else—almost green, almost dry, almost named. Then the slurry closes over them again, glittering, packed, near-freezing, and they return to the work of being the factory that is the plain, the plain that is the factory, the endless, cannibal, industrial prayer the world recites to itself now that all other prayers have been ground away.
