The water holds them first as it holds everything else: without distinction, without hurry. Before any human shape resolves, the basin is already thick with lesser tenants who treat the grown halls as weather and furniture both. Schools of char turn in the long sight-lines, their flanks the exact pale of winter granite, so that a sudden wheel of them can vanish against a wall and reappear as a single thought three rooms away. Shrimp hang in glittering, indifferent weather under every eave, so dense in places that the light itself seems to pulse. Newts the length of a forearm walk the fiber floors with the unhurried proprietorship of things that have never been told they do not belong; they pause at thresholds as if considering rent. Diving beetles tick against the inner skins of the stems like slow clocks. At the few crowns that break into air the frogs keep their wet-iron chorus, the sound traveling down the living columns the way speech travels inside a pipe. And the otter-things—sleek, insulting, never quite named—use the lowest rooms as thoroughfares, leaving neat cairns of fish-heads on the matted floors as if the city were a larder they had merely consented to share.

Only after the eye has accepted all of that do the people themselves come into focus, and even then they arrive the way the light does: gradually, as a thickening rather than an entrance. There are never many. Two hands would more than count the grown ones; a third hand would cover the children. They do not fill the basin any more than the towers do. They occupy it the way a few large, slow thoughts occupy a quiet mind.

Their bodies have taken the temperature’s instruction. They are long, not tall exactly, but lengthened by a life spent more in water than out of it, limbs economical, joints that fold close. Skin the color of the palest granite, or perhaps only stained that way by years of green-gold light and the faint tannin of the plants; in certain angles it shows a faint mottling, like silt that never quite settled. Hair, when it is not bound, hangs heavy and dark, moving a half-second behind the head as if still deciding whether to be plant or person. Eyes are large and set a little farther apart than the air-world would ask, accustomed to grainy distances, to seeing a dropped awl on the living floor from three chambers away. They do not shiver. The cold has been folded into them the way the waterfall has been folded into the lake: it is simply the medium. Flesh here forgets its edges not as a failure but as a courtesy. A hand extended to harvest pith unfolds the way a blade of the giant plants unfolds—deliberate, complete, then still again.

They wear almost nothing that the water cannot take back. Garments, when they exist, are thin panels of cured inner-blade, the fiber left in the cold until it sings if drawn taut, then cut and bound with fish-spine needles. Most days the cloth is more suggestion than covering, clinging, translucent, more a way of carrying tools than of hiding skin. Bone awls ride in loops at the hip. Granite flakes rest in shallow pockets grown into the stems themselves. Everything is old in the hand, polished by the same generations that cut the underwater stairs.

Their dwellings are the plants, and the plants are still themselves. To enter is to pass through a living threshold that remembers it was once asked only to stand. The hollowed stems are not dry. Water fills them as it fills everything, but the growers have trained certain laterals to hold pockets of breath—small, ringing rooms where the pith is dried and where a person can rest a face and take the air that the mountain still permits. Those air-bells sound when a current crosses their skin, a faint glass note that is the only clock besides the fall. Floors are packed fiber, springy, dark, furred in places with the same black moss that has claimed the old stone stairs. Windows are simply places where the plant was never asked to close; through them the next tower stands visible as a darker vertical, swaying half a degree out of true. There is no furniture that is not also growth: ledges of interwoven laterals, niches that have thickened around a stored flake or a coil of cordage. The work of making and remaking is continuous and nearly invisible. A stem is wounded in spring, bound, left. Decades later the wound is a doorway, the binding a load-bearing ring. Growth-rings record the same careful injuries their grandparents made. The city replaces itself in kind, one joint at a time.

They move the way the temperature requires: long stillnesses broken by seasons rather than hours. Harvest is a slow unwrapping of pith, the white interior lifted in sheets and carried to the air-bells. Children learn the water before they learn the names of the walls; they are born already half-fluent in the cold, their first journeys a weighted walk along the fiber floors with an elder’s hand at the nape. Speech is economical. Voices do not carry far against the waterfall’s continuous thunder, so they have kept the older language of the stems. Certain mature columns, struck in the right place with a knuckle, return a note that is neither wood nor bone. The growers say it is the mountain answering through the plant. They do not decorate the fact. They use it to find hairline fractures before they become failures, and to call across the open water when the fall is too loud for throats. A conversation can travel the length of the basin as a series of low, woody syllables, each tower repeating, slightly altered by its own girth and age.

Death is a walk. The body is weighted with the same dark stones that litter the talus at the deep end, then accompanied down the last slope where the roots give way to bare granite and the light thins to a rumor. The char go still there, as if the water itself were holding its breath. No one speaks of after. The place is already an after: a finished argument between water, plant, and height that continues, very slowly, to grow in place.

Once a year they surface. Not for long. The crowns must be tended—dead blades cut, new laterals trained toward the light—and in the hardest weeks a thin plate of ice forms across the basin, clear as old bottle glass. They stand in the air that hurts, watching it. Then, without drama, the ice lets go. They go back under. The ringing of the air-bells resumes. The waterfall files its notch another hair’s breadth. A child learns the name of a wall by swimming its length in the dark. The mountain answers, when asked, in the voice of a living stem. 

They are still becoming the place that already holds them. The settlement is the city. The city is still growing, very slowly, in place.
