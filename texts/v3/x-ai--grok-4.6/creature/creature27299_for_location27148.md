The people of the hamlet are few enough that you learn their silhouettes before their names. They move through the slots between leaf and pipe the way the water moves through the race: unhurried, knowing every obstruction by heart, never quite dry. Their clothes are the same grammar as the sheds—canvas and their own coarse matting, always a little dark at the hems, always smelling of steam and the faint sweetness of retting liquor. You see the same faces at the stamper, at the zinc tables, at the lock gate after the dynamo is shut down. Three generations, sometimes four, living in the six or seven boxes. No one arrives and no one quite leaves; the crushed-shell road drowns twice a year and the reeds close over any thought of elsewhere.

They are not tall. The pipes and the trained canes have seen to that. Heads stay slightly ducked, shoulders narrowed, as if the body itself has been sistered to the architecture. Skin stays pale under the greened light, but the hands and forearms are stained a permanent tea-color from the tannin pans and the canal. Nails are short, cuticles split. They carry cloths the way other people carry keys—always one in a pocket or looped at the belt, for the sweat that beads even when they are still, for the walls that weep behind the furniture, for the condensation that collects on the underside of every leaf they brush. Laundry is taken in still damp and hung again the next morning; no one comments on the smell of leaf and boiler.

The oldest among them remember the bad year only as a story their parents told while pointing at the raised sills and the moss that now belongs to the newer brick. They watch the crack in the boiler the way one watches a relative’s cough: not with alarm, but with a practiced, daily noting. Three successive hands have watched it. The current boilerman is a woman whose hair never dries completely; she banks the fire at the same hour her grandmother did and speaks to the pressure gauge in the same low, continuous mutter the stamper makes. The younger ones learn the paths by walking them behind an elder, learning which roots have begun to lift which planks, which lashings will need twisting before the next wet month, which aroids must be cut from the windows and which must be left because they shed water better than any paint they have ever been able to keep.

They eat what the canteen urn and the managed stands provide, plus whatever can be traded down the canal when the lock is open: tea that is always hot, a dense bread that never quite loses the taste of canal-silt, fish sometimes, though the heron is usually the only one who succeeds at that. Talk at the tables is close-range and practical—gutters, rotations, the color of the liquor this week—because the stamper fills every other frequency. In the dwellings the interiors are greenhouse-warm and the furniture is raised on bricks or short legs so the water-glint between the floor planks stays only a glint. Children are taught early not to step between the boards, not because they will fall through (the lid is stronger than it looks) but because the dark water beneath is the same water that has always been there, and it is not for playing.

At evening, when the gold light catches in the steam and the quieter sounds come forward, someone always walks the plank to the lock with a lamp. It is not a duty so much as a continuation of the day’s known path. They stand a moment looking at the black reeds against the still-pale sky, then turn back. The hamlet settles around them the way the living posts have settled around the timber frames: not as owners, not as guests, but as the latest layer of the same long negotiation. They sleep with the windows shuttered against the afternoon damp that has not yet finished arriving, and they wake already hearing the overflow falling into the race, already knowing which root will need lashing before the belts start again.

What lives here is not separate from the wet, the rust, the planted roofs, the watched crack. The people have the same weather as the mill. They have been a little wet for a long time. They remain.
