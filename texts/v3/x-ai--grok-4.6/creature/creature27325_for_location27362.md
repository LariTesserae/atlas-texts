The men came first with their charges and their lamps, but they are not the oldest thing here and they know it. They move like visitors who have overstayed, six-hour ghosts in dripping wool and leather, their faces already glazed into the same expression the rock wears: patience mixed with a slow surprise that this heat was waiting. They grease, they shovel beetle-silt, they lay another course of pale brick that will never sit quite true. At the end of a shift they climb the dripping ladders without looking back, because looking back would mean admitting the chamber has already begun to keep them. Their sweat joins the seeps. Their discarded crusts become another generation of the dark insects. In twenty years the ones who remain will have the same unfinished look as the walls.

The beetles were already the limestone’s secret when the first shot went off. They lived in the hairline joints, tasting the film that the deep water left, a patient nation no larger than a thumbnail. The blast was their flood and their continent at once. Now they are the moving dark. They sheet the wetter walls until the stone itself seems to crawl. They skate the pans in glittering rafts, they pack the unpointed mortar, they lift in soft, rice-on-tin storms when a lantern swings past. They do not bite. They do not need to. They eat the green smear that tries to become algae, they eat the spilled grain, they eat their own dead when the grain is gone, and their castings settle in the lowest corner as a fine black silt that is already soil. Generations pass in a fortnight. A man who stands still too long feels them exploring the salt on his boots, mapping him the way they map everything else. They are the chamber’s first true citizens, and they have not yet decided whether the men are furniture or food.

Behind the engines a continuous high wire sings: the pale crickets, thumb-long, almost translucent, their song never quite drowned. They live in the cooler fractures, in the gaps between brick and slag pad, coming out only when the dynamo stutters and the four caged bulbs dim. Their music is the sound the heat would make if heat had a throat. No one has found their eggs. Perhaps they do not lay eggs. Perhaps they are the limestone remembering how to chirp.

In the third month the frogs appeared, small and glass-clear, sitting in the warmest seeps with throats pulsing like tiny bellows. No one saw them arrive. They might have come down the chimney with the bats, or up through some hairline that the water opened, or they might have been waiting in the rock the way the beetles waited. They sit with their toes spread on the steaming glaze, perfectly still except for that pulse, and when a beetle blunders too close they take it with a motion too fast for the dim light. Their tadpoles—if they have tadpoles—have never been seen. Perhaps they do not need them. Perhaps the chamber simply grew them the way it grew the first calcite skin.

Bats come down the chimney at what the surface calls dusk. They hang in a trembling pelt from the highest unshored span, a single dark organ that breathes. Their droppings hiss when they strike the hot floor. They are the only thing that still remembers the sky, and they treat the chamber as a throat that happens to be warm. When they lift, the beetles rise with them in a double storm, dark on dark.

Something larger lives in the unfinished adit at the north end. The men hear it at the edge of sleep: a scrape that is not quite claw, a settling that is not quite stone. No one has gone far enough to put a lamp on it. The ones who have gone a little way come back speaking of a heat that is different there, wetter, older, and of a smell like crushed beetles mixed with something ferrous and alive. It may be only the rock still finding its joints. It may be a colony of the same beetles grown strange in the dark, fused into a single slow body that drags itself along the drill-holes. It may be the chamber’s own first attempt at a larger animal, assembled from heat and silt and the memory of whatever swam here when the limestone was still a sea. The rule is simple: you do not go alone, and you do not go far. The adit listens. Sometimes, when the beam engine misses a nod, the sound from the north answers it, as if the two great lungs of the place were learning the same rhythm.

Even the plants are trying. In the unpointed cracks a few stems uncurl, fern-pale, almost white, then blacken at the tips where the heat finds them. A brass-colored lichen maps the cooler western wall in continents no larger than a palm. They are not a forest. They are a rumor of forest, the chamber experimenting with the idea of green the way it experiments with soil. No one tends them. No one could. They live the way everything else lives here: provisionally, in the pause between one seep and the next, between one generation of beetles and the silt they leave behind.

All of it—men, beetles, crickets, frogs, bats, the unnamed thing in the adit, the pale stems, the lichen, even the rust that weeps down the bolts—occupies the chamber the way furniture occupies a room that is still deciding its own size. Nothing here is finished. The pans will be a creek by next year. The ninth structure will either stand or become another pad of slag. The soil in the lowest corner will either hold a root or wash away. The place is still being born, and every living thing in it is part of the labor: eating, dripping, singing, scraping, uncurling, dying into silt that the water carries a little farther each day. Tonight they are all awake together in the dim overlapping circles of the bulbs, learning, very slowly, how to belong to a room that did not exist last season and may not exist in twenty years. The heat holds them. The water writes their names in temporary calcite. The beetles keep count.
