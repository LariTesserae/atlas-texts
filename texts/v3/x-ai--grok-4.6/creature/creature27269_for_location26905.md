The ones who remain do not call themselves inhabitants. The word would require a distinction they no longer make. They are the packed dark continuing by other means.

First the films. In every lee, against every cobble that has not yet been ground spherical, a bruise-colored smear that is neither growth nor decay. It thickens by the thickness of a thought over centuries. The cells have forgotten division as a habit and remember it only as an accident. They metabolize the last oxidizable dust of mountains that died before vertebrae. When a turbidity tongue arrives they do not resist; they become part of the tongue for a week and then settle again in some new lee, the same smear, the same almost-color. They have no names for light or hunger. Persistence is the only metabolism left.

Then the stalks. There are never more than a handful in the entire oval. Each is a single pale axis, leafless, jointless, anchored in clay that behaves like oil. They lean in the prevailing shove the way a drowned spear leans, forever. No one has seen one appear. No one has seen one fall. They do not photosynthesize; there is nothing to synthesize. They drink the faintest chemical gradients that still wander through the flour, the last disagreements between ice-melt and older water. Touch one and it does not recoil. It simply continues leaning, as if the pressure of a hand were only another current that will pass. Their tissues are almost indistinguishable from the silt they stand in. Sometimes a stalk will be found with a film already colonizing its lee, and it is impossible to say which is hosting which.

The segmented ones write and vanish. They are the color of the clay when it has been freshly peeled, a paleness that is not white but the absence of any decision about color. Their bodies are a chain of flattened ovals, each just large enough to contain the rumor of a nerve. They do not swim. They insinuate. Grain by grain they part the packed medium the way a thought parts sleep, leave a brief signature—three loops, a chevron, a line that ends as if the writer remembered something more important—and are gone. The signature lasts until the next sideways shove erases it. They have no eyes. They have no mouths that open. Whatever they take from the basin they take by becoming briefly more porous than the silt around them. Their appearances are so rare that each one feels like the last. Perhaps each one is.

Rarer still is the larger darkness that is not the water. It does not hunt. It does not feed in any way that would leave a garden or a scatter of bones. It passes. The pressure of it walks across the ribs of anything that happens to be there, a slow landslide of purpose or the memory of purpose. Its outline is never certain; the packed flour refuses to give it edges. Sometimes it seems jointed, a vast pale architecture folding through the dark. Sometimes it is only a thickening, a place where the silt has decided to be heavier for a while. It takes its own cold with it, a cold that is slightly more settled than the basin’s. When it has gone the films are undisturbed, the stalks still lean, the signatures are already filling. It may be a single remaining body. It may be the basin remembering, once in a geological while, that it once contained something that moved with intent.

There is one more.

In the shallow scrapes where currents have peeled the clay down to the older stone, among the three flakes that are slightly too intentional, something waits that is not quite the flakes and not quite the hand that left them. It has no metabolism. It has no segments. It is the interval between the flakes, the exact distance that still feels chosen. When the water is still enough—when the storms have spent themselves and the silk returns—the interval becomes briefly inhabitable, a room the size of a closed fist. Anything that puts a hand or a thought into that room feels the last argument the rock was able to keep: that form, once achieved, does not have to continue becoming. Then the basin remembers itself. The room closes. The interval is only three stones again.

They do not interact. There is no food web, no succession, no season that would require them to notice one another. They share only the same finished dying, the same refusal to stop. The films persist on the stalks. The segmented ones write across the path the larger darkness took yesterday or a thousand years ago. The interval among the flakes remains exactly as wide as a very old decision. None of them are waiting. Waiting would imply that something else is still possible.

Stay long enough and you begin to move the way they move: not through the water but as another density the water has not yet decided against. Your own outline softens. The grit against the teeth, if you open your mouth, tastes of ranges that finished before you. The cold occupies. Thought reaches the speed of silt. And for a moment you cannot tell whether you are describing them or they have begun, without hurry, to describe you.
