The dwellers of the nameless cirque do not arrive or depart so much as condense and dissolve with the same slow fidelity as the rime. They are the next mineral layer, the one that still remembers how to breathe.

They are short, yes, but not stunted; the thin air and the ceaseless lateral light have simply refused them the luxury of height. Their bodies pack densely, like the rooms they inhabit: broad through the chest, joints wrapped in greasy felt and hide until the silhouette is more mound than figure. Faces are dark not from pigment but from the endless ricochet of brightness off ice and wet granite; the skin there has the matte, slightly pitted look of stone that has been breathed on for generations. Eyes are pale, almost translucent at the iris, as if the glare has worn the color out of them. They do not squint. They have learned to look through dazzle the way fish look through water. Hair, when it shows at all, is kept short or bound under hoods of the same greasy felt, because anything long would freeze into a helmet of rime by the time a person crossed a plaza.

They move as the goats move: along the tops of walls, through covered ways barely wider than a shoulder, never quite stopping. A woman with a hide-bucket of meltwater will pause only long enough for a fox to pour past her ankles like smoke; the fox will wait with equal courtesy if she is first. Children (there are always children, though it is hard to tell their ages; they are simply the smaller, quicker versions) spend their hours at the tarn’s crawling edge, scooping milk-colored shrimp with bone ladles worn smooth as river stones. They eat them raw, standing, the tiny bodies popping between their teeth with a sound too small for the air to carry. The old ones sit in the lee-plazas, backs to the weeping wall, and flake scrapers from whatever glacial cobble the last slump has delivered. Their breath is a private weather, a thin white script that the rime immediately edits.

They do not speak of themselves as a people with a beginning. The oldest rooms, the ones welded to bedrock by calcite and time, bear tool-marks no living hand can match. The present inhabitants maintain. They restack the course the seep has undermined, pack moss and felt into joints, catch what the tarn offers, and move the animals when the animals decide it is time to move. They refer to the bowl the way one refers to a grandmother who has always been ancient: with a mixture of practical intimacy and unexamined awe. They do not claim to have built it. They complete it.

But the people are only the most obvious pulse. The goats are the architecture’s second skin. Smaller than any valley breed, they are the color of old snow, ears translucent enough that the veins show like faint blue script, hooves of something that looks like frosted glass and sounds like nothing at all. They sleep in the lee of chimneys so thickly that the roofs appear flocked, a living thatch that shifts and resettles without bleat or complaint. At certain hours they become a river along the wall-tops, hooves clicking once, softly, then never again. The white foxes are the alleys’ blood. Cat-sized, silent, they pour through cracks in ribbons that seem to have no individual beginning or end. They wait. They are waited for. Their eyes are the same pale, worn color as the people’s.

Ravens occupy the highest stacks like a second, darker hamlet. They are huge, almost soundless; a wingbeat is the sound of a page turning two rooms away. They do not speak. They watch the tarn’s ice-plates override one another and seem, at times, to be reading them. The frost-moths lift whenever a hide-door is pulled aside: clouds of wings that are literally plates of ice, so thin they melt on the nearest wet stone and are replaced by the next generation before the door has swung shut. They are the place’s thought, the one it keeps having and forgetting and having again.

In the deepest, warmest seeps—where the mountain’s internal heat still remembers itself—live the unnamed. Colonial, slow, they look like lichen that has learned a pulse. They breathe. You cannot put a palm to those walls without feeling the faint, regular suction, as though the stone itself were tasting you. The inhabitants do not name them. They do not need to. The shrimp are already in the drinking water; the unnamed are already in the walls. Everything here is already inside everything else.

Water itself behaves like another inhabitant, one that has forgotten which way is down. Films creep up cistern walls. Beads travel the undersides of lintels and drop upward into the rime-beards, as if the mountain were inhaling. Ice-plates on the tarn collide, override, drown, and write brief, glittering sentences that dissolve before they can be finished. Steam-devils walk. The hanging glaciers at the rim seem, if you look too long, to breathe. Perhaps they do. Perhaps the entire cirque is one long, established shudder that found its shape ten thousand years ago and has been faithfully performing it ever since.

There is no night, only a ceaseless weather of phase. Time of day is a habit imported from below and soon dropped. The light has no source you can point to without also pointing at the snow, the wet stone, your own hands. In this light the dwellers are not separate from the place; they are the place continuing. A fox waits for a woman. A child scoops shrimp that are also the water. An old man flakes a scraper whose curve he cannot quite match to the marks in the oldest rooms. A moth settles, melts, is replaced. A wall slumps and is restacked without comment, the way one breathes.

They are all of them the packing. They are the hush that feels like a rule. They are the storm that found its shape and has been too quiet, too bright, and too full to interrupt ever since. To enter is to become another body in the press, another film of water climbing a wall, another small regular sound of stones being restacked because the seep has undermined them again. The cirque does not argue with gravity anymore. It only weeps. And they, all of them, are the weeping given hands and hooves and wings and pulse.
