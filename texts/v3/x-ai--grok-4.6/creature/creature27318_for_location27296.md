The chamber’s first inhabitants are not the ones you notice. They are the film, the black-iridescent skin that coats every cooler seam and every vent-mouth that has not yet opened. It is neither lichen nor rust. It grows by drinking hydrogen that leaks from fractures too fine for any larger body, and it dies by being rasped away, molecule by molecule, by the undersides of everything that walks. The film has no name for itself. It simply thickens in the dark hours between vent-cycles and is planed back to a sheen so thin it reads as polished stone. Where the film is thickest it stores a faint warmth of its own, a private battery the grazers harvest without ever lifting their heads.

Above the film the pavement begins. The grazers are flattened, heat-dark, sintered along their dorsal plates until neighboring bodies fuse at the edges into a single false basalt. Each is the size of a man’s palm or smaller; the largest are the size of a spread hand and those are old, their plates crazed with healed cracks that have themselves been overgrown by younger siblings. They do not walk so much as circulate. A slow shear travels through the living floor the way a shiver travels through a horse’s flank: one plate lifts a fraction, another slides into the vacancy, a third rotates a degree to keep the mosaic closed. Their mouths are ventral rasps, endless and patient. Their only sense is a constellation of heat-pits along the anterior margin, dull coals that register the next vent before it opens. They have no preference for direction except toward warmth. When the warmth shifts they become a tide that never quite breaks.

The predators live in the interstices of that tide. They are paler, longer, built of segments that telescope rather than flex. Their legs end in pads that never quite touch the basal layer; they stand on the grazers themselves, using the living pavement as both floor and larder. A predator does not stalk. It waits until the coordinated drift of the floor brings a grazer whose heat-signature has begun to fail—the first sign of a molt or a wound—and then it drops. The strike is a single unfolding, a pale scythe that opens and closes in the time it takes a vent to exhale. Two body-lengths later another predator, previously indistinguishable from a cooling ridge of lava, takes the first. The vacancy closes from all sides at once. Nothing is wasted. Even the pale hunter’s own hemolymph, when it spills, is rasped up by the grazers before it can evaporate.

Higher, on the walls and in the vault’s irregularities, the economy changes. Here live the filterers: bodies like articulated hooks or like folded fans of chitin, hanging in seams that were once perhaps something else. They do not hunt. They wait for the rain of dry fragments—the shed plates, the emptied husks, the powdered remains of whatever the floor has already finished. When a chain of them releases its harvest it is not a decision; it is a change in the air’s chemistry that you feel behind the eyes as a sudden pressure, an invitation that has no voice. The fragments fall. The grazers accept them without looking. The filterers fold shut again and become part of the wall.

Scattered among them are the drinkers. These are smaller, almost translucent, clustered around the few places where a corpse has not yet been fully reduced. They sip the last interior moisture before the air can steal it. Their bodies collapse afterward into papery shells that the hooks will later take. Nothing here is individual for long. Even the drinkers, in their brief glut, are already becoming the next layer of glaze.

At the vault’s highest point hangs the mass that may be one animal or a fused colony. It is darker than the rest, veined with a phosphor so faint it is visible only after you look away. It pulses on a clock slower than any vent. Each pulse is a contraction that warms the entire chamber by a fraction of a degree, a communal exhalation that the floor answers with a new drift. The mass does not eat in any way you can see. Perhaps it lives on the chemistry the others produce, or on the heat itself, or on something older that still seeps from the rock. When it pulses the heat-pits of every other creature flare in answer, a dim shifting constellation that maps the chamber’s attention. The mass has been there longer than the iron spike in the calcite seam, longer than the ring of fitted stones now packed with nest-material. It does not remember those things. It simply occupies the geometry they left.

There are no elders, no young. Size is only heat-history. A grazer that has survived a hundred vent-cycles is larger and darker and more thoroughly sintered to its neighbors; that is all. Reproduction is a quiet splitting, a plate that thickens and then shears along an invisible line, the two halves remaining side by side until the next drift rearranges them. The dead are not buried. They are incorporated. A body that fails becomes first a drinker-site, then a hook-harvest, then another irregularity in the pavement that the living will climb over for centuries until even the memory of the shape is gone.

They do not know the plateau above them. They do not know the chimney or the bruise-colored slot of sky. The chamber is the world, and the world is a closed grammar of heat, chemistry, and occupancy. The old human marks—the scraped niche, the iron bloom, the ring of stones—are only cooler or warmer patches now, another set of ramps and basins to be used without preference. The animals have no word for artifact. They have no word for themselves. They continue.

If you stand still they close around your feet with the same small accommodation they give a cooling vent or a fallen husk. Your pulse becomes another tick in the grammar. Your heat becomes another invitation. When you leave, the vacancy fills before you have climbed the first turn of the chimney. The map of the chamber stays on your skin for a long time, a memory of a place that has already forgotten the fluctuation you were.
