The first lives are not creatures so much as decisions the water has not finished making. They coat every surface that will hold still long enough: a greasy iridescence on the tea-dark films, a velvet nap on the undersides of the door-sized leaves, a faint clouding inside the weeps that run from the half-stone. Touch one and it comes away on the skin like oil that remembers being alive. They eat light that has already been used and they exhale the same heat the basin was born with. Nothing hunts them because they are the plate everything else eats from; when a mouth opens they are already inside it.

Above that film the smaller walkers begin. They are the second venation the leaves wear, threads and commas and flattened commas with too many legs, all the color of wet bark or the pale inner meat of a snapped frond. They do not travel so much as they occupy. A single broad leaf may carry three dozen, each locked to its own vein, jaws working the same microscopic pasture they worked when the leaf first unrolled and will work when the leaf is finally horizontal and becoming ground. Their bodies leave no trail that lasts; the moisture closes behind them like a mouth. Some have wings that are only rumors of wings, veined sac-things they hold closed until a vibration in the air tells them the instant has come to open and then close again. The sound they make together is the high end of the factory roar, a continuous dry sizzle that the steam conducts as readily as the air.

In the standing sheets the next order lives by displacement. They are longer than a hand, some longer than a forearm, bodies that have not chosen between eel and lizard and something that stayed in the egg. Feathery gills still fringe the necks of many; the gills do not recede because there has never been a drier hour to recede into. They hang in the warm lenses with only the upper surface of the head breaking, eyes like dark seeds set high, mouths that hinge wider than the skull should allow. When they move it is a single slow flex that sends a bruise of silt blooming and then settling into the same bruise it has always been. They take whatever the surface delivers: the skating insects, the fallen crawlers, the smaller versions of themselves. The taking is soundless except for the wet clap that is already part of the chorus. Vacancy after a taking lasts the length of a held breath. Another body, identical in every scar and every length of gill, occupies the same pane before the silt has finished falling.

The ground-mattress belongs to the heavier ones, the ones whose bellies have never learned to lift clear. They are built low and wide, four limbs splayed like the roots they walk among, skin pebbled and mucous, the color of the unfinished clay itself. Some carry the suggestion of armor that never quite hardened; others are naked and shining. They do not walk so much as they shoulder the wet sediment aside, leaving troughs that fill at once with the same water they displaced. Their heads are broader than their bodies, jaws set with teeth that are still deciding whether to be pegs or blades. They wait more than they pursue. Waiting here is not patience; it is the same posture held until the world delivers. When it delivers they do not lunge so much as they become, suddenly, the place where the delivering thing ends. The copper smell that follows is indistinguishable from the chlorophyll; both are simply the basin digesting itself.

Higher in the stacked green the climbers live, dark amphibians wedged into every crotch and axil as if the plants grew around them rather than the reverse. They are the size of a clenched fist or a little larger, throats that pulse with the mid-frequency roar, toe-pads that never dry. They do not leave their stations. A particular fork of two fern-trunks holds the same occupant it has always held; if that occupant is taken, the replacement arrives already wearing the same damp, the same half-lidded stare, the same exact pressure of body against bark. They sing without needing night. The song is not conversation. It is the announcement that this volume of air is still occupied, the same announcement that has been made since the first horsetail found the first welt of firmer ground.

The air between the leaves belongs to the wings that have learned to aim. They are not birds. They are hinged things, some no larger than a thumbnail, some with spans that would cover a face, bodies like slivers of the weeping stone given motility. Their flight is never far and never high; the steam will not permit either. They beat from one hanging drip to the next, from the flank of a larger animal to the underside of a leaf, and the sound they make is rain that has chosen targets. Many are taken on the wing by tongues that unfold from the climbers or by the sudden rise of a water-dweller. The taking does not thin them. The air remains occupied at the same density, the same pitch of wing-noise, as if the basin keeps a fixed number of wingbeats the way it keeps a fixed temperature.

Larger still, half in the water and half in the rumor of bank, live the long ones that are almost crocodilian and will not complete the resemblance. They lie so still that mosses and small crawlers use their ridged backs as another layer of ground. Only the eyes and the nostrils break, and even those are the color of the tea they rest in. One in particular has become a landmark simply by never leaving its broader sheet: a presence the length of several people, so constant that the plants at the margin have grown their leaves to accommodate the exact contour of its stillness. It does not hunt on any schedule a witness could learn. It opens, it closes, the water stills again. Whatever it takes is replaced before the ripples reach the far side of its pane. It has no name because nothing here has ever required one. It is simply the large wet length that occupies that water, as the water occupies that depression, as the depression occupies that part of the unfinished basin.

There are others, less distinct. Things that might be colonies rather than individuals, mats of pulsing jelly that creep the sediment and leave it richer. Things that hang from the slower architecture of the high leaves like seed-pods until they are not pods. Things whose outlines refuse to settle: a suggestion of shell that is also a suggestion of leaf, a cluster of limbs that might be roots still deciding to walk. They do not accumulate into kinds. Kinds would require a history of divergence, and the basin has never granted time enough for divergence to finish. Everything remains in the middle of its own argument with form.

They see by the dimness and by heat and by the way a mass of green is slightly too thick. Their eyes, when they have them, are large and lidless or nearly so, black or the sullen gold of the light itself. Many do not bother with eyes at all; the steam carries vibration and scent so completely that sight is almost an extravagance. They do not sleep in any way that pauses the chorus. They do not travel farther than the next identical thicket. They do not grow old in any direction a count of seasons could measure, because there are no seasons and the count would have nowhere to land. When one body ends, the basin does not record the ending. Another body, wearing the same wet, the same station, the same exact hunger, is already there. Replacement is not succession. It is the refusal of absence.

What lives here is therefore not a population. It is a single crowded moment that has learned how to move inside itself without ever leaving. The heat has no source and they have no origin. The ground will not finish and they will not conclude. They are the roar that does not travel, the green that has reached its argument, the unfinished made flesh and then made flesh again, forever in the hour of becoming, perfectly balanced because nothing is permitted to tip. Stand among them long enough and the suspicion arrives that they are not living in the basin at all. The basin is living in them, displaying itself through a thousand wet throats and a thousand slow jaws and the endless, identical, unblinking occupation of every volume it can reach.
