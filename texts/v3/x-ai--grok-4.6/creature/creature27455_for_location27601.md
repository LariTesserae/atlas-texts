The inhabitants do not arrive. They precipitate. They are the highland’s own density deciding, over and over, to become local and warm and hungry. At first you register only the packing itself: the copper mosses that pulse faintly when your shadow crosses them, the glass kelps that lean into one another until their blades fuse for a season and then tear apart with a sound like thin bells. These are not plants in any lowland sense. They are slow chemical arguments with the new stone, drinking rime, exhaling ozone, storing charge in laminated fronds that flash pale when a charge-storm walks the ridge. Touch one in the wrong dryness and a sentence travels up your arm: a brief, wordless claim. You become, for a few heartbeats, part of the conversation.

Between and through them move the plated ones, the gravel-herds. They are not herds any more than a landslide is a committee. Each body is a mosaic of hexagonal plates grown rather than assembled, plates that shed frost in sudden sheets and then drink the melt before it can become a rivulet. They click as they travel, a sound like cooling metal, and they are always faintly electrified. Their passage leaves the moss darker, fed. They do not graze so much as complete a circuit. Sometimes a whole slope of them will halt for days, plates aligning, and the air above them grows a low corona that the filterers come to sieve. The filterers hang in the fault-scarp updrafts, membranous and loud, great living flags whose veins carry dissolved mineral and whose song is a chord you feel in the sinuses. They are the highland’s lungs turned inside out, tasting what the young crust still exhales.

Larger still are the idle ones, the engine-beasts that squat in the new cirques. They are weather given flanks. Their hides are ridged and vented, ticking with heat that the mosses steal and the cold administers. They crack the pale stone the way a tongue finds a sore tooth, slowly, thoroughly, for the metals that have not yet learned to hide. What they return to the air is a smell of rain on copper and a bass so constant it becomes the silence you notice only when it shifts. Sparks walk them at the rumor of dusk. They are not solitary. A dozen of them will stand within a day’s walk of one another, their vents speaking in voltage, their weight rearranging the drainage of an entire hanging valley across decades. A calf, if the word can be used, is simply a new vent that has decided to walk. It stays near the parent flank until its own idle is deep enough to keep the frost from winning.

There are smaller intervals still, the ones that fill what the others leave. Things that come in buckets and sing in stacked harmonics, their bodies more chamber than flesh. Things that are mostly antler and lens, never looking up, carrying their own private glare. Things that are half-mineral already, sessile until a mineral seam sings, then suddenly mobile, leaving a pale scar that the mosses rush to caulk. And there are the braided places, the valley-wide guts where fauna and flora and stone have forgotten they were ever separate. Here a thousand bodies lock into one slow peristalsis that eats the mountain in place and lays down, century by century, a finer, paler mountain already being recolonized. You can walk those corridors if you accept that walking is a negotiation. The ground hums one note. Compasses sulk. Time thickens.

None of them build. None of them own. A bone is only ore that has not yet been claimed. A hide is a solar surface and then a meal. They converse in voltage more than scent, in the long dull news of hunger and claim and the opening of a new scarp. After weeks among them you begin to feel the sentences moving underfoot, slow as the uplift itself: this ridge is mine for the season; that vent is shared; the light is sufficient. They do not mind you. You are only another moving dampness, briefly brighter than the rime, a small additional metabolism whose opinions the highland will digest along with everything else. Stay long enough and you understand they are not living on the high country. They are the high country, still cooling, still rising, still teaching the white engine overhead what a finished edge might one day be.
