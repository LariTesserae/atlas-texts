The high country does not keep inhabitants so much as it keeps densities, and the densities keep changing their minds about what they are.

Begin in the wet galleries, because that is where the air first thickens with other breath. The pale many-legged things are already there when the ferns uncoil. They are the color of steamed bone or of the ceramic stone that extrudes along the faults, and they do not so much walk as accordion. Each segment can lock or loosen independently; a creature the length of your forearm can flatten itself into a film no thicker than the condensation it farms, or rear into a tower of clicking plates when a new terrace shears up beneath it. They turn the moss the way a hand turns soil, but there is no hand and no farmer, only the habit of aeration. They eat what they aerate, or they do not; sometimes a gallery of felt-moss will be riddled with their tunnels and still grow thicker, as if the turning itself were the crop. You smell them first: wet mineral, a sweetness like overheated sugar, and underneath that the faint factory-tang of whatever the vents are exhaling that day. They do not flee. They part around your ankles and close again, a living grate repairing itself. If you stay still they will farm the moisture from your clothes.

Higher, on the knife-edges that were not there at first light, the jointed ones pick their way. They are the size of goats only in the way a memory of a goat might be the size of a goat. Extra articulations bloom wherever the rock demands them: a knee that bends backward to hook a lip of basalt, a shoulder that telescopes so the animal can flatten against a wall while the wall itself is still rising. Their hooves (if they are hooves) are already wearing the glossy scab-stone smooth in places, but the stone keeps arriving faster than the wear. They browse the hour-old ferns, the ones that lignify even as they are eaten, so that a mouthful can go from frond to splinter between one chew and the next. They do not call. When a ridge lifts they simply fold, extra joints collapsing them into the nearest seam, and wait out the held note of rising rock. Sometimes the industrial leak takes them: you will see one whose spine has the regular spacing of rivets, or whose horns have cooled into perfect hexagonal prisms. It does not seem to mind. It keeps walking the edge that is still deciding whether to be an edge.

The dark gliders occupy the dim the way silt occupies still water. They hang as if the iron sky had pinned them there, wings (or vanes, or sheets of something that is not quite skin) outstretched and motionless for hours. Then a thermal—or a revision in the local gravity—passes, and they peel away in overlapping planes, a single flock becoming a dozen, becoming a weather of their own. They are the color of the sky’s underside, wet iron, and they feed on the thumb-sized insects that live in the hollow reeds. Or they feed on the mist. Or they are the mist, briefly deciding to have edges. When they land it is on the warm skins of the fumaroles; they drink the cycling breath of the vents and leave behind a fine slag that the next rain will drink or the next lift will reabsorb. Their bones, on the rare occasions one dies and is not immediately taken back by the country, show the same regular spacing you see in the leaked animals: rivets, or the memory of a process that has not yet chosen between geology and manufacture.

In the reeds themselves the tickers keep time. Insects only in the loosest sense; they are the size of your thumb and the temperature of the stone they live against. Their bodies are segments of the same hollow warmth as the plants, and they tick—very softly, a sound you feel more than hear—in exact phase with the nearest fumarole’s inhale. The rhythm is not communication. It is occupancy. A stand of reeds can sound like a single slow machine if you stand among them long enough, the ticks layering until they become the held breath of the place itself. Sometimes the living leaks the other way and a reed will pulse, once, like a cooling flow of stone that has remembered it has a heart.

There are others that do not stay still long enough to be named by habit. In the deeper clefts, where heat and pressure practice smelting without product, things condense out of the process and then dissolve back: plates of laminated stone that briefly have the gait of crabs, rods of copper-hearted glass that flex like eels before the next tremor claims them. A cooling slag-flow will sometimes sprout legs of exactly the wrong number and take three steps toward a hanging tarn before it hardens and is only stone again. These are not births. They are the country trying on the idea of locomotion and then folding the idea away.

The climate-cells have their own temporary nations of life. A warm wet bloom can produce, in a week, a hanging forest so dense the rock vanishes and you walk on a grate of living reed and moss and the pale tenders moving through both. In that density everything touches everything: breath on both sides of your body, the tick of insects in the walls, the faint uphill run of condensation that has forgotten which way gravity is supposed to go. A dry cell a valley over stays mineral, and the only inhabitants there are the jointed walkers and the occasional glider that has misjudged a thermal and now waits, pinned to a new ridge, for the next rewrite. The boundaries between cells walk. A creature adapted to one will simply become, over the course of a day, adapted to the other, or it will be reabsorbed, or it will leave behind a hexagonal leaf, a riveted bone, a length of wire that is also a root, and continue.

Nothing here remembers being built, and nothing here needs to. The fauna treat the instability as climate because that is what it is: the weather of a physics still in draft. They occupy the cracks, the films, the undersides, the warm skins. They are near-touching. If you stay, you become another density among them—another set of joints learning the new edges, another breath in the galleries, another small warmth the stone can feel through whatever you are wearing. The country will keep arriving. It does not particularly need you for that, but it will use the space you occupy just the same.
