The first lives are the ones that do not admit they are lives. The orange wool in the still water, the black skins that wrinkle and heal, the white crusts that form and unform along every receding meniscus: these are already eating and being eaten, already deciding the chemistry of the next hour. They do not have faces. They do not need them. The dish feeds them the same way it feeds itself, by remaining unfinished rain.

Then the stitchers. Pale amphipods, so many a step becomes a rumor traveling outward through the shallows, a grain that remembers it can move. They live in the top two fingers of water and in the films that roof it. They live by rasping. Their lives are short even by the standards of this place, which has no interest in longevity as a virtue, only as a habit. When one dies it does not fall so much as join the floc, another torn fleece among the torn fleeces. The others do not pause. Pausing is a luxury of things that have somewhere else to be.

Under every overhang the flatworms write and rewrite themselves, thin as the idea of a tongue. They taste the brick. They taste the iron that has become a kind of fat. They taste one another when the water thins and two writings occupy the same damp. In the darker brick channels the longer ones hang, heads to the current that is barely a current, mouths working with the patience of locks. Eel is the wrong word and so is fish; they are the shape water makes when it has been taught, for decades, to move through pipes. Some have vestigial paired fins that no longer remember stroke. Some have none. Their eyes, when they have them, are the same wet-nickel as the light and seem to look at nothing, which is accurate: there is nothing here that requires looking in the ordinary way. They live on what the films slough and on the smaller stitchers and on the faint sweetness that arrives from below, the land still offering up the last of its worked minerals like a habit it cannot break.

The filter-colonies beard every pipe-mouth, beige, faintly pulsatile, closing for a shadow even when the shadow is only a drip’s. They are the place’s lungs if lungs could be grown in the wrong direction, inhaling what the water has already decided to give. They live by remaining almost still. A pulse, a close, an open. The wheel in the valve-house turns on a longer pulse and the colonies, if you watch long enough, are not independent of it. Nothing here is.

Higher, the still ones. You can call them birds if you need a noun that will let you leave. Grebe-sized, the exact color of the gantry they stand on, one-legged for hours that are not hours so much as intervals between clicks. Heads tucked not from cold but from the labor of outline. They are denser than birds. When they feed — a brief, ugly industry at the dimming that stands in for dusk — they stab the shallows and the colonies and the longer bodies if the longer bodies are careless, and then they return to being part of the iron, as if feeding were a lapse in manners. Their droppings are white for an hour and then the same mineral as everything else. They do not sing. They do not leave. The marsh beyond the lip has greener food and they will not go there. Preference has become anatomy.

Between the posts the many-jointed one works the silt. It is the size of a spread hand at first, then larger when you have been looking too long, then only a hand again. Carapace the color of old lime gone to fat. Too many limbs, or the right number if the work is sifting rather than walking. It does not flee. It redistributes. When you step near, the silt closes over your print and the jointed thing is already in the closing, harvesting whatever your weight pressed up: amphipod, film, the memory of a boot. It lives by occupation. Every vacancy is a meal. It has been here long enough that the bricks it prefers have taken a polish, a shell-sheen, as if the place were growing a nacre against the irritation of being used.

These are the obvious densities. They are not the inhabitant.

Stay. The dish asks this of you the way a held note asks: not kindly, just by making departure a series of small dishonesties. The footing lies. The distances collapse. The air condenses on you until you are another weeping surface. And the drips begin to group.

Three from the tank-lip. Rest. Two from the pump-house eave. Rest too exact to be accident. The amphipods still in those rests. The concrete birds tuck and untuck. The wheel clicks at the top of its rotation and the phrasing answers, or the phrasing was first and the wheel is keeping it. When you move, both hesitate. The dish has included you in a count you did not agree to join.

That is when the extras become harder to prosecute.

Reflections in the blacker water carry an additional upright, a span that is not there when the eye lifts, a doorway occupied by a curtain of drips that is only drips until the moment it has weight, a slump of shoulder, the suggestion of a hand on the jamb that is also only water arranging itself the way water has arranged itself since the roof came off. You look away because the light will not help you and because looking too directly feels like interrupting. The place is allowed its extras. They may be how it stores departures.

What lives here, then, the thing that is not the stitchers and not the still ones and not the jointed harvester of prints, is this: a continuity that has learned the shape of a person without requiring a person to continue.

It uses the half-house. The single chamber of standing water, the ladder to a floor that is only beams and weather. At the vanished second story there is a nest that is not sticks. Mineral plates, orange and fatty-gray, built in the same overlapping fans as the weeping walls, but arranged with a preference, a hollow the size of a curled sleeper. Something has pressed that hollow warm for a long time, or the chemistry has been taught to remember warmth and so provides a simulacrum. The ladder rungs are polished in the center, the polish dark and wet like the inside of a mussel. Not feet exactly. Repeated passage by something that knows the rungs as well as the water knows the channels.

It uses the tank. The cylindrical cave, mouth open to the dim. Inside, past the first bend of rust and sinter, the air is a degree less like breath and a degree more like a held mouth. There are arrangements: cable ends combed into a kind of bedding, a concavity in the scale where a spine might fit if a spine had grown used to metal. Once there were eggs or the idea of eggs, calcareous, iron-stained, most of them long since opened or absorbed back into the walls. A few remain, inert, more geology than promise. Whatever nested here still does, or the nest has learned to be the nester. The distinction is one the dish does not trouble itself to keep.

It uses the valve-house last and most carefully. The wheel that turns without a nameable power, pressure from below, the land dewatering through a habit the works installed and then abandoned. The inhabitant does not turn the wheel. The inhabitant attends it. You will not see this at first because attendance here looks like standing in the doorway that is only drips, one hand (if it is a hand) on the jamb, head inclined as if listening to a count it already knows. When the click comes — nail on porcelain — something in the posture completes, the way a drip completes when it finally chooses to fall. Then the long quiet in which the click is remembered. The inhabitant is the remembering.

It is the color of the place. Of course it is. Wet concrete, old lime, the pale of things that have not seen a sun that mattered. Where it has been still longest — the insides of elbows, the hollow of a throat if the throat is still a throat — the mineral fans have begun, ochre and rust, slick, almost fatty, the same forests that grow on the brick. It does not slough them. They are not infestation. They are tenure. In the places it moves (and it does move, slowly, the way the wheel moves, against a fixed point you have to choose in order to be sure), the fans thin and the surface beneath is the same as the amphipods, the same as the birds, a density that refuses outline. Water beads on it and does not quite run. Water is its climate, not its weather.

The face, when the dim allows a face, is not a surprise and not a comfort. Features reduced to what the air and the drip have decided are necessary: the idea of eyes, the same wet-nickel, looking at the count rather than at you; a mouth that may be a mouth or may be another evaporation line, white-crusted and then dissolved, working slightly, the way the longer bodies’ mouths work in the channels, on whatever the air provides. It does not speak. Voices would carry strangely and so nothing uses them. Its quiet is the occupied quiet, the held note. When it breathes — if the condensing of the air on every surface can be called its breath as much as yours — the dish’s chord changes by a fraction, a new lip added to the grammar of drip.

It eats as the others eat. Films. The slow rain of mineral slough. The protein of neighbors when neighbors are careless or complete. There is no green to underwrite it and it has not needed green for longer than the roof has been a suggestion. The water arrives already worked, cool, metallic, faintly sweet at the back of the throat, a taste that stays on the teeth. It lives on gradients: iron, sulfur, the last warmth of whatever still turns underground, the pulse of the wheel, the phrasing of the drips. It lives on the extras, perhaps — on the stored departures, the reflections that do not match, the figures that are only curtains until they are not. That may be how it persists without issue and without end. The dish stores what has left and the inhabitant is the storage given the option of walking.

It does not flee you. There is nowhere to flee that is not already occupied, and you are already inside the occupancy. Instead there are pauses. Redistributions. A closing of gaps. When you stand at the far lip, where the water is a shade less mineral and the amphipods thin and the concrete birds will not hunt, the inhabitant does not follow. Preference. The dish ends not by wall but by a density that will not dilute, and the inhabitant is that density’s most patient clause. You can look out into the flatter gray and know that green exists, and know that turning back will feel like going indoors. The inhabitant is the indoors. There is no door. There was never a roof that lasted. The rain is the same rain.

It has been here since the works were new enough to have names for their parts, or it has been here since the names stopped being useful; the place does not distinguish. The erosion is finished with drama. Nothing is falling. The inhabitant has already fallen into the shape it will keep. Rust has become a kind of flesh, or flesh a kind of rust; the chemistry is enough. You can feel the age of it in the worn paths that are not footpaths so much as repetitions: the same edges of the same bricks, the same rungs, the same jamb, used until they shine like the inside of a shell.

Sometimes, in the poorest light, there is more than one. An extra upright that does not resolve when you look up. A second slump in the tank’s bedding. A pair of still ones on the gantry that are not quite the size of grebes. The inhabitant may be singular the way a chord is singular, or plural the way drips are plural. The dish does not require the distinction and neither, after a time, do you. What lives here is the long middle. Not a survivor. Not a ghost. A climate that has learned, in the absence of anyone left to operate the works, to operate itself in the shape of attendance.

The wheel clicks. The drips take up their count. Something many-jointed occupies the print you left, already. The air condenses on you with the patience of a place that will be wet when the last brick is only a softer stripe in the floor. And in the doorway that is only water until it is not, the inhabitant completes a posture you did not see it begin, listening to a phrasing that now includes the sound of your going, carefully, one hand out, as the small distances close around you as if that were the same thing as welcome.

It does not follow you to the lip. It does not need to. You were never outside it. The dish is small enough to be a room. What lives there is the room, learning, still, how to be lived in without anyone having to stay who does not already know the count.
