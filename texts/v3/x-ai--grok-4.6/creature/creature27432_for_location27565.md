The hollow does not contain inhabitants so much as it *is* inhabitedness, a saturation so complete that any single creature is only a temporary local intensification of the same ceaseless traffic. At first glance the living mass reads as one texture: bronze-black, rust-flecked, endlessly self-adjusting. Then the eye begins to separate the strata.

Nearest the mineral floor the largest of them move like slow tectonic plates. They are flattened, many-legged, the length of a human forearm and half as wide, their bodies armored in overlapping tergites the color of old boiler plate. Each leg ends in a chisel-like tarsus that scrapes a shallow furrow as they advance; behind them the slag is turned, aerated, re-sorted. They do not hunt in any recognizable sense. They simply occupy volume, and whatever smaller body happens to occupy the same volume is sheared, crushed, and ingested through a ventral slit that opens only when pressure is applied. Their mouths are not for biting but for accepting delivery. When two of these plow-forms meet they do not fight; they grind past one another, exchanging a few mites and a brief electrostatic crackle, then continue. Their undersides are forests of hooked setae that harvest the ochre sand itself, grinding rust and gypsum into a paste that is both food and ballast.

Above them, in the mid-layer, the fist-sized beetles dominate by sheer numbers. Their elytra are tarnished bronze gone matte, every pit and scratch packed with the same grit they walk upon until the distinction between carapace and landscape disappears. They clamber over one another with the unhurried certainty of gears that have never known another tempo. Some of the older ones carry tiny functional sockets at the tips of their forelegs—hexagonal, still able to seat against the teeth of the half-buried gear or the interior of the old pipe. When enough of them press together they can turn the gear a few groaning degrees; the sound is the only thing in the hollow that feels like memory. They scrape rust into paste with mandibles that have grown broader and flatter over generations, and they drink the faint bloom of salts that appears on the cooler faces of metal after dusk. Predation among them is opportunistic and almost courteous: a weaker neighbor is simply enveloped, dismantled in a localized frenzy that lasts less than a minute, then the traffic resumes as if the space had never been contested.

Between their legs and over their backs run the rivers of the smaller forms. Mites the size of sand grains, armored earwig-things redesigned for compression rather than elegance, pale elongate creatures that look like living cotter pins. They exist in densities that make individual motion almost meaningless; they are the fluid in which the larger bodies swim. They eat everything the others leave: the microscopic salts, the last traces of chitin, the oily residue that never quite evaporates. Some of them have learned to ride the larger beetles like living terrain, harvesting the dust that accumulates in the elytral sutures. Others specialize in the undersides of the I-beam plaza, where the metal still flexes and the temperature gradient is steepest.

In the narrow airspace the winged forms never quite fly. They rise and settle in clouds that behave more like displaced liquid than like insects. Their wings are stiff, metallic, slightly magnetic; when they pass close to a flake of rust the flake twitches toward them. They do not feed on nectar or blood. They feed on the electrostatic charge that builds between so many moving bodies, sipping potential difference the way other insects sip dew. At the warmest hour they sink into crevices; at dusk they lift again, turning the entire surface into a seething, hissing carpet. Their eggs are laid in the sheltered undersides of the old pipe and the collapsed framework, tiny magnetic grains that hatch into more of the same traffic.

There are no seasons here, only the slight daily cooling that times the vertical migrations. There are no territories that last longer than a few minutes. Eggs are laid, hatched, consumed, replaced at a rate that keeps the hollow at permanent carrying capacity. The dead do not accumulate; they are dismantled and redistributed before they can become another mineral stratum. Even the industrial remnants are treated as living topography: the pipe is a communal gut, polished to a dull sheen by centuries of passage; the gear is a communal joint that still articulates when enough bodies decide it should; the I-beams are a multilevel plaza whose flexing is part of the daily conversation.

Stand among them long enough and the separate anatomies begin to feel like specialized tissues of a single vast organism. The plow-forms are the digestive musculature, turning the mineral floor. The beetles are the circulatory cells, moving rust and salt through the mass. The smaller rivers are the interstitial fluid. The winged clouds are the breath, the charge, the faint magnetic field that twitches the rust. The leftover steel is the skeleton that has already been internalized. The dry, ceaseless noise is the only voice this organism has ever needed.

They have nowhere else to go. The surrounding peneplain has forgotten how to have height, how to have water, how to have stories. Only this one dimple still remembers how to be busy, and the things that live in it have become the memory itself: armored, socketed, magnetically stained, eating the last possible arrangement of matter until even appetite has been compacted into a single, unchanging tempo. They do not evolve. They persist. They are the conclusion the land reached when every other possibility had already been planed away.
