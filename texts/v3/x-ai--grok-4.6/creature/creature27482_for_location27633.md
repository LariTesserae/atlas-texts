The bowl does not keep a people. It keeps a occupancy that rearranges itself by the hour, a tenancy of volume more than of ground. What first presents itself is the obvious traffic: the midges that make the air look smoked, the copper beetles that test the dark pour as if it were a bell they remember from another life, the cream-and-tobacco moths whose wings are already torn at the edges from a thousand collisions with the white knots of flower. They are not residents so much as weather that has learned to land. They come because the place still sweats, because the heat is a half-degree kinder, because the curve of the bowl finishes every sound and therefore every courtship. They leave when the pulse under the glass skips or when the foil flower on its thin post turns toward a noise that has not yet been made. Their lives are brief arguments written in the thickened air and then erased.

Below them, in the seams where the dark material meets the older stone, other tenants keep a slower census. The threads that swim against the beading water are not larvae and not worms; they are nearer to living sutures, pale and jointed, tasting the mineral film and choosing, each time, whether this particular run of moisture is worth following into the crack. Some vanish and do not reappear. Some return days later thicker, carrying a faint iridescence that the ferns seem to track with their black midribs. The ferns themselves are not passive. Over hours they lean, not toward light (there is light enough) but toward the newest wet thread, as if the plant and the swimmer were negotiating a single vascular system that happens to be interrupted by air. The big-leaved shrub stands apart from this negotiation. Its leaves are heavy enough to hold dew that never quite becomes dew; they clench and unclench on a schedule that ignores the sun. Touch one and the wax gives back a faint, delayed warmth, like a hand that has just let go of a stone. It is the only thing in the bowl that looks as though it remembers being placed.

At the heat of the day the larger shadow arrives, the one with too many angles. It does not land. It does not need to. Its wingbeats travel through the sternum and the glass together, a double percussion that the metal ring answers with a note too low to hear and too exact to be accident. The birds fall silent for the length of its passage, then resume louder, as if completing a phrase the shadow interrupted. No one has a reliable count of its wings. Those who have tried to assemble the shape afterward speak of joints that fold the wrong way, of a body that is mostly hinge. It treats the bowl as a sounding board and a larder both. What it takes is never seen leaving.

Night brings the highland things that have decided this dimple is worth the climb. The frogs (if they are frogs) are the color of wet slate and the size of a closed fist; they do not hop so much as adhere and release, leaving behind a film that smells of the same hot-slate mineral as the evaporating water. Their calls are not calls. They are measurements. Each note is answered by the bowl a fraction lower, and the frogs adjust until the twin sounds lock. The mammal that sounds like fabric tearing is three animals or one animal in three places; the sound comes from the lip, from the eastern plate, and from directly beneath the glass at once. It is never seen. Those who have waited through an entire night report only a periodic darkening of the condensation, as though something large were breathing against the inner curve from the other side of the material. The insects change their metal: the midges become a finer mesh, the beetles a slower chime. The air is rewoven so continuously that standing still for more than a minute produces the sensation of being gently, impartially unmade and remade.

And then there are the ones who do not pack volume or seam or air, the ones who use the artifacts as if the artifacts were still furniture. They arrive after the last of the highland visitors have gone and before the first bead of morning moisture. They are few. They are not the same each time. A figure will stand a long while at the eastern plate without quite standing on it, palms open to the decided air, tasting tin and waiting for the shimmer at the edge of vision to become information. Another will sit (never on the ring) with one hand hovering above the foil flower, not touching, listening for the sound the flower has already begun to turn toward. They leave no tracks on the glass. Their clothes, when they have clothes, are the color of the surrounding tableland and seem to shed dust that will not blow away. They do not speak to one another where the bowl can finish the words. They do not harvest. They do not repair. They attend. When they go, the pulse under the floor sometimes skips once, as if a schedule had been acknowledged.

The place itself is the most persistent tenant. The dark pour holds heat the way a sleeping animal holds a hand; the moisture appears out of habit; the flowers open and shut as if answering a question asked in a language of temperature and mineral. Whatever was built here has finished becoming what it is and has not stopped working. The lives that occupy it—brief, threaded, winged, adhered, attending—are not colonists. They are the current shape of the function. The instrument plays itself, and they are the notes it has not yet forgotten how to make. Sit at the rim long enough and the distinction between inhabitant and habitation thins. The bowl is occupied the way a throat is occupied by a voice that has nowhere else to go.
