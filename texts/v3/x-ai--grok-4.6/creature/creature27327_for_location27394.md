The grazers come first because they are the easiest to mistake for landscape. High at the shoulder, slate-dark, they move in threes and fours through the cane and the wetter benches with a deliberation so complete it erases itself. Their feet suck at the peat and then forget the sound. Mosses take them early—first the flanks, then the withers, then the long slope of the neck—until an older animal is no longer quite an outline. Small flowering things open on them in sequences no one has mapped: pale cups that last a week, then tighter clusters that last a month, then a single hanging bloom the color of old copper that opens only after true rain and stays open until the next. The grazers do not browse the gardens they wear. They eat the cane, the reed, the sweet vent-lichen when they find it, and they do it without hurry, as if chewing were another form of standing still. Their breath is warm and mineral. When they pause—and they pause for hours—the forest grows over the idea of them. A herder who has followed the same group for years will sometimes lose them in plain sight, then find them again a little farther on, a little greener, the same three or four shapes wearing a slightly different century.

Above them the canopy argues.

The birds—if they are birds—are hot-blooded and loud and iridescent in ways that have less to do with pigment than with interference, a kind of laminated shine that leaves a second pair of wings just out of true when they turn. They keep to the second sky of interleaving fronds and they speak in phrases that take hours to finish. One will begin a rising, metallic clause from the eastern edge of a basin; another, half a morning later, will answer from the western rim with a variation so slight it might be agreement or correction. The argument travels. It fills the hanging benches. By late afternoon the whole mid-country seems to be the one speaking, a single long disagreement about something that happened before the spines were geology. They do not sing to attract or to warn. They sing to continue. Nestlings learn the older phrases first, the ones that have already been answered a thousand times, and only later add their own small emendations. A surveyor once tried to transcribe a full exchange and filled three notebooks before realizing the argument had looped, or perhaps had never started, and that the basin itself was keeping time.

Lower, in the films and the black pools, the amphibians refuse a single skin. They hang in the sweating sheets on the cliffs, translucent, the pulse visible as a slow lantern, and when they drop they do not fall so much as thicken into the runnel they join. Some never leave the water that is always deciding. Others crawl a little way onto the warm stone at dusk and sit with their mouths open, tasting the air that smells of minerals and that sweetness no one names. Their eggs are not eggs exactly. They are soft, lenticular thickenings in the films themselves, and the young hatch already half-dissolved, already part of the pouring. They carry news the way water carries heat: without intention, without stopping. A pulse changes in one pool and, days later, a different pulse answers in a weep a ridge away. No one has proved they think. No one has proved they do not.

Insects are weather. Some are ordinary, hammer-simple, chewing and pollinating and dying in the usual numbers. Others leave afterimages—wings that persist a half-second behind the body, as if the air had been folded once and the insect were walking both layers. At certain hours the cane-groves fill with a metallic din that is not quite stridulation and not quite the old metal remembering itself. It comes in slow waves. It has a key, and the key shifts with the vents, a little higher when the white breath stands thicker, a little lower after rain. The insects that make it do not seem to hear it. They continue.

People pass through and do not stay. That is the agreement the country offers: you may walk the chain in a hard week, you may sleep in the lee of a spine or under a sweatwood, you may gather the lichen or follow the grazers or try, once more, to finish a map. You may not remain without accepting the clock. Those who have accepted it are no longer quite people. They are rare. You find them, if you find them, in the longer pauses—the ones who have let the moss take a shoulder, or whose speech has slowed until it matches the intramural pouring of the cliffs. They do not herd or gather anymore. They sit in the warm runnels or lean against the glassy remnant of a wall and they watch the water decide. Their tools, when they still carry them, have become unrecognizable: edges that cut in a direction you cannot point to, objects that fit the hand and then slowly refuse to leave it. They are not dying. They are settling. The country is not cruel about this. It simply continues.

And then there is the thing that moves in the deeper cane.

It is large. That is the first and last reliable fact. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It goes on. You hear it as a change in the way the cane leans, a long, low displacement that takes the better part of an afternoon to cross a basin. Sometimes the garden-beasts stop and face the sound, not in alarm, only in recognition, the way they face a vent or a spine. Sometimes the arguing birds fall silent for a clause or two, then resume a slightly older phrase, as if reminded. No one has seen it clearly. The glimpses are always partial: a flank the color of wet stone and older moss, a limb that might be a root, a slow lifting that might be breath. It wears the forest the way the grazers wear their gardens, only more so—epiphytes queued along its length, pale cords disappearing into it and not coming back as the same organism. It may be one thing. It may be several things that have forgotten they were several. It may be what the builders became when they finally agreed to the clock, or what the plateau itself uses to remember that it is not finished, only very slow. At dusk, when the tripod in the Kettle gives off its note just below hearing, the thing in the cane answers—or the cane answers through it—with a pressure that is not quite sound. The marsh-lights hold still. The films on the cliffs brighten for a moment, as if the water had briefly decided to rise.

Everything here lives by remaining. The heat does not bleed off. The water does not quite escape. The spines hum their one instruction into the stone. Life has learned the instruction and made it flesh, or moss, or argument, or the long unseen passage through the cane. You could walk the habitable chain end to end and meet only these: the grazers becoming copses, the birds continuing a sentence no one started, the amphibians thickening and thinning, the insects laminating the air, the rare settled ones watching the runnels, and the large thing that is still going on. You would not be done knowing them. Knowing is one of the patient processes. They were here before the last map was abandoned. They will be here when the next surveyor decides, once more, that a week is enough.
