The bowl does not keep animals because animals would hurry it. What lives here instead has already finished hurrying.

It is not one thing. It is the thirty, and the water that joins them, and the stone that remembers the joining. Each plant stands in its allotted terrace or crack or dark pool as if it chose the spot after long consideration and then forgot the choosing. Their leaves are the color of steeped iron. When the light lingers half a breath too long, the leaves accept the extra brightness without comment, the way an old body accepts another year. Their stems shine because they are always wet; the wet is not weather but conversation. Roots show in the shallows like pale, patient hands that once arranged something and then decided arrangement was finished.

They do not speak in any way a throat would recognize. They speak in the rate of drip, in the exact width of a groove cut over centuries, in the brief iridescence that forms when one of the pale plates dissolves and its dust settles on the nearest saucer. Pollen does not travel. Nothing here needs to persuade anything farther away than the next pool. The plates themselves lift only every handful of years, thinner than memory, trembling in still air as if recalling a wind that no longer exists. Then they return into the wet and the plant is itself again, slightly more so.

The stone lives too, but slower. Heat rises through it as a continuous, unhurried fact; the mountain has not cooled because cooling would be a decision it has already declined. The inner faces weep not from any named stream but from the air’s own condensation, a private weather the bowl invented and then kept. Seams issue water the color of weak tea. Saucers hold it perfectly still until a drop arrives from a hanging curtain far above and the single note sounds. That note is how the stone acknowledges the plants. The plants answer by drinking so slowly the level changes only across decades, a tide no calendar bothers to name.

At the rim the air leaves as a faint breath. That breath is the only movement that ever exits. Everything else stays: the mineral sweetness, the shadows that pool a half-breath late, the single flake of flint under silt whose edges have become as soft as the rock’s own lips. Whatever hand once held the flint has long since become weather. The gesture remains only as geology, which is how the inhabitants prefer their history.

Night does not interrupt them. The stone keeps its warmth; the weeping continues; starlight lies on the wet leaves exactly, and a little too calmly. Toward dawn the vapor lifts from every pool at once, a single lung exhaling into the colder ridge. For those minutes the thirty and the water and the stone are visibly one creature, breathing. Then the skin of mist goes out over the lip and the bowl is again a room of warm rock and sparse, dripping green, holding its own brightness the way it holds its own water.

Nothing else is required. The rest of the mountain continues around it, drier, windier, unfinished. The inhabitants have already decided. They live by remaining.
