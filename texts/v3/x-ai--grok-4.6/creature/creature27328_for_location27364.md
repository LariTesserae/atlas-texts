The lives here do not arrive. They have been the plateau’s own grammar for longer than the factories have kept their count, longer than the mountains finished arguing with water. You know them first as a change in the sponge underfoot, a thousand small mouths rasping the iron-sweet film the heat lays down, then as a heavier patience that pauses on a ridge until the pause itself begins to think. Nothing here is decorative. Presence is structural: a stirring in the peat-fluff, a displacement that travels through black pools like a rumor of weather, a note that was not in the layered roar until it was.

The grazers are the constant. Flattened, coiled, jointed in ways that let them pour through mineral fluff and collapsed plant-bodies without tearing the mattress, they work the pulsing mats and the rubbery sheets that drink vapor through every surface. Their mouths are rasps, or filters, or slow chemical kisses. They leave warmer trails; the next growth follows those trails the way water follows old grooves. When the vertical lake comes they do not flee so much as become the flood for a time, riding silt and shed skins to new hollows, then settling again as the draining begins. You feel them as a living give in the ground, a crowd so fine it is almost the texture of the place itself.

Above them the mid-tangle browsers push. Bodies like fronds or like wet armor, they move with the exact give of the vegetation so that a leaf slapping your wrist might be a leaf or might be a flank deciding you are only weather. They tear sound-ferns; the ferns clap back in frequencies that make the teeth ache, a conversation or a warning or both. These browsers taste of the same iron and sweet rot as everything that grows. Their blood, if they have it, is the temperature of the stone. They hide by becoming the wetness around them. A hand put out in the dark meets something that yields like hide-moss and then is gone, leaving only a warmer patch of mist.

The patient ones use the ridges. Something heavy, spread-footed or unspread, moves a step and then listens with its whole weight. The listening lasts. You begin to believe the ridge is considering you. Sometimes it is: there are ridgelines that inhale, a slow rise in pressure you feel in the ears and the sinuses, then exhale a mist sharper with mineral. Whether the walker and the ridge have ever been separate is a question the plateau no longer bothers to ask. These large lives hunt by the shape of displaced water and by heat that does not match the vents. They also lick seeps, drinking the slow bleed of metals, so that predator and geology share a tongue. When one of them slumps with a ridge — and ridges slump, accomplished, without drama — the forest goes with it in that long vegetal sigh, and the walker, if it still distinguishes itself, goes too, or becomes the next layer of the mattress.

In the hanging marsh the swarms live as a second weather. They are density more than bodies, silk you walk through when the pressure drops and the mist thickens toward lake. They filter what the vents breathe and what the plants exhale. They inquire with the whole surface. A sudden coolness on the lips is them tasting the iron and lily and exhaust on you, then rising again or being snapped up. The membranes that hunt them are not wings. They are surfaces that row the thick air, that crack wetly to locate and to be located, that carry spores or heat or the plateau’s own chemistry from club to hanging bell. Some of the membranes are light enough to be mostly gas the planet exhaled; they ride the coasts of the darkness, the places where the highland forgets to continue. Their young, if the word applies, learn the air by the way it fails to be water.

The pools hold the other lives, the ones that treat the root-ceiling as sky. Small tickings when the drips are the only sound: scavengers of drowned prairie stems, of skins the flood brought. Larger, older, solitary: bodies that have never chosen air, lying along warmth or hanging, tasting machine effluent as it seeps down. The pale cables belong here, or belong everywhere. They twitch. They conduct. A whole lake may pulse at once and the sound-ferns on the banks lift as if a name had been spoken in a register older than mouths. Animal, leftover nerve, root that learned the grammar of current — the distinction has been weathered away. You can put a hand in a pool (the water blood-warm, never still) and feel a cable consider you, or decide you are only another drip.

The herds make distance into a circuit. They travel in numbers that turn pools into temporary seas, following vents whose taste they remember across a generation of shorter lives, following the particular song of an engine that has run so long it is a mountain. The young are born already leaning toward that sound two days west, the one that missed a beat and caught again. Their hides — thick, or moss-colonized, or themselves a kind of sponge — shed heat into the ground so the plateau feels them coming as a traveling warmth. Predators move inside the same weather system: a turning of the herd is a seiche, an approach is a wrong temperature in the stone, a kill is a sudden quiet in one register of the roar. The circuit is wide enough that the land itself seems to migrate, slowly, on their backs.

Then the ones that were never willing to stay in category. A flock that is also a single decision: calls that do not answer but complete, beginning in one gorge and already finished three valleys over. A pipe putting out mineral flowers that sip the cycling liquor and prickle the skin with heat that smells of copper and lily. A gear-housing that has become a nest, the turning part of the season, the young learning downstroke as pulse. Whole valleys that are one organism pretending to be forest, roots conducting news of slump or flood the way the cables do. These are not ruptures. They are the long habit of a world that never agreed with eyes. Shapes persist. Processes continue that brighter physics would have corrected out of shame.

And there are those who might once have been the builders, or the attendants, or who have simply stayed until staying became another kind of geology. They are scattered like the erratics. In the stamped-metal hut a body occupies the chair that faces nothing. It does not look. It listens, skin perhaps the temperature of weeping rock, or grown over with the same rubbery sheets the plants use, or simply wet as everything is wet. It may tend a valve, or remember a purpose the rust has already revised into sculpture, or only sit, arranging its own heat in the crowd. Others move between factory terraces, tasting the liquor that glows without light, leaning against pistons that have not stopped, feeling the higher note the machines take when flood comes. They leave marks the plateau immediately begins to translate: plaques beading into texture, cairns softening into gardens of moss-hide, trenches becoming linear ponds full of cables and small tickings. If they still use tools, the tools have grown into the hand or the hand into the rust. They are few enough that solitude is possible, many enough that the plateau feels attended. Perhaps they are the industry continuing to believe in itself. Perhaps they are the forest thinking, slowly, with water and heat and old current.

You stand still long enough and the lives close in without approaching. Mouths in the sponge. A ridge inhaling. Membranes snapping somewhere the hanging marsh is thicker. A cable pulsing under a pool you cannot see. The far factory catching its rhythm. The chair, occupied or not, still facing the dark that has weather and coasts. Nothing brightens. Nothing empties. They eat and are eaten, nest in gears, conduct through roots, migrate on circuits wider than memory, breathe as land, decide as flocks, sit as if sitting were another vent. The loudness stays structured, full of overlapping purposes. The warmth comes up through the soles. Water finds the skin as if it had been looking. This is who lives here: the crowd that is also the country, already going, translating every shape into its wet equivalent, old enough that every life you meet has already solved the same long problem of heat and water and time.
