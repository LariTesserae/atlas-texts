The pale ones come first to any eye that waits. They are the length of a forearm and no thicker than two fingers laid together, the color of bone that has lain in running water until the marrow-memory has been rinsed out. Their joints are too many: a necklace of hinges that lets them fold into a crack no wider than a blade and then unfold on the far side as if the stone itself had merely thought them from one seam into the next. They travel by deciding. A hairline becomes a road because they have already placed their weight on the idea of it. Their sound is the smallest correction inside the basin’s greater noise—a dry ticking, porcelain against nail, the test of whether a wet face will hold. They eat the films, or rather they graze the decision the films have not yet made. There are never many. Half a day of walking and you will see none; then three will appear in a single dripping alley as though they had been queued in some register the stone keeps. They do not like to be seen, and the basin, which sees without eyes, seems to grant them that courtesy.

The films themselves are older than the pale ones and younger than the rock. They occupy the wetter seams as a graphite bloom, a faint rust, a translucent wrinkle that lifts when a sheet of water passes and then lies flat again, obedient. They do not grow toward the pewter light. They do not argue for photosynthesis. They may be chemistry still rehearsing the gestures of biology, or biology that has set the argument down and forgotten where. When the water hesitates—in those crooked chimneys where three or four plates lean together and the falling drops lock into a single held note—the films sometimes thicken, as if listening. A palm pressed there comes away colder, and the cold lasts longer than it should, as though something had briefly considered the warmth and then declined it.

Lower, where the basin floor drops another increment and the water stands rather than runs, the black shallows hold drowned edges and a sound more gulp than strike. Things move there that are not the pale ones. You see them only as a slow rearrangement of darkness, a density that travels just beneath the surface and never quite breaks it. They may be the same jointed creatures grown larger and darker, or they may be something the standing water has assembled out of its own patience: long, jointless, the temperature of the stone around them. They do not surface. The tiles of the old floor, those slightly cupped squares the color of old tooth, lie not far from this shallows; the water treats them as it treats everything else, and the mineral rind that has begun to overgrow their edges is already learning their geometry. The previous visitors left the tiles, the short regular walls, the oxidized posts the plates have closed around like a thought. They passed through, set a few marks to prove the visit, and understood the basin would not keep them. What remains of them is being metabolized. The rind is not lichen. It is the same dark mineral as the plates, growing with the slowness of a decision.

Higher, the idea of birds crosses and does not descend. Their cries arrive late and doubled, bent by the moisture so that one call becomes two, as if the air itself were repeating the news to make sure. They are not residents. Nothing that needs the ordinary sky is a resident here.

What lives most densely is not any of these. It is the crowding itself: the plates leaning mid-collision, the water that films and wells and hangs and sometimes, in the chimneys, consults before it falls. The sound is not accompaniment; it is occupancy. Tones stack until certain corridors become instruments, a pulse felt in the teeth, a held note that is the basin trying to remember a word. Hours change the chord. The mineral noticing has no center and no need of one. It is the wet unclenching, the new surface still deciding whether to be aquifer or ground, the attention that occupies every seam and every drip. It has no eyes because the entire basin is the seeing. Stay long enough among the plates and the spatial crowding becomes something else: a pressure of regard, slow and loud and without malice, that does not care whether you are there and yet registers the heat of your palm, the interruption of a drip, the fact that you are a visitor who will climb back into older country where the rock has had time to forget its own sharpness.

The pale ones move like guests. The films wait to be told what they are. The old floor waits to be stone again. The noticing does not wait. It is already here, working through the long first century of being a place, rewriting its own chord by millimeters, densely itself, unfinished, and in no hurry to finish.
