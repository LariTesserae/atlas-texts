The first things you notice, if you stay still long enough for the heat-shimmer to settle, are the grazers. They move like weather that has decided to have legs. Most are no bigger than a crouched child, low-slung, their bodies a tessellation of the very salts and oxides they scrape from the crust. Twelve or fourteen jointed limbs, each ending in a rasp of living mineral, they inch forward in loose, continent-wide herds that take days to pass a given terrace. Their carapaces drink the light and give it back as a dull, dry glitter; when one dies it simply becomes another ventifact, indistinguishable from the stones around it except that the wind takes a little longer to finish the rounding. They leave trails of finer grit that the next gust erases, and they do not startle. A human foot can come down a metre away and they will not alter course. Their metabolism is a conversation so quiet it can be mistaken for the terrace cooling.

Larger shapes follow at even greater intervals. The tripeds—three massive, geode-knuckled legs supporting a torso that looks poured rather than grown—browse the higher, paler terraces where the gypsum has been baked almost to glass. Their joints click once every few minutes, a sound like distant gravel settling. Inside those joints the crystals keep forming, dissolving, reforming; you can watch a new facet appear over the course of an afternoon if you have nowhere else to be. They never hurry because hurrying would crack them. Membrane-wings ride the thermals for weeks, thin as kiln-paper, their bodies taking on the exact ochre of the smoke-banners they pass through. They fold themselves into the cooling vanes of the great generators and stay there until the metal’s own temperature gradient has taught their tissues a new geometry. Colonial mats, neither fungus nor crystal but something that remembers both, creep across last month’s slag at a centimetre a night, binding loose sinter into something denser, more useful to the next cycle of machines.

The people who live among all this have learned the same tempo. They are spare, long-limbed, their skin the colour of cooled clinker and their eyes narrowed against a glare that never quite becomes night. They dress in layers of woven slag-fibre that have the same pitted patina as the pistons they tend. Conversation happens only in the long pauses between strokes, and even then it is spare: a tilt of the head that means the ore-grade has shifted half a percent, a single syllable that means the conveyor three kilometres west will need a new roller in another local month. Children are taught to walk the same way the lithovores graze—never lifting a foot until the previous one has finished settling. They do not name the star that rises and sets with such mechanical regularity; they simply adjust the angle of the sintering halls by a few degrees every handful of years so the light continues to fall exactly where the processes require it.

The machines themselves have become another kind of inhabitant. Their casings have weather now—pitting, mineral blooms, the slow polish of centuries of grit. A smelting cycle lasts a month because that is how long the metal needs to remember it is metal. The people do not so much operate them as keep them company. When a colony of membrane-wings nests in a generator’s vanes the machine runs cooler, more evenly; when the lithovores stay away from a particular outfall the clinker cools too fast and cracks. No one can say which side began the arrangement. It no longer matters. The relationship is simply another slow chemistry, like the one that turns atmospheric acids into carapace or slag into brick.

Higher still, where the air is thin enough that even the factories have become little more than maintenance outposts around engines whose original purpose has been forgotten, the inhabitants grow stranger. Here the walkers are almost purely geometric: living crystals that have decided, at some geological moment, that locomotion was the next logical facet. They watch from a distance with eyes that are only additional planes. They do not approach because there is nothing left to approach toward. Everything that lives on the Heights has already reached the only state worth reaching: a dry, warm, almost silent continuance, waiting without urgency for the next terrace to rise, the next plume of dust to migrate, the next piston to complete its stroke. 

They are not waiting for anything in particular. They are simply continuing, the way the land itself continues, the way heat continues to live in the rock long after the furnace that made it has been forgotten.
