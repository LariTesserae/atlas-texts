The traces come first, as they always do here: a quarter-turn on a valve you passed an hour ago, the bronze curtain braided aside and fastened with a clip of pale bone, a smear of filings left in a junction box as if someone had fed it and then forgotten the gesture halfway through. The smell arrives later—iron, chlorophyll, and that specific living warmth that has not known air for longer than memory. It is not a crowd-smell. It is the smell of two or three bodies that have become weather.

They do not announce themselves. A figure simply is, occupying a slot-alley the way the water occupies the basin, with the same unhurried claim. At first the mind tries to assemble a person from habit: a torso, limbs, a head. The assembly keeps failing in small, polite ways. The skin is the colour of the limestone’s paler veins, but it has taken on a faint translucence, so that the slow green pulse of the gardens shows through at wrist and throat as if the blood itself had learned photosynthesis. Fine holdfasts, no thicker than root-hairs, trail from the forearms and the nape; they are not parasites. They taste the water continuously and adjust the body’s chemistry by increments too slight to name. Hair, where it remains, is not hair. It is a crest of the same bronze-tea blades that hang from the lintels, combed back and living, each blade storing a little extra warmth against the dimming that passes for night.

The eyes have given up on splendour. They are large, the colour of steeped oak, and they do not dart. They rest. When they move it is with the same molasses patience as the flywheels, taking in a doorway or a pressure change the way a plant takes in a season. The mouth is used rarely and never for words; speech unravels here, and they have long since let it. A hand-sign is enough: two fingers drawn along a pipe means the governor-arm needs another winding of living strap; a palm pressed flat to a column means the oldest one in the middle court is holding its note and all is still agreed. Their tools look grown rather than made—wooden shafts swollen into the exact curve of the palm that has held them for years, edges replaced by living grout that knaps itself sharper against rust. They carry the brownish jars as casually as another might carry a lantern, except the glow is cultured, fed, spoken to in the same silent grammar. When they pass you the water barely stirs. A frond may rest against a shoulder for the length of a thought and then be carried onward, uninjured.

There are never many. You might count five in a week if you were counting, which no one is. They appear, ease a thing, braid a thing, feed a thing, and recede into the curtains as if the work and the worker were the same gesture. Some look older than the brick, their holdfasts gone woody, their skin mapped with the same mineral layers that stripe the walls. Others are younger only by the slowness of the place—still smoother, still learning the warmth-maps, still sometimes startling (almost) when a pale eel unspools from a pipe to taste them and return. The young ones linger longer at the unused rooms, where the coolness reads as a kind of invitation they have been taught to refuse. They do not go up. The grilled shaft in the western rock is not a prohibition so much as a completed thought; the surface has its weather, the basin has its weather, and the understanding is older than any of them.

Other lives move through theirs without hierarchy. The finger-long eels are not pets and not pests; they are the settlement’s continuous sampling, brief complete lives that taste and report back into the pipes. Once in a long while the thing with too many slow joints—neither crab nor millipede nor anything the old surface had a name for—walks a cornice or a fused knuckle of brick. The plants close behind it. The workers do not remark. It may be another descendant, or a visitor from a deeper gallery no census has reached, or simply the basin thinking in another tempo. Shells drift, inhabited by the same greenery that inhabits everything; whether that counts as animal has been tabled so long the question itself has grown a film of silt and been forgotten.

And then there are the columns.

They are not architecture. They are the oldest inhabitants, load-bearing and hollow, braided of overlapping blades and bladders, each one a hanging garden that has decided to stand. The workers tend them the way one tends a relative who has become landscape. They braid back a doorway, they replace a split bladder with a graft taken from their own crest, they listen with an ear (if the water still permits an ear) to the held subsonic vowel that is not machine and not plant but the basin agreeing, again, to continue. The oldest column in the middle court is thicker, hollower, almost a throat. Press close and the note fills the chest the way the heat-exchangers do, a poultice of sound. Nothing answers it because nothing needs to. The workers have become, over the uncounted fathoms of time, another octave of that same vowel—flesh that learned to pulse at the garden’s tide, blood that learned to store warmth and mineral, thought that learned to take a year to forgive a whirl.

They are not dying out and they are not arriving. The next slow census will find them, or find the place wearing them the way it wears its streets, microscopically remembering the paths still used. Some nights (the brown dimming) a worker will rest against a column until the holdfasts knit and the distinction between skin and blade becomes a doctrinal matter no one bothers to table. In the morning the column is a fraction thicker, the worker a fraction more vertical, and the work—already done, still being done—continues at the only speed the water will allow.

You do not meet them so much as notice that the settlement has, for a little while, grown another pair of hands, another crest of living bronze, another jar of residual glow carried through the amber dusk. Then the hands recede, the glow recedes, and the agreement holds, as it has held since the flood that was not disaster but completion. The basin keeps its weather. What lives here is the weather, walking.
