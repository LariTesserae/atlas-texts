The groves are the first thing that answers, though they do not hurry to do so. Each stand is a single, compounded organism that has been arguing with itself for longer than the flooding has been finished. Thirty-meter ribbons of bruise-bone tissue rise and lean and contradict one another; their interiors are not habitat but viscera, a vertical gut of overlapping blades and dripping epiphytic films that digest the faint mineral breath of the plateau. They do not photosynthesize. They remember. Every holdfast is a mouth pressed to old rock, mining residual warmth and the dissolved chemistry of whatever the high land was when it still knew weather and sunlight. Reproduction is not sex but migration: a grove will spend decades sending pale explorers under the silt, then allow its trailing edge to slough and brown so that the entire cathedral has walked a few hundred meters, leaving a palimpsest of dead anchors the currents slowly bury. The process is their speech. The wet thunder of millions of blades is not weather; it is debate, liturgy, the long complaint of a people who have only one kingdom left and intend to keep conducting its business in vegetable time.

They treat the stones as older relatives who have forgotten how to grow. Sometimes a ribbon-forest will thread itself through a corbelled doorway and out the vanished smoke-hole, incorporating the hut the way a tree incorporates a fence. Sometimes the plants keep a scrupulous, circular distance, a halo of bare silt around a menhir as if the stone still holds a title the water has not revoked. The rings of standing stones drop the temperature another fraction when you pass inside them; the plants refuse to explain this. They simply bow, collectively, when the great currents reverse, and the key of their groaning changes as though acknowledging a function no one now assigns.

Between the groves there is almost nothing that moves of its own will. Kilometers of scored silt, the faint parallel writing of currents that have run the same sentences since the world agreed to stay drowned. No burrows. No tracks. The ecology has only one voice, and it is loud the way an empty nave is loud. Yet the water itself remembers in excess of physics. Swim a line and hours later the current still prefers that line, faintly, like a habit it cannot quite break. Events arrive late and leave residue. A spore-cloud, released in one of the rare coordinated hisses that turn the water to milk, will settle as a new film and then, weeks afterward, the groves will flower in perfect silence—a held breath the entire plateau seems to take, louder than their usual roar. In that silence the wrongness thickens. You feel watched by something that has no eyes and does not need them.

What lives here, besides the groves, is the memory of the people who stacked the slate and pecked the spirals when the land was still high and the water had not yet decided to stay. They are not ghosts in any theatrical sense. They are the viscous remainder, the late-arriving events that never quite left. Their tool-marks have been worn nearly flush, but the water continues to honor the old paths as though they were still roads. Their rings still perform a cooling no one asked them to perform. Their huts fill with pale mud at the same patient rate they once filled with peat-smoke. The plants keep their distance or grow through them according to an etiquette the drowned highlanders never wrote down but that the plateau has not forgotten. Time here is the same temperature as the stone that forgot sunlight: settled, excessive, unwilling to let anything finish. The former inhabitants have become part of that reluctance. They prefer certain currents. They drop the temperature inside their circles. They make the groves hesitate, as if the stones still have rights.

Further out, where the plateau finally surrenders and the water darkens into something that has never heard of height, even this remainder thins. The groves do not follow. The built things stop. What remains is only the agreement itself—stone, cold, plant, noise—continuing to happen, loudly, without witnesses of any other kingdom, because the witnesses have already been folded into the medium. They live the way the silt lives: rewritten daily, never quite erased, preferring the paths they once walked when the land still believed it was high. You could drift for a very long time and meet nothing that would contradict this. Another grove leaning. Another hut filling. Another silence that is louder than thunder. The country has already happened. It is still happening. Whatever lives here has learned to inhabit the happening itself.
