The lives here do not arrive. They occupy the same way the water occupies the highland: already complete, already old, already doing the single thing they will always do. You notice them last, after the roar and the cool and the impossible intervals, because they refuse the eye the way the green cells refuse it — present in every litre, decisive only when you stop looking for movement that would count as news.

First the water itself is inhabited. The faint mineral green is not tint. It is a standing population of cells so fine they have the texture of dissolved light. Each cell is a plant, yes, but also a citizen of the closed account: it divides at the exact rate it is taken, photosynthesises at the exact rate the light (always the same light) allows, and dies into the same nutrient that will become another cell in the same stratum. There is no bloom because there is no surplus and no deficit. Swim through it and you swim through a census that has already been taken.

Against the trunks the great sieves keep their places. They are the size of dinghies only if you insist on a comparison that no longer applies; they are the size they have always been. Flattened ovals of living cartilage and filter-membrane, the colour of wet basalt, they cling in constellations that have not rearranged since the current learned its one path. Their attachment is fusion now — tissue grown into tissue, the bark’s flutings continued across their bases so that you cannot say where the mill ends and the feeder begins. Each one generates its own soft secondary roar, a nap of sound laid over the industrial thunder, water drawn through crowns of cilia that never pause and never hurry. They do not wait for food. The water arrives already containing exactly what they will extract. Their bodies pulse with a rhythm that is not a heartbeat so much as a second, smaller turbine. Press a hand near one (the current they make will tell you where) and the vibration is cooler, higher-pitched, still perfectly even. They have no seasons of spawning. Whatever young they once produced have already become the next fixed stars in the same unchanging sky of bark.

Near the stacks, where the laminated plates hold their unvarying charge, hang the drinkers. You see them as interruptions in the green grain, then as shapes, then as the impossibility of so many fins. They do not swim so much as occupy a volume, bodies like knotted, elongated hearts from which fins erupt in clusters — seven, eleven, fourteen — some broad and veined as vanes, some reduced to trembling threads, all slightly asymmetric so that no drinker repeats another yet all perform the identical stillness. They face the stacks or they have no faces; orientation is something the field decides. Along their flanks the lateral lines glow a dull constant phosphor, not signalling, not answering, simply registering the hum that has one pitch. They drink that pitch. The charge moves through them the way sugar once moved through leaves, and they convert it without remainder and without storage beyond the next identical moment. A fin will adjust, a millimetre, once in a long while, the way a building settles. The glow does not flicker. They are many. You can swim an hour through charged water and see three, then none, then a loose congregation that is still not a crowd. Their niche is full and has always been full.

In the long galleries between the great bodies the shoals condense and evaporate. They are the visual paradox made flesh: abundant, then absent. When they are present they are a single density, small bodies (palm-length, perhaps, or shorter) whose skin is more translucent membrane than scale, showing the faint green of the cells they sieve. Extra fins again, smaller, used for the minute corrections that keep the cloud a cloud. They occupy a sunbeam of water the way dust does — brightly, briefly, locally — and then the volume is empty again for a day’s swim, or a week’s, the statistics holding even if no particular fish returns to the same coordinate. Their mouths work the same angle of the same wheel. Nothing hunts them in a way that would thin the next condensation. Nothing they eat is ever scarce. They have the look of things that remember how to school but have forgotten why the memory was needed.

Rarer still, and slower, the road-beasts use the trunks as the trunks have always been used. You meet them as moving polish, a darkness that takes the better part of an hour to pass a given fluting. They are long — some longer than the sieves are wide — low-slung, their undersides a living abrasive of cilia and mineralised skin that has worn the bark into the bowls and overhangs and highways whose smoothness is older than the idea of a road. They do not travel so much as continue. Their path is the current’s path, the one path, and they follow it with the same refusal of revision. What they take from the cambium is exactly what the cambium has already budgeted to give; the bruise, if you could call it that, closes behind them into the identical dimpled skin. Fish escort them for a time, then peel away on their own fixed errands. The beasts add almost nothing to the roar. Their passage is a quieter frequency, felt more than heard, the sound of stone remembering it is also flesh.

On the rare plateaus, where the tissue spreads and the water shallows to the height of a standing body and the light is almost air, other walkers appear. They are the only lives that use limbs the way limbs were once imagined: jointed, several, the number not standardised — five, or six, or an uneven seven — holding a compact body clear of the living floor. They walk the dimpled skin and leave no print that lasts. The million mouths that are also leaves that are also turbines shout around their ankles, the white even noise loudest here because here the work is nearest the surface. These walkers graze the fine continuous rain, or the cells that settle, or perhaps they graze nothing and their walking is itself the metabolism. Smaller fish travel with them like temporary thoughts, then remember other circuits. Far across the same plateau another walker, identical in function if not in the precise count of legs, completing the same traverse that has no starting flag.

Deeper, where the green goes grainy and the grain is still plant, the silt-lives are less animals than weather that happens to be organised. They are the night-dust given just enough coordination to be fauna: minute, numerous beyond counting, occupying the same strata they have occupied since the strata formed. Some filter. Some are the filter. Their motion is Brownian until it is not, a collective drift that never accumulates into a bloom or a crash. The seafloor-cambium accepts their lightest touch the way it accepts a keel: a bruise that is already healing into the sentence that was always going to be written.

Inside the trunks, in the halls and the vascular gorges and the stacked plates, there are the interior ones. You smell them before you see them — a slight intensification of wet iron and chlorophyll and that clean electrical note, as if the air (the water) itself were more inhabited. They are pale, or they have no need of pigment. Some are worm-long and thread the bundles; some are flattened and cling to the undersides of the living laminations the way the sieves cling outside. They tend nothing and they are not parasites. They live in the pressure of sound that has become a medium, sensing by the unchanging pitch, metabolising whatever exact fraction the great body allots them. Their loops are shorter, tighter, still loops. A shout in those halls is accepted without residue; so are they.

All of them — cells, sieves, drinkers, shoals, road-beasts, plateau-walkers, silt, interiors — share the finished quality. They do not sleep because the plants do not sleep. They do not fruit, do not migrate, do not wait for a change of shift. Reproduction, if it occurs, is the replacement of a part by an identical part so that the constellation, the shoal-statistic, the polished road, the hanging field, remain what they were. Time has nothing to push against in them either. Their abundance is statistical; their presence is sparse; the paradox is the same one the forests make. You can live among them a lifetime (the light will not teach you otherwise) and never see a birth, a death, a broken pattern, a silence where a roar should be.

What lives here is the work, continuing. The elevation holds it. The water fills it. Between the great bodies the long lit roaring rooms stay empty in the way that matters and full in the way that counts, and the lives that occupy those rooms are already the lives that will occupy them. They have the strangeness of a motion that has arrived at every position at once. Stay until the loudness is quiet and you will hear them as part of that quiet: not metaphor, not machine, not forest, only the highland sea being itself, inhabitant and inhabited, without remainder.
