The first lives are the ones that never quite decide whether they are part of the water or the air. Midges lift in dense, trembling sheets from every still surface the moment a footfall or a valve-clang disturbs them; they hang for a few seconds in the pewter light like a second, living steam, then settle again so completely that the black mud looks furred. Their drone is not separate from the engines. It occupies the same register, a continuous low vibration that the larger flies ride and thicken. Those flies are the colour of wet coal when they rest and the colour of oil-slick when they move. They drink from the silica seeps, their abdomens swelling with the same mineral that crusts the rock into brittle shelves. You feel them before you see them: a sudden weight on the back of the hand, a prick that draws a bead of blood already half-evaporated by the heat.

Below them the frogs have claimed every margin. They sit in the shallows that are merely blood-warm, throats pulsing in time with the distant beam engine, and they sit on the cooler brick ledges where condensate runs in thin, endless threads. Their croaks overlap without pause or hierarchy, a wetter, looser engine that never misses. Some are no larger than a thumbnail, the colour of algae film; others are fist-sized, mottled the exact rust of the ironwork, and they watch with the same unhurried ownership as the dogs. When something larger approaches they do not flee so much as subside, becoming another slick lump among the sinter.

The reeds that manage to grow in the least scalding corners hold the water-rails. You hear them first: a repetitive, metallic *kek-kek* that sounds like a small, poorly seated valve. They move through the stems with necks extended, bodies the colour of wet mudstone, and they vanish the instant the eye tries to hold them. Grebes occupy the wider pools, diving in water that would scald a hand held still. They surface with nothing in their bills more often than not; whatever they eat is already part of the closed circuit, the same water lifted and returned. Their calls are sharp, almost impatient, as if the place has already lasted longer than they expected.

Rats use the drier walkways and the undersides of the galleries. They are lean, short-haired, the same grey-brown as the unweathered rock, and they move with the confidence of creatures that have never needed to hide. They drink from the weirs, they take whatever scraps the works produce (hot oil rags, the occasional crust of bread that appears from the engine-house doors), and they ignore the dogs. The dogs themselves are everywhere and nowhere in particular. Short-haired, ribs visible, coats permanently darkened by steam and mineral drip. They trot the boardwalks and the short iron bridges as if the entire basin were a single familiar room. They drink standing in the cooler pools, they sleep in the lee of the cooling towers where the brick is only warm rather than hot, and they regard the human figures with the same mild interest they give the frogs. Nothing here is owned. The dogs simply live at the same density the heat and the refuse will support.

The people appear later, the way the light does: already used, already condensed. They are few. The buildings stand far enough apart that you can walk the circuit without meeting more than two or three at a time. They come out of the engine-houses and the corrugated sheds to oil a gland, to seat a valve, to listen with a practised ear to the pitch of a pump. Their clothes are dark, heavy, permanently damp; the fabric never dries and never quite rots. Skin stays flushed, always beaded, the colour of the iron-oxide stains that weep down the brick. Faces have the same unweathered quality as the young rock: planes still sharp, no softening of years because the years do not accumulate here. They speak little. When they do, the voice is hoarse, mineral, and the words are pressures, temperatures, the exact travel of a piston. They move at the tempo of the beam engine, neither hurried nor idle. A hand rests on hot iron only long enough to confirm what the ear already knows.

Children exist, smaller versions of the same economy. They wade the least dangerous shallows, catching frogs that they do not keep, or they sit on the walkways watching the steam veils that never lift. They do not play so much as occupy the intervals, the same way the buildings occupy the wet ground. There is no school hour, no meal hour that is not also a maintenance hour. Food appears from the same closed circuit: fish that live in the warmer pools (pale, almost translucent, tasting of metal and heat), algae scraped from the cooler brick and dried on the galleries, the occasional tough rush-stem chewed for the faint salt. Nothing is brought in. Nothing leaves.

At night that is not night the lamps inside the deepest sheds throw a duller version of the same pewter. The people sleep in short, irregular watches, always one or two awake to walk the circuit, to listen. They sleep in hammocks slung in the warmer corners of the engine-houses, or on pallets in the sheds where the corrugated iron ticks with retained heat. Dreams, if they have them, are the continuation of the same sounds: drip, suck, the slow rise and fall of the beam, the frogs never missing a stroke.

Something else lives in the vents themselves, or perhaps it is only the vents living. When the steam lifts in those low, unmoving veils you sometimes see a thickening, a brief coherence, as if the mineral air had decided to remember a shape. It never quite becomes a figure. It never quite dissolves. The people do not look at it directly, or they look at it the way they look at a pressure gauge: information, not surprise. The young rock, still showing the planes along which it split, sometimes weeps faster, as if answering. The silica crusts grow a fraction thicker overnight that is not night. Nothing has changed. Everything continues at the only rate it has ever known.

You can walk the raised boardwalks until the circuit becomes familiar as a pulse, and still the lives do not resolve into a single image. They are the drone and the croak and the wet slap of a dog’s paws and the hoarse word exchanged between two figures oiling the same gland they oiled yesterday and will oil tomorrow. They are the grebe surfacing with empty bill, the child standing motionless in water that would blister a stranger, the rat pausing on the iron bridge to drink condensate from its own whiskers. They are the place, continuing. The basin holds them the way it holds the steam: perfectly contained, perfectly reflected, a standing wave of heat and water and iron that has already reached the only density it will ever need.
