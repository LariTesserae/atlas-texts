The ones who remain are not many, and they do not announce themselves. You would walk the entire floor twice before you understood that the denser shadow under the fig was not shadow, that the faint regularity of three basins was still being kept. They have the patience of the stone they live against. Their bodies take the same long rounding: shoulders like worn buttresses, fingers that end in the same pale, slightly swollen pads the succulents use to hold water. Skin the color of the tea in the basins, or of the vertical seeps—damp, mineral, never quite dry. Where the vents breathe nearest them a faint translucence shows, heat moving just under the surface the way it moves under the flowstone.

They do not speak. The agreement the animals made long ago—not to spend themselves on noise—includes them, or began with them. What passes between them is slower than gesture: a shift of weight that makes a leaf unstick, a pebble added to a niche or taken from it, the way one of them will stand a long time with a palm against a warmer patch of wall as if listening to a pulse the rest of us cannot quite hear. Children are infrequent. When they come they are small and already quiet, born or hatched or simply appearing in the dimmest hour when the basins cloud with that milkiness that is not reflection. The adults do not crowd them. They are left among the mosses until they learn the heat and the drips and the difference between a root and a snake.

They tend without seeming to tend. The steps that are half-swallowed by the fig’s roots are cleared just enough that a foot can still find them; the lintel’s underside is wiped of new soot when soot appears, though no fire is ever lit now. The pebbles in the niches are rearranged after the rains, or after a vent has been particularly insistent. No one could tell you why the pattern matters. It has been the pattern longer than memory. They drink from the seeps and eat the stacked-coin leaves and, once in a long while, one of the moths that has no mouth. They do not hunt the plated thing or the lizards. Those belong to the same density. When something is taken—when a space opens in the vegetation and later closes again—it is accepted the way the bowl accepts a drop: heard, then returned to the quiet.

At the hours when the thermal tide turns they move with it, a slow drift from the hottest rock toward the water and back, never hurrying, never leaving the bowl. The rim is not a boundary they test. The world above has thinned itself of meaning for them, or they have thinned themselves of it. Their attention is the same slight wrongness the plants show: they track heat more faithfully than light, they open and close with the vents, they seem at times to be waiting for a note that the stone is already holding. Stand among them long enough and you feel it too—the pressure of a sound just under hearing, the sense that the cathedral-room is an instrument and they are the ones who know which surfaces to touch.

They have been here long enough that the question of whether they made the first cuts or simply inherited them no longer has an edge. They are the balance now. When you leave—if you leave—you carry a little of the heat in your soles and the memory of a drip that landed exactly when one of them turned their head, as if the drop and the turning had always been the same event. The bowl holds them the way it holds everything else: completely, without comment, and without ever quite letting the light that arrives be enough to name them.
