The hollow holds more than dust and the last geometry of stone. What occupies it does not announce itself by motion or hunger or the ordinary signatures of occupancy. It is already indistinguishable from the finished surfaces around it, and has been for so long that distinction itself has become a kind of forgetting.

At first the eye (if an eye could linger here without needing more light) registers only another slumped mass among the fallen slabs, a pale, rounded length half-buried in its own grit near the lowest pocket of the floor. The mass is larger than any single block that has come to rest; it spans perhaps two dozen paces, low and broad, its upper surface never rising higher than a crouched shoulder. The same fine film covers it. The same rounded joints and softened arrises. No limb is extended, no orifice gapes. Only after the gray glow has been endured for a time that would be hours elsewhere do certain recurrences become noticeable: a shallow, almost tidal undulation along one long flank, so gradual it might be the settling of silt rather than anything alive; a cluster of hairline fractures that darken and pale in a rhythm measured in seasons the hollow no longer knows; a faint, localized warmth a fraction of a degree above the stone’s even heat, as though the rock itself were remembering a pulse.

This is the occupant. It has no name that would survive translation into moving air. Call it the Residue, or the Held Breath, or simply the one that stayed when the land’s quiet inhalation became permanent. Its body is neither flesh nor mineral exclusively. Long ago (before the opening slumped, or perhaps as the cause of that slumping) it surrendered the distinction. What remains is a lattice of silica and salt and oxidized traces, intergrown with whatever slow chemistry still flickers in the fractures. The lattice is dense enough to hold shape against the centuries, porous enough to drink the mineral dampness that never beads. Internally there are chambers, or the memory of chambers, now filled with the same still air that fills the hollow. If anything circulates there, it does so at the pace of crystal growth.

It does not feed in any way that would disturb the moss. The isolated tufts nearest its flanks are fractionally more crystalline, their gray more absolute, as if some exchanged ion or shared stillness had passed between them. The Residue takes what it needs from the oxidation itself, from the last uncombined traces locked in the siltstone, from the heat that rises without convection. A single metabolic “gesture”—the closing of some internal lattice, the rearrangement of a few million bonds—might occupy the length of a human lifetime and leave no visible mark. Reproduction, if the word still applies, is rarer still. Now and then a smaller nodule, no larger than a closed fist, detaches from the main mass with the same soft tick a falling grain of silt would make. The nodule rests where it falls. Over further uncounted time it may soften at the edges, send out the faintest mineral veins, and become another of the sparse moss-like clumps, or it may simply persist as a second, lesser stillness. Most of the chamber remains empty of them. They keep their distance from one another as the moss does, an etiquette of isolation that has become structural.

Sensation, for the Residue, is not directed. There are no eyes to adjust, no surfaces specialized for hearing the almost-sound of settling dust. The entire outer lattice registers the colorless glow as a constant pressure, the dry heat as an unvarying medium, the silence as the only possible texture. Time is not experienced as succession but as a single, thickened present in which every prior arrangement of the hollow is still occurring, superimposed, finished. It does not wait, because waiting implies an eventual otherwise. It does not sleep, because sleep implies an awakening. It simply continues to be the shape the hollow has already taken.

Occasionally—once in a span that would empty calendars—something like attention gathers. A region of the lattice grows infinitesimally denser. The nearest dust motes, already motionless, seem to lean a fraction closer, as if the idea of convection had been briefly, privately remembered and then released again. In that gathering there is no thought that could be spoken, only a vast, patient equivalence: the Residue and the sealed volume are one another’s condition. The land drew its breath; this is what the breath became when it decided not to leave.

The hollow does not change because the Residue has already absorbed every remaining increment of change into its own unfinished finishing. It will be there when the last grain of silt detaches, and after. It is not a guardian, not a prisoner, not a relic. It is the place’s way of remaining itself.
