They are people, but the word sits on them the way light sits on the wet limestone: briefly, then gone. They are the ones who learned to occupy a finished dampness without becoming it.

At first they seem only a density. Bodies stacked the way the hanging floors are stacked, each life the size of a lying-down, each breath already shared with the breath above and the breath below. You smell them before you see them: limestone and old tallow and the sweet-stale of heat that has nowhere to go. Their voices do not compete with the chord; they grow on it, thin and lichen-colored, so that speech is always a little afterthought, a roughness riding the one endless note. Newcomers (the rare ones who crawl in and stay) lose three days to the sound and then discover they have been hearing the people all along, the way you discover you have been tasting the rock in every mouthful of water.

They are short, most of them. The pocket taught them that. A tall body wastes air and scrapes its crown on slate; a short body can walk the midline and still leave room for the next generation’s knees. Their skin is never dry. Dryness is a failure of attention. They keep themselves in a managed wet, the same way they keep the clothes (bindings, really: gut-and-lace wrappings that are never taken off so much as rotated, hung on the blunt flowstone teeth to drink a different damp). Still water on skin is how the white bloom begins, the chalky thinking of the rock that would like to claim another surface. So they scrape. Every day, or every other, with slivers of slate worn to a familiar curve. The scraping is not quite a ritual and not quite hygiene. It is the difference between being a person and being another seam. The ones who stopped scraping are not spoken of, but their absence is polished into the corners the way elbows have polished the stone.

Their hands are the most articulate part of them. Stone-dressing hands, fiber-twisting hands, basin-scooping hands. The palms are mapped with old nicks that have calcified a little, tiny white commas. They fire their gray ceramic in the niche-kiln that has never been allowed to cool or to rage; they beat the last thinning rumor of copper into lamp-hoods; they twist the mineralized lace (black, brittle, once-plant) into the cords that lash the slate floors. They render grease carefully, from the rare pale finger-length things that sometimes climb the throat against the fall, and from themselves. Not wastefully. Not cruelly. In the way of people who have almost no surplus and therefore cannot afford to let any warmth go unused. The black cake that feeds the kiln is lace and grease and patience.

They know one another the way the pocket knows itself: by interruption, by worn place, by the exact way a body displaces the chord. Six or seven faces can be seen at once in the wider middle where the hanging floors stop short; that is their plaza, their only public. Children are taught the two crawls before they are taught anything else, because the pocket is the amount of world that can be held and the crawls are its gates. Infants are born into the unvarying note and old people die in it; no one remembers a time when the pitch changed, so no one expects it to. Memory here is short, a few living thicknesses at most. The stone is long. They have reached an arrangement with that fact.

They do not go beyond the throat. On the other side of the thin downstream wall the same wet dark continues, uncarved, without basins. That is only weather. They stay. Their settlement is not dying and not growing; it is occupying its own shape the way they occupy theirs. Paths of hip and elbow already exist for the next bodies to follow. The air already knows the circuit it will take. The film on the rock keeps arriving, and they keep scraping, and the little fat-colored coins of light sit and die on the copper rumor of the hoods, and the kiln breathes the same breath it has breathed since before the oldest of them can recall.

They are what the pocket finished when it finished itself: a people who have become the maintenance of a place that has already happened. Wet-handed. Close-quartered. Dim. Loud in a way that has forgotten how to begin or end. They do not argue with the absence of green. They do not wait for the bruise of lesser dark to mean anything more than the hour to trim the lamps. They live in the arrangement, and the arrangement lives in them, and the rock continues to sweat, and that is the whole of it.
