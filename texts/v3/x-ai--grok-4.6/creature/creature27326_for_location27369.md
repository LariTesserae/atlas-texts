They are few, and they do not hurry.

That is the first thing you notice if you stay long enough for the cold to stop feeling like an event and start feeling like a grammar. The plateau does not crowd. Neither do they. A city of several hundred is a metropolis here; most settlements hold fewer, sometimes only a few dozen who still answer to names. They space themselves the way the cities are spaced — not from dislike, but because the air is thin and the distances between thoughts have grown honest. When they gather, it is in courtyards already half-claimed by moss, around cisterns whose water tastes of stone and a faint metallic green. Their voices stay low. The thin air takes volume as a kind of tax.

They are human in the way a last draft is still a sentence. Arms, legs, faces that remember other climates without quite believing them. Skin the color of the plateau itself — tea-stain, shade-bone, a bruise that has forgotten its cause — weathered past youth even in the young, because the light is complete and the cold lives inside it. Hair is dark or the dull copper of the prostrate shrubs, worn long and often felted against the wind, or cropped close so frost cannot make a weight of it. Eyes are pale, not from any northern romance but because the honest daylight has sanded the pigment down; they water easily in the rare pollen-sheets, and they do not look away from distances. Fingers are broad, pads thickened, nails often split and filed against stone. They dress in layers of the copper-leaved shrubs beaten into felt, lichen-dyed in blacks and olives so deep they drink the pale sun rather than reflect it. Boots are hide from nothing living now — old stock, repaired until the original animal is only a rumor in the stitching — or wrapped fiber and moss. Everything is mended. Everything is passed.

They do not farm in rows. They tend. In the invited courtyards and the damper seams they harvest the wall-mosses, the cistern-skins (scraped, dried into cakes that taste of cold algae and patience), the occasional unrolling fern whose slowness they have learned to wait for. They cut blocks from the plateau with wedges and mallets, listening for the stone’s old agreements about where it will split true. They twist fiber against the thigh. They resharpen the scarce metal — a knife whose story is longer than its edge, a needle that has sewn three generations of felt, a thin saw that sings a note no one else remembers. Fire is kept small, in pits lined with the harder seams, because wood is a visitor and peat a memory. They eat little. The body here learns to be a cistern: store, release slowly, do not insist.

Children are uncommon and not hurried into being. A birth is marked by hanging extra moss to dry, by a new cairn at the settlement’s edge that the weather may or may not rearrange. The young learn the mason’s marks first — the language of notches that is consistent even when the stone is still deciding — and only later the names of the groves and the long empty intervals. They learn that a day’s walk can become three or none, that a doorway can take from noon until the light fails, that a house may appear fitted and empty over a winter and must be occupied the way weather occupies a valley: by arriving and remaining. No one calls this magic. It is the garment’s old seams. They live inside the fraying without trying to reweave it.

Between cities they walk. A single figure on the tea-colored stone is a mark the wind practices erasing. They leave cairns that become ground-plans, then nothing, then a grove that should have taken a century. They do not get lost in the ordinary sense; the plateau is too finished for that. They get delayed, or translated, or returned to a street they left last season with the season still in their legs as a debt. When they reach the next city they are greeted without surprise, given water that has been waiting, a place by a fire that has been kept. News travels the way pollen travels: in sheets, inconsistently, gold for an afternoon and then settled.

They do not hunt. There is nothing. They do not keep animals because the country edited that motion out long before any of them arrived — or perhaps they arrived already knowing the edit. The lushness has no mouths, and they have learned not to be mouths in return. Pollination is weather. Growth is a clock with half its gears removed. They move through it the same way: processes without appetites they are willing to admit. They repair roofs against a wind that has been saying the same phrase since before speech. They sweep the mysterious new houses. They sleep in the groves sometimes, waking with hair beaded and a single condensed image they cannot keep, which they treat as another kind of harvest.

There are other things that live here, if living is the word that still fits.

The groves themselves keep a slower citizenship. You can stand among the packed, waxed leaves and feel the air change — damper, almost kind — and know you are inside a respiration that does not require you. Some trunks hold private weather: a closet-sized squall, snow on moss that flowers out of any calendar the cities keep. Leaves stack in ways that imply a third side. Put your hand in and you touch wet bark, the idea of wet bark, and the dryness of a drought ten thousand years gone. The groves do not speak. They continue. People who sleep there too often begin to move like them — unhurried past the point of human patience, hair taking on the silvered undersides of the leaves, dreams arriving already frost-thin.

The stone lives in another tense. Builders say they do not always know whether they are adding a course or uncovering one the plateau had put aside. A chisel left on a wall becomes a fern whose fronds remember the tool-mark. A stair that was stone at last light is sheathed in living green by morning, lush and already old. Districts soften overnight, carvings losing their edges the way events lose theirs. The cities grow the way talus grows: intention is included and not required. No one claims the new work. Occupation is weather. Sometimes a person will put a palm to a wall and feel it sweating the way living things sweat, and also the way stone sweats in a thaw, and also the way a finished world sweats when it dreams it is not quite done. They do not pull the hand away.

And there are the ones who are no longer quite people, or not only.

You meet them rarely, usually at the margins — the last street before the plateau resumes its single continuous sentence, or deep in a grove whose interior night has its own climate. They have the same tea-stain skin, the same pale eyes, but the cold has finished something in them that it only began in the others. Their breath does not hang; it condenses immediately into the same thin frost that writes on every horizontal. They speak little, and when they do the words arrive late or early, wearing each other’s coats. One will tell you a story that happened yesterday and you will realize, walking away, that the mallet-blows in it are the ones you have not yet lifted. Another will stand so still that lichen begins its unthinkable advance across a boot, a thumb’s width in what should have been a childhood. They are not ghosts. Ghosts would be excess motion. They are drafts that stayed. Some tend the groves as if the groves were cities that learned leaves. Some walk the long gaps until a cairn rearranges itself into a room and they simply live there, sweeping, lighting a careful fire, hanging moss, until the room becomes a small grove in turn.

No one worships them. No one exiles them. The plateau is too level for hierarchy and too old for surprise that lasts. They are part of the same last sentence: green where green can insist, stone arranged into shelter by hands that may or may not still be the original hands, a quiet in which processes continue because stopping was never the same as ending.

On an ordinary afternoon, in the honest light that has nowhere to put a lasting shadow, one of them — or one of you, if you have stayed — will rest a palm on a wall that is also a terrace that is also a ridge of a mountain so finished even its absence has been worn smooth. The wall will sweat: living, mineral, the thin intermittent moisture of a world that is done and still, somehow, not. Enough.

That is who lives here. That is what. The distinction, like the edges of the plateau, is something you cannot reach in a lifetime of walking.
