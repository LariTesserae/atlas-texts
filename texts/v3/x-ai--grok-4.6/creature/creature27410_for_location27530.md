The geckos come first to the eye, though they never announce themselves. They are not quite translucent; the word implies a glassiness they lack. Their skins hold a milky depth, like the last film of water left in a limestone dish after the sun has taken the rest. Organs show through as faint, drifting smudges the color of old bruises. They climb the brightest walls in increments so small they register as stillness interrupted. A foot lifts, hangs, settles. Minutes pass. Another foot. They pause longest where the reflected noon is fiercest, heads tilted as if drinking the glare through the thin membranes of their eyelids. Whether the light nourishes them or merely stuns them into a kind of vegetal trance is impossible to say. They leave no tracks. When one finally reaches an overhang it flattens itself into the residual glow beneath and becomes, for a while, another pale streak in the stone.

The moths are even less substantial. They appear only at the periphery, never in the open volume, as if the concentrated white at the pit’s center would unmake them. Their wings are the exact bleached tone of the limestone after centuries of polishing rain; when they move they look like flakes of the wall itself deciding, briefly, to become airborne. They do not fly so much as un-settle. A cloud condenses out of nothing near a hanging sheet, hangs trembling, then dissolves again. Their bodies, if they have bodies, are too small and too pale to hold outline. What they eat, if they eat, must be the lichens’ microscopic dust or the faint mineral dampness that beads at dawn. They make no sound the ear can keep.

On the floor the beetles work with a diligence that feels ceremonial. Thumbnail-sized, matte black except for the dust they roll, they gather lichen scrap and powdered stone into spheres no larger than a seed. The spheres are pushed in slow helices that last for days, perfect until the next almost-imperceptible breeze erases them. The beetles never collide, never pause to rest in any way a human watcher would recognize. Their paths cross and recross the same few square meters around the evaporite dish, as though they are writing and rewriting a single sentence whose meaning is the act of writing. After rain, when the dish holds its palm of water, they approach the edge, drink without wetting their legs, and resume. The water itself never clouds.

Farther out, spaced so widely they seem like accidents of perspective, the tall grazers move. They are deer only in the loosest sense: the legs have too many joints, folding and unfolding with a deliberation that turns walking into a kind of unfolding architecture. Their coats match the limestone so exactly that at midday they vanish except for the slow darkening of a shadow that should not be there. They graze the lichens in circuits that take hours to complete, heads lowered, mouths working with the same unhurried rhythm as the beetles. Their hooves, whatever they are made of, never click. Even when one of them crosses a patch of loose flakes the stone remains silent. At rare moments two will pass close enough that their flanks almost touch; they do not acknowledge each other. Their eyes, when they lift them, are the same pale as the rest of them, pupils contracted to pinpricks against the brightness. They give the impression of creatures that have forgotten they are separate from the place that holds them.

Dusk changes the population without ever filling the space. From fissures too narrow for any hand, the sail-things emerge. They are not bats. Their bodies are attenuated to the point of suggestion, little more than a central knot of tissue from which vast, veined membranes unfold. The membranes catch the weak thermal rising from the still-warm floor and they drift, never beating, never steering in any obvious way. They ride the column of air the way dust rides a sunbeam. There are always more than the eye can count, yet they never crowd; distance itself seems enlarged around them. They do not collide. They do not call. When full dark comes they fold and slip back into the same cracks, leaving the pit to the geckos that have not yet finished their climb and to the beetles still rolling their spheres by starlight.

Other lives exist at scales the first glance misses. In the thin seep that traces its dark line down one wall, thread-like worms the color of wet limestone pulse so slowly they might be mineral deposits deciding to remember motion. They surface only when condensation is heaviest, drink, and withdraw. The lichens themselves are not passive. Under the strongest light their orange and dusty-green maps shift by fractions of a millimeter over weeks, advancing, retreating, writing slow cartographies no one will read. Microscopic mites, visible only when the sun hits a particular angle, walk the lichens’ surfaces like pilgrims on a painted desert.

And then there are the ones that do not punctuate so much as inhabit the volume itself. They are felt before they are seen, if they are ever seen. A thickening in the air at the center of the pit, a place where the brightness seems to fold back on itself. Sometimes, late in the afternoon, a shape coheres there: taller than the grazers, thinner, the same pale, but upright. It does not walk. It occupies. The walls appear to lean toward it slightly, or perhaps that is only the way the light wraps. It has no face that can be held in memory. If it has hands they are the hands that once, longer ago than the present shape of the flutes, left those almost imaginary grooves and the polished patches at shoulder height. It does not use them now. It simply stands, or the idea of standing occurs in that spot, and the low sub-audible pressure of the pit grows denser, like a held breath that has decided not to end. The animals do not flee it. They do not approach. They continue their circuits and their climbs as if it were another feature of the stone, another slow process the pit has already finished becoming.

At night the heat radiates back from every surface and the air never quite cools. In that lingering warmth the presence, if it is a presence, thins again until it is only the memory of density. Dawn brings the silvering, the brief return of water that the stone has never quite forgotten, and then the light gathers once more, brighter than it has any right to be. Everything resumes exactly. Nothing has hurried. The lives that belong here have no need to become anything other than what the pit, in its long patience, has already allowed them to be.
