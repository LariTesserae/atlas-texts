The ones who belong here do not arrive. They precipitate.

You become aware of them first as a change in the way the rime decides to form. A surface that should glaze uniformly instead leaves a narrow, body-shaped absence, a warmer ghost of negative space that the ice refuses to occupy. Then the absence fills, not from outside but from within the film of water itself: mineral-white filaments thicken, braid, take on weight and intention. What stands up from the seep is neither the filamentous flicker you crouched to see nor anything that remembers being born. It is simply the local chemistry agreeing, for a while, to walk.

They are few. Never more than the buildings, often fewer. Seventeen would be a crowd; most circuits of the walkways encounter none. When one does appear it is always alone, always the same height as the slender connecting spines, a column of the same continuous material that has learned, temporarily, to bend at what would be joints. The surface is matte where the being has been still, polished to dark glass where it has recently moved. No clothing, no tools, no distinction between skin and structure. The light that passes through the walls of the buildings also passes through them, so that at certain angles they become almost indistinguishable from the architecture they step out of. Only the gait gives them away: each footfall is placed with the exact slowness of a slab of rime deciding whether to shear.

They do not breathe in any way that joins the general downward seep. The air around them remains a condensing medium, but their own exhalation, if it occurs, is indistinguishable from the hydrothermal plumes. Inside the buildings the dry cold does not change when they enter; the stone simply continues to glow at the same even intensity. They stand for hours in the precise centres of rooms, or they lie along the floors so that their length matches the pulse you feel underfoot. When they leave, the temperature gradient they occupied lingers like a held note, then equalises so gradually you cannot mark the moment it is gone.

What they eat is the difference between the two regimes of the cirque. They pause over the warmer seeps and the filamentous white withdraws into them rather than from them. They pause under the falling glitter of frozen vapour and some of the crystals do not melt; they are taken inward through the same continuous surface. Nothing is wasted, nothing accumulated. Their mass remains constant. Growth, if it happens, is expressed only as a further refinement of the planes, a slightly more deliberate geometry, as if the mountain’s unfinished lesson in weather is being practised on a smaller, walking scale.

They notice you. The noticing is a tiny shift of alignment, the same almost imperceptible correction the buildings make as the young rock continues to rise. One of them will turn its featureless upper surface toward you and the light passing through it will cool by a fraction, the colour of late dusk arriving a little early. Then it will continue. They do not approach. They do not recede. The ceremonial openness of the bowl is left intact; emptiness remains the dominant material, and they are merely another expression of it.

At night, when the dripping slows and the stone listens to itself, they become harder to separate from the structures. Sometimes a walkway that was empty will have a second, parallel drip-line by morning, as if something stood there in perfect stillness while the ice formed around the idea of legs. Sometimes a tower that sheared a slab of rime will show, in the fresh break, a hollow the exact negative of a torso, already beginning to fill with new, reluctant water. They do not die. They un-precipitate. The filaments thin, the braids loosen, the weight returns to the seep or is carried upward again as glittering snow. The mountain has not withdrawn its consent; it has simply finished this particular sentence.

What remains is the same patient weeping, the same seventeen volumes plus spines, the same feeling that the place was never built so much as briefly allowed. You can walk the circuit again and meet no one. The pale bird planes across the rim and is gone. The only motion is water, ice, vapour, and the almost undetectable breathing of stone that has not yet learned it is supposed to be inanimate.

They will be there when the next uplift shoves a new pocket into the sky. Or they will be the pocket. The distinction, here, is not one the rock has ever needed to make.
