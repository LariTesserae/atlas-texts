The first thing that lives here is the interval itself—the measured pause between one heavy drop and the next, the half-second the mist keeps a sound after the stone has already forgotten it. That interval has weight. It occupies the empty air the great plants refuse to fill. It thickens in late afternoon when the bowl turns gold-green and the rock looks as if it could be bitten. Nothing with a heartbeat needs to announce itself yet. The place is already inhabited by its own patience.

Then the webbing stirs. Not quite mycelium, never quite root, it threads the peat in pale, slow silver that only shows when a frond is lifted and the sponge sighs. Signals move through it as changes in wetness, as a leaf on the far side of the bowl lifting a fraction because another has begun to flag. The webbing is older than the notches in the fluted rock. It remembers weather and hand as the same long abrasion. It does not think in images; it thinks in gradients of damp, in the faint electric taste of iron-tea water circulating in the deepest pit. If you stand still long enough your own ankles become part of its map.

Something uses that map. Not often, and never quickly. In the hanging garden—the cliff-face that looks like one organism wearing another—the liverworts part along a seam that was not there yesterday. A pale length withdraws, the exact color of wet limestone, segmented like a thought that decided to have joints. It is no larger than a forearm. It has no face you would recognize, only a cluster of moisture-sensitive pits that drink the mist and the sound of the falls at once. When the afternoon wind arrives and the rock becomes an instrument, the length stills completely, as if listening were a form of feeding. Then it is gone again, the garden settling its own weight over the absence. You could watch for a week and see this only twice. The third time you would understand it was not hiding. It simply has no reason to be continuous.

Farther out, nearer the firmer peat where knees once found a place and then forgot it, the catchment plant’s enormous leaves hold more than water. Each ribbed door-sized blade is a shallow lake that drains into the next, but in the angle where two leaves overlap there is sometimes a second, smaller shining—an eye that is also a droplet, held without evaporating. It belongs to a creature that has learned the tree-fern’s lesson: height is unnecessary if the world comes to you. The creature is mostly membrane and patience, folded like damp linen against the trunk of old leaf-bases. Its body takes the color of whatever it last touched: moss, iron stain, the faint blush the rock shows when it is almost dry. It does not walk. It is walked by the slow redistribution of its own water, a peristalsis so gradual the peat accepts it without the usual held-breath sound. Once in a season it unfolds a single pale frond of its own, not for sunlight but to taste the particular sweetness the flowering tree releases when one ashamed white blossom opens. That is the only haste it knows.

The flowering tree itself is not merely waited upon. Its bark the color of wet ash is warm to the palm even at night, holding the day’s ordinary light the way the stone holds warmth. Between its roots the webbing thickens into a knot that pulses on a cycle older than the wind’s daily visit. Something lives in that knot that is neither plant nor the silver threads: a density, a reluctant clump of memory that has forgotten whether it was once an offering or a root that lost its way. The twist of bleaching fiber in the cleft is its discarded skin, or its flag, or the last thing a vanished hand tied there so the place would remember a name. The tree opens its flowers one at a time so this density can finish tasting each before the next arrives. Seven blossoms in a year is conversation enough.

No birds come down because the air inside the bowl is already spoken for. The pale thumbnail crab is not a rumor; it is a scout, the same limestone color, lifting itself over moss rims to test whether the circulating water in the central pit has changed its mind. It reports by disappearing. Larger shapes in the hanging garden are usually the garden, but not always. Sometimes the garden is being rearranged by a will that prefers the cliff to remain one organism. The few insects that appear do not stay; they are metabolized into the pitch of the dripping, into the faint geometric stubbornness with which each great plant holds its territory of empty air.

What lives here has no use for startling you. It has already accepted the notches too clean to be weather, the lips too even, cut once with something harder than fingernails and left for a thousand wet seasons to belong. It lives in the profession of change: the channel that will be a finger’s-width deeper after you are gone, the leaf that yellows over a week and takes another week to let go, the mist that thickens and thins on a rhythm the wind cannot hurry. If there was ever a kneeler, a carver, a bringer of fiber, that one has been wept over until the distinction dissolved. What remains is the bowl’s own inhabitant—the slow silver argument that water will come to the surface, that time is the only plenty, that a room can be lush with almost no one in it and still be full.

Stand in the center long enough and the falls stitch you into the same cloth. Your breathing finds the heavier drops. The warmth comes up through the wet and down from the sky. You are not joined, only accompanied by the roar that has holes in it, by the chord that never resolves, by the waiting that is already loud. The place does not end. Neither does what lives there. It simply continues to weep, and to wait, and to be the interval that learned how to stay.
