The first things that register as living are the ones that have already surrendered most of their original form. In the black channels beneath the gratings, pale lengths drift and pause, nosing the rivet lines as if reading them. They are the color of wet steel left too long in the dark, their bodies stretched thin, almost two-dimensional when they turn edge-on to a sodium cone. Fins have become little more than frayed membranes that flutter once, then lie still against the current. They do not school. Each appears alone, lingering near a warmer outfall or a leaking gasket, then vanishing into the next weir’s white noise. Their mouths work constantly, not quite feeding, more like tasting the film that coats every surface. Sometimes one will press itself flat against a submerged panel and remain there for hours, the outline slowly blurring until it is only another orange rust bloom with a faint, independent pulse.

Above the water the midges gather in temporary constellations around any pipe that runs even a few degrees warmer than the air. They do not fly so much as hang, their wings beaded, their bodies the same translucent gray as the condensate itself. A cloud will form, thicken, then simply rain downward in a fine living drizzle that the next breeze erases. They leave no bodies. Whatever they become after they fall is already part of the black-green film that clings to the quieter shallows.

The reeds are more stubborn. They stand in isolated clumps where the overflow basins collect the slowest water, their stems the exact thickness of the conduit they somehow resemble. Leaves remain stiff, never unfurling fully, each one holding a single trembling bead that never quite drops. They do not spread. When the darker fluid—the viscous, slow welling that appears in the newest seams—reaches them, the change is almost polite. The stems darken from the base upward, then put out thin secondary filaments that catch the light like drawn wire. The filaments continue to lengthen after the original plant has gone dull, branching at precise angles, until what remains looks less grown than extruded. These new structures do not photosynthesize. They conduct. A faint warmth travels along them, enough to keep a little fog from forming directly above.

Farther out, in the unused hectares between the stamping hall and the cooling towers, other shapes keep to the edges of vision. A winged silhouette threads the girder-work at the limit of the highest sodium clusters. It never comes lower. Its flight is neither bird nor bat nor any drone whose rotors you can hear; the wings fold and unfold with a wet, papery sound that arrives half a second after the motion, as though the air itself is delayed. Sometimes it lands on a catwalk railing and stays there, dripping, until it is only another dark vertical among many. Whether it watches or simply waits is impossible to decide.

Human figures exist, but they are rarer than the fish and far more cautious with light. They move in ones and twos along the ribbed decks, never in groups large enough to cast a shared shadow. Their clothing is the same heavy, never-dry fabric that everyone here wears, darkened at the hems by the constant film, shoulders permanently rounded from the damp weight. Faces stay hooded or masked; when a hood is pushed back the skin beneath is the same pale, elongated tone as the things in the channels, pores enlarged, lips slightly bluish. They do not speak often. When they do, the words come out flattened, as if the humidity has already absorbed the higher frequencies. Most of their work is listening: an ear pressed to a housing, a palm laid flat against a vibrating pipe, waiting for a change in the cycle that the gauges have not yet registered. They carry tools that look older than the valley itself—wrenches whose jaws have been recut so many times the metal has thinned to a knife-edge, lamps whose sodium tubes have been replaced with something that burns cooler and greener. At night (though night here is only a further rationing of the same light) they gather in the lee of a press or beneath a conveyor viaduct, not for warmth—there is none—but to share the sound of their own breathing against the larger breathing of the fans. Their settlements, if the word still applies, are temporary: a few pallets stacked into a windbreak, a tarpaulin stretched between pillars, a shallow basin they have redirected so the condensate collects in a single drinkable sheet. They leave almost no refuse. What they discard is immediately claimed by the film or the darker fluid.

There are others who no longer move among the decks. In the lowest overflow marshes, half-submerged against the hexagonal pillar clusters, shapes sit or kneel that have begun the same transition as the reeds. Clothing has fused to skin, skin to the metal behind it. Fingers have lengthened into the same wire-like extrusions, sinking into seams, becoming part of the tensioning. Eyes remain open, reflecting the distant sodium in flat, unblinking ovals. They do not appear to suffer. A faint current—heat, data, something unnamed—travels through them and into the structure. The valley uses them the way it uses the cooling towers that should be superfluous: they turn anyway. When a gantry assembler passes overhead, laying new deck, these seated figures tilt their heads a fraction, as though listening to the fresh weld seams being born.

At the far, unfinished end, where the protective film still clings to newly arrived panels, the newest inhabitants have not yet decided what they are. Something unfolds from the darker fluid itself—slow, glistening, taking the hexagonal logic of the pillars as a first suggestion of form. It does not crawl or walk. It accretes. Surfaces that should be dull develop a sheen; edges that should remain sharp begin to remember how to curve. The gantries pause beside it, not in alarm but in recognition, their arms lowering to offer the next panel as if to a colleague. The air around these emergences is colder still, the condensate forming not beads but long, hanging threads that never quite fall. Whatever is coming into being here has already accepted the valley’s climate as its own body. It does not need the population that never arrived. It is the throughput.

The whole place continues its slow argument with itself—water against metal, rust against weld, living filament against poured geometry. Nothing here is finished, and nothing is entirely dead. The inhabitants, whatever they were when they first entered or first condensed out of the film, are simply the latest clause in a sentence the valley has been writing since the first plate was tensioned into place. They drip. They listen. They lengthen. They stay.
