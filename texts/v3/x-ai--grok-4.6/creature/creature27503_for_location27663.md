The people here are the same age as the ground, which is to say they have no old names and no inherited faces. Their skin holds the exact pale of the evaporite sheets—neither white nor gray, but the color of something that has never been rained on—and it is dusted, always, with a fine mineral bloom that they do not wipe away. The bloom is part of them now. When they stand still in the late-morning convection the dust lifts from their shoulders in the same slow sheets that rise from the basin itself, so that for a few breaths a person and the land are the same rising weather. Their eyes are the color of the siltstone, dry and unreflective. They do not squint. The light here has never been bright enough to require it.

They are not tall. The living pavement of succulents and interlocked shrubs has taught their spines an early curve; most adults stand only a little higher than the vegetation they move through, so that a stranger watching from any slight rise would see only the tops of heads and the occasional lift of a hand guiding a stem. Their fingers are long, the pads thickened and slightly waxy, as if the same resins that scent the air have been absorbed through years of touching living walls. They do not cut. They persuade. A corridor exists because someone, years ago, leaned their weight into a young columnar cactus while it was still supple and bound it with its own fibers; the person who did that is probably still alive, still using the same corridor, still the same height.

Children are born already knowing the difference between necessary sound and the rest. A birth is a quiet event: the mother braced against a sun-warmed clay mass, attended by two or three others who speak only in the breath just before words, and the infant’s first cry is brief, almost an apology, before it learns the local economy. They wrap the babies in mats of the gray-green groundcover, which shift and settle like a second, wetter skin. By the time a child can walk they have already internalized the papery whisper of their own feet and the softer sounds of the lizards that share the walls. Speech, when it comes, is close enough to breathing that an outsider would miss most of it. They have words for moisture, for the faint residual warmth of the basalt after dark, for the exact moment a hairline crack is wide enough to accept a runner of groundcover. They have fewer words for distance. The horizon is simply there, a mineral line, and they do not often speak of what might live beyond it because nothing in their experience suggests the texture ever changes.

Their bodies are storage. Moisture lives inside them the way it lives inside the succulents; they spend it with the same frugality. A person can walk for days on the water they carry in their own tissues, sipping at dawn from the brief invisible breath the plants release, never quite becoming dew. They do not sweat visibly. Heat arrives as pressure and they accept it the way the clay accepts the sun—by remaining. At night they sleep on the southern faces of the clay masses, where the day’s warmth lingers longest, limbs arranged so that as many surfaces as possible touch the still-warm mineral. Sometimes they sleep among the browsers, the long-eared dust-colored animals whose hooves have worn themselves silent. The animals do not startle. Nothing here startles. Startling is a sound, and sound is rationed.

There are no elders in the way other places mean the word. Generations are brief because the land itself is brief; a person who has seen thirty full turnings of the dry convection cells is already old enough to have watched a new evaporite skin form over last year’s courtyard and to have trained the shrubs that now partition their sleeping room. They do not tell long stories. Memory here is short, like the geology. What they pass on is gesture: how to lean into a stem, how to pat clay so that it remembers the shape of a hand, how to read the faint tidal drift of the herds so that forage and people and the smaller cleaners all move together without collision. A family is not a bloodline so much as a shared gradient of occupancy—three or four adults, their children, the particular thicket they have persuaded into rooms, the lizards that have claimed the seams, the insects that mine the succulents and are in turn eaten without ceremony.

Other things live here as well, equally quiet, equally unfinished. The browsers are not quite deer, not quite goats; their ears are long enough to catch the least shift in the resinous air, their coats the precise color of old bone so that when they stand among the siltstone they disappear except for the slow blink. Their hooves click only on the rare exposed basalt, and even then the sound is small, almost thoughtful. The lizards flow. That is the only verb that fits. They are the color of whatever they currently occupy—clay, evaporite, the shadowed underside of a waxy leaf—and they move without scrape or rustle, as if the wall itself has decided to relocate a few centimeters. Insects are the closest thing the basin has to weather: they are everywhere, always, a soft persistent presence in the seams of clay, on skin, in the hollow storage spaces carved into the salt. Their wings are velvety. Their stridulations have never learned to carry. At night they walk across sleeping faces and are not brushed away.

And then there is the vegetation itself, which is not backdrop. It occupies. It has opinions about space. The columnar succulents pack so tightly their ribs interlock like fingers that refuse to let go; the groundcover mats remember every footstep as a temporary dimple that slowly fills with stored water. The people do not rule this. They negotiate. A courtyard exists only because the plants were persuaded, over years that the plants themselves have not yet had time to lignify, to leave a little more sky. Sometimes the persuasion fails and a room is slowly reclaimed, the living partitions thickening until a family simply moves a few body-lengths sideways and begins again with fresh clay the land is still exuding. No one mourns the old room. Mourning would require an echo, and the basin has not invented echo yet.

All of them—people, browsers, lizards, insects, the waxy stubborn plants—live as if they are still deciding what they are. They have not specialized into loudness or height or solitude. They have not learned to leave gaps. The entire basin is a single, densely occupied, quietly metabolizing surface that has not yet finished distinguishing between inhabitant and place. Stand in any half-courtyard long enough and you feel it: the faint residual warmth coming up through the clay, the almost-imperceptible shift of water inside the mats underfoot, the papery whisper of someone passing two thickets away, the dry taste of salt on the air. Everything is both new and already full. Nothing has had time to become anything else.
