The highland is occupancy before it is landscape. What lives here does not arrive; it continues. The compressed body underfoot has never consented to be only ground, and so its motion has simply changed scale and register. Every tunnel is a throat. Every rasp is a joint remembering how to speak. The chew-note that never ceases is not background; it is the oldest conversation still being held, generations of xylophages the size of calves working the same galleries their parents opened, their mandibles keeping time with the hydraulic shout of rings still being added somewhere far below. They are not pests. They are peristalsis.

Above their drone the air is sawn into hymns. Stridulators stand on the hot bark at angles that look like prayer and turn the day’s heat into scraped sentences no one translates because translation would imply an outside. Their legs are rasps, their bodies tuned boxes; a single one can fill a shade-canyon with a note that makes the condensation beads tremble before they fall. When many answer, the plateau acquires a second wind, a dry singing that travels farther than any storm.

The flyers own the columns of rising air the way lungs own breath. They are hinged and chitinous first, winged only as a later concession. Some are large enough that their passing shadow is a movable room of cool you can walk three steps inside before the sun returns and the room is gone. When a flock banks, the sound is structural failure given voice—a cathedral door torn backward through its own hinges. They do not nest so much as dock, clamping to the hanging atria of the meristem-towers, drinking the private weather that condenses there, leaving behind the spines that people later work into awls. Their larvae are pale, heavy, and already armored; they drop into the cistern-galls and are born swimming.

Closer to the grain, the categories loosen. Bark-wolves move with the patience of erosion. Their jaws are adzes, their pelts the same weathered cork as the dunes they cross; they do not so much hunt as prune, taking the thread-deer that walk in single-file stitches so thin they look like the plateau mending itself. The deer’s necks are longer than sense, their bodies reduced to the minimum needed to carry eyes and a gut that can live on the fibrous dust and the occasional mobile fruit. They leave almost no tracks. The gall-otters taste of tannin and old rain. They slide through the swollen cisterns as if the liquor were their original element, surfacing only to blink and dive again, their fur permanently stained the color of steeped bark. People who drink from a gall an otter has used say the pulse against the palate is stronger afterward, as if the animal had taught the liquid a new rhythm.

Then there are the ones that fail the old names. Herds of photosynthesizers travel the open plate with green-black sails along their spines. At noon they stop as one body and stand with those sails spread, feeding on the thin hot light until their edges shimmer. They are meat again by late afternoon, wandering on, leaving the ground slightly cooler where they stood. Colonial mats flow like thought given shadow. They digest fallen chitin and the smaller dead, polishing the grain behind them until it shines like wet stone. They have no front. They simply arrive where hunger is.

And there are the things that fruit.

In the lee of a tower, after the rare self-made rain, a slope that was bare at dawn will erupt. What were buds yesterday have become bodies: legged, eyed, already tasting the air with mouths that were sepals hours ago. They graze the new wet, they copulate without ceremony, they are eaten or they join the roar. By evening the slope is grazed to the dark plate again. Some of them last a season. Some last an afternoon. The wood does not distinguish. It grows through the ones that fall, and the ones that fall were always only the wood’s way of briefly walking.

People are the minority weather, a thinner front that still changes the day.

They are few because the ground heals faster than they can mark it and because there is little to build with that is not already house and kin. They cut platforms into the inner walls of shade-canyons where the air still remembers wet; the cuts shine with old sap that never quite dries. Their ladders of lashed rib and vine sometimes lead nowhere—the tower they served having shifted in its sleep and sealed the door. They work resin until it hardens into adzes, fold the green copper the plants concentrate in certain galls (cold, patient folding, no fire until the last necessity), and they use flyer-spine for anything that must be both sharp and light. Fire is a relative they ask to visit briefly and then insist must leave. They have stories in which fire stayed too long and a seam screamed for a week, sap flashing to steam in confined galleries, a tower’s private weather dying in a column of black. They remember the sound the way other peoples remember a war.

They date by seams and swellings, not years. They walk the flats at noon when even the flyers are stunned and grounded. They wait for the photosynthesizers to finish their light. They never camp on a seam that is warm after dark. Water from a new gall is left until the pulse slows; too-new and it is hormone, and the dream that follows is the tower’s own—hunger arranged as endless landscape, rings adding themselves forever, no sky without a crown in it. They speak messages into the pale pith-valleys where sound has weight; a whisper travels a mile along the floor and arrives cooler, intact. Whether the wood carries the words or answers them is not a question they spend daylight on. The fauna uses the same valleys. The roar there has grammar.

They sleep in cavities smoked against the smaller tenants and wake with resin in their hair and a new, perfectly round hole in the wall, already occupied. They are not visitors. They are another way the body continues—hand and heat and the long apology of careful cuts. When they die the wood grows through them the same way it grows through everything else. Their tools remain a little longer: a cairn of spent blades, a rack of drying bladders, a carved sign half-swallowed so that a prayer becomes only another knot in the grain.

All of it—the chew, the scrape, the wing-thunder, the brief wet bodies that were flowers yesterday, the people moving like a slower weather—is the same fact wearing different speeds. The emptiness between towers is real. The fullness inside every tunnel and every shadow is real. They have had geological time to learn they are not opposites. What lives here is the highland still refusing to finish dying, and doing so with so many mouths that the refusal itself has become a country.
