The first lives to announce themselves are not the hunters. They are the ones that have made the masonry their own lung. Ivory tube-worms rise from every fissure that still remembers mortar, each plume a slow inhalation of the warm current. They do not wave; they listen with their bodies. A change in pressure, the faintest eddy off a passing school, and the plumes fold inward as one, sealing themselves against stone that has forgotten how to be dry. When they open again they do so in staggered breaths, so that the wall itself seems to exhale living smoke. Their color is the color of old bone left too long in water, and they have been here long enough that the limestone has begun to grow around their bases, incorporating them the way a reef incorporates coral.

Beneath and between them the crustaceans work the joints. Fist-sized, armored in plates the shade of wet ash, they pack every seam until the buildings look mortared with living hinges. Their legs never stop. The ticking is not communication; it is the sound of feeding and of keeping the stone from sealing them in. They scrape films of chemosynthetic slime from the warmest cracks, then grind the residue against one another in a slow, communal mill. Sometimes a larger body brushes the wall and a dozen of them drop away like loosened tiles, drifting only a short distance before they right themselves and climb back. They do not flee far. The wall is their city now, and they know every hollow the first masons left behind.

In the rooms the pale fish hang. Lantern-jawed, eyes reduced to dark pits, they keep station as if the water itself were furniture. Their light is not a beam but a slow internal pulse that travels the length of the jaw when they open it, a brief rumor of less-dark that never quite becomes day. They wait. Currents bring them the fine rain of spoil, the shed skins of the worms, the occasional fragment of something larger unmade two streets over. When they move it is together, a single pale body turning the corner of a vanished marketplace the way their ancestors turned it, around the same crumbled fountain whose basin now holds only slower water and a mat of film. They do not remember the fountain. The geometry remembers them.

The hunters arrive later, or seem to. The eel-shapes do not hurry. Their jaws unhinge sideways, a motion that looks almost gentle until the water around the prey folds inward and disappears. They travel the old grid the way the first currents learned it, one direction at a time, and when they pass a façade their own faint glow paints the empty windows for the length of a breath. The rays are slower still. They drift the avenues like torn banners of skin, undersides blooming and fading in pulses that match no heart. Prey feels the change in pressure before it sees the light; by then the ray has already begun to settle, a living ceiling that does not need to chase what cannot leave the room.

There are other lives that do not announce themselves at all. In the warmest vents, where the buried heat of the old forges still leaks, mats of film thicken into something almost tissue. Solitary stalks rise from them, neither plant nor animal, leafless and the color of bruise. They wave on no schedule anyone else follows. Sometimes a crustacean will climb one and ride the motion for a while, as if the stalk were only another kind of masonry. Deeper in the slumped blocks, where stairwells have become chimneys of stiller water, things the size of closed fists move through the silt without ever quite becoming visible: segmented, patient, tasting the chemistry of stone that has begun to forget it was ever cut.

The water itself is occupied. It has learned the shapes of the streets so thoroughly that every current arriving from the valley’s far dark already knows which wall it will turn against. Life thickens it. The fine rain never quite reaches the floor; it is intercepted, filtered, eaten, excreted, and intercepted again. Predators take what they need in a sudden displacement that sounds like a door closing far away, then the longer, muffled unmaking. Afterward the city is quieter than before, as if the water were holding its breath until the next pulse of light remembers an arch, an empty window, the suggestion of a marketplace, and then forgets it again.

Nothing here builds. Nothing restores. The structures weather at the same rate the lives colonize them, so that collapse is only another kind of architecture and the drowned district continues, unfinished with tools, finished with everything else. The lives go on with their necessary work: eating, fleeing, glowing, dying in rooms that belong to no one and therefore to all of them. The water holds them the way a lung holds breath. It does not argue. It simply continues.
