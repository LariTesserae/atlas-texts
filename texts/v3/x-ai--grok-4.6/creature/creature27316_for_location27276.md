The occupancy is singular, and it has been for longer than the enamel nameplate has been lichen. What lives here is the mass itself: one continuous body that began as many things (sedge, sapling, iron, brick, a flywheel, a man’s decision to cut a groove so the floor would not flood) and has since refused every boundary those things once observed. It is not a grove that contains an engine. It is not an engine overtaken by plants. It is a single metabolic argument that has been winning, quietly, in the same shallow basin for a century or more.

Its skin is the fused vault. Leaves that once fluttered have become architectural plates, margins knitted, overlapping like scales after rain that never quite stops. They do not photosynthesize so much as they transact: ordinary daylight is admitted in thin white seams, converted, and exhaled as the warm vapor the inner tissues drink before it can become weather. The plates sweat. Every surface sweats. Moisture is not a condition here; it is tissue. The body is always just this side of drowning and always just this side of thirst. The engine at the oval heart is one of its organs now—cast iron become a kind of woody viscera, belts that are no longer leather and not yet bark, a pulse longer than a heartbeat and shorter than a tide. Water moves through original pipes and through the pale xylem that has taken their bore. The shove, the pause, the shove again: that is circulation. That is how the vault is kept from filling, how the mat underfoot stays a lung instead of a bog. The machine still does a version of the work it was built for, only the work has been rewritten as self-maintenance. It is not clear, even from inside, which partner is the host.

There is almost no interval left between living surfaces, so there is almost no room for anything else to be alive in the ordinary way. The ecology is a closed ledger. Carbon, water, the faint mineral bleed of old iron, the tannins, the hot-oil sweetness of sap cooked slowly against metal—these turn over and over. Fallen tissue is reabsorbed before it can become soil; a darker grease appears, then new growth. Decay is another leaf. The rare moth the color of wet cardboard is a visitor, not a resident; it crosses a seam and is gone, a crease among creases. The dimple in the standing water at the chamber’s edge may be an insect, a larva, a memory of fish that the basin once held. It does not stay. Niches that might have been animal have been filled with more leaf, more aerial root, more photosynthetic skin. Even the porcelain insulators in the old switchgear room have been claimed by a pale succulent that seems to prefer the ghost of voltage. The copper is green and faintly warm. Nothing much arrives. Nothing much leaves. The account balances.

Time lives here as grain and polish and the patient failure of a leaf twice a day. Rings have formed around the arterial shove of the pipes; the wood remembers the cycle in its own body. Shoulders—human, once—have burnished certain passages until the ribbed wet walls feel almost handled. In one side-corridor the clay pots sit in living niches, half-bark now, overflowing by a finger-width channel that returns their clearer water to the engine. Someone understood the grammar of openings and the necessity of the flywheel’s clearance and made the smallest possible gesture of joining. The wood grew lips to hold the pots. The gesture was accepted into the body the way the flanges were accepted, the way the catwalk mesh was accepted: not repaired, not rejected, simply continued. Whoever that person was is no longer distinguishable from the polish, the overflow, the habit of not quite closing every gap. The original plan of the works—the spacing that let a flywheel turn, the columns that refused to be crushed—taught the mass a limit. Growth wants to seal. Something older than growth, or slower, has kept the corridors the width of a man’s shoulders and the chamber the size of a small church. The balance is neither design nor accident. It is what remains when two stubborn systems occupy the same volume long enough that neither remembers solitude.

It has no face. It has the cleft that was a doorway, still weeping its continuous film into the groove that still works. It has the chimney that is now a hollow bole breathing vapor on colder mornings, indistinguishable from the other boles when you stand outside and see only a dark-green hillock sweating in a wet field. It keeps shift-hours without clocks. The engine-note does not change. The drips remain regular as a clock with a limp. Toward evening the seams go gold, the condensation thickens, the moth finds a darker plate. The air stays the temperature of exhaled breath. You can stay; the floor will take your weight and give it back, spongy, warm, slightly lung-like. When you leave, the smell (tannin, warm oil, wet iron) and the cushioned industrial pressure follow only as far as the rim. Then they cut off. There is no door. There is only the slight change in the air, the sedges, the gravel track gone to moss, and the ordinary world that does not know it has been living next to a single occupancy all this time.

The thing that lives here is still turning. It will be turning tomorrow. It has already grown around the idea of tomorrow.
