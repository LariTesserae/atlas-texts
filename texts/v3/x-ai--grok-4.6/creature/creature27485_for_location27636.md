The people of the bowl move as the seeps do: not in streams but in patient, darkening stains that collect, are used, and sink again. They are small-boned, compact, built for the cool that never quite becomes cold and the slopes that still remember they were once elsewhere. Their skin holds the same damp gray-green as the cloud-light; it does not tan, only weathers, taking on a faint mineral sheen where it is most often wet. Hair is worn long and bound with living lichen or the pale fiber they twist from fern-pith; it never quite dries. Eyes are the color of the basins after rain—shallow, reflective, always watching the rim.

They do not speak often. When they do, the words stay close, already half-swallowed by the air that prefers not to carry them. Their language is full of verbs that mean both “to gather” and “to wait,” nouns that name a plant only after it has been cropped and begun to unfurl again. Children learn first by touch: the exact pressure of a dew-cup against the lip, the grain of a newly shed flake that will make a good cutting edge, the way a browser’s flank cools the air a moment before the animal itself arrives. They play in the wide intervals between houses the same way the moths glide—silent, spaced, never colliding.

Their work is the work of fitting. They stack the pale angular stone the cliffs keep offering, leaving gaps so the mist can still decide to become water on the lee side. They plant the extravagant greens in shallow terraces that follow the seeps rather than fight them, pruning with the same light mouths the browsers use: never stripping, always leaving enough for the vegetal haste that answers. In the covered workshops they work wood that still weeps when cut, bone that remembers the long-legged gait, occasional copper beaten thin as a moth-wing and used for the small, ceremonial basins they set at the lowest point. They do not name what those basins catch besides water. They only know the light is dimmest and greenest there, and that the herds pause longest, and that if you stand still the whole bowl begins to breathe through you.

The browsers are the town’s other citizens. Pale, long-legged, their coats the exact shade of used light, they pass in a continuous unhurried flow that has nowhere else to go and therefore belongs exactly here. They do not bunch; they keep the same sparse interval as the giant ferns and the houses. A single animal cropping a lower frond is never alone—there are always more coming over the western lip, always more disappearing east, so that the bowl feels less like a pasture than a throat they have chosen to keep open. Their hooves make almost no sound on the wet stone; you feel them first as a change in coolness against the skin. They drink from the same seeps the people use, never competing, as if the water itself has already decided the shares.

Smaller lives move with them and between them. The moths—hand-sized, silent, gliding rather than fluttering—ride the same air that thickens sometimes into translucent pulses that may be insects or may be the mist remembering it once had other shapes. Ground-crawlers the color and texture of flaking cliff-stone wander the open ground like pieces of the mountain that have grown restless and decided to walk. Occasionally something larger, moss-grown across the shoulders, pauses at a seep and drinks with the slow patience of stone that has not yet finished becoming stone. None of them startle. None of them call. They simply continue, as the cliffs continue to shed, as the dew continues to form, as the ground continues to settle with small ticks and sighs.

At the lowest point the people have made their largest catchment, a series of interlinked basins that look almost ceremonial. Here the plants lean closer, the stone is darker, the light greenest. The browsers linger. The people linger. No one has said what else is being caught. After a time you understand it is the motion itself: the arriving and the departing, the condensing and the sinking, the pruning and the unfurling. The bowl is still new enough that everything in it is still arriving, including the ones who live there. They have not tried to own it. They have only learned how to live inside its breathing, leaving the lanes wide, the walls unmortared, the quiet full of everything that has chosen not to make a sound.
