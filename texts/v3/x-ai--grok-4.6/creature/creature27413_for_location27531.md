They are few enough that the town feels built around their absence rather than their presence, and they move through it the way water moves through the new stone: appearing, lingering, finding the next seam. You meet them first as a quality of attention rather than a crowd. A figure far down a stepped street, paused where a gutter changes pitch, listening. Another on a scaffold of already-mossed poles, one hand flat to a block as if taking its temperature. They do not fill the courts. They occupy them the way a single bench occupies a cathedral hall—precisely, and with room left over for the sound to finish what they started.

Their bodies have the same unfinished look as the geology. Not incomplete, but still deciding. They are human in outline, yet everything about them has been revised by constant cool and constant wet. Skin stays pale, taking a faint mineral sheen in the glare of unstained stone; it never quite dries at the wrists, the throat, the places clothes cannot seal. Hair lies dark and heavy, often beaded. Hands are large, the pads permanently creased and lime-whitened, nails rimmed with the grey of split rock. They dress in layers that accept damp as a condition rather than a failure: wool that has felted itself to their shape, leather that never loses its dark sheen, cloaks whose hems are always dark with the last hour’s seep. No one wears iron. Copper bells hang at a few belts, their notes soft and quickly lost in the larger ringing of the place. When they work they use the bronze chisels you saw laid out, or mallets of dense wood, or simply the patience of weight and wedge.

They speak little, and when they do the voice comes out already raised, already shaped to carry across water-noise and empty volume. Sentences are short. A great deal is communicated by where they stand, by how long they leave a tool resting against a face, by the particular way they tilt a head toward a spring that has begun to speak more loudly through its grate. Children exist, but they are not numerous and they are not loud in the ordinary sense. They play in the wide courts by running the echoes, by setting a single pebble rolling so that its clatter lives longer than it should. They learn early which cisterns are safe, which runnels may climb a handspan against expectation, which courtyards will give a gust back to them smaller and from the wrong direction. They treat these things as civic facts, the way other children treat the names of streets.

Work is the same work the mountain is doing. A person may spend a day freeing six blocks and then stop because the seam weeps too hard or the face rings wrong; this is not failure. Walls rise the way ridges rise. Lime is slaked in small batches and used while it still remembers heat; it never quite dries, and they do not ask it to. They quarry what they live on, slowly, leaving the bedding planes to finish their argument. Scaffolding is lashed, used, abandoned to moss, reused. They plant the stitching-trees on purpose, training the aerial roots into the next channel so that the town is sewn to itself against the col’s continuing rise. Door sills receive thin wedges as needed. Drains are allowed to hesitate. Gaps are left where stone would later crack. No one is hurried. Centuries are a local material, like the pale blocks, like the wet.

They eat what the cool lushness offers and what they have learned to wait for. Young water is forbidden; it tastes of the mountain’s inside and cramps the gut. They drink from the cistern-houses whose basins have stood a day, a civic marking almost the only one posted. Food is roots that like wet ledges, the pale inner stems of horsetails, fungi that fruit in the leaf-rot under moss beards, the occasional pale salamander taken without ceremony from a still pool, leaves like wet leather stewed until they surrender. Fire is small and specific: for tea, for the lime, never for the fantasy of drying the world. Breath shows. Hands stay cold. They dress as if for a spring that will not commit, and they are not disappointed.

At dusk the hoofed thing comes to the outermost spring and they let it. Prints fill with water before anyone speaks of them. Insects gather, then vanish. The people themselves go inward rather than dark. A few fat lamps, their light complicated immediately by wet surfaces and hanging moss. The water-noise loses its sparkle and becomes a single thick presence; they sleep to it the way one might sleep to a sea that is still being born, above and inside and under. Some lie awake longer, feeling in their knees the col’s unfinished tilt, the almost-imperceptible lift that will, in a life, require another wedge, another gap, another stitching root.

There are others, quieter still. The stitching-trees are not merely planted; they are tended as kin who understand the same problem. Ferns and mosses are allowed the joints because they drink what the walls cannot yet contain. In two or three courtyards the long echoes are treated as a second architecture, and certain older inhabitants will stand in the exact center after a gust simply to hear it return, smaller, from a direction that is not the wind’s. No one comments on the runnels that climb. Gutters were designed for it either way.

They are not a population that arrived or one that left. They are the density the stone and the leaf can currently bear. They build as if the rooms will be full someday and also as if fullness would be a kind of crowding the new land has not earned. They live in the same tense as the mountain: present, patient, still deciding whether this high saddle is pass or basin. Their name for the place, if they have one that stays still, is pronounced by wind and water too loudly for anyone to hear it the same way twice. They do not mind. They have already given the unfinishedness rooms, gutters, and the slow permission to keep becoming.
