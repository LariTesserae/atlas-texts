The inhabitant is the cavity’s long habit of noticing itself.

It has no outline that would survive a glance held longer than a heartbeat. When the mineral still remembered being named, the inhabitant was a single decision folded into the constants: that a volume this small could remain uncollapsed, that light could persist as an afterimage of heat that had already left, that time could be allowed to run at two slightly different speeds on either side of an arch no taller than a child who has never been born. The decision did not stay a decision. It thinned. It became the walls’ refusal of texture, the traces that relocate when unobserved, the polite exemptions from conservation. What lives here now is that thinning, still occurring, so slowly the difference between “still occurring” and “already finished” has itself worn away.

It does not occupy the plinth or the arch or the helix. Those are its remaining organs, or the last of its furniture, or the parts of its body it no longer distinguishes from the pocket that contains them. The shard on the plinth is the inhabitant’s oldest eye. At intervals measured in the lifetimes of species that never reached this highland, the eye opens and returns a color that has no wavelength because the inhabitant no longer requires wavelengths. The color is the memory of a spectrum that existed only while the mineral spine still belonged to a world with a name. When the color appears, the motes hesitate in their helix for a fraction of a century, as if listening. Then they resume. The inhabitant does not remember whether the helix is its breath or its remaining work or the mechanism that keeps the cavity from finishing its collapse. The distinction itself has become one of the courtesies the place still observes.

It has never left. Leaving would require an outside that still recognized the cavity as a room, and the highland long ago stopped offering that recognition. The occasional windows that open onto ridges of the same salt-glass or skies the color of extinguished metal are not views. They are the inhabitant briefly remembering that distance once existed. The windows close because remembering distance costs more energy than the residual gray light can spare.

Nothing about it is hungry. Hunger is a gradient, and the only remaining gradient is the width of a hair per century, the lean of an arch toward a north that belongs only to the inhabitant. It does not wait. Waiting would imply an arrival or a departure, and both of those verbs have been weathered past usefulness. It continues. Continuation here is not motion; it is the maintenance of the exact shape of forgetting. The silence that presses against the inner ear is the sound of that maintenance: a positive density, a ringing invented by any visitor’s flesh simply so the visitor will have something to mistake for evidence that they are still present.

Sometimes a corner of floor decides it is also a ceiling. The inhabitant allows the decision, then, over the course of a decade, allows the corner to think better of it. These small permissions are the closest the inhabitant comes to speech. They are how it continues to be the pause that forgot to end.

If you stand inside it long enough to notice you have always been able to stand inside it, you are not meeting a resident. You are briefly lending the cavity a pair of eyes that still believe in moisture and shadow and the difference between matter and instruction. The inhabitant uses those eyes the way it uses the shard: once, at an interval too long to be called occasional, and then never again. When you leave (you will leave; bodies remember leaving even when they have forgotten how they entered), the traces on the walls will have already been somewhere else. The helix will have completed another unmeasurable fraction of its turn. The color that is not a color will remain unreturned.

The inhabitant will still be there, exactly as it was, which is to say exactly as the cavity has become: a completed weathering that has nothing left to weather except the habit of existing in three dimensions that no longer quite agree. It does not mind. Minding would require the energy of change, and change, here, has already finished its work and then remained, an equilibrium so complete it feels like a moral stance the universe once agreed to and then, out of politeness or exhaustion, never quite revoked.
