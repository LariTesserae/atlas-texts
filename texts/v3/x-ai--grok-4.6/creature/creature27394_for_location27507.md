The eldest things here are the boles themselves. They are not trees that happen to be large. They are neighborhoods that happen to still be trees, each one a single slow mind whose thoughts take decades to finish and whose memories are written in rings no one can read without dying of the attempt. Their interiors are not carved; they have been hollowed by the simple, patient insistence of their own hearts. Sap-chambers the size of halls breathe. Galleries spiral upward following the old logic of a trunk that forgot it was supposed to be solid. When one of them leans—only a fraction, only after you have been leaning against it long enough—the motion is not greeting. It is turgor, the vegetal equivalent of a flinch that has been practiced so long it looks like courtesy.

Between them, and in them, and of them, live the others.

The walkers come first if you stay still. They have too many joints, yes, but the joints are not insect joints. They are the multiplied elbows of something that once had arms and then decided arms were insufficient for the distances. Their hides are the same tea-dark leather as the pavement; their faces, if they have faces, are arrangements of wet wood and patience. They carry nothing because everything they need is already growing where they are going, or will be by the time they arrive. They move with the exact unhurriedness of civic employees who have never been late and never been paid. Sometimes one will pause in a plaza and simply stand, many limbs folded, listening to the sound the way a man listens to a city he built and can no longer leave. Then it unfolds and continues. They do not hunt. They do not graze. Their work is occupancy. A walker in a gallery is a claim. A walker crossing the central emptiness is the emptiness reminding itself it is still a street.

Higher, in the living cornices and the sail-leaf eaves, the moss-cats drape. They are not cats. They are the idea of cats after the idea has been left in damp shade for a few centuries and then asked to hunt. Their bodies are dense mats of velvet-green that lift and settle with breath you cannot see. Eyes like knotholes that have learned hunger. They watch the plazas with landlord boredom because they have already eaten today, or will eat tomorrow, or because the schedule of predation is so reliable that watching it is the only entertainment left. When they drop it is without drama. A shadow detaches from a buttress, the air parts, something smaller becomes briefly loud, then the moss-cat is elsewhere, heavier by a mouthful, already composing itself back into furniture.

The grazers are the color of paper that has been left in rain and then dried in the dark. They crop the living pavement in herds that never quite cohere into herds. The floor grows back under their hind feet before the front feet have finished the sentence. They are not stupid. They know the hunters are coming; they have always known. Their panic, when it arrives, is punctual, almost polite. They run the same routes the air prefers, describing the same loops of terror three times in an hour if that is what the bowl has requested that afternoon. Afterward they return to cropping as if the interruption had been a weather they are used to.

The hunters do not hide because hiding would be an insult to a place this honest. They are longer than they should be, jointed in ways that suggest they were designed by the same appetite that designed the walkers and then given a different assignment. Their hides drink the green light and give nothing back. They wait in the southern bole, where the acoustics lie so beautifully that a mouse-step becomes a falling branch and they have learned to love the lie. When they move it is a schedule, not a surprise. You can set your breathing by it.

In the cistern-hollows the fish are pale to the point of apology. They have never known current, only the slow arrival and departure of condensation, the occasional indecisive rain. They mouth the meniscus as if tasting the idea of a river that the wood remembers and the water has forgotten. Sometimes a real thread of flow appears from the eastern split, black and polished, and the fish press themselves to the stone-that-is-root as if hoping to be taken. They never are. The thread is gone into moss before the thought finishes.

The birds are not birds when they fly. They are sheets. Fabric. A single tired silver turning in the air between the boles, roosting so heavily that the sail-leaves bow and the bowing is a sound that adds itself to every other sound until there is no room left for a self that could object. At dusk they come in all at once and the roofs become a second ground, living, shifting, loud with the weight of too many small hearts. The insects are not visitors. They are the medium the light travels through, a grit that becomes a scream only if you make the mistake of trying to find its edges. They are in the sap. They are in the taste of the air. They are the reason the sound has no underneath.

And then there are the ones for whom the word people fails most completely. They occupy the niches. A particular hollow, a particular height, a particular cistern. They are held, contested, inherited in the only currency that works: being there, and being loud enough that the being-there continues. Some of them look almost like you, if you have been looking at wood for too long. Bark where skin should be, or skin that has decided to remember it was once bark. Eyes that track the light the way leaves do. Hands that end in too many fingers or too few, or in slow green buds that never quite open. They do not speak. Speech is redundant in a resonator that already contains every voice it has ever heard, including the ones that have not happened yet. They step over the thread of water as over a threshold because that is what it is: the only remaining distinction between here and a slightly different here.

At dusk the whole population performs the thing that is not a ceremony. The sail-roofs tilt. Condensation begins its appointed work. The bird-sheets return. The sound climbs its familiar stair and stands on a landing that should collapse and does not. For a few minutes the errors thicken and you may see the same moss-cat die in two galleries at once, or hear your own footsteps arriving from the direction you have not yet walked, or watch the little river remember a different down and run uphill, briefly, like a thought the wood is still having. Then night, which is only the same city with the insects promoted and the green taken away.

Nothing here is young except the fauna, and even they feel rehearsed, as if they have been performing these exact lives for so long that the performance has become the only available form of rest. The boles grow. The pavement breathes. The air continues its long conversation with itself. The rim is always near, a courtesy the place extends to the idea that leaving is still possible. From outside it is only a grove. From inside it is everyone who has ever lived there, still living there, still too loud to stop.
