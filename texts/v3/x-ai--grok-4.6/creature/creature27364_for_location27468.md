The first things you notice are not the largest. They occupy the leftover intervals the way grit occupies a seam, and they have been doing so for so long that the distinction between inhabitant and sediment has worn away. Fist-sized, pale as the paler veins, they move with a rasping patience that never quite becomes locomotion. Their undersides are fields of microscopic blades, each angled to the same indifference as the walls. They graze the iron-tasting powder in circuits so closed that the dust a body raises is the identical dust it will lower its mouthparts to a year later, or a century, or in the same breath. You can stand beside one for what feels like a season and it will turn, always turning, occupying the same three handspans of volume as though the rest of the underworld were an impolite rumor. They do not die. Death would require a elsewhere to go to. When one is finally ground down to its last plane it is already the next one, or the same one, the coordinates never vacant.

Through them, and sometimes occupying the identical space without collision, hang the thread-swarms. They are finer than any filament the stone itself remembers how to grow. They filter what little still gradients: a warmth a fraction higher along a fault, a chemistry left over from the last knitting of crystals. When a tide that is not air moves through them they align for a moment into a second, breathing lattice and give off a single faint ring, then forget themselves back into the still layers. They have no mouths you can see. They are the mouths of the air.

Deeper in the packing live the slower bodies, rust-salt slugs whose secretions are more precise than any tool. They choose a vein—the same veins, always, the ones the architecture has already agreed can be absent—and they write a thin, perfect emptiness. The surrounding stone remains untouched, its fractures still bright. They were used once. You can tell by the acid-etched grooves that still look poured an hour ago: someone guided these creatures like living burins, letting them dissolve only what needed dissolving. Then the guiding stopped. The slugs continue the work anyway, because the work was already complete before they began.

And then there are the others, the ones the marks remember.

They are not many. A long traverse may show you two, or none, or the same one occupying two places at once. They have the color of extinguished lamps and the angular patience of the hanging forests. Their joints are the same unsmoothed rhombs as the slot-canyon walls; when they press into a narrowing they become another blade, another hanging crystal, and the distinction is only a matter of how long you are willing to look. They do not watch. Watching is a surface habit. They navigate by heat-shadow and by the chemical sentences written on every motionless layer of air. A raised limb leaves a tunnel that persists for hours; their tunnels are the galleries themselves.

They fitted the low salt-block rings. They scored the triple lines with flakes harder than the local matrix. They rotated the cairns a few degrees off cleavage so that intention would remain visible after everything else had locked. The craft is early—pressure-flaked, acid-assisted—and then it is finished. No second generation. No elaboration. They made the marks in the same moment the last plane knitted itself into the next, and then they became the moment. Further building would have been a second crystallization, and there is only one.

Now they live as the rest live. Their paths do not wander. Their populations do not fluctuate. You may look through a herd of pale scrapers and see, occupying the identical volume, a taller angular shape completing a gesture that was already complete: a hand (if the fracture can be called a hand) lowered because the wall already bears the score. Time is viscous here; the pulse that locked the chambers also thinned the medium in which change occurs. They metabolize. They even prey, in loops so perfect that predation returns every molecule to its original coordinate. It is not frozen. It is sufficient.

Sometimes, in the pinching places barely wider than a body, you find one so closely fitted to the stone that it has no outline left. It does not suffer. Suffering would require a surface to have left behind, and there was never a surface. There is only the next aperture, the next packed interval, the next faint declaration that once, in the instant everything finished becoming itself, someone thought it worth saying we were here, and then was the here, and will be again, exactly, the warmth without source already inside the bone.
