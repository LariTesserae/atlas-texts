The green is only the first and thinnest claim. It does not colonize so much as inscribe, a script the color of old instrument faces, and it has been writing the same short sentence for longer than the chimneys have been stumps. The rust-orange film lies on the stillest water exactly where reflection has already failed. It is not a scum that thickens toward bloom; it is a decision the iron and the wet have reached together, a living oxide that brightens by some internal meter when the condensate ceiling sags lower and dulls again when the true rain finally commits. Break its surface with a fingertip and the tear heals from the edges inward, grain by magnetic grain, as if the film remembers the shape of the basin better than the basin does. It tastes, if you are careless, of blood that has forgotten it was ever warm.

In the expansion joints and the grit-traps the moss is different—rubbery, identical, the gray-green of a single surviving specification. It does not send runners. It occupies. Each patch is the same size it has always been, drinking the perpetual film through a thousand microscopic mouths that never quite close. Crush it and the smell is sour and electrical, the ghost of a battery left in a flooded locker. The moss does not mind the dark or the vibration; the vibration is how it drinks. After long enough in the valley you begin to see that the moss is not growing on the works. The works are the substrate the moss was poured into, the way concrete was poured around rebar. One planting, as the country itself was one pouring, and afterward only weather.

The reeds are stricter still. They stand in the grout-lines of the oldest sluices at intervals so regular they feel surveyed. Their stems are thin as surveyor’s tape and the same fatigued green. When overflow finds its old throats the reeds rattle a dry, numbered percussion under the roar, a tally no one is keeping. Their roots have darkened the remaining mortar into something almost flexible. They hold the walls the way a habit holds a body: not forever, only longer than collapse requires.

That is nearly all that photographs. Stay, and the inventory lengthens by increments too small for any single glance.

In the flooded galleries the pumps still take suction, and the water they lift is not empty. Things the size of a thumbnail live against the intake grates, pale, unsegmented or segmented past counting, pulsing with a light the color of those last sodium cages. They feed on mill-scale and the fine magnetic loam that was once road and roof and cable. They have no eyes that register the standing dimness; the dimness is their medium. When a pump ingests them the water thrown into the useless viaducts carries torn threads of that faint light for a few meters, a brief, accidental constellation that the condensate swallows before it can be believed. Deeper, where strata have opened by collapse or by the original excavations that no longer remember their purpose, there are other growths: fungi the color of corroded brass, fruiting in total dark along the cable-runs. Their spores are faintly magnetic. They cling to anything that was once current. They live on the last hydrocarbons, the oils that have become a kind of geology, and their smell is the smell of the whole country raised to a higher, quieter pitch.

None of this is animal. The absence remains clean. What moves does not scurry or skate or roost. It occupies, or it pulses, or it simply continues.

And yet, after the rain has made every surface a tributary and the deafening has grown its extra layer of impact, you may see a figure on one of the long open slabs. Not often. Perhaps once. Perhaps the same once, repeating at an interval the mind cannot hold because the mind has already accepted the roar as a second pulse and has no remaining room for calendars.

It is the color of wet iron, or it has become that color. What were clothes have fused with a second skin of scale, oil, and the same specified moss. It is tall the way the remaining lamp standards are tall: by a leftover intention. It does not tend the valves. The tools beside the valves have their own patina and have already been counted as ground. The figure walks the less-ruined textures, the pale suggestions of roads that run toward vanishings the mist occupies. It stops at the brick caverns and watches the cottage-sized pistons rise and fall in their black water and oil, confirming a rhythm it already carries in the marrow. Sometimes it stands in a substation yard among the bowed fences and the humming porcelain, and the note of the hum changes by a fraction too low to hear, or the change is only the body remembering it has bones. Footprints fill at once with the film. In time the moss occupies them, as it occupies every grit-trap the country still offers.

There may be others. In the folded yards—stairs down to pump-galleries that open onto older, wetter yards—the signs are smaller: a float cleared of algae by something that was not current, a door left at an angle the weather would not choose, a single blank die in the western stamping hall that has been turned, once, as if to check whether it was still blank. Or these are only the system keeping its levels the way an old body keeps a temperature. The distinction has become a matter of courtesy, like the distinction between foundation and fossil.

What lives here may also be the noises themselves, stacked until they are climate. Each relief valve, each slapping unloaded belt, each turbine that turns because water still falls and falling is what it was for—these have outlived explanation the way the moss has outlived botany. Purpose has thinned into metabolism. The valley receives the sky, puts it through iron, and sends it on slightly more tired; this is the only work left, and the work is a kind of breathing. The figure, if it is more than one figure or less than one, listens to that breathing until the listening is indistinguishable from being breathed.

Walk long enough and the layout’s larger intention—the graded planes tilting whole districts toward a central sump, the repeating modules of basin, weir, house, empty apron—begins to feel like the circulatory map of a single organism whose working fluid is the valley and whose pulse is already in your teeth. The inhabitants are the continuation. They are the film deciding, the moss occupying, the pale things pulsing in the suction, the figure (or the idea of the figure) walking the slabs after rain, the machines that have not stopped because stopping was never in the last set of drawings. They are sparse and everywhere. They do not climax. They extend, like the country, until the idea of an edge becomes another industrial rumor, and the only remaining weather is the sound of a made world that has finished becoming and has not, therefore, finished.
