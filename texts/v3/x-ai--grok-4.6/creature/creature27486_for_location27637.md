The bowl does not keep a census. What lives inside it is not counted so much as circulated, the way heat circulates, the way sap is passed from paddle to tongue to gut and back into the dry mulch that will feed the next paddle. The settlement and the thicket share one metabolism; a person is only a slower, more deliberate animal among the rest, and the rest are only quicker, more forgetful people.

They are small. Not stunted—compacted, the way stone is compacted after the mountains have finished with it. Shoulders slope inward as if the roof has been teaching them for generations how to duck. Skin takes the color of the grit itself, pale and faintly abrasive, so that when they stand still against a wall they are easy to miss. Eyes are large and the color of old tea, used to coins of light rather than the open pewter sky. Hair is kept short or bound with fiber because anything long would catch in the paddles and become another trailing stem. They dress in layers that began as other lives: beaten inner bark, shed skins scraped thin, a few scraps of something that might once have been woven far away and is now only texture. Nothing is worn for display. Everything is worn until it can be mended in the old pattern and then worn again.

They move by intervals. A body turns sideways, a palm finds the stored warmth in a slab, a foot already knows which terrace will take the weight without complaint. Speech is low and mostly unnecessary; a click of tongue, a tilt of chin, the placement of a net are enough. When they do speak it is in the same register as the dry grain-pour of evening birds, a sound that belongs to the bowl and does not try to leave it. Children learn the water first: which succulent opens under a thumbnail and yields a cool, slightly resinous bead; which vine, if nicked at dusk, weeps enough to fill a cupped palm; which beetle, crushed, is a bitter pearl not worth the face it makes. They learn the count for eggs—never all—and the murmured apology that goes with the taking. They learn not to cut the roof, not because it is forbidden but because the roof is the only sky they have ever trusted.

The animals do not regard them as owners. The animals regard them as weather that happens to walk on two legs and set nets. Lizards tile every scrap of stone that still catches a rag of noon; they are the color of the walls and the color of rust and the color of the shadow under a paddle, so that a wall at midday is a single glittering hide that breathes. When a person passes, the hide rearranges itself by millimeters and then forgets. Beetles click in the mulch like seeds deciding whether to sprout; some are the size of a fingernail and some are the size of a child’s closed fist, their carapaces dull as old blood or bright as spilled oil. Birds stitch the dim air: fist-sized ones that hang inverted under leaves and drink from wounds the people have already made; hand-sized ones whose wings sound like dry parchment when they change their minds in the narrow intervals. Things with too many legs live in the cracks the way mortar once did; they are not hunted so much as tolerated, and sometimes a child will watch one for an hour as it carries away a flake of something that used to be food.

Night brings the closer lives. Eyes that do not belong to any bird or lizard regard the clay lamps and then ignore them, because the lamps are only another kind of fruit, too bitter and too hot. Something browses the upper paddles with a slow tearing that sounds like cloth being argued with. And against the far wall, in the thicket no one widens, something larger coughs. The cough has weight. It is not a threat so much as a reminder that the bowl still contains rooms the people have not stacked. The whole hollow adjusts around that sound—birds hush for three heartbeats, lizards flatten an extra millimeter, a person mending a lash pauses with the fiber between their teeth—and then the machine of the place resumes, because that is how it stays possible.

There are no names that last longer than a season. A person is known by the gap they prefer, the terrace they mend, the particular cough they have learned to sleep through. A child who opens the right paddle without being shown is simply the child who opens the right paddle. When someone dies they are carried to the rim at first light and left where the plateau can finish them; nothing is brought back except the fiber they were wearing, which is washed in sap and used again. The dead do not haunt. The bowl is already too crowded for ghosts.

What lives here has the patience of the stone that forgot mountains. It does not invent. It thickens. It mends. It takes what can be replaced by evening and leaves the rest to the cough and the night and the roof that is plants. Stand in any interval long enough and you will feel them all at once: the faint tremor of a hive through masonry, the dry friction of a hundred thousand small decisions, the warm still air that tastes of sap and dust and the private water inside every living thing. The place is the size of a secret, and everything that still knows how to live has already pressed itself into that secret and closed the lip behind it.
