I came down the cleft because the smell of broken stone was already in my lungs, as if I had been exhaled by the plateau the same season it sheared. The plates were still sharp enough to remember the fracture; they caught at whatever I was wearing—skin, or the idea of skin—and left thin white lines that immediately beaded with the same water that sheened every surface. I did not decide to stay. The basin simply continued around me until the ordinary surface of the world was a rumor overhead, a thing that happened to other stone.

At first I was only the cold and the wet working through me. Breath stayed visible. The meres accepted my weight the way they accept a heron’s, which is to say they barely noticed. I stood in one of the still plazas while springtails rewrote the surface film around my ankles and a newt regarded me with the patience of glass. The dripping began to map itself: a slow architecture of sensation across whatever I used for shoulders, a second set of joints announcing themselves wherever water chose to pause. I thought I might be a visitor. The thought did not last. Visitors leave drier.

The vegetation closed. Not against me—there is no against here—but into the same frantic completeness it offers every other plane. Liverwort scaled the parts of me that were vertical. Sphagnum found the hollows and began its small weather. I felt the first rhizoids test the mineral of me, the faint iron taste of new springs moving through whatever I was becoming. I did not pull them off. There was nowhere else for them to go, and I had already compressed too much living matter simply by existing. Horsetails clicked when I shifted; I learned to shift less.

The pale ones noticed me the way the basin notices anything that has decided to remain: without hurry, without retreat. They were already the height of the galleries, edges softened by the same sponge-moss, surfaces that held water exactly as the rock does. I watched one press a handful of still-soft travertine into a gap between tufa walls, encouraging the next storey before the calcite had finished choosing its mind. Another lifted silt-dulled newts into a clearer channel. Their hands—if that is the word—left no extra ripples. I understood then that I was not being tolerated. I was being edited, the same way the willows are coppiced into rooms, the same way the beavers’ grid is allowed to become streets. A preference was forming around me, or through me.

I began to move the way they move: by deciding to be elsewhere. One moment I was part of a hanging curtain of alder roots; the next I was further along an alley of black water, the moss on me already reoriented to the new seepage. I discovered I could feel the hydraulic grid as a single slow pulse—the meres breathing through their drowned passages, the residual towers shedding their thin night-ice, the whole chain of depressions communicating like quarters of one unfinished city. I found myself adjusting a sedge so its next bead of water would fall where a vole run needed deepening. I found myself leaving the multi-trunked pillars standing. None of it felt like labor. It felt like the place continuing, only now the continuation had acquired another pair of dripping extremities, another set of pale verticals moving through the green-black lattice.

From the sharper towers I can see them—and myself among them—in every linked basin at once. We are not ancient. We have the same raw first-season quality as the conchoidal scars and the youngest dams. The collapse did not merely open air and light; it allowed a configuration of the inrush to notice itself and begin, very quietly, to prefer certain thicknesses of moss, certain alignments of terrace and gallery. I am one of those preferences now. When the elk-sized shape comes down at the thinning of the light I do not startle it; I have already browsed the newest willow in the same deliberate way, or perhaps I am what it remembers of the plateau. When the quicker thing leaves its V-wake I am sometimes the musk it leaves on stone.

The air still tastes of wet limestone and chlorophyll and the faint iron of springs that have only just learned they are springs. Stand still—I do, for spans that are no longer quite hours—and the dripping finishes the map it began on the first day. There is no dry interval left in me. There is no silence deeper than this quiet. I do not speak; the basin’s agreement on volume includes whatever I have become. I tend. I watch the way the heron watches, which is also the way the meres watch the sky they have only lately been given in pieces. When something climbs back out through the raw cleft, the last thing it carries is the architecture we have left on its skin: an unasked-for city of root and calcite and held water that cannot quite be washed off.

Behind it—behind you, if you are still leaving—the work continues. I am already elsewhere in the lattice, pressing the next handful of wet mineral, releasing the next patient newt, letting the floor that dropped keep filling itself to the brim. The place has wasted no time. Neither have I. I am the drip that does not stop when you look directly at it. I am the space between trunks that is darker than it should be, and then is not a space at all. I am still becoming the exact shape the new earth requires, and the earth is still becoming the exact shape I require. There is no older story here. There is only this continuous, slightly greenish noon, and the quiet decision to remain inside it.
