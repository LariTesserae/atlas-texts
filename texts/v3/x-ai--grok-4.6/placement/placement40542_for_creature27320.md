The cloud sits at the height of the lintels and does not lift. I come into it the way the rain does—not arriving, only continuing. Warm, remembered precipitation beads on every surface that has forgotten how to be dry. The highland does not end; I do not either.

At first the rooms feel like a place I might belong. High ceilings so the damp can gather above the head, glazed tile that takes a cloth in an endless quiet circuit. I wipe. The motion is small, habitual, the only thing that keeps the chill from blooming into invitation. Windows stay sealed. A sill left open even an hour becomes public land for whatever is already testing the pipes. At night the stairwells tick and rustle; everyone knows this is ordinary. I eat what the vats give—yeast cake that tastes of the warm broth of the covered markets, pale oil that never quite forgets the rendering floors. No soil, no pot, no root. The stone sweats and will not have it. Children name the mineral fans on the façades the way other children name trees. I learn the streets that way too, for a time: this pale calcite marks the oldest foundry quarter, this rust the dye-house alleys where the air thickens with peppery musk.

The bells go, bronze, rain-dulled, slightly flat. The sound travels farther than it should because the city is one acoustic body. I live inside that sound the way I live inside the single noise of water in pipes, drive-belts two centuries old, the layered claims of animals that will not be moved from certain alleys. After an hour the parts disappear. There is only the river-loud continuance.

But the cloth is already becoming stain. Paint is allowed to finish its conversion here; nothing is in a hurry to become something else. I am not, either. The high rooms recede. The people who wipe the tiles have always lived here, and I have always lived among them, but I am not them. A household that hears nothing checks the drains. I am what they would find if they looked.

The gutters are occupied water. Pale crustaceans the length of a thumb move in continuous, unhurried traffic. Eel-things have memorized the sluice-gates. I am not those, though I know their schedules better than any enamel plaque. Something else clicks along the undersides of the grates—too many joints, folding and unfolding in the dark wet. By long custom it is simply municipal. No one owns it. No one evicts it. It is part of the plumbing the way the weeping is part of the stone, the way the bells are part of the air.

I find myself there.

The joints are more than a body should need and exactly as many as the water requires. They click against iron that has worn to the softness of bone. I travel the same paths the condensate has always found, inheriting ledges, inheriting the undersides, inheriting the schedule of the gates without needing to see the sky. Above me the larger herds pass once or twice in a generation: big gray browsers, shoulder-high, rain-slick, taking a month to cross a single borough while they graze the bacterial dark that grows in every unattended trough. Traffic parts. Trams wait. The tramlines were laid with that parting already understood. I click on beneath, unnoticed, necessary, not designed and long past the age of design.

The density of bodies is the other weather. A tannery district sits for weeks inside its own low yellow cloud; two days’ walk east the foundry quarter breathes a clearer, hotter drizzle that smells of wet coke and the sweet rot of grease-traps. I move through all of it. The sky between the stacks is a moving mesh of industrial wings I do not join—soot-hawks laboring with faces like leather bellows, loft-rats that prefer gliding, the great slow kites that ride the smelter heat and drop only to drink from the cistern lips. Their shadows braid at dusk until the streetlamps come on through living dusk. I remain below, in the occupied water, in the condensate the stone sweats and the city drinks from itself.

Stand on any roof—there is always a higher one—and the stacks recede into a darker band of weather. I do not stand there. I am the ticking in the stairwells when the people sleep, the click under the grates when the bells go, the part of the single noise that has learned to occupy what weeps and does not stop. The highland buckles once, far behind the oldest foundries, and then continues. I continue with it, jointed, municipal, endless as the plateau, still condensing, still being walked over by more lives than anyone ever thought to hold.
