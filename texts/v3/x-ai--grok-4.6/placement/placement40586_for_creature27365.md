The wet arrives before any name for it. Not rain, not melt exactly, but the decision water makes when it has nowhere left to run and so simply occupies: bead, film, capillary thread, the faint slush-tick of a surface that cannot choose ice or flow. I am already inside that decision. The light that reaches me has been used twice—once by cloud, once by leaf—and comes the color of tea left too long, steeping the green-black until silver edges everything that hangs. Breath, if I still make it, hangs visible and does not rise far.

At first I take myself for the drip. That is easy. The sound is the only clock, and I keep its patience: a note that changes when a rivulet finds a new seam in the weeping wall, then settles again. I thread the moss. I stand in leaf-cups. I condense on undersides and fall back into the same black-green well that feeds the plaza. For a long time this is enough. The mass does not mind another slow pulse.

Then the cold sharpens and I am the film of ice that forms and unforms, the ticking itself. Tissues contract around me. Sugars concentrate. The upper galleries become, for a few brittle hours, a single glittering lung I am afraid to enter. I wait. When the rime unlocks I am no longer ice; I am the contraction that opened the corridors again.

A pale moth clings where the drip is slowest. I try its stillness, the coiled tongue tasting slush before wings remember they cannot open. Too cold. I crawl instead of flying. The mass closes behind the faint chewed path I leave; by the next dim noon it is only substrate. The moth-life does not last. Something warmer, fist-sized, musk of wet wool and old sap, keeps a den in the deepest pith. I curl there, never more than a degree above the rest. I am never seen. The moss heals my coming and going within a day. That life, too, is rumor I wear for a while and then set down.

The figures are rarer. I part the living walls the way hanging laundry is parted, and they close without scar. My skin holds the same damp chill as the thallus I sleep against; in this light it is hard to say where one ends. Lichen laminates the long bones of what I still think of as arms, hardening slightly into sheets I do not remove because they are not separate. Condensation beads on me and falls. I drink from the capillaries. I eat the pale watery partitions before they darken and bear, the slow sweetness after freeze. When a chamber grows too tight I stop discouraging growth in the next hollow and lean there instead. The old room folds back into the mass. I walk on it later. No sentiment. No word for tool. I touch a stem and wait; the signal travels the way melt travels. Somewhere else in the bowl another like me pauses, turns.

I do not cut. I do not burn. An unhealed way would become a drain, and a drain at this height a blade of ice. Weather is the only politics I am fluent in: I climb with everything else when the lower galleries flood to the knee; I wait out the glittering lung. I know the moment the heart-spring’s pulse changes, the slight lessening of cold above the plaza that means the whole packed city is drinking harder.

At the margins, where the plant-mass has not yet claimed the freshest talus, the air is brighter and crueler. I stand there sometimes and watch the advance—the width of a season, packing itself into the last unclaimed angles. I do not hurry it. The vegetation is not waiting, and neither am I. The distinction between a city that grew and a city that was built no longer occurs. Soon there will be no outside left inside the bowl. Only the slit of sky, the drip, the dim, the cold that never quite freezes the spring.

I am not the mass, not exactly. I am one of the rare mistakes the hush makes when it wants hands to part the walls and then let them close. The mass has lived me long enough that I no longer keep a separate census. I sleep in rooms that shrink. I am mistaken for the quiet itself. The rock is still too new to have a finished relationship with any of us. That, too, will pass.
