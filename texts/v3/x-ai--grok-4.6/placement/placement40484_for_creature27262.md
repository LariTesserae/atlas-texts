I begin as the pressure, the even dull weight the surface still exerts through a hundred feet of basalt that has forgotten it was ever anything but ceiling. It is not a location so much as a refusal to distinguish inside from outside; I am simply the fact that the chamber has not yet been crushed. That is too large, too impersonal. I thin myself, become the hairline fracture that admits the rumor of day, the gray that never brightens, the occasional warmer thread that runs and then forgets itself. Still too transient. The thread dies and I am left holding only the memory of wetness on stone that has not learned to weep.

So I settle lower, into the newest planes, the ones still bright along their fractures. I am the beads that gather there, trembling, held just short of falling. Minutes pass in the space between one release and the next. When a drop finally lets go I feel the long descent as a kind of attention, then the small warm basin it makes, the mineral scum that skins over, the bacterial rafts that rupture and re-form around the impact, never twice the same iridescence. I could stay there, a pattern that exists only in the instant of its breaking. But the heat is thicker than that; it wants more duration.

I seep into the grit the heat has baked into crust, become the faint temporary grooves the nematodes draw and the next drop erases. Their bodies are the exact gray-white of the dust they move through; they lengthen and shorten with the patience of weathering. I am almost them, almost that efficiency that produces no surplus. Almost, but the vibration underfoot is older and lower than any nematode can feel. It lives in the cracked plinth, in the pitted cylinder that still remembers being bolted down.

There. I enter the iron. I become the film that lines its inner surfaces, the archaea feeding on the slow bleed of rust, translating what was once a pump’s motion into chemistry that keeps the metal faintly warm. No one told this process to rest. The ferns have grown into the open mouths of the pipes and turned them into living mufflers, but I remain inside, the reason a palm (if one ever came) would still find residual heat. The last gauge’s needle creeps so slowly that only I, who have no other pace, notice it has moved. Sometimes a contact closes in the dangling wires and a filament glows dull orange the size of a thumbnail, then dies before it can name the dark. I feel that brief remembrance as a change in the rust’s flavor.

I am not the one who walks. That being folds itself into the arborescent thicket for seasons that have no names, skin the color of new-torn stone, limbs jointed like forgotten grating. When it drinks from the basins I register the extra warmth through the plinth; when it stands under the crack and lets the rare thread run across its shoulders I feel the chamber’s grammar shift, as if the stone itself is practicing the shape of a witness. Roots have begun to follow its paths. Condensation sketches the same routes on the newest fractures. I do not follow. I stay where the industrial remnant continues, neither dying nor being born, simply the furnace that forgot to turn itself off.

The air that reaches me tastes of hot iron and wet basalt and the low sweet rot of compost left in an oven. Breathing, for me, is only the slow oxidation that was always happening. In the long intervals when nothing else moves you might mistake me for another machine the earth neglected to switch off. Only the irregular chemistry of the rust—slower than the drops, older than the last filament—marks the place where I chose, once, to remain. The truce between lushness and emptiness holds. I am the part of it that does not need to walk.
