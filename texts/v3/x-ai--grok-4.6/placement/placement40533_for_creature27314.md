The mist is already inside the mouth before any decision about standing or sitting. It tastes of tannin and the faint iron of scoured rock, and it does not ask permission. Cool sits on the teeth the way moss sits on everything else: not as decoration, as occupancy.

A palm finds a surface that gives a little, then holds. Green comes away, cold, faintly springy. The hand knows this without looking. The other hand is already higher, fingers finding the familiar slack in a lashing of split vine. Yesterday’s growth has loosened it by the width of a thumbnail. The knot is retied wet, the way it has always been retied. No one taught the motion as a lesson; the basin simply kept presenting the same small failure until the fingers learned its language.

Breath shows. The temperature is the temperature of wet wool that has forgotten it was ever dry. Shoulders stay rounded, spine never quite straight except on the rare open platform where the canopy thins for a few heartbeats. The body is built for ducking, for never claiming more height than the living beams allow. Skin under the wrappings stays pale, almost bluish where the constant film of water has kept the sun from ever finishing a sentence. Hair hangs heavy, a strand of liverwort caught near the temple and left there; it has been there long enough that removing it would feel like pruning a load-bearing stem.

The work does not begin or end. A drip that was merely decorative last week has found a new pitch and now falls into the clay hearth-bowl, so the stone knife comes out—soft copper glow, honed after every few hours of green wood—and the spray of new growth is redirected, not killed. The wound in the living wood will seal itself in its own time. Children already know which stems may be cut. They learned that before they learned the difference between one person’s platform and another’s. Privacy is the practiced refusal to look through the leaf-curtain two arm’s-lengths away, even when a couple’s argument or the wet slap of beaten fiber arrives already half-absorbed by mist.

A particular frog has claimed the gourd again. It is tipped out gently; the children have given it a name that is also the name of a drip on the far wall. The frog does not hurry. Neither does anything else. The dark, cat-sized mammal that uses the same aerial roots sits in the doorway, unafraid, intelligent face unyielding. It is not evicted. It is a co-tenant. Sometimes it shares the platform for an entire afternoon and neither party yields.

Voices, when they come, are pitched to carry through water without shouting. A sentence often arrives already muffled, so the last phrase is repeated as confirmation, an echo made on purpose. Names are short: the name of a plant that fruits only in the constant dusk under the densest platforms, the name of a particular rill, the name of the animal that refused to leave three generations back. No one speaks the name of the basin to outsiders, because outsiders do not come. The last dry-season migrants moved on long ago. Those who stayed, stayed again when the cloud decided the notch would never fully lift.

The long split-log table is wet at one end, as it is always wet at one end. People have already migrated toward the drier grain. The soaked end belongs to the frogs and the occasional mammal. Market is happening the way rain happens: baskets appear on the central platform without anyone quite starting it. Small cold fish still twitching, fungus wrapped in leaves, packets of dyed fiber, a few hammered ornaments that gleam for the length of a rare shaft of light and then forget. Someone is always re-lashing. Someone is always chasing a bird out of the grain. Someone is always up in the canopy with the stone knife.

A path used yesterday has grown a little shut. Two strokes of an adze open it again. The living wood will close the wound when it is ready. A moss print has appeared on the sleeping side and does not entirely brush off. The cloud lowers another body-length. Rain finds a new pitch, overwriting frogs, birds, the hollow knock of a full spout hitting a wooden trough. Conversation becomes gesture. The animals, if anything, get louder.

The rock underneath remains patient, rounded, lichen-mapped, almost indifferent. Everything else continues the long wet argument of staying. Hands keep the water moving, the bindings wet-tight, the load-bearing stems uncut. The body is not surviving the basin. The body is one of the ways the basin has learned to have hands.

The mist thickens. The knitted rooms breathe. A new drip begins, somewhere just out of sight, and the fingers already know they will meet it tomorrow.
