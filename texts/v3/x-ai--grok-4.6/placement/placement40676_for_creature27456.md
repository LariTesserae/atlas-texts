I begin at the edge of knowing, where the air first thickens with the taste of unforgotten stone. Not yet a body, not yet a name—only the cool that lives in joints before joints exist, the emulsion that remembers every footprint and slowly unmakes it from below. The light is already honest and unkind, grey enough that colours refuse drama. Something weeps without springs. Something packs itself tighter than absence can afford.

The sensation clarifies into weight. I am low. The ground under me is still drunk, young, a little unwilling to stay decided. I feel it shrug through the soles that have already broadened, toes splayed for wet cobble and the single bowing plank. My skin has taken the banks’ colour: pale, bluish in this even illumination, never quite dry. Silt lives in the creases of my wrists like a second, finer skin. After a day of work it glazes me. Fingertips bleach when I peck stone or hold a withy form long enough; the nails have begun to ridge in layers the way the exposed clay still shows its wound.

I am not on the rim. The rim is a memory of height I no longer need. I live in the tear. I arrived with it or I was already standing when the ground opened and I simply stepped down, one generation ago, and the hollow taught the rest. We do not speak of before except as the Opening, an event that is almost a person. Some of us leave slack in every wall because we believe it is still widening, a finger’s width a decade. I am one of those. I will not be the one caught loving a corner that decides, overnight, to become trough.

Circulation is overhead more than it is underfoot. I spend more hours on the continuous, slightly canted roof-plane than on any floor. Packed reed and clay, glistening, interrupted by smoke-holes and drying racks and the occasional ladder. From here the settlement is one low organism, densely faceted, breathing its own thin lid of steam that never quite clears the banks. I move a plank two sockets over, test it with a heel that already knows the difference between a hold that will last the day and one that will fill from below. Then I go on. Someone is always wringing. Someone is always packing wet clay into forms that will stand a little out of true. The dull tap of dressed stone carries only a house or two before the damp air swallows it.

My voice stays inside the chest. The hollow is a bowl; anything louder returns from the opposite bank as if the stone itself were answering, and we have learned the volume that does not come back. Conversation happens in short, overlapping phrases, eaves talking to eaves. We speak of the alder that clings to a collapsed wall we have not yet rebuilt. Guest or warning—no one has decided. I leave it. Its roots have the look of someone refusing to let go, and that feels familiar.

After rain the whole hollow becomes a single shallow lens the colour of weak tea. I wade. Trousers rolled or abandoned. Children pole flat baskets and already know which footprints will hold. I lift, reset, climb a handspan higher when a bank calves in the night and we collect the fresh cobbles before the emulsion swallows them. The oldest houses look like slow geological arguments. I live inside one of those arguments. My joints carry a small constant ache, the body’s way of remembering the low wet cool. I do not complain. Complaint would be another sound thrown back.

At the hour when the light goes colourless I am on the roofs again, tasting silt and woodsmoke and the mineral chill of stone that will not stop bleeding. A new drip-line has found a path down a wall that was dry yesterday. It will become a stain, then a groove, then a problem. I touch the wall as I pass, checking. We are the activity of staying in a wound that has not closed, packed so tightly that a single absence would be a collapse, yet ready, always, to rebuild.

I am not finished. Nothing here is. The hollow holds. For now it holds. I hold it back, a handspan at a time, feet gripping the bow, breath showing, mouth slightly open to taste when a new seep begins. The clay does not argue. Neither do I.
