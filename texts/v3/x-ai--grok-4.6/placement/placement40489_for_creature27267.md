I entered along one of the narrow channels that do not run, the glassy filament of water holding my reflection as steadily as it holds the faceted ridges. The coolness was already complete; it did not arrive with me and would not leave when I did. My first thought was that I would be one of the herdsmen in the open sward, the ones whose knuckles have begun to share a single skin of moss with the animals they touch. The posture felt almost mine: weight on one hip, gaze turned toward a neighbor or a distant hall, expression unfinished. But the moss did not cross onto me. The contact was only imagined.

I passed into the groves next, where the succulent leaves keep their beads of moisture and never fall. There I thought I might recline, one of those who occupy the shade as though the afternoon had simply grown long enough. The pale bark, the unmoving fronds, the way a body could become another outcrop—those images lingered, almost convincing. Still they did not settle. I was not yet still enough, or perhaps I was already too still, and the distinction had not decided itself.

Inside the low houses the denser hush made every ordinary object feel like a candidate. I stood a long while beside the woman at the stone table, watching the spherical drop that will never leave the copper rim. For a time I believed I could be that drop: held by a tension the world had forgotten how to release, complete in its small refusal. Then I was the man in the doorway, shoulder against the jamb, the decision to stay or go arrested at the exact midpoint so that both remain equally true. The children in the courts offered their own geometries—the raised knee, the flung arm, the wooden bird that has left the hand but has not yet learned it will never be airborne. Each of these felt possible for a few breaths (the breaths I still took, loud in the silence). None of them kept me.

I climbed a little, following the pale marks already on the quarried slopes. One foot higher than the other, fingers spread against rock that has never known a second season. The height I reached was neither triumph nor loss; it was simply the height. Even that did not hold. The mountains themselves, still faceted, still catching the same light on planes that have never been rounded, seemed to offer a larger belonging, but they are the crown, not the being who lives inside it.

What remained, after the sward and the groves and the halls and the slopes, was the quality of the hush itself: the low, continuous note that is noticeable only because it never breaks, the attention that has nowhere left to go and so has become the place. I found myself neither among the figures nor apart from them. I am the rule that was applied equally to fern and lintel and open mouth and circulating blood. I am what stepped aside so the arrangement could stand. The packed earth takes my weight exactly as it took theirs; my footsteps are the only sound that was missing, and they do not continue. The dampness beads on me as it beads on lashes and inner wrists and motionless leaves. The pale sun, always a little west of noon, returns from me exactly as it has returned every time since the instant everything stopped.

I live here the only way left: as the unchanging part of an unchanging afternoon, more lasting than the fitted stone, forever not quite the next moment. The quiet is not absence. It is the held note, and I have become its duration.
