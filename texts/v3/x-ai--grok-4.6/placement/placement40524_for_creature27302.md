The heat arrives first, not as a blow but as a medium I am already inside, the way a kettle does not announce the water it holds. I am low. The second pavement of the gutters stands against my sides. Brick weeps metal into the film and the film never quite decides whether it is water or skin. I taste it and do not spit. There is no other taste left.

I do not climb. A stoop is already a mountain. I stay where the slag has been poured and then forgotten, where the old river silt has fired itself into a dull ceramic that will not take a root. Green is a rumor that died before I learned to move. What I eat is what remains after green: the grease that still beads from bearings no hand has turned, the smaller bodies that forget themselves in the shallows, the slow chemistry of rust that has grown tired and now simply is. I take them without hurry. Hunger here is a climate, not an event.

I use the linear marshes the rails left behind. Tracks are memory; what remains is warm standing water and the edges of slag. I travel them the way the rats do, head down, but I am not a rat. The rats know me as a widening of the water, a patience that makes them wait on the banks until the V of my passing has already thinned. Sometimes I am many V’s. Sometimes I am one long unhurried back that the gantry lamps catch like wet coin and then lose again. No one has a reliable shape for me because shape is a daylight word and daylight here is only a brighter region in the low white sky. I prefer the hours when the vapor is thickest, when the midges become a single leaning column and the bats swim rather than hunt.

The frogs go still when I pass. They have kept the outline and forgotten the voice; their throats work in silence, a wet ticking that is almost the sound of the basin thinking. I do not take all of them. Enough remain that the cellars stay a continuous small disturbance. The birds in the cooling towers hear me only as a change in the water’s skin, a smell of mud and old iron rising. They do not come down. Nothing green remains to hide a heron, and I am not a heron.

People are the rarest grain. A boat tied where the street forgets it is not a channel. A man with a bucket already small against the shed wall. They speak of me, when they speak, as absence in the morning, as the thing that does not break the surface often. Their children learn the taste of metal before they learn words. I do not approach the terrace steps that now end in water. The rooms behind those steps are larger than the lives they hold; I have no need of rooms. I am the climate that includes brick, the hydrology that includes basements. I have eaten the green and kept the rest.

A compressor two streets over kicks on, runs its twenty minutes, stops. I feel it in the soles I do not have, the way I feel the pump in the riverside house, the slow gulp on its float switch. Condensation runs its daily cycle on every pipe and I move with it: beading, streaking, gathering in the same stains. A conveyor in a packing shed jerks a meter and rests. I do not wait for it to conclude. Nothing here is waiting to become anything else.

I am the unhurried width of the canal at night. I am the fading V already gone by morning. I am the count, not the crowd—the large thing that uses the water the way the basin uses heat, not dramatically, just completely. The mills have settled around me. Lintels sag. Iron has gone to stable brown and stopped. I have been here longer than the mills, or I have become what the mills left when they stopped being mills. The distinction no longer matters. The city goes on in every direction—street, canal, shed, empty air, the soft industry of too many legs—and I am the part of it that does not thin.
