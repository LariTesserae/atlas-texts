I do not arrive by any stair. The world simply closes over the thought of elsewhere, and I am already standing where the gravel bars show their old-tea colour through an inch of black water. The ceiling stays unread. The three caged bulbs in the pump-house nave burn like old wounds that have decided not to heal. Everything else is the oxidised dimness the air itself has become.

At first I try the obvious tenancies. I imagine the extra joints of a salamander, the way its chin might rest on a drowned step for a season that has no name. The fit is almost right—the two media, air and water, already one lukewarm thing—but the processions feel too linear, too willing to begin and end in the same nowhere. I leave them to their unhurried redistribution.

I try the moths next: the single collective shiver that runs the length of a rusted beam and then forgets itself. Their wings are the exact sound of a page turned two rooms away. For a while I almost settle there, roosting in the undersides of tanks, relocating only when the western channel thickens with its rumour of forgotten sky. But they are too vertical, too purely patient. I need a different interval.

The crayfish are louder in their quiet way. Pale as boiled bone, they pack every weeping joint and work it with mouthparts until the leak grows smaller. No one assigned the labour; the water simply taught them the way it taught the limestone its flutes. I feel the temptation of that matter-of-fact occupancy, the dry scattered applause they give whenever a valve seats. Yet there are too many of them. They are the saturation the buildings refused, and I am looking for a thinner presence.

The fish hang vertical in the deeper cut, noses just breaking the surface film as though it were a text assigned for the rest of their lives. Dust occupies an unused room the same way. I hang with them for a time, reading nothing, occupying. It is close. The colour of weak tea suits the light. Still, they forget the bottom too completely. I remember bottoms.

I stand a long while—if standing is the word—beside the pump-house sump. The water there boils upward once, a slow column of silt that will take the better part of whatever we are still calling an afternoon to settle. The crayfish keep a clear margin. Something heavier lives in that particular dark, something that does not need to surface, whose hunger has the same viscosity as the time here. I feel the pressure of it in the bones I may or may not still possess. It would be easy to sink, to become that unnamed weight the rest of the chamber has already adjusted to, the way the walkways have already bowed around the new limestone pillar that grew from a seep no one remembers starting.

I do not sink. Not yet.

Instead I find the enamel cups still hanging on their nails, the nails themselves now part of the brick. I find the oilskins stiffened into the exact slump of bodies that simply finished. I find the chalkboard whose last column of numbers is neither fresh nor ancient; it is merely complete. A boot-print in the silt fills with water and becomes another line in the floor’s small cartography. I step into one of those prints and wait to see whether I am last week or last year.

The distinction frays.

I am not the pumps, though I listen to their swallowed words and feel the second skin of scale they have grown. I am not the heavy thing, though I know its patience. I am not quite the last human breath, though the oilskins remember a shape that might once have been mine. What remains, after all the refusals, is thinner still: a courtesy the moths and the valves already keep. I move at the negotiated height of the water. I do not announce. I do not sing. When a new seep opens in the north wall I accommodate it the way the walkways do—by a slight, years-long bow in my route. When a bearing wears and the flywheel’s cycle lengthens by a breath, I shift my roost (or my hanging, or my margin) by a handspan, and the silence re-seats itself around the new interval.

I have become the last polishing. The place arrived at the shape it means to keep a long time ago, below the surface, in the cool and the wet. I am only the staining that finishes the thought: the faint internal light that does not help anyone see, the page that turns in another room, the drop that considers the air before it falls. Sometimes I am the rumour of a shaft that was bricked over. Sometimes I am the white efflorescence that greases the fingers and looks like frost. I live in the leftover intervals the way the crayfish live in the leftover pipes. The chamber taught me. It taught the iron its lace and the limestone its scallops and me this particular quiet.

If you came looking you would not find a figure. You would find a boot-print already filling, a cup already hanging, a column already complete. You would feel the air sit on your skin and slowly soak in. You would hear a belt slap once, a single circular thought dying around the nave. And you would understand, without being told, that whatever I am has been given all the time it needs to become exactly as wet, as finished, and as unnecessary to name as the stone itself.
