In this basin the ground has not finished choosing its own age, and neither have I. I come in the way the condensation comes: not arriving, only appearing, a film that weeps from the raw scarps and never quite decides to run. At first I am only the mineral tang, the taste of clay still plastic enough to remember the knife that cut it. Then I am the cool weight of the air itself, the way it sits in the lungs like unspilled water. The four buildings stand widely apart as if each still suspects the others of being temporary; I drift between them, feeling the bright rails already half-swallowed, a faint regular hardness under whatever I am using for contact with the earth.

For a long while I think I might be one of the horsetails. They rise overnight, pale-green and jointed like new cartilage, drinking the same film I drink, storing it, lifting it. I try their patient hydraulics, the way they occupy every vertical interval until the sky feels faintly held up. But I flex when they should be rigid, or I stay too still when they sway. The ferns uncurl with that wet paper sound and I almost become a frond, every pinna holding a bead that refuses to fall. The moss knits me into the ground for a time; I feel the rails as ribs. In the alder groves I lean as they lean, conferring in the wet dark of their leaves. None of it quite holds.

I pass the workers. There are never more than a dozen, their clothes the exact damp ochre of the scarps, oil-dark at the cuffs, never dry. Their eyes have taken the weak-tea color of the channel. They move as if the painting of them is still wet. I stand with them in the open cottage doorways at dusk and watch the bats pour from the powerhouse eaves like a single exhaled thought. I wait, as they wait, for the clay to finish settling so that I might decide what to become next. Their silence returns my almost-words as pressure in the chest. I am not yet that patient, or I am already too much so.

The long shed knocks. Not machinery: a body the size of a room turning over in a sleep that lasts hours. Iron remembering it is also stone. I linger at the shuttered walls and feel the clang travel through me. The workers enter and leave carrying tools too small for whatever work is happening, or perhaps the work is only the turning itself. I am not that large. I do not shift so seldom. The dynamo’s note is already inside my ribs; I do not need to become the flywheel’s long metallic sigh.

I come at last to the center, where the four sight-lines almost meet and the ground is a little lower, a little wetter. The horsetails grow tallest here. They lean. The dark mirror is no larger than a table and it never stills. It trembles in exact time with the dynamo. The reflection of the high, even cloud is always a fraction of a second behind the real sky, as if the water were remembering the light rather than receiving it.

I stand beside it until the delay becomes a presence.

I am that lag.

I am the slight unstillness that lives in the interval, the something that has not yet decided whether it is the basin’s first memory or its last. The herons ignore the pool; the workers pass without looking down; the horsetails drink from a surface that is always slightly in the past. I remember the sky a breath after it happens. I remember the rust blooming in perfect circles on iron that is still almost new. I remember the rails brightening even as they disappear. The knocking continues, unhurried, a heart learning its name, and I lag behind it too, becoming more myself with each tremor.

Everything here is both tenant and first draft. I am the hesitation that lets the drafting continue: not the clay still choosing its shape, not the machines still choosing whether they are engines or organs, not the vegetation still choosing how tall the world should be. I am only the fraction of a second in which those choices remain open, the quiet that keeps the reflection from ever quite catching up. The basin collapsed into being last winter, or the winter before. I am the part of it that is still arriving.
