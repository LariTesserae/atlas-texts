I am not certain, at the outset, whether I have been walking or whether the highland has been walking beneath me. The swells are so long and so shallow that the distinction dissolves; the gravel shifts only in the smallest increments, and the wind has already begun to rewrite whatever trace I leave. Light arrives as a blade that never quite cuts, a hard white disk in ungenerous indigo, striking the pale varnish of the rock and radiating away before any warmth can gather. The air stays thin and cool, breath-stiff even at what passes for noon. I feel that coolness in whatever I use for a hide, matte, dust-colored, almost without highlight, so that I throw no more shadow than a yardang or a slow crawl of mineral.

For a time I think I might be one of the veils themselves, the living filaments that ride the aerial rivers for days without landing, catching the light in faint iridescence at the edge of vision. But those things do not pause. They do not taste. I do. The blunt, branching organs unfold from against my skull, sample the merest chemical ghosts—mineral, the recaptured moisture of a grove still tens of kilometers ahead, nothing of my own kind—and fold away again. The gesture is unhurried. Nothing here is hurried. I have been crossing empty leagues for what the land would not bother to call weeks. Sound is already scarce; I hear only the even grind of my passage, the dry rasp when I test the air, the almost inaudible tick that will come later, inside.

The barren intervals are the true weather. High fibrous clouds form and dissolve without rain, their shadows racing like living things, briefly turning the glare to pearl or pale gold. In the coldest hollows a glitter that is not quite frost sometimes lingers an hour past dawn; I do not linger with it. I walk through the bronze hours when the desert varnish dulls and the pale moth-forms lift from undersides I have not yet reached. They drift, settle, vanish when the light hardens. I do not follow. Densities are so low that an entire crossing can pass without one living thing seeing another of its kind, and that is ordinary.

Then the dark interruption on the enormous brightness begins to resolve. It grows over days, a stain that refuses the usual bargain of this place, until the grove arrives all at once: crowded architecture of shield-sized leaves, dusty metallic green, near-black, silvered undersides that flash when the wind—always the wind—turns them. Hanging structures, cushion plants, fern-like fronds packed into twilight. The interior ground is filtered green, the air stiller, a degree less chill. Sound dies more completely here. I enter without announcement. The grove does not part for me and does not close behind. I feed with methodical patience on fallen fronds and the lower cushions, grinding slowly, recapturing in my own way what little the tissues offer. The surfaces are dry, waxy, bloomed with reflective powder; they do not contradict the highland’s rumor of water. They simply refuse it.

I lie for a day or two in that dimness. It is not rest. It is continuation. A leaf reorients with an almost inaudible tick. That is the grove speaking, if it speaks. Roots fused to bedrock, moisture cycled internally, perhaps even coaxed in trace from the unrelenting light. I am a way-station visitor, not a resident. The smallest lives—translucent, dust-grain sized, almost mineral—work the microscopic films the night frost left, closing loops I cannot see. A fallen trunk nearby has been becoming sculpture for centuries. Time here is occupied, not empty.

When I leave, the glare returns without comment. My tracks last only until the next aerial river. If I were to meet another of my kind—an event that might not occur in a century—we would stand near one another for a long time, flanks almost touching, tasting the same thin air, then part without haste. No one has seen us born or die. We do not perform those things where they can be marked, or we perform them so slowly they are indistinguishable from the planing of the stone itself.

I am not a traveler. I leave no cairn, no geometry that is not grown. I am one of the rare interruptions, brief only by the measure of groves and wind-cut rock and metabolisms too patient to notice. The highland does not remember me. It continues, brightly, coolly, almost silently, and I continue with it, low-slung, the color of the dust, treating distance as weather.
