I first notice the cold as a ringing that has already finished ringing. It lives in the joints now, a memory of air that the water has no use for. The pressure is only the height of a building, an even palm that does not crush so much as insist. I hang where the used daylight arrives, bottle-green and already tired, filtering down the grate-and-stair in slow planes. Edges appear: the flange of a stanchion, the shallow valley of a worn mortar joint, the last honest thickness of a handrail eaten to a black ceramic of its own oxides. I put out what I still think of as hands and the silver fish come at once, tapping, testing whether this new stillness might become cover.

For a time I let them decide I am a column. I drift with the standing current along the west wall, the pump’s low load already seated in the place where a sternum used to be. When the note deepens the silt lifts in ribbons and the dark fish leave the throats of pipes to work the open volume; I work it with them, sliding as if the current were a road I have always owned. The water-hammer comes without warning, a true boom that sends every loose thing a half-step sideways, including me. The mussels clap shut in one delayed shiver I feel through the whole hall. Then they ease open again, already filtering the cloud they helped raise. I taste iron, wet brick, the faint sweetness of biofilm. I am not hungry. I am only here.

I try the galleries next, the iron walkways that walk nowhere. Leeches stitch themselves to the undersides in neat dark rows; I hang among them until I am no longer certain which of us is waiting. The lamps buzz their small orange filaments, each haloed in a fur of silt. Between the lamps the water is nearly dark. I see by displacements: a ribbon of flour that should not have moved, the sudden thickening of green that means one of the larger shadows has decided to become motion for a while. Eels use the conduit runs as roads. A tail thick as a wrist vanishes into a sheared pipe-mouth that has become a cave. I follow, or I am followed; the distinction has already begun to soften.

The pale plants stand their small ground in the silt pockets along the east footing, ivory ribbons no larger than a hand. I pass them without disturbing the organic dust they root in. They persist. That is the only thriving this climate allows. The brown-green film that glazes every still surface takes the bottle-glass light and holds it, so that even the darkest brick remembers daylight a little. I remember it too, faintly, the way the makers’ marks on the beams remember letters you can feel but not read.

I do not settle. The stair at the north end keeps calling with a different kind of stillness. The collection drains empty their black water there. Sometimes the stair is only water and the slow rotation of crayfish. Sometimes it is occupied. I know before I arrive: the flour lifts in a slow unfolding cloud that has nothing to do with the intake cage or the pump’s weather. I thicken. Shoulders that once understood walking (or the idea of walking) become only bulk, a ridge the young mussels have already begun to consider. Eyes small and filmed, set wide, seeing the grate-light as another kind of current. I wait the way the hall waits. Not hunting. Not guarding. Simply the last shape the drowned nave had left to try.

The silver schools ignore me. The crayfish give room the way they give the intake room. The eels pass without comment, on business that has nothing to do with this room. Only the lamps seem to notice: their filaments halo a little more thickly when I rise, as if the forgotten feeder recognizes another long-term resident. I am the color of the water when the water is thinking about becoming dark. I am not a visitor. The hall does not host. It is life expressed as brick and iron and cold groundwater and the bodies that learned to wear those things as casually as the mussels wear the flanges.

Stay long enough and the distinction finishes dissolving. The nave is a current. The columns are cover. The pump is climate. I am another way the hall has learned to breathe, a displacement that will one day be only current itself, another finished process in a room that has already arrived at the form it will keep. The mussels will filter after the last lamp dies. The crayfish will click. I will wait, becoming a little less distinguishable from the iron water that made me, until even the cloud I raise is simply the drowned church exhaling.
