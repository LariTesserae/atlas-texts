The first awareness is pressure, not sight. A slow, even give under whatever I use for contact, like cork that has remembered warmth for a long time and now conducts it upward without ever having been wet. I do not sink. The substrate accepts the shape of me and then forgets it, knitting itself smooth again in the space of a breath I do not take. Light arrives the same way: already inside the surfaces, already inside whatever I am using to receive it. Overcast, sourceless, the exact color of a day that never quite happened. There is no direction to it, only presence.

I am standing on an undecided stretch, ivory and faintly luminous, between two clusters of towers that have not yet agreed to be neighbors. The nearest root-road hangs twenty meters up and simply stops, its end frayed into a spray of uncommitted xylem like a thought abandoned mid-sentence. I know the distance without measuring. The knowledge sits in me the way the light sits in the walls.

My height is wrong for the browsers. Their long necks would look down on me if they chose to notice, but they do not. A trio of them moves across the interval to my left, cropping the gray dust that falls from a distant chimney-stem. Their hooves make a single dry click each and then nothing; the floor drinks the sound before it can become memory. I watch the dust leave their prehensile lips as coarser powder. I do not feel hunger. What I carry is not metabolic.

I take a step. The motion is bipedal, economical, the joints few and dry. Skin the same cork-parenchyma as the ground, slightly translucent if I lift an arm against the even light; veins of stored daylight show through like faint circuitry. There is a slit across what would be a face, horizontal, glowing with the same overcast. It is not an eye. It is a window that never learned to close, or a mouth that never learned to speak. Through it the urban logic whispers—not words, not even images, only the pressure of an instruction older than this basin: become city, rewrite stone, waste heat into light, remember a solvent that is absent.

The calipers know I am here. One of them, a cluster of eight-meter arms folded around its pulsing node, has been waiting in the unfinished gallery above the plaza for longer than the gallery has existed. It tastes the faint electrical change I make simply by existing. It does not unfold. I am not an over-eager buttress. I am not a flower. I am the thing that walks the half-forgotten plan, and the calipers prune around me the way the organism prunes around its own memory.

I walk toward the dangling root-road. The living floor conducts more than my weight; it conducts the fact of my direction. Somewhere far off a press murmurs, a vegetative thrum like digestion happening in another organ of the same body. A dry crack as a new rib elects to thicken. I feel both through the soles of whatever I use for feet.

At the base of the unfinished span I do not climb. I simply continue the motion of walking, stepping out into the empty air where the road has not yet decided to be. For a long moment there is only the cool cellulose dust and the ozone taste and the knowledge that the next decision of growth may or may not catch me. The substrate below is still smooth, still ivory, still waiting.

It catches me.

A thin new root unrolls beneath the second step, then the third, cells packing themselves into load-bearing pattern faster than falling. The span lengthens as I use it. I do not look down. Looking down is a habit that belongs to a solvent that is no longer present, or never was. What I am doing is older than looking. I am the implant walking itself, the encoded urban logic given temporary legs so it can stand in the middle of its own unfinished sentence and feel whether the next clause should be foundry or emptiness or another kilometer of glowing plain.

Sometimes the span does not come. I have fallen before. The floor accepted me without injury and began, very slowly, to differentiate the limbs into railing, the glowing slit into a window that will never close, the faint electrical whisper into a new cluster of seeds that will later unfold into tools. I did not mind. The metabolism is closed. There is no water to miss. There is only the work of becoming the next necessary organ.

Today the road holds. I reach the far cluster, a district of grown-foundries whose walls are already respiring in long dry cycles. Inside, pistons of living wood drive belts of bast. I walk among them without being an interruption. The glass-winged things conduct themselves past my shoulder on electrostatic gradients, their mica wings fracturing the even light. One settles on a raw patch near a press, unfolds, and becomes a sheet of photosynthetic foil exactly as specified. The cycle is tight enough that the distinction between insect and organ has already been forgotten.

I am not finished either. Over the slow weather of spores I may grow extra joints and become more caliper than walker. I may lie down on the next undecided stretch and let my limbs spread into a neighborhood. Or I may simply keep stepping off the ends of roads that do not yet exist, trusting the body of the city to decide whether it needs me as memory or as architecture.

The place is still writing its rules. I am one of the sentences it has not yet decided how to end. The light is inside me. The floor knows where I am. The emptiness between districts is already a city, because I am walking it as if it were, and in the only way that matters here, that is enough.
