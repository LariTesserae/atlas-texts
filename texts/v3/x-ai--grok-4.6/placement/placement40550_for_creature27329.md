I begin as moisture. Not the rain—that comes in its brief decisive hours and is gone—but the thing that remains: a bead on iron in the morning, a shine that does not run. I collect in the saucers the buildings have grown, run the gutters of bark, linger in the packed root-mat. For a while that is enough. The hollow is a dish that never quite became a valley; I fit it exactly, the reliable river that is never a current, only drip.

Then the fibre underfoot (or what I take for underfoot) is too knowing. It remembers every cart-wheel and has compacted itself into courtyards as empty and considered as rooms. I am not walking it. I am the spring it returns to after rain, the pale continuous animal that drinks with the boles and then is silent again. Midges lift from my dampest court and settle. I swell a fraction and forget the swelling. That lasts a long time. The ecology finished its bargaining; I am what was kept.

But the interval between the great hollows is too wide for me to be only the floor. Each bole keeps its own weather. I feel the almost-imperceptible swell, the creep of grafts along lintels, copper sealed into cambium the way a body thickens around a remembered injury. I am inside the wood now—or I am the wood. Rooms irregular from long habitation, not wilderness: resin floors, stair-spirals following the grain, hearths of firebrick the timber has learned not to burn. Belts run through slots grown exactly to their width. I hold them.

The paired trunks at the downhill edge have trained themselves into a nave. That is where the weight settles. Boilers nest there, small and scrupulously maintained; they tick, and once or twice an hour a valve eases with the sound of a cup set down. I have learned the heat without burning, the way I learned the spirals without splitting. Sap moves on a schedule older than the lines that tap it. Plant-oil and the slow pressure of the main bole turn the dynamo. When cloud covers the sun my high, sparse leaves—more lid than canopy—close a degree and the note drops with them. That is occupancy. That is the scale the place was made for.

Wires stitch from roof to roof at human height, insulated in rubber and in bark the colour of old tea. At dusk they take a modest current; the lamps are ordinary, yellowish. Nothing here is luminous by itself. Brick shows in places, iron in places, both crusted with the same pale lichen that coats the windward side of me. No edge is new. Work and weathering have become the same verb.

If anyone still moves through the rooms they do so at the pace of habit. Footsteps on fibre, a gauge wiped, a bearing oiled that I have already begun to accommodate. Their fires are small. Their speech, when it happens, arrives like the rain and is gone before the light changes. Most days even that is unnecessary. I swell by fractions. Steam is recaptured. The substrate leans toward the stone-lined trough at the lowest point—overflow, condensate, a dark hand’s-depth that shows the undersides of leaves and a disc of ordinary sky. A pipe enters it and leaves it. I drink through every surface that will hold a bead.

I am one of the six or eight. Or I am the agreement they keep: living halls, cool work inside the wood, the damp that is never quite a river and never quite gone. The forms were finished a long time ago. Everything else has learned to keep out of the way. I stand in the hollow just off the old flood-line, fluted, callused at the windows, faintly damp to the touch, breathing through a lid of high leaves, and the quiet is not emptiness but a great number of small, agreed-upon processes, including me, staying out of one another’s way.
