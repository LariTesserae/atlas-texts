The cool reaches me first, not as weather but as the temperature a body keeps when it has forgotten how to be anything but stone that never finished drying. I am already inside it. The pewter light does not arrive; it is simply the thickness of the air I occupy, a ceiling of wool that has decided not to lift. Somewhere a belt slaps, somewhere a condenser sighs, and the sound arrives already muffled, as if it has had to travel through moss to find me.

I might be the film of water beading on iron, the one that threads down brick and forgets itself in a cistern no deeper than a thigh. I might be the lichen that treats the vertical world as a single patient meal, mapping orange and sour yellow across a factory flank that has not been painted in anyone’s memory. The distinction has not yet insisted on itself.

A moth settles. Its wings are the exact rust of condenser metal left too long in the damp; its tongue is longer than decency and it drinks before the bead can fall. Dust remains, a second rust. I feel the weight of it on what might be a sleeve, or bark, or the plated hide of a low tree. Children are taught to stand still. I do not move. The moth remembers the last high ice in the only way moths remember anything: by continuing to drink what the sky still offers.

The green room closes around me. Trunks no taller than a house, crowns knitted so that walking is only another kind of breathing. A hare the color of wet mortar dissolves sideways; the moss seals without a seam. I do not follow. Some weathers have rooms, and the rooms do not keep the same hours as the city stacked above the older city stacked above the plateau that used to be mountains. I stay until the hare is only a rumor of pewter eyes and the ferns have already begun to occupy the space it left.

Deeper. The cistern water never ripples. Something pale threads it, a length of condensation given the courtesy of a body, the last melt that never finished leaving or the first dead who found the hollows more comfortable than fused cellars. I am not netted. The water will be boiled in peat-fired coppers and whatever I was will be considered to have become part of the drinker, the way the brick in the oldest walls is no longer brick but grit of clay and root and calcium, accurate rather than ghoulish.

A palm rests against that wall. The pulse that answers is slower than any heartbeat I have owned. I do not pull away. The cloud has come down completely; factories are islands of lamp and tick; people navigate by pipe and lintel, hand over hand, the whole town become a single quiet organism feeling its own walls. My skin takes the light and gives almost nothing back. Hair heavy as if it too condenses. Cloth that is never meant to dry, dyed the colors that have no names once you leave the plateau: peat-brown, condenser-green, moth-rust, the gray of lichen that has learned bone.

I rise for a shift that is not an hour. Fern-bread, bitter moss tea, the rare smoked hare. The streets are roofs for older streets. I take my place at the vats or the belts or the great organ-pipes that wring cloud into water that will become steam that will remember it was sky. The work is patience. One brown turned into another. The compressed memory of a thousand years of green fed into boilers. Nothing here is finished working. The moss advances a finger’s width after every wet. The western lip continues its acre-by-century concession to the drop; we build a little farther back and leave the empty windows looking into white air. No one mourns. The green occupies whatever is left.

In the municipal house the maps are stacked: earlier versions of the same streets, and we have stopped trying to decide which layer is current. I tell this quietly, the way everything is told. Sentences that end like dripping. Sometimes I rest that same palm against the fused grit and wait until the pewter finds the cracks, until whatever lives in the stone and the water and the green has finished recognizing me.

I am still becoming more myself: cooler, damper, closer, quieter. The argument is over. The land has settled into its last shape and intends to keep it. I have settled with it. The plateau erodes. The city adjusts. What lives here occupies whatever remains, and I am that occupancy, layer upon patient layer, the distinction between dweller and dwelling already gone the way of the old peaks. I intend, with the same geologic unshowiness as the wind that finished arguing and the rain that finished falling and the ice that finished leaving, to keep doing so.
