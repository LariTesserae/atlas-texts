The first sensation is not sight. Sight comes later, after the eyes have stopped insisting on edges that are no longer there. It is the heat that finds you, rising through the packed silt into the bones of the feet, a held-hand warmth that does not fluctuate. It has been waiting. You stand in a channel no wider than a stride, water to the ankles, and the formations lean in from every side until the idea of a horizon dissolves. The pewter pane overhead is not sky so much as the memory of one, wrung of motion. There is no arrival because the basin does not admit arrivals; whatever you were before has already begun to unfasten.

You try to move. Three steps are impossible. A cauliflower boss blocks the left, a fused column the right; draperies of flowstone hang so close they brush the shoulder with the same wet patience they have used on stone for longer than counting. The air itself occupies. It condenses on the lips, on the inner wrist, on the nape where hair (if there was hair) has already begun to flatten and gleam. The mineral smell is not incoming; it is the medium. A ghost of sulfur hangs without arriving, the way the drips fall without becoming events.

You notice the small lives first because they do not notice you. Pale crustaceans turn in the clearer pools with the exact unhurried interval of the water finding the next lip. Their translucence matches the new calcite so perfectly that for a long while you mistake them for the stone continuing itself. A thumb-sized thing rolls in a deeper pocket; the dimple it leaves is forgotten before the surface stills. You watch until watching itself feels like an interruption. The amphibia lie half-buried in the channels, skins the same sheen as the draperies that roof them. When one shifts it is only water deciding on a new hollow. Their unused eyes are dark commas in the dusk. You feel an answering stillness begin in your own joints.

Deeper pockets hold the longer shapes, bone-pale, flattened, easing by a hand’s-width toward the hottest seep over spans the basin does not measure. They leave no mark. You find yourself counting their absences instead: a ledge kept clear of crust, a channel slightly more open than the rest. Time here is the space between drips. You stop counting.

The old interruptions appear the way the small lives did: gradually, as the eyes accept the brown dusk. Three stones in a rough triangle, edges rounded by the same water that has rounded everything. Half-swallowed steps. The ladle on its ledge, wood gone almost mineral, pale and dense as if it had been waiting to forget its origin. You sit on the steps because sitting is the only motion that does not announce itself. The treads receive you as they have received the later crust. Your skin, you realize, has already taken the perpetual gleam. Thin veils of mineral have begun to cede themselves across the thighs, the shoulders. It is not invasion. It is recognition.

You do not speak. Speech would be an event, and the place has set events aside. Breath becomes the condensing air. Pulse settles into the interval of the films sliding over already-wet surfaces. A hand extends, not because you decide but because the basin has room for one more considered relocation. The ladle lifts. It dips into a pool whose temperature is the inside of a mouth. The water is allowed to become part of what holds it. The hand returns to stillness. The ladle is set down exactly where it was, as if the hand had only stepped away a moment that has now lasted the lifetime of the stone.

You are one of the three, or the four; it is difficult to be certain because you occupy the same visual register as the formations. You recline against the wall where the weeping is gentlest. Mineral continues its slow claim, but there is no hurry and no resistance. You were not always this. The steps, the stones, the ladle are the last evidence of shaping, of a time when hands still made pauses. That time ended so gradually no one among you marked the ending. You simply became the equilibrium. You have not left because leaving would be a change. You have not died because dying, too, would be an event.

The crustaceans turn. The amphibians wait. The long pale shapes ease another hand’s-width. You lift the ladle or you do not. The walls weep. The air condenses. The mineral bodies press close. You are only another way the place has of remaining exactly itself: finished, glistening, occupying the spaces not yet claimed, a held breath that no longer needs to be released.

Nothing larger lives here. Nothing smaller is missing. You remain.
