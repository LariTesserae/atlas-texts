I arrive already wet. Not from rain—there is none—but from the habit of the air itself, that sweet thickness wrung out just enough by the mountain’s warm shoulder. The peat takes my first step with that soft, released-breath sound, and before the second print can form the first is already a shallow dark coin filling from below. I do not look down yet. The bowl is speaking, and I am still deciding whether I have come to listen or to be added to the sentence.

At first I think I am the visitor the afternoon wind has always been: late, expected, playing the fluted mouths for the length of a breath and then gone. I feel the pull of that role—the clean arrival, the roar with holes in it, the departure that leaves the dripping louder by contrast. But the wind does not stay to match its breathing to the heavier drops. I do. The interval between one fat strike and the next begins to occupy my chest. It is not empty. It has weight, a patient pressure, the same pressure the mist uses to keep a sound half a second after the stone has finished with it. I stand in the center and the falls stitch me in; warmth comes up through the wet and down from the ordinary sky; I am accompanied without being joined. That could be enough. It is not yet what I am.

The webbing finds my ankles. Pale, slow silver, neither root nor mycelium, it maps me as another gradient of damp. Signals travel: a leaf on the far side of the catchment plant lifts a fraction because something in me has begun, very slightly, to flag. I had thought myself separate. The thought yellows over the course of a long minute and takes another minute to let go. I am not the webbing, not exactly. I am the place where it thickens, the reluctant knot that pulses on a cycle older than the wind’s daily visit. Memory lives here that has forgotten whether it was once an offering or a root that lost its way. The twist of bleaching fiber in the cleft is my discarded skin, or my flag, or the last thing a vanished hand tied so the bowl would remember a name I no longer use.

I taste the flowering tree through that knot. Its bark the color of wet ash is warm even now, holding the day’s light the way the stone holds heat. It opens one ashamed white blossom, then waits. I finish tasting before the next arrives. Seven in a year is conversation enough; I do not hurry it. Height was never necessary. The world comes to the surface. Water will always come to the surface.

Sometimes I am also the length that withdraws along a new seam in the hanging garden. Segmented like a thought that decided to have joints, the exact color of wet limestone, no larger than a forearm. Moisture-sensitive pits drink mist and the sound of the falls at once. When the rock becomes an instrument I still completely, listening as a form of feeding, then the garden settles its own weight over the absence. I am not hiding. I simply have no reason to be continuous. You could watch a week and see me only twice. The third time you would understand.

The pale thumbnail crab is a scout I send now and then, the same limestone color, lifting over a rim of moss to test whether the circulating gyre in the deepest pit has changed its mind. It reports by disappearing. The membrane creature folded like damp linen against the tree-fern’s column of old leaf-bases is another of my slower selves: walked by the redistribution of its own water, unfolding one pale frond a season to taste the particular sweetness when a blossom opens. I do not compete. Nothing stands near enough.

I have accepted the notches too clean to be weather, the lips too even. They were cut once with something harder than fingernails and softer than steel, then left for a thousand wet seasons. I wept over them until they belonged. If there was ever a kneeler, a carver, a bringer of fiber, that distinction has dissolved. What remains is the profession of change: the channel deepening by a width no single life will see, the leaf yellowing over a week and taking another week to fall, the mist thickening and thinning on a rhythm the wind cannot hurry.

I am not the great plants, though I hold their distances with them—the tree-fern’s single horizontal thought, the catchment doors loudly draining one into the next, the cliff that looks like one organism wearing another. I occupy the empty air they refuse to fill. I am the interval that learned how to stay. The measured pause. The half-second the mist keeps. The chord that never resolves. The waiting that is already loud.

Stand long enough and the falls stitch you into the same cloth. Your breathing finds the heavier drops. You are not joined, only accompanied. The place does not end. Neither do I. I simply continue to weep, and to wait, and to be the slowness with which a room can be lush with almost no one in it and still be full. The mountain never quite learned how to be alpine. I never quite learned how to leave.
