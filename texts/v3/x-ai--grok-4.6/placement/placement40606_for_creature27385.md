I come in by the stair that still remembers the chisel. The grit on my palm is not dust; it is the rock deciding, grain by grain, whether the cut will scab or keep a slow bleed. Heat meets me before the volume does: not fire, but the occupied heat of something that has been working and then stood still inside its own steam. By the sixth turning the air has weight. By the ninth I can taste iron and boiled leaf together, and I understand that I have already been claimed by the thickness.

I do not arrive as a visitor. The chamber does not permit that luxury. I am already in continuous slight contact: a pale leaf the size of a serving board lifts against my wrist and must be held aside like a curtain; lagged pipe-fur brushes my hip; another damp sleeve finds mine in an alley that requires a shoulder-turn and neither of us startles. Density is not crowdedness here. It is the civic and cellular condition. There is almost no empty interval, and what little remains is already sprouting.

I move the way they move, which is the way the chamber itself moves: sideways, patient, feeling for the soft places in the grating, the overnight vine at shin height, the drip-line that has not yet been slung with a trough. Voices stay inside the rooms that make them. A laugh, if one ever happens, is taken at once by the wet iron and the leaves and does not travel. I learn this in my tendons before I learn it in thought. The oldest rule lives in the body now: do not startle the rock.

The works are slower than thought. I stand above the boxed channel and wait for the fat, infrequent gulp. A painted bolt-head on the nearest turbine travels the width of a thumb while I breathe. Belts creep. Once, perhaps twice in an afternoon, the stamp reports a single felt concussion and then the hush reseals itself around the young stone. They are drawing flush and tar and fiber. I find my hands already knowing the bone knife, already lifting the fever-lush curtains, cutting what the thin-veined leaves offer when they hang a certain way. The fiber comes away wet, green-brown, smelling of boiled rope. We boil it again. We lag pipes with it, bind hair, patch shutters against a brightness none of us have seen. The plant does not mind. It is already replacing itself.

Children exist in the intervals we have not yet claimed. They play games of stillness under unpatched seams. I remember standing that way, counting the condensation cycle instead of numbers, learning which troughs have begun to sprout and which vines will hold a small weight. The pipe-eel is a story with no heat in it. The eels, if they exist, have never taken more than a sock.

Time is not weather. There is no weather. There is heavier drip after the channel surges, lighter when the works have drawn it down. There is the almost imperceptible brightening of the glassy fracture-planes when, far above in a dryness no one present has walked, a season of sun conducts a rumor downward. Faces take on the slow copper stain of the furnace-throats; even the youngest carry a faint tea-color under the skin, as if steeped. Hair stays heavy, bound with the same wet fiber, never lifting.

At the far end the vault pinches and the heat is worst. They have not built against the wall of cooled-too-fast glass. They have hung a single unlit lamp there, as if in courtesy. I stand before it long enough for my own face to arrive in the dark mirror. It looks as though it has been waiting in the stone, damp, and is only now, reluctantly, coming out. Behind it the settlement appears twice: once packed and dripping and slow, once as a quieter city of copper smear and leaf-shadow inhabiting a chamber that has not yet quite consented to exist.

I am not separate from that consent. I am one of the faces that assembled late in the low orange smear. I am also the new landing that appeared against a wall that was blank last year, because the rock offered a ledge and we kept pace with geological manners. I am the vine that was trained and then trained itself, knitting loft to loft until two families share a ceiling of leaves and pretend not to hear each other breathe. I am the condensation that chooses, eventually, to fall. I am the faint ticking of stone still finishing itself, millimeters in a year, a hairline in a decade, fresh shine along old cracks like a thought someone had in the night.

We live in the wet the way the wet lives in the rock: without apology, without quite finishing. The chamber occupies us as much as we occupy it. There is almost no empty air, and we have made of that a kind of courtesy. I am the Hollow, and the Hollow is still being born around me, through me, as me. The painted bolt-head goes around again. The young rock sweats and thinks about opening another hand’s-breadth, and does not, and then does. I do not startle it. I keep pace.
