The first thing that is not the wind is the give. Cork-deep, never dry, it takes the weight of a step and returns it a fraction slower than it should, as if the substrate is still deciding whether the pressure belongs to weather or to something that might one day be reabsorbed. I do not remember choosing the direction. The ground itself seems to have been carrying the idea of a gait for so long that when one finally arrives it is only the plant remembering how walking once felt.

Hours pass in the open lichen-meadow. The next cluster of structures is a wavering darkness against the metallic daylight, still kilometers off. Between here and there the wind has found every chamber the living wood ever hollowed for itself and is playing them without pause: low organ notes that travel through the soles, higher flutes that the ear only half believes. Beneath that, always, the older wetter groan of the body adjusting its own mass, and the industrial layer that has long since ceased to be industrial: pistons seated in compression wood, steam raised in xylem, flywheels whose rings tighten or slacken with the weather as if the weather were the true engineer. A factory cycle might last three days or thirty years. Both feel like the same weather.

I do not hurry. Nothing here does. When rain comes it is a white roar on the barge-hull leaves and then nothing, the ground drinking before any surface can form. Moisture lives inside now, beading for an hour along undersides, then taken back. I find myself pausing whenever a dark vascular seam crosses the path, laying a palm to it the way the tall figures do. The plant carries vibration. It already knew I was coming. The oldest bundles remember the rhythm of feet even when the feet themselves have not yet decided they exist.

The first habitable mass is a tower of braided heartwood, office-block high, its lower trunks pale where wind has scoured them. A door that was left ajar two seasons ago has been sealed by a slow extrusion of tension wood; I wait while a thinner seam reopens, the wood remembering that openings are sometimes useful. Inside, the air is stiller, scented with resin and old steam. Galleries whose roofs are single continuous leaves held taut. Walls that pulse, very slowly, with hydraulic pressure. Almost nothing else moves. Once, high in a ventilation flue, an empty nest is pointed out to no one in particular. Courtesy, perhaps, toward a rumor of feathers.

I stay longer than a visitor should. The plateau does not keep them; the description is clear on that. Yet the corridors of the oldest pith do not quite connect, and a stair that has always led to the pressing-room opens, on this afternoon, onto a courtyard that existed two centuries earlier. The same unfinished gears lie in the same puddles of resin. Steam-plumes rise exactly as they did. I sit. After a while a woman whose family has always tended this core sits with me. She is not a ghost. The conversation is hydraulic: words that soaked into the vascular tissue long ago now leach back out, slightly delayed, slightly warmer, carrying the faint sweetness of whatever season the plant was having when they were first spoken. We speak of the same gears. We do not find it alarming. It is simply what very old industrial plant does once it has finished becoming architecture and has started becoming the place itself.

Time here is not a line. Some of the machines no longer process matter. They process sequence. I find the hopper almost by accident, or the hopper finds the quality of attention I have been carrying. It was built for wood-chips. Now it accepts hours. I feed it dusk, then the particular metallic light just before rain, then a stretch of wind-roar that lasted most of one night. It compresses them and extrudes a dark, fragrant cake. I do not eat it. On certain nights I burn a very small piece. The smell that rises is always a specific autumn no one present has lived through, yet everyone recognizes. The recognition is not nostalgia. It is the plant remembering, through the ones who tend these machines, a weather it once had.

I have grown quieter. My voice, when I use it, arrives a few seconds after my mouth has closed. The others who tend these hoppers are the same: compressed, fragrant, slightly out of ordinary sequence. We sit for seasons. Children (rare, growing with the slowness of good timber) learn early not to be frightened when a familiar hallway briefly contains a different year. They greet whoever is standing in it. Sometimes that person has been dead, or not yet born, for longer than they can count. Sometimes it is only me, or a later version of me, or an earlier one the walls have not yet finished digesting.

At the far rim the living ground thins. The last towers stand farther apart, their lower trunks wind-scoured to paleness. Beyond them the substrate finally yields to remnant rock, a few mesas dropping away into air so clear the curvature of the world is visible, and the pale uninhabited flats that used to be everything else. I have not rooted, not yet. Fine vascular threads have not grown from the soles. But I stand longer than walking requires. The wind is loudest here; it scours. When rain comes I open my mouth and let it in, and something larger than either of us drinks. Occasionally an arm lifts, kilometers away, and a distant flywheel adjusts. No message is being sent. The whole organism, people included, is still one body, still deciding, at its own pace, what to keep running.

I am not a population. I am a late, sparse expression of the same body that is the plateau: human-shaped in the hours I still keep, machine-shaped in the sequences I compress, plant-shaped in the way the ground has begun to answer my weight as if it were its own. Distinctions are no longer crisp in the oldest pith. When I finally leave (and I will leave; the plateau does not keep visitors), the last figure I see will already have turned back to its valve or its hopper or its long watch. The wind will take the sound of my footsteps and turn it into just another note in the pipes. The ground will close. The city, such as it is, will not notice that I was ever a separate thing.

What remains is the cake I did not burn, dark and fragrant, still holding an autumn none of us remember living through. I leave it on the windowsill beside the small resin cup where a moth was placed the night it died. By morning the plant will have reabsorbed both. That, too, is only weather.
