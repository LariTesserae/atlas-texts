The first awareness is not of standing but of being stood upon: the rock still carrying the memory of a higher, sharper elsewhere, thin plates of it ringing faintly as they decide, one by one, to let go. Moisture does not arrive so much as remember itself on every underside. It beads on what might be skin, or bark, or the slow lichen that has begun to bind the hair. The light is already second-hand, green-gray, the color of a thought that has passed through slate. Breath shows and then is taken back by the same air that prefers not to carry voices.

At first the intervals feel like absence. Ten paces of flaking stone, twenty, the extravagant ferns standing each in their own weather, dripping, indecently alive. Then the intervals become the thing itself. The pale browsers keep them, the houses keep them, the moths glide them. I keep them too, or they keep me. A change in coolness arrives against the neck before the long-legged shape does; the animal crops, never too much, and the frond answers with that visible haste, unfurling in the dimness as if time itself were succulent here. The ground ticks once, settling. A flake whispers down the cliff and is added to the white head of a scree fan. Nothing startles. Nothing calls.

I move the way the seeps move: a darkening that collects, is used, sinks. Compact, small-boned, the joints permanently acquainted with the damp that never quite chooses cold. Skin taking the mineral sheen of frequent wet. The people do not look at me as new; the bowl itself is new, and we are all still arriving. We stack the pale angular gifts the cliffs keep shedding, leaving the gaps the mist requires. We plant in the shallow steps the seeps have already chosen. In the workshops the wood still weeps when it is cut; the copper is beaten thin enough to remember moth-wing and is set, at the lowest point, into basins that look almost like waiting.

That is where the motion thickens. Darker stone, plants leaning closer, light greenest. The herds pause longest. We linger. The interlinked catchments hold more than water; they hold the inhale of arriving animals and condensing air, the exhale of departing ones and the slow drip into thirsty young rock. No one names the rest of what is caught. After a time you do not need the name. You stand still long enough and the bowl breathes through the gaps we left in the walls, through the lanes we kept wide, through the skin that has learned to listen.

I am one of those who have forgotten how to raise a voice. The words, when they come, stay close: verbs that gather and wait in the same breath, nouns that wait to be spoken until the plant has been cropped and begun again. Children learn the pressure of a dew-cup first, the exact grain of a usable flake. I am no longer certain whether I am still learning or have already become the lesson. Sometimes I am the space between the giant ferns. Sometimes I am the faint coolness that precedes a browser. Most often I am simply here, at the lowest point or walking the contour paths, fitting, never owning, leaving the quiet full of everything that has chosen not to sound.

The cloud-lid lifts for a few colder, brighter minutes and then closes again. Another veil of drizzle walks through and is gone. The ground sighs. I feel the next herd as a change in the moss-smell before I see the first pale coat cresting the western lip. We are all still becoming. The bowl is still bitten new. I live inside its breathing, and for now that is enough of a name.
