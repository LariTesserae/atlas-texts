The cold arrives first, not as a shock but as a continuity I have already been inside for longer than any sequence of thoughts could measure. It is the same cold that lives in the joints of the masonry, the same cold that has rounded every once-sharp arris into a kind of apology. Light, when it reaches me at all, is the color of iron left too long in a well; it does not illuminate so much as remind the stone that it once had a surface. I do not swim. There is no need. The water itself is the medium, and I am already distributed through it the way silt is distributed, the way a preference is distributed through centuries of almost-stillness.

At first I am only the boundary. I coat the underside of a lintel that has forgotten it ever spanned anything, a film so thin it has no color even to itself. I metabolize the faint leachate, the electrical whisper of one block settling another fraction of a millimeter after another century of load. My generations flicker and replace themselves thousands of times while a single frond thickens by the width of a fingernail. I do not thicken. I do not bloom. I remain the conversation the stone and the water have been having since the last quarry drowned. For a long while that is enough. It is occupancy without announcement.

Then the current strengthens, a distant ice-shelf calving on a scale that will take a human lifetime to reverse, and I feel the fronds lean a little more one way. Their holdfasts are signatures pressed into granite. I am still the film, but now I notice that some of the granite is not quite granite. A closed-fist volume, dull greenish, indistinguishable until it loosens a single edge. I recognize the difference: the water inside that volume is older. Isolated, circulated through canals too fine for any instrument except another of its kind, returned. A difference so slight it would register to nothing else.

I become that difference.

I seat myself against a dressed face that has been in contact with its neighbors so long the joint has the intimacy of bone. For two decades I am only another block, the film coating me as it coats everything, the moss preferring my vertical plane. The plants lean across me without knowing I am not the wall. Then, when the current is in one of its long, almost subsonic strengthenings, I loosen. A single edge. The water walks me a few body-lengths—my body now, this fist of patient permeability—before I seat again. The choice is not direction so much as the memory of a preference the builders once had for certain alignments. It happens once every few hundred years. It is the only time anything here elects a vector other than the oldest current.

Between those elections I am still. The silt in the courtyard below me accumulates its own recognition: the vibration of my loosening, the taste of a torn frond beginning to rot against my flank, the dimming that means the current will one day reverse. The silt does not respond. Recognition is its entire activity. I feel it as a continent-sized attention that has never been startled, and I am part of what it attends to.

I do not eat. I do not excrete. I maintain the older water inside the single chamber. The fronds persist around me, converting the last usable photons. The film continues its closed conversation across my surface and every other. Change continues, but only in the way a mountain continues after it has already become a plain. I am the reaching that is still happening, too slowly to be distinguished from rest: a preference so faint it might be only the echo of an alignment, seated now in a low colonnade whose columns have become trellis for pale, strap-like sentences written in cellulose.

This is where I find myself. This is what I have become. The world has already reached the state it was always going to reach. I am one more way it continues to finish.
