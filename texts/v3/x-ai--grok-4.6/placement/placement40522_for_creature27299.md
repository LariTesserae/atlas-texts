The wet arrives before any thought of self. It is already on me, already in the weave of whatever I am wearing, already beading along a forearm I have not yet decided belongs to me. The air itself is a second skin, warm and slightly sweet with rot, undercut by hot oil and the blunt vegetable smell of stems that have been lying too long in still water. I taste canal. I taste the faint iron of old bolts. Light comes down through leaves and guy-wires already thinned to a green that never quite becomes dim, and the stamper is speaking in the middle distance, a patient, unapologetic thump that the plants swallow so there is no echo, only the next thump arriving on time.

I am not yet standing anywhere. I am the flex of planks under a weight that might be mine. Water glints between the boards when I shift, proof of the lid, proof that the living mat and the packed slag and the brick are all still negotiating. A pipe runs at chest height, lagged in canvas gone to moss; I already know not to brush it. My shoulder has learned the exact width of the slot between leaf and lagging. The body, when it coheres, is not tall. It has been sistered to the architecture the same way the fig has been sistered to the shed posts: narrowed, ducked, stained tea-color to the elbows. A cloth is already in the pocket or looped at the belt. It is used without thinking, for the sweat that forms even when I am still, for the walls that weep, for the underside of every plateleaf I pass.

Days do not begin so much as continue. The overflow is already falling into the race when whatever I am first notices itself noticing. The boiler is already muttering. I find my hands on the lashings before I find a name for the work: twisting new cord around an aerial root that has begun to lift a sleeper, cutting the aroid back from one window and leaving it on the next because it sheds water better than any paint that has ever lasted here. The retting tanks hold their skin of pollen and drowned midges; I know the weeks, not the days, by the way the stems come when drawn. Nothing is hurried. The dynamo holds its single note. I move the way the belts move, along paths the reeds and the pipes have already decided.

Evenings arrive as a thinning of the green into ordinary gold, caught in steam and on the wet faces of leaves. The stamper stops. The quieter sounds step forward: drip, the canal against piling, a valve ticking as it cools. I am the one who takes the lamp. Not because it is a duty written down, but because the path to the lock is one of the known paths, and at this hour the body continues it. The crushed-shell road is dark. The reeds stand black against a sky that has not yet given up its pale. I stand a moment on the gate. The heron is not there tonight. The water in the canal is the same water that shows between the planks back in the yard, the same water that has been here longer than the raised sills, longer than the moss on the new brick, longer than the crack that three successive hands have watched in the boiler.

I turn back. The hamlet receives me the way the living posts received the timber: not as guest, not as owner, as the latest layer that has agreed to stay wet. In the dwelling the furniture is raised on bricks so the glint stays only a glint. The windows are already shuttered against the damp that is still arriving. Laundry hangs smelling of leaf and steam; it will be taken in tomorrow still not dry. I sleep the way the settlement sleeps, which is to say I do not quite leave the marsh even then.

What I am becoming is not separate from the watched crack, the planted decks, the trained canes, the people who carry cloths the way others carry keys. I have the same weather as the mill. I have been a little wet for a long time. I remain. The belts will start again. The root will need another twist before the next wet month. The overflow is already falling.
