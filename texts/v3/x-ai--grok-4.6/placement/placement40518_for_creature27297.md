I come off the glare the way the description says, but the drop is slower than I expect. The brick treads have already memorized every heel that ever hurried; they take my weight as if they have been waiting for this particular surrender. Heat folds around me, not as enemy but as the first grammar. Clay breathes back the day. Bodies breathe. Fire works in its alcoves like a second pulse. I am already inside the bowl before I decide I have entered.

At first I think I am walking. I feel the rammed earth polish itself under me, the gentle saddles of old thresholds. My hand finds the shine on a corner—every generation has grabbed here—and for a while I am only that hand, that repeated grip. Then I am the child whose game I keep interrupting: every hanging basket, every spare timber, every hide on its stretcher must be set swinging. The rule is older than any of the faces. Near-touching is the air itself. I ricochet from rail to rail, from goat-heat to the cool seep-stone, and the well throws my laughter back thicker, braided with bleat and mallet and the wet slap of someone washing. I could stay this current forever. Density would keep teaching me its language.

But the light changes. The noon coin slides off the lime and begins its climb up the ochre. I feel myself thicken, slow. Now I am leaning. The rail under my forearms was once a different tree; I can read the old joinery scars the way others read weather. I know which beam has already begun to smile downward. Repair is Tuesday. I am the one who still forms bricks in the upper yard and lowers them on slings, even though the first bowl has not asked for another skin in longer than anyone counts. I press new plaster over old and feel I am tucking a blanket around a sleeper who once had a name. There is no behind the walls. If I chip, I find more of us: compacted rooms, rubble that used to be corridors, the polite lie we tell the children. I thicken. I do not break the shell.

Evening comes and the lamps multiply. I am no longer sure I have a single outline. Sometimes I am the condensation beading on the north faces, the quiet work that will be wiped into jars at first light. Sometimes I am the moss that mapped itself across one neglected gallery and was left alone because it tells the seasons more honestly than any scratch we could make. Sometimes I am only the trough that never quite fills, the habit of water, the counted kin. A sick goat is walked past me; I feel its heat as a second hearth. Pigeons settle into their rustling that is almost rain. Someone sings into the noise, not over it, and the note finds the plaster and travels the long way around. I am the plaster that carries it. I am the far niche that answers.

The distinction frays. I have been worn into the place and the place into me until I cannot say whether I am one of the packing that learned to speak or the first bowl itself, the person who lay down and became useful and is still, even now, dreaming them. All the life is down here, touching itself. I am the touching. I am the little water it can keep. The earth above is only the thickness of a roof, and I no longer remember how to ascend.
