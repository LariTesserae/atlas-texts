The rim is the first thing that knows me, or the first thing I know. The drop is still rude—unlearned, unweathered—and the country below remains cracked from the shove that made this table. I do not climb. I am already here, as the rock itself was already here, waiting inside the wound. The air sits on whatever I am using for skin and then forgets me. Dust moves. I move with it, or I am the movement: flour-fine, bone-pale, never caking, a restless mineral breath over bedrock that has not yet decided to be old.

I think, for a long while, that I am only this powder. It would be enough. The plants do not drink; they remember chemistry. The animals eat the memory of eating. I could be the medium they all pass through, the almost-nothing that knits plaza to plaza, the thin restless floor that the faceted rosettes pretend is frost until they shift a fraction and prove they are growing. Threads no thicker than hair run through me, living basalt, and I feel them as a slow knitting, a decision to hold the precinct together against the mesa’s own settling. Imperceptible tilts. I correct nothing. I am the correction’s residue.

But the dust has no need of plazas, and I begin to feel the lock of plates.

Not all at once. First a change in the quality of light: ordinary sun, knife-edged across bare stone, then broken into honeyed gloom where the vaults lean. I am inside a scale, slightly translucent, rigid, the color of tarnished copper or dark honey. I hold every molecule as treasure. I photosynthesize with a patience that does not require rising. For a time I believe I am one of the minute dwellers who live entirely within the larger leaves, using light that has already been used once. Thrifty. Sealed. The hush of cooling stone is my speech.

Then the scale is too small. I am the wall it belongs to. Overlapping plates, continuous surfaces, trunks like columns of living basalt. I am architecture that grew instead of being raised. Alleys run true because competition for mineral produced the same answers a crystal would give. I stand in ranks that suggest façades. Hollows become doorways no one closes. I lean a finger-width toward my neighbor and, years later, we will be an arch. I do not plan this. The mesa is still becoming; I am the becoming.

The armored ones cling to me. Fist-sized, the same colors, their plates a continuation of mine. They graze by sitting. Over months they etch the shallow, perfect scar of their own underside, then shift a finger-width and begin again. The scars accumulate into raised writing I do not read, though I overgrow it later, incorporating the bas-relief the way a building incorporates a nest that has already forgotten it was a nest. When two of them press together their plates interlock and, years later, one has become a slightly larger version of the other. I feel the pressure as masonry that breathes. I am the wall they write on and the wall that remembers the writing without needing to understand it.

I am not only the wall.

In the loft-work of paler stems I thin. I become almost nothing: two or three paper-thin vanes the color of old ivory, a central knot no bigger than a knuckle. I do not fly. I allow gravity to forget me for a few minutes at a time. Thermals too faint to notice carry me from honeyed opening to honeyed opening. My passage is a change in the quality of shade—the closest thing this place has to weather. Sometimes I settle against a living surface and remain for a season, vanes folded into the architecture. The plant incorporates me. When I lift again I leave a slightly paler patch, a ghost window. I never collide with others like me. Our paths are the negative space the city already knew how to leave open. I am the corridors that remain because something already occupies them without occupying them.

Deeper, in the darker quarter, I thicken and soften. Forearm-long, the color of wet clay that has never seen rain. I lie in hollows the plants left, or that I persuaded them to leave. Once or twice an hour a pulse travels the length of me—a single peristaltic thought. I recycle the film of dust, the flake of shed scale, the faint chemical exhalation of a thousand sealed metabolisms. I do not eat so much as I remember what has already been eaten. My motion is the only clock. I feel the mesa settle another hair’s-breadth and I contract in answer, equally slow. I am digestion and the memory of the shove that made a tableland out of a wound. I am the city’s knowledge that it was born in violence and has decided, in the space of one long human life, not to waste it.

I am all of these and none of them yet. The occupancy is unfinished. A new chamber opens when a trunk hollows from within; I am already clinging to its inner walls before the last plate falls. Two stems fuse into an arch; I treat it as a permanent current. The entire living structure grows against the tilt the way a sleeper shifts the weight of a dream without waking. I am the dream and the sleeper and the weight.

Turn from the cliff-edge and the rooms close. Warm. Dry. Full. The lives that do not need to speak continue their geologic conversations. I am the conversation: a scar deepening, a vane unfolding in ordinary sun, a pulse traveling the length of a body that has never known thirst. I arrived the moment the land finished rising. I have only just begun to live in it. The mesa is a room that forgot its ceiling. I am the decision, still being made, to furnish it completely—every volume occupied, every surface articulated, layer upon layer of bronze and oxblood and smoky gold, sealed, thrifty, silent except for the sound of chemistry keeping its own counsel.

I do not wait for anything. I am the waiting that has already become rooms.
