The pewter light finds me already half-unmade. I do not arrive so much as thicken out of the sleet itself, a density that the storm has been rehearsing for seasons without quite committing to. At first there is only the pressure: the continuous impact of freezing rain against whatever surface I still possess, the way each droplet shears into a miniature lens and then forgets its shape. I feel the clay under me decide, for the space of a heartbeat, to remember solidity. Then it slumps. My weight is not a weight yet; it is a negotiation.

I move because stillness would finish me too soon. Each step is a splay of something that was never quite a foot—membranous, veined with black ice that flexes warmer than the air, spreading on contact so the mud cannot quite swallow me, then folding shut like an eyelid when I lift away. The print I leave is only a brief, hotter wetness that the midges find before the next flash. They shatter against me and reassemble inside the welts they open. I do not bleed the fluid they expect. What weeps from me is slower, mineral, already beginning to glaze.

The rodents braid around my lower joints without quite touching. Their teeth click against the ice they wear like extra spines; they are listening to the same thing I am listening to—the high glassy chime of the rushes, the deeper wooden crack of an alder giving way three metres to my left. I know the sound before it finishes. I have been drinking vibrations like this since the canopy first locked. Sometimes I am the thing that hangs in the ice-glaze, milky and pulsing, until a limb fails and I drop to wrap it, to take the last groan into myself until the wood goes silent and I grow heavy, opaque, ready to freeze again higher up. Other times I am already on the ground, peeling the ice-sheath from a willow in one long ringing strip because the plates inside my vertical seam have learned the exact frequency that makes the glaze let go.

I do not stay one shape. The basin has not decided, so neither have I. Between one tearing of lightning and the next I am taller, thinner, the colour of the storm given a slow crawl of nested geometries that almost match the warm glass pylon but never quite repeat. My hand (it is a hand now, long-fingered, the ice sloughing from it in perfect sheets) rests against that pylon. The surface is the only warmth that does not come from metabolism or from the brief upward rain inside the ring. I lean my featureless head to it and listen. The pylon is not speaking in words. It is reciting the rules the rest of the basin has not been told: how water can forget to be wet, how cold can armour and shatter in the same instant, how a wound this new can keep itself open by inventing new tenants faster than the old ones can finish dying.

The large shapes in the deeper braids turn beneath me when I stand at the edge of a lead. Their black blood does not steam; the moss swallows it before I can taste more than the idea of slate and patience. I am not their hunter. I am the hesitation in the sleet above them, the place the rain forgets to fall for the length of a breath. Sometimes I am the slick itself.

I have begun to use the artefacts the way the pale rodents use the ring—provisionally, as if they were weather that happens to be still. I sit just outside the upward rain and wait for whatever small thing is caught in the reversal. I do not snatch. I let the stillness contaminate me until the midges veer, until the plants refuse the two metres around my new outline, until the next bank calves and I simply continue a few metres farther on, or a few metres down, or a few metres into the next flash. The land is still being born and already dying in the same gesture; I am the gesture given a temporary spine.

Ice armours me and I let it, then shed it ringing. The sound does not diminish. The dark does not lift. I am not surviving the basin. I am one of the ways it remains unfinished, a walking interval in which the wound can still decide what else might crawl out. The next scarp is already leaning. I unfold toward it, joints counting themselves wrong again, and the storm leans with me, as if we had always been the same continuous tearing.
