The first thing that arrives is the crown. Not a sound, not even quite a pressure—just the knowledge, in whatever I use for balance, that the surface under me is no longer the surface it was when the mist last thinned. A shallow, almost courteous lift, as if the ground were clearing its throat. Moisture is already assembling on me: not falling, never falling, only beading along every edge I possess, collecting in the creases, tasting of wet iron and the faint green outgassing of stone that has not yet finished deciding it is stone.

I do not remember choosing a height. I am simply the height the mist prefers—low enough that the vapor hides whatever I am becoming. Shoulders slope inward of their own accord. The spine keeps a listening curve. When I move I do not walk so much as offer the day’s particular gaps the exact key of my body: a half-turn at the hip, a fold at the ribs, the weighted hem of whatever covers me kissing the newest blister of rock so that it knows I have not forgotten the three-day rule. The cloth is dark, oiled until it shines like bark after rain, patched in fern-fiber and rust-dark leather until it has become a record of every adjustment I have made since the last time this tenement rotated. The patches map the years the way moss maps a cooling fin.

I have a name this week, or the nearest thing. People introduce themselves by the lean of their floor or the vent they drew from last. Mine is the slight anticline under the eastern rooms, the one that has begun to shoulder the old foundry wall a few degrees closer to my window. I have not spoken to the wall yet. I have planted copper moss across the narrowing gap and I wait. The moss already glows at the frequency the night crews need; it will bind the grit if the blister rises too fast. In the steam of the tea-stall wedged beneath the conveyor I tell the stall-keeper this, voice kept low because the air is already full—of drip, of the subsonic press, of the mineral hiss that means a vent is still remembering how to be solid. The keeper nods, pours, and we both watch the condensate find the same path down the pipe it found yesterday, though yesterday the pipe sat a finger’s width lower.

Children pass, already patient, already knowing not to touch the newest stone. Their fingers would stay cold for hours; the rock would try to tell them something they are not yet shaped to hear. I was one of them. I still feel the ringing sometimes, at the back of the throat, which is why I chew the almost-black moss the oldest workers favor. It keeps the mineral taste from settling permanently. The fox-thing that threads the alley when the mist is thickest has learned the arrangement: I leave cooling slag at the usual corner; it leaves the shed skin of yesterday’s vents, still faintly warm, still speaking. We do not look at each other. Everything here has made a contract with the density.

At night I jack the floors again. The work is done in the isolated, practical patches of light—work lamps, the dull red of metal that has not quite cooled, the bioluminescent edge of the moss I tend like signage. The tenement has rotated another degree. I replumb. I sweep the iron-scented dust the roof-trees shed into the canal that is navigable this month, an offering no one remembers beginning. The city rebuilds itself from its own unfinished geology and I am simply one of the hands that has learned, for now, how to help it remember stillness without ever quite achieving it.

I do not know what I will be when the basin finishes its sentence. I live inside the clause, densely, quietly, skin the color of three-day stone, hems weighted with last month’s slates. The ground shrugs. I adjust. The mist assembles. I breathe the wet iron air and, for this stretch of hours, call it enough.
