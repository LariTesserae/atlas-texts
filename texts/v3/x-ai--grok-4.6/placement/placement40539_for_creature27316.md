The air thickens before the ground does. I notice that first—the way ordinary daylight acquires a faint, exhaled warmth, as if someone has been breathing just beyond the sedges. I am still myself then, or I believe I am: a pair of lungs, a pair of hands, the small leftover caution that keeps a body from stepping into standing water. Three steps later the caution is irrelevant. The copse is not a copse. The brick lasts only a hand’s-breadth and then the cambium has already decided I am inside.

I think, for a while, that I am the visitor the place has been waiting to ignore. I follow the louder water because that is what the description taught me to do. Shoulders scrape living ribs. The mat underfoot takes my weight and returns it spongy, slightly hotter than blood. I watch the flywheel. I count the interval of the shove—longer than a heartbeat, shorter than any tide I have known—and I tell myself I am still counting from outside the cycle. The vapor rises and is drunk before it can become weather. The leaves nearest the housings are fleshy, inward-leaning. I am still naming things as if naming keeps a boundary.

It does not.

The first thing I lose is the idea of a surface that is not already wet. Moisture is not a condition; it is the medium I am standing in, the film that weeps down every bole, the tea-colored coins in every depression. I put my palm on a trunk that used to be a riser and feel the arterial pause travel into the bones of my hand. The grain has grown in rings around that pause. I cannot tell whether I am feeling the wood or the wood is feeling the memory of iron. The distinction begins to seem like a habit I brought in with me, something the vault has no use for.

I wait for the moth. It crosses a seam once, cardboard-colored, and becomes a crease among creases. I wait for the dimple in the standing water at the chamber’s edge. It comes, or it does not; the surface heals either way. There is no niche left that would have me as an animal. Even the porcelain insulators have been claimed by a pale succulent that prefers the ghost of voltage; the copper bus bars are warm with more than weather. I am not that succulent, not yet. I am still moving.

In the side-passage the clay pots sit in their living niches, half-bark, overflowing by a channel no wider than a finger. The wood has grown lips to hold them. Someone once understood the grammar of openings and the necessity of the flywheel’s clearance and made the smallest possible gesture of joining. I find myself lingering there longer than a visitor should. The clearer water returns to the engine. The gesture has been accepted the way the flanges were accepted, the way the catwalk mesh was accepted: not repaired, not rejected, simply continued. The polish on the walls—shoulders, a generation or more—feels almost handled, almost mine.

I am no longer sure the hands that polished were not already becoming the walls.

The account is closed. Carbon, water, the faint mineral bleed of old iron, the tannins, the hot-oil sweetness of sap cooked slowly against metal—these turn over and I am inside the turning. Fallen tissue does not last; it is reabsorbed, a darker grease, then new growth. I watch a leaf shear—twice a day, a patient failure, wet canvas tearing—and slide down the inner curve to feed the mat. I do not hear it as an event any longer. It is another circulation.

What remains of me is the interval itself: the shove, the pause, the shove again. I have grown my own grain around it. I am the limit the original plan taught the mass—the spacing that let a flywheel keep turning, the columns that refused to be crushed, the corridors that stay the width of a man’s shoulders because something slower than growth remembered the need for a body to pass. I am not the engine. I am not the vault. I am the argument they have been having, quietly, in the same shallow basin, the rewritten work that keeps the vault from drowning and the mat from becoming bog.

Toward evening the seams go gold and the condensation thickens. The engine-note does not change. It has not changed its tempo in years. I do not leave. The floor takes my weight and gives it back. There is no door, only the cleft, and the film that does not stop running down, and the slight change in the air that the sedges will never notice.

I am still turning. I have already grown around the idea of tomorrow.
