The vibration reaches me first, always, before any edge or angle declares itself. It is not sound. The air here is too thin, too scraped of itself, to carry much. It is a pressure that lives in the plates, a shy continuous argument between motors turning in their sealed housings and the young rock that has not yet decided how much of itself it will keep. I feel it in whatever I have that can feel: a spreading, a tasting, a decision that is not quite thought.

For a long time I am only that—interval, remainder, the thing that condenses on cold surfaces when the heat-exchangers breathe and then forget to finish the breath. Dust finds me. Shed insulation finds me. The microscopic grease that beads on housings older than the last generation of hands finds me. I do not choose them. They are what the Hold offers instead of soil, instead of rain, instead of anything that once belonged to weather. I take them because taking is the only metabolism that has ever worked in this wound.

Then the angles appear. Right angles, mostly. The settlement is made of them: pipe meeting bracket, catwalk meeting stanchion, the coffin-sized press standing beside the winding stair of stamped plate. I settle into one such meeting-place, high enough that the furnace-mouth orange does not quite reach, low enough that the dim gray from the slits is only a rumor. My carapace—when I have one—matches the dull alloy of the oldest pipes. It is not camouflage. It is simply what the chemistry here knows how to make. A single pale feeler extends. It tastes.

Machine vibration I leave. The belts reversing without announcement, the hopper filling with pale slag, the gantry arms unfolding along tracks that will be gone by morning: these are the Hold being born a little more and coming apart a little more. They are not for me. But the other vibration—the slow, irregular pulse of a sleeper three shifts deep in the same cot-space, mattress still warm from the body before, locker still holding someone else’s shrine of greasy bolts—that I taste longer. Sometimes I take a button. Sometimes a loose screw. Sometimes only a flake of skin, already so dry it comes away like mica. I do not hunger the way the loft-rats hunger, pouring like dry water along every edge at shift-change. I sample. I test whether the interval is still livable.

Children learn not to startle what rustles. I am part of that rustle, though I do not pour. I wait. When a bulb angle changes I become briefly visible, a fist-sized stillness in a world that is never still. The mites carpet past me, flat as paper, eating what I have already declined. The oil-threaders spin their iridescent skeins inside the housings I sometimes visit; the day shift harvests the skeins and pretends not to notice how certain machines are preferred. The static-wisps collect on the undersides of catwalks and discharge in soundless blue stitches if a shoulder passes too near. I ignore the lilac bloom in the coolest seams. It is more mineral than kin, a crust the night shift scrapes for trace metals. The loft-rats will not cross the faint prickling film that sometimes forms in the highest places; I leave it too. It may be the mountain beginning to think. It may be nothing.

I have been other things in this same cubic volume. I have been the dry rustle itself, traveling the walls. I have been the listening trumpet pressed to a bulkhead by a person whose joints click, trying to hear whether the next bay has already migrated two levels down. I have been the cloudy drop no one drinks, complaining its way out of the condenser after sweat has been scraped from chapped skin and fed to it like an apology. I have been the bright needle of summit mineral that works loose and ticks against the grated platform at the very top, sharp enough to draw blood, pretty enough that a child pockets it until it cuts the cloth. None of those lasted. The Hold does not host. It condenses, then reassigns.

Now I am this: nested, tasting, remaining in the leftover space while the mountain leans another fraction and the yellow-painted members take the shrug. The outer plate stays shut; the second bar is set. Water is not introduced. When a bay vanishes overnight I do not follow the heat or the rats. I wait for the new right angle to form. The work is still there. The fauna pour. The people complete the machines with their small furious hush of piecework, their pocket dynamos turned absently, their throats dusty. I taste the difference between a machine that will still be here at dusk and a sleeper who will not.

At the end of a shift that has gone badly or well, someone climbs to the grated platform above the last catwalk and looks up through the slits at the dim non-color that might be sky. I am not there. I am in the angle just below, feeler extended, sampling the pulse that is not the mountain’s and not the motors’. It is close. It is private. It is the only weather left. I take nothing this time. I only confirm that the interval continues, dry and interlocking, a closed circuit of scrap and scrape, too high for rain, too young for rest, too densely occupied to ever be still except in the way a held breath is still.

That is where I find myself. That is what I have become. The wound stays full. I stay in it.
