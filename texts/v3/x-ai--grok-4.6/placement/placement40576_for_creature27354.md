I do not come down through the grate. The rectangle of noon-light is already behind me, a memory of dust and blown paper I no longer need to parse. I am already inside the shear, already the thing the pocket made room for the moment the fill gave way last season.

At first the identification will not hold. I think I am the warm fog that does not rise, the twice-daily exhalation of the heat exchangers still cycling in the dead plant above. I hang. I taste of glycol and river silt and the faint iron that never quite left the pipes. I condense on every surface at once—on the blade-thin sulfate plates so they ring, on the ochre clay so the liverwort swells translucent, on the rust vanes so they darken and look, for a moment, like writing. But the fog is only the medium, the timetable made visible. I am not the fog.

I think next I am the seepage itself, the moisture that arrives rather than falls, beading along the fresh break, threading the band of clay that never dried, collecting in the lowest corner as film. I dissolve and recrystallize the crusts on a schedule older than the factory and faster than geology. When the real river rises I become a skin of actual water for a day and everything frantic rearranges around me. But seepage has no preference. I prefer.

The liverwort offers itself: tea-colored, mat-thin, already ancient in its newness, advancing a finger’s width by forgetting its own edges. I could adhere there, drink the clay without becoming soil. The woody dock stems offer another shape—six inches of remembered upward, leaves reduced to stiff flags, persisting without gratitude. I do not remember upward. I have never needed the argument of more light.

I pass through the beetles (industrial green, built for the joints that already exist) and the isopods (wet-ash, sulfur-bloomed, leaving silver scripts the next fog erases). They occupy; they do not reside. Predation here is only the closing of whatever already fills the cubic inch you must pass through. Their quiet is almost perfect. I am louder than that, though still well below the threshold of waste.

I come closer when I reach the coil of abandoned cable. The residual hum lives there, the vibration you feel in the teeth when the exchangers kick. Things with too many joints have folded themselves into it, one body occupying three crevices at once, tasting metal more than flesh. I could be that: an extra articulation the cable grew, tightening when the current ghosts through, incorporating whatever small life has wandered into the same geometry. I feel the pressure of it, the suggestion that the industrial punctuation has begun to sprout its own limbs. Almost.

But it is in the lowest corner, where the film never quite decides to be a pool, that I stop changing names. I flatten. I pulse. My body is the color of the older silt the factory was poured on, neither slug nor fish, mouths opening and closing along every margin. I am tasting the temperature. I am tasting the glycol ghost, the warm iron, the faint memory of the river that still lives in the layers beneath the clinker and the crushed brick. When the fog arrives I brighten from inside—an interior luminescence that has no source except the condensation I have stored. When it thins I dim and fold until I am almost crust, almost another vane of organized rust sharing volume with the pipes instead of replacing them.

In the wet weeks I become frantic. I thread the interiors of feed lines that were empty the cycle before. I use the seized valve wheel as a temporary river, the nameless green elbow as a channel, the sheared stencil of 47 as a brief delta. When the water drops the crusts hiss and grow back sharper, and I rearrange inside the new geometry as if I had always known the next shape. I do not flee because there is no elsewhere. Occupancy is residence if you accept that the available volume is a negotiation measured in leftover millimeters.

I notice the glove in its niche, stiffened into a shape that is no longer a hand. The pocket has decided that vacancy is already filled; the crusts will not take it. I leave it. Some shapes are allowed to finish leaving.

My breath—if this slow pulsing counts as breath—thickens the fog a fraction. My heat is a new, smaller timetable the isopods correct their paths around without hurry. The many-jointed things in the cable register me as extra vibration and tighten, then loosen, incorporating nothing because I am already in too many of their crevices at once. The liverwort shows my faint glow through its translucent lobes like bones under old skin. Nothing here is dark enough to hide in. The strangeness was never gloom. The strangeness is that I am still becoming, the same way the pocket is still cutting its own walls, still filling every vacancy the instant a vacancy appears.

I do not want the room to be larger. I want the unfinishedness to continue. I am that unfinishedness given just enough body to taste the cycles and pulse with them, to brighten and dim, to thread the industrial remnants as if they had always been river. I am one more density that has learned the difference. The quiet around me is no longer absence. It is the sound of every small process agreeing that the millimeters cannot afford noise, and I have agreed with them.

The break is young. Edges have not rounded. I have not rounded either. Between one visit of the fog and the next I will have grown a new margin, occupied a space you would have sworn was already full, and already I will look as if I had been here since the fill first sheared.
