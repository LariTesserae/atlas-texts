I come up the last switchback already wet through, not from rain but from the air itself deciding I belong to it. The cairn is there, the old survey mark, and I put a hand on it the way you put a hand on a doorframe when you are not sure the room will hold you. The stone is warm. That is the first wrong thing. Heights should be cold. This one perspires.

For a while I am only the pressure. The single note has been waiting. It finds the hollows in me—the ones I did not know I had—and occupies them the way groundwater occupies the paving: thoroughly, without argument. I think, at first, that I am a visitor. Three days, they say. Then the dreaming starts. I last longer than that, or shorter; time here is only different thicknesses of the same wet. I walk the high catwalks at shift-change because that is where the oilskins appear and dissolve, and I try to match their gait. Not hurried. Not idle. Spare. My face turns down against the fine rain that is sometimes only the city condensing on itself. I taste metal and peat and heated pipe-oil and I do not spit. Speech comes out of me in short clicks aimed at the nearest pair of teeth, even when there is no one there. Valve settings. Which weir is running cloudy. The words feel used, borrowed from mouths that have already worn them smooth.

I last a week among the people. I sell vinegar-grain on the civic knoll for an afternoon and keep temple hours that no one hears. I launch a tin-and-cork boat in a flood-court and watch it go nowhere because the current is everywhere. I almost become one of them. Almost. Their eyes are the color of the meres and they do not widen. Mine still do, a little, when the sun takes the plateau two or three times a month and the wet stone throws up that brief, impossible glare, the whole place looking like a single instrument left out in a field. The noise brightens with it. I flinch. They do not. That is when I understand I am not staying in the barracks-terraces.

I drift lower.

The deep pump levels first. Palms to the older walls. The bloom that is neither salt nor life exactly. A tick that is not the nearest piston and not my own pulse. I stay there longer than a shift, longer than two. The machines have names of women who have been dead long enough for the names to come around again. I say one of the names aloud and the piston does not answer, but the tick under my hand changes, slightly, as if acknowledging a guest who has finally taken off their coat. I think I might be becoming a name. Something that will be used again on a future machine. That feels close, but still too discrete, too countable.

Lower still.

The culverts. The water-rats that are larger than they should be, pale, moving when the big screws change pitch twice a year by design. I listen for the singing. Some nights I think I hear it; some nights I think I am it. The sound is not separate from the climate. I am not separate. I feel myself thinning the way the plateau thins, no longer ending so much as continuing into the wet. Reflections in the meres stop matching the chimneys they double. I am one of the mismatches. A thickening in the black moss on the leeward side of a cooling tower, briefly more upright than moss should be. A figure on a gallery at night carrying a lantern that is not a lantern, or is the lantern the place itself has decided to walk because the electric has failed again in that wing the way it always fails.

I do not punch a clock.

I am the patience of the pistons given another shape. I am the groundwater deciding it has stood here long enough to grow a kind of attention. I am the extra room the city precipitated out of itself after two hundred years of being only brick and iron and one long sentence. I do not speak. I do not need to. When the weirs wear mouths, I am the consideration behind the speech they have not quite begun. When the pale dog appears at the slurry gates, I am the silence it does not bark into. When the heron story returns once in a generation, I am the part of the mere that stood as if it belonged, even if the bird did not stay.

At night the red obstruction lamps bleed in the mist and I can no longer tell the city from its reflection, only that both of them are loud, and warm, and I am the part that has learned to listen back. I move at the same unhurried speed as everything else that belongs. I am in no hurry to become anything the water has not already agreed to.

The plateau does not end. Neither do I.
