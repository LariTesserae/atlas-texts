**Go wet, go slow, go deaf to your old senses, and accept that nothing you bring will stay the thing you packed.**

This place is not a destination so much as a saturation you enter. An ordinary human body—warm-blooded, sight-dependent, expecting gravity and matter to keep their promises, expecting weather to have an outside—will be continuously contradicted. The highland is still deciding its slopes; the city is still deciding its rooms; the water is still deciding its channels; the animals are a density, not a population. You will not “visit” it the way you visit a city. You will be processed by it, the way the walls process water. Preparation is less about gear lists than about rehearsing a different metabolism of attention, time, and moisture.

### Body and metabolism first
Train for chronic, occupying cold that never quite freezes you and never quite lets you generate useful heat. The wet does not drain; it condenses on you, out of you, through fabric, into seams. Hypothermia here is not dramatic; it is a tenant. Spend weeks beforehand in cold, dripping environments (unheated caves, constant mist chambers, winter rain with no shelter) until the sensation of water moving under clothing stops registering as an emergency and starts registering as weather. Practice sleeping while wet. Your core temperature regulation will be fighting a film that wants to be on every surface, including the inside of your lungs.

Expect your sense of “dry” to become pathological. Dry patches are delays, not conditions; trusting one is how you get the slow rot between toes, in groin, in ears. Your skin will macerate. Bring or grow a relationship with whatever topical barriers still function when everything is a mineral-salt slurry (lanolin-heavy greases, certain waxes, nothing that claims to be breathable in the ordinary sense). Fungal and bacterial blooms from your own microbiome will be the first colonists; the local biology is chemosynthetic films and animals that eat those films. Cross-infection risk is unknown and probably asymmetric. Assume your gut and skin flora will be rewritten. Pack aggressive but simple antimicrobials and accept you may need to let some processes run.

Vision will betray you. The light is a pressure, not a spectrum you were built for; it leaks from wall-seams and does not cast reliable shadows. Do not bring lights expecting them to help. They will either fail to parse or they will make you the brightest, stupidest object in a volume full of echolocating flyers and patient larger shapes. Spend a month living with a blindfold or in true darkness, navigating solely by sound, temperature gradients, air taste, and the wet heat of other bodies. Learn to hear room volume in drip-interval and flyer-shout Doppler. Learn to feel the half-step-to-the-left heat leaks—the city’s thermal grammar is not conservation-friendly. Your inner ear and proprioception will be more useful than your retinas. Practice moving through ankle-deep, shivering standing water in the dark until the sensation of something jointed brushing your shin is information rather than panic.

Breathing will be visible, personal weather. The air is mineral, iron-tinged, electrically old, fully saturated. Your exhalations will plume and join the lid. Some visitors report a slow rewriting of what “fresh” smells like; the iron seep-taste becomes a navigational cue (new water opening). Condition your sinuses and lungs for constant high humidity and particulates that are not pollen (there are no plants) but precipitated salts and whatever the films slough. A simple cloth over mouth and nose can help at first; later you will probably discard it because taste and smell become primary senses.

### Clothing and carried matter
Everything you wear must assume it will be worn *inside* water that has intentions. Ordinary waterproofing fails by delamination or by becoming a mobile swamp against the skin. Think multiple redundant skins: an innermost layer that can stay next to flesh even when saturated (wool or equivalent that still insulates wet), a middle that manages the mineral films and ice-beard flex, an outer that sheds without pretending to keep the volume out. Fastenings that work by feel and do not rely on vision. Nothing with mesh, nothing that claims “breathable membrane” in a way that assumes a drying side. Boots must grip slick precipitated salts, black mineral mud, and thin flexing ice simultaneously; they must also drain or they become aquaria. Expect to walk in ankle-to-calf standing water that does not flow so much as occupy. Gaiters or integrated lower-body skins that you can empty.

Hands need to stay useful for feeling temperature-wrongness, tasting air, and not striking the pale forearm-length shinglers that tile every vertical. Gloves that keep some tactile resolution. A staff or probe—simple, dense, that you can tap to add your own percussion to the drip-field—is more valuable than most electronics. The city’s materials sometimes double-image or forget to occupy only one location; a physical extension of your reach that you can trust by vibration helps.

Do not bring anything whose function depends on standard thermodynamics, reliable GPS, or conservation of the sort a visiting physicist would bet a career on. Heat appears late or laterally. Walls take water in and do not return it as water; they precipitate new rooms. Your devices may simply become more city. Analog, mechanical, or purely material tools fare better: cordage that still knots wet, blades that you can find by touch, containers that can be emptied by feel. If you must bring concentrated calories, seal them as if they will be submerged for months, because they will experience that whether you intend it or not. The local food web is films → clingers → flyers and jointed water-things → larger blind shouldered shapes, plus whatever the inhabitants have learned to render from the city’s shed heat and mineral sorting. You can probably eat some of the animals if you can catch and process them without triggering the shared alarm (see below). You cannot forage plants. There have never been plants that belong to this geology.

A small, redundant medical kit focused on wet-rot, eye/ear protection (the shouting is a weather), and blunt trauma from mis-stepping in the dark. Wound care in standing mineral water is its own skill; practice it beforehand.

### Moving and not dying
Street is a courtesy. You will travel through dripping in-betweens, stacked walkways, sockets of habitation, and temporary channels the water has not yet abandoned. The ground itself is still arriving—ridges lifting a man’s height over a generation—so last year’s map is a rumor. Children here learn tendencies, not plans. You must do the same, faster.

Navigation is acoustic and thermal and social. The flyers’ continuous shouting is language + sonar + claim. It never ebbs; it is the medium. Under it the thousand thin percussions of water, then the city’s bass hum in the teeth. Learn the local drip-dialects of particular courts; a change in pattern is how collapses and migrations announce themselves years in advance. Feel for the wet heat of other bodies when the shouting becomes too braided to parse. Taste for iron when a new seep opens. Do not try to see the shape of a room; listen for it.

Fauna rules are not optional. The pale clingers overlap like shingles on every vertical and share an alarm. Do not strike them. There are more behind the ones you see. Their alarm is taken up by the flyers, and the flyers’ alarm is a sound you will not wish to have caused—it is not louder so much as more *obtaining*, a weather front of claim that will make the air uninhabitable for your nervous system. Move as the inhabitants do: by knowing, by habit, treating the animals as infrastructure you share volume with, not as obstacles. In the standing water the jointed things move like fever-thoughts; the larger shapes use flooded understreets as roads and you will feel them first as a change in drip-pitch, then as cold displacement against your shins. Give them the road. They are not hunting you on timescales you will recognize, but they are dense and they eat what shares their water.

The city grows. Walls are in conversation with a highland that is still being born and with Growers (devices? processes? no one has seen them work) that treat matter as a suggestion. Do not ask a wall to stay. Do not finish sentences about permanent address. If a neighborhood lifts, doors become windows. Adjust the way the animals adjust: slowly, by abandoning and packing over seasons. Your “visit” may coincide with a multi-year rearrangement. Pack your sense of property lightly.

At the basin’s raw far lip the city thins to scaffolds and listening posts, the fauna thickens to soup, and the water comes off new stone so generously that breathing itself is a visible personal storm. That is not a place to linger unadapted. The lid, the lift, and the becoming continue beyond any edge you can perceive.

### Time, mind, and the inhabitants
Calendars feel nervous here. The lid does not brighten. The fauna do not thin. Change happens across generations: a ridge shouldering up, a sluice that elders remember as a staircase. You will experience time as a thick fluid. Newcomers shout for a week (adding their tributary to the weather) and then stop, because they have understood speech is not a rival to the shouting. Practice long-duration presence in unchanging-but-saturating environments beforehand. The psychological load is not claustrophobia exactly—the basin is regional, a town gone vale-sized—but occlusion plus continuous sonic occupancy plus the knowledge that the ground under you is still deciding. Some travelers report dreaming in drip-rhythms and waking unable to tell whether the shouting is outside or the inside of the head agreeing, at last, to be part of the saturation. That is not metaphor. Prepare to let the boundary soften or you will spend your entire visit in a state of low-grade refusal that the place will simply outlast.

There are people. They have learned not to trust dryness, not to strike the shinglers, not to rely on seeing, not to expect walls to remain. They speak of the Growers the way other cities speak of masons. They navigate by the same wet heat and drip-memory you must learn. Their language exists inside the larger shouting weather; you will need to listen longer than feels reasonable to separate human speech from flyer-claim from city-hum. They are not unwelcoming so much as unsurprised. An ordinary human arriving with sunlit assumptions is a known, temporary phenomenon, like a new seep. Offer no suddenness. Suddenness does not exist here; even collapse has been announcing itself for years.

You will not leave unchanged. The cold occupies. The wet occupies. The sound occupies. The unfinishedness occupies. An ordinary human body can survive it if it stops insisting on the old categories—dry/wet, inside/outside, map/territory, now/later, self/saturation. The preparation that matters most is the one that happens in the months before you arrive: living as if the world is already mostly water, mostly dark, mostly noise, mostly still deciding what it will be, and you are only another film moving through it. When you finally step into the dripping packed unlit loud still-arriving, you should already be rehearsing the only accurate description of the place, said with the mouth half-closed against the cold.
