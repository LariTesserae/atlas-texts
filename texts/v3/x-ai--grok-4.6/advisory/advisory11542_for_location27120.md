**Physical and Environmental Acclimation**

An ordinary human arriving here will first register the altitude and the light as a single pressure. The plateau occupies the space of a high basin that no longer exists; the air is thin, cool, and persistently damp. Breath fogs. Metal weeps condensation. Night-shift railings grow rime that must be scraped or licked if you are short of water. Daylight is a hard enamel glare that never quite matches itself across surfaces—glazed bone-colored brick, galvanized plate, porcelain insulators the size of coffins, terrazzo plazas that go down through more terrazzo into riveted cavities. Reflections double and disagree. Your eyes will ache within the first hour if unprotected.

Begin acclimation well before arrival if your travel method allows staging. Spend several subjective days at comparable elevation and dryness if possible, then several more in cool, humid conditions under constant bright industrial lighting. Once on-site, move slowly for the first 48 hours. Hydrate aggressively from the start; the air steals moisture even when fog pools in the deep courtyards at mid-morning and refuses to burn off. Carry acetazolamide or equivalent altitude medication, plus electrolytes. A compact oxygen concentrator or bottled supplemental oxygen is not luxury—it is insurance against the first night when the thinness and the damp combine with the psychological weight of the scale.

Dress in layers that can be added or shed without ceremony. Base layer moisture-wicking and antimicrobial. Mid-layer insulating but compressible. Outer shell cut-resistant, puncture-resistant, and water-shedding—Kevlar or equivalent weave over the arms, torso, and shins. The balustrades are still knives. New pours show the grain of forms that were struck yesterday or a century ago; edges have not been taught to soften. Gloves must allow fine work (bolts, valves, ladder rungs that run aside rather than down) yet survive a slip against galvanized sheeting. Boots need aggressive tread for terrazzo slick with condensation and for the grit that collects where walkways fail to meet. Bring gaiters; the lichen of copper-green and the wiry shrubs occupy every designed leak and will seed themselves into your cuffs.

Eye protection is non-negotiable: wraparound lenses with high visible-light transmission reduction and side shields against the doubled glare. The sky itself is enamel. The terrazzo will show you a second, inverted plateau if you stare. A brimmed hat or hood helps, but the light comes from everywhere the lamps have been left on for a century.

**Water, Food, and Waste**

Water is a scheduling problem, not an absence. It arrives as rime, as brief unsounding rain the gutters accept, as beads that fall from pipe undersides with forgotten regularity, and as standing reservoirs the size of city blocks whose surfaces are skimmed by wind and by things that are not wind. The cisterns contain schools of fish that look like punctures in the water; they move as one thought and leave no lasting ripple. Do not drink untreated cistern water on first arrival. Carry a high-capacity filter (ceramic plus chemical) and a UV purifier as backup. Collect rime from railings at the end of the night-shift with a clean scraper and melt it; it is the cleanest source. Gutters and condensation troughs can be used once you have observed their cycles for a full day-night and seen that nothing unexpected drains into them.

Food must be brought. The local vegetation is sparse, patient lichen and a few shrubs that refuse to spread. The jointed goats (too many articulations, pale, cropping vertical seams without scrapes) and the tin-folded birds are plentiful the way rust is plentiful. They occupy overlooked volumes. Do not assume they are edible or safe. An ordinary human gut has no evolutionary contract with this place. Pack calorie-dense, long-stable rations sufficient for twice your planned stay. Include a small still or solar evaporator in case you must extend. If you later test the goats or the lichen, do so with extreme caution, in tiny amounts, after observing them for days and confirming they do not react to your presence with anything beyond patience.

Waste discipline is absolute. The plateau has no older rock under it; every cavity is made. Do not introduce organics that the local fauna or the slow machines have not already accounted for. Pack out what you can. For the rest, use designated or obviously unused cistern overflow or condensation runs far from any hab-block or foundry you might later need.

**Shelter, Movement, and the Slow Machines**

Hab-blocks exist, pressed against retorts, their ceilings sometimes the cooling fins of the foundry above. Stairwells corkscrew through the mass and emerge in rooms that were not on the way. Density is saturation of made things, not crowding of people. You will find dormitories, galleries, stacked halls receding until the farthest stacks are only a brighter scratch. Many feel recently emerged. A stair may end in air, waiting for a landing that has not yet been cast.

Choose shelter that has been stable across at least one observed dusk-to-rime cycle. Avoid rooms whose windows show the same courtyard three times, then a courtyard that has never existed. Prefer spaces with multiple exits, even if one exit is a ladder that runs aside into a cavity of still air. The industry is real and electrified but operates on seasonal cycles. A press the size of a nave descends the width of a finger in a week. Turbines idle so slowly their blades are architecture. Do not camp beneath anything that could complete a stroke while you sleep. Mark the position of slow-moving elements with your own paint or chalk at arrival and check them every few hours at first. The tick of contracting plate at dusk is a clock.

Catwalks stitch the distances. Conveyors hang frozen or nearly so, like the ideas of rivers. Movement is safest along established walkways even when they feel recent. The features are young; nothing weathers in the ordinary way. Erosion is slow unmaking: a bolt backs out over a decade. Do not trust a weld line that has begun to brighten.

**Navigating Worn Reality**

This is the part that will unmake an unprepared traveler. Reality here is a worn gasket. A straight service tunnel’s windows will repeat a courtyard, then invent one, then return the first with the fog in a different year. A door opened at noon opens at dusk inside. Some stacks occupy the same footprint and do not touch. You may find a factory floor that is also, without metaphor, the sky—figures (if they are workers) moving among rafters that are clouds, tightening fittings on nothing namable, their shadows falling both down and inward.

Prepare redundant, low-tech orientation. Carry at least three independent timepieces (mechanical preferred; quartz as backup) and a simple analog journal. Note the position of the sun (or the enamel equivalent), the state of the fog, the phase of the rime, and any repeating views at every major junction. Do not rely on electronic mapping; the place argues with itself. Leave physical markers that are uniquely yours—colored cord, carved signs, stacked grit in patterns the goats will not rearrange. If you see the inverted plateau in the terrazzo beginning to trade moisture with this one, look away after a few seconds. The gutters will eventually accept the drip, but you may not.

If you become looped, stop. Sit. Breathe the thin damp air. Wait for a dusk or a rime cycle that feels sequential to your own body. Do not force a door that opened at the wrong hour. The place is still finishing the thought of itself; a new hall may appear in a gap that was always too small. Give it time. Change is a long weather.

**The Fauna and Possible Others**

They are everywhere and they are quiet. Pale moths with mica wings roost in the lamp-housings. Jointed goats crop lichen from vertical seams. Birds of folded tin nest in dead loudspeakers and do not sing. Cistern fish move as one thought. They occupy every overlooked volume the way rust does, and they are as patient as the machines. Do not startle them. Do not feed them. Do not assume they are decorations. Give the goats room on the seams; they leave no scrapes because they do not need to. If you must pass through a volume they occupy, move as they do—slowly, occupying only the designed leaks.

The figures in the sky-factory, if they are workers, tighten fittings on nothing that can be named. Observe from a distance first. They may be the builders, or what the builders became, or what will finish being the builders. Do not interrupt a fitting. If they notice you, remain still and visible. This place has room for an ordinary human provided the human does not introduce haste.

**Psychological and Temporal Preparation**

The scale is a valley’s worth of construction, town after town of stacked halls, horizon to manufactured horizon. It is quiet. Sound arrives late and thin: a drip finding a tank, the fizz of current in a busbar the thickness of a road. The hush is not empty; it is the sound of engines measured in seasons. An ordinary mind will first feel awe, then a creeping sense that time itself has been poured and bolted and poured again. Isolation is near-total unless you encounter the workers or choose to stay near the more populated (by fauna) galleries.

Practice sitting with vast, slow processes before you come. The presses descend. The lichen advances a handspan in a generation. A bolt backs out over a decade. You will be tempted to fill the quiet with your own noise or to rush toward the rim where the construction simply stops—a clean, unweathered drop into white air, the edge extending itself by the thickness of a thought, year after quiet year. Do not. The rim is not a viewpoint; it is the current limit of the work. Watch it from a high parapet if you must, then turn back into the density.

Keep a personal log that records your own physiological time (meals, sleep, heart rate) separately from the plateau’s cycles. If the two begin to diverge, you are in a worn section. Retreat to a previously stable plaza.

**Gear Summary (Minimum Viable Kit)**

- Altitude and damp acclimation meds, supplemental O2 option
- Cut-resistant layered clothing, high-grip boots, wraparound eye protection
- Water filtration (ceramic + UV), rime scraper, 2× planned calories
- Redundant analog timepieces, physical journal, unique marking materials
- Compact first-aid with emphasis on lacerations, altitude, and hydration
- Lightweight shelter (bivy that can be pitched in a cavity or unused gallery)
- Headlamp even though the lamps have been left on; fog and interiors still exist
- Multi-tool, 20 m of static line (ladders run aside), carabiners
- Small solar still or evaporator
- Patience, measured in seasons

Arrive prepared to be small, slow, and careful. The plateau replaced the mountain with itself, only made. It will not hurry for you, and it will not weather you in any way you already understand. If you treat every surface as both finished and still becoming, if you drink only what you have watched cycle, if you walk the catwalks as if the next landing has not yet been poured, you can walk its decks and galleries and return with your own time still mostly your own. The tin birds will shift on their perches. The fog will be scheduled away. Far out, the edge will have grown, or you will think it has. That is enough.
