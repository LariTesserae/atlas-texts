**Prepare as if you are about to enter a newly finished, still-curing machine for light and water rather than a ruin or a cave.** The chamber is a deliberate, recent human construction: a small, fully dressed stone box (roughly room-sized, swim-acrossable) sunk one story down, filled nearly to a high waterline with warm, actively circulating water under a single intense circular shaft of daylight. It smells of wet lime and iron, roars far louder than its volume should allow, has almost no ecology yet, and is accessed from above by what looks like an ordinary well-mouth you could easily walk past. An ordinary human body can visit it, but the combination of glare, confined echoes, moving water, alkaline construction residue, and total isolation requires specific physical, sensory, and practical readiness. Do not treat it as a swimming hole or a historic site; treat it as a live hydraulic installation that is still learning its own shape.

### Physical and physiological readiness
You will almost certainly get wet to the chest or deeper, and parts of the floor slope toward the inlet so standing is uneven or impossible in places. The water is warm (blood-warm, sun-heated conduit water) so there is no cold shock, but it is in continuous motion: a sheet pour from the ceramic inlet creates surface currents, standing waves against the far wall, and a weir overflow. You need to be a competent, confident swimmer who can tread water, push off walls, and recover from being spun or pushed by the pour. Practice in a noisy, echoey indoor pool with an artificial current if possible; the roar here will mask your own breathing and any splash you make. The space is intimate—you can touch opposing walls in one stretch—so panic from enclosure plus noise is a real risk. If you have any history of claustrophobia, vertigo in bright-to-dark transitions, or sound sensitivity, consider whether the visit is worth it; the chamber does not offer easy retreat once you are in the water.

Skin, eyes, and mucous membranes will contact water that has been in contact with fresh mortar (alkaline lime), bitumen joints, and iron tool residue. This is not yet a biologically mature system; it is chemically young. Expect possible mild irritation, especially if you have cuts, eczema, or wear contact lenses. Test a small skin area first if you can sample the water; otherwise assume it is slightly caustic and plan to rinse thoroughly afterward with clean water you bring. Do not drink it. The inflow source is unseen and could carry sediment, agricultural runoff, or simply high mineral content. Bring and use your own drinking water. The air will be humid from splash and vapor; combined with physical effort this can dehydrate you faster than expected.

Hearing will be assaulted. The pour plus stone reflections produce a constant roar overlaid with ticking air, gulps, and hisses. Prolonged exposure (more than 20–30 minutes) can cause temporary threshold shift or headache. The oculus can add a flute-like wind note. There are no animals to listen for, only water talking to new stone. Decide in advance whether you want ear protection (which dulls the “languages” of the room) or full immersion (which risks discomfort).

Vision: the descending column of daylight is dense enough to look solid until the surface breaks it. Afterimages and contrast blindness are immediate; your eyes will take time to adjust to the underwater brightness that seems to light the masonry from within. The sun-walk across the floor creates moving glitter and sudden dusk if a cloud passes. Bring nothing that relies on unaided low-light vision for exit.

### Access, clothing, and kit
From the surface the only obvious entrance is the circular well-mouth set in paving or turf. You will need to descend a vertical or near-vertical shaft perhaps 3–4 m to the water or to the dry gallery ledge that runs around three sides above the waterline. The ledge is wide enough to crouch with a lamp or bucket but is wet from splash and vapor; the fourth wall is the inlet. Assume you must rig your own descent: a static rope, harness, descender, and backup, plus a way to ascend (ascenders or a second rope). The rim may be slippery. Helmet with a chinstrap is non-negotiable—head strikes on the barrel ceiling or while being pushed by current are possible. Do not free-climb or jump.

Clothing must tolerate full immersion, abrasion on fresh chisel-marked stone (arris still sharp), and alkaline water. Quick-dry synthetic layers or a thin wetsuit/shorty are better than cotton. Water shoes or reef boots with good grip; bare feet will meet unfinished edges and possible lime residue. A thin hood or buff helps with splash and temperature if you stay long. Secure or omit anything that can snag (jewelry, loose straps). A small dry-bag for essentials, clipped to you, not left on the ledge where splash or a change in water level could take it.

Minimal kit (everything redundant and waterproofed):
- Descent/ascent system and helmet as above.
- Headlamp plus a second light (the sun column can be blocked or you may visit near dusk; the chamber goes suddenly dark).
- Whistle (sound may not carry out the oculus well).
- Compact first-aid: irrigation for eyes/skin, pH-neutral soap or rinse, blister/abrasion care, any personal meds.
- 1–2 L clean water and a way to drink it without contaminating the chamber.
- Small towel or absorbent cloth for the exit (you will be dripping lime-scented water).
- Optional: silicone earplugs or custom filtered plugs, swim goggles (to keep lime water out of eyes and to see the flashing blocks clearly), a simple pH strip or test kit if you want data, a notebook in a dry bag if you intend to record the “smaller languages.”
- Do not bring food that crumbs, plants, animals, or anything living. The room has only a dust of algae and one deliberately placed, undecided clump of reed/lily. It is too new and too sealed for accident; you are an invitation it has not yet received.

Leave a surface marker or note of your intended time in/out if anyone knows you are there. The place is easy to miss and the surface world may have no idea the chamber exists.

### Sensory and psychological preparation
Visiting feels like entering a lantern that is also a throat. The glare arrives first, then the realization that the volume is smaller than the sound. Once your eyes adjust, the entire room is one bright, warm body; ripples climb the walls and the submerged blocks flash as if breathing. It is intimate and slightly voyeuristic—the light moves just behind your shoulder. There is no unworked rock, no ancient patina, no inhabitants. The newness (proud mortar, even chisel tracks, smell of tools) makes it feel like a decision that has only just been kept. You are not discovering a ruin; you are present while the water is still learning the box it was given.

Expect disorientation from the mismatch of senses: tropical-intensity light underground, a roar that belongs to a larger river, warmth that feels already part of your skin, and a complete absence of life. The space can feel held or waiting. Some visitors will want to float in the sun-column, listen to the weir, or crouch on the gallery watching the ellipse of light walk across the floor. Others will find the enclosure plus noise oppressive after a short time. Decide beforehand your purpose—pure sensory experience, documentation, quiet, or simply to have been inside it—and set a time limit you will keep even if it feels timeless.

Do not assume ritual use, but the niche with the bound stems and the possible functions (hold, measure, baptize, cool, forget) suggest it may have been made for more than storage. Move slowly, introduce nothing, take nothing. The sluice-gate is raised and the system is open; you do not know who or what might change that, or whether the inflow is seasonal. Watch the waterline. Know your exit.

### After
Rinse skin, hair, and gear with clean water as soon as you surface. The lime smell and possible residue linger. Check for irritation over the next day. The experience itself—the way the chamber is smaller than its sound, the way light thickens underwater, the feeling of a pocket that has not yet been given its inhabitants—tends to stay with you as a sharp, warm afterimage rather than a story. An ordinary body can carry it, but only if you entered prepared to be fully inside the machine rather than merely looking down the well.
