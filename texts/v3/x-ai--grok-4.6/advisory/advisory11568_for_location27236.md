**Bring a heated dry-suit system rated for near-freezing water, a long-duration closed-circuit rebreather (or a compact habitat/submersible), redundant low-output lighting, slow-movement propulsion, and the psychological equivalent of a vow of silence.** This place is not an ocean and not a ruin you tour. It is a finished, filled, cold volume whose only remaining events occur on geological and botanical timescales. An ordinary human body will experience it as constant, intimate contact with near-0 °C water, almost no photons, dense pale vegetation, and masonry that has forgotten it was ever architecture. Everything you do will be an intrusion measured in silt curtains that last days and in the loudness of your own breathing.

### Thermal and physiological reality
The water lives next door to ice. Even a few hours of immersion without active heating produces progressive hypothermia; days require a dry suit with thick undergarments, electrically or chemically heated vest/core layers, insulated gloves and hood, and a full-face mask or helmet. Exposed skin (even a few square centimeters) will ache then numb. Joints stiffen. Fine motor control degrades. You will need to monitor core temperature continuously; the plants and stone will not give you any thermal cue except the same uniform cold. Plan rest cycles inside a heated habitat or vehicle if you intend to stay longer than a single long dive. Eating and waste management become suit-or-habitat problems: liquid nutrition, catheter or diaper systems, and the knowledge that you cannot “surface” because there is no surface in the ordinary sense. Decompression is modest (depths are usually only tens of body-lengths to the nearest plane) but still real if you linger in the deeper courtyards; treat it as a cave-dive profile with no emergency ascent possible.

### Breathing, vision, and the silence
A rebreather is mandatory for duration and for not filling the channels with bubbles that would be the loudest, most alien sound this world has heard in centuries. Open-circuit scuba is possible for short scouts but will announce you everywhere and limit you to hours. Carry spare sorb, oxygen, and batteries as if resupply does not exist.

Light is the color of old iron at the bottom of a well and there is almost none of it. Your eyes will never fully adapt to useful vision. Bring multiple redundant lights (helmet, handheld, vehicle-mounted) but use them sparingly and preferably at low intensity or red/amber to avoid bleaching the last faint yellow-green of the chloroplasts and to reduce backscatter from the ever-present silt. Image intensifiers or photon-counting cameras help more than white light. Expect visibility of a few meters at best even with lamps; if you disturb the silt it drops to zero for days. The plants themselves (strap-like pale fronds the width of a forearm, plus finer cold moss) form a three-dimensional curtain you must part with your hands. They lean uniformly with the oldest current. They do not react to you.

Sound is almost private between water and stone: a subsonic sigh of current, rare millimeter-scale clicks of settling joints, the occasional slow rotation of a detached frond. Your own equipment, heartbeat, and breathing will dominate. Hydrophones will record more of the world’s actual voice than your ears.

### Movement and navigation
You swim or you ride a small, quiet DPV or compact habitat that can thread the remaining channels and rest on the low, interlocking masonry. Walking is impossible. The geometry is dense—an overgrown library whose shelves have grown into one another. You are never more than an arm’s length from worked stone or living tissue. Dense fronds require you to part them continuously; they can snag hoses, fins, or limbs. Move as if the silt is sacred and the current is the only map. Landmarks repeat: undulating plazas planed into single sheets, colonnades become kelp trellises, aqueducts now serving as current highways. A magnetic compass, inertial unit, and breadcrumb transponders (or simply accepting that you will wander) are more useful than any visual piloting. One current system may take a human lifetime to reverse; treat direction as a slow, almost theological fact rather than a heading you can fight.

### Psychological and temporal preparation
This is a place that has already reached its last possible arrangement. Change continues only the way a mountain continues after it has become a plain. You will feel the weight of finished labor, of builders who left only the shape of their effort, now occupied by slower lives. The emptiness of the ecological niche (nothing eats, nothing pollinates, nothing scavenges) produces a specific loneliness: you are the only animal, the only rapid motion, the only source of heat and noise. Sensory monotony (cold, dim iron light, tactile sameness of rounded stone and slick fronds, near-silence) can produce both profound calm and a creeping existential unease or time-distortion. Days here do not feel like days. Prepare with practices that tolerate long stillness—meditation, acceptance of non-event, the ability to simply be a warm, temporary anomaly. Some travelers report a sense of courtesy toward the place: move slowly, leave no marks, do not cut the plants unless necessary for passage, do not shine bright light into every courtyard. The world will not notice you leaving, but you will notice having been the only thing that happened.

### Practical kit and contingencies
- Heated dry suit + underlayers + backups.
- Long-duration rebreather or small lock-out habitat/submersible that can sit on a plaza.
- Redundant lights, low-lux cameras, hydrophone.
- Slow DPV or habitat thrusters; shears or a dull knife only if entanglement becomes life-threatening.
- Navigation (compass, INS, transponders) and a way to mark a return path that will not itself become silted or overgrown in your absence.
- Food, water, waste, and medical for the planned duration plus large margin; treat hypothermia, entanglement, and equipment failure as the primary risks. There is no rescue and no local biology that will help or hinder you.
- Recording gear if you want evidence; the place itself keeps no visitor book.

Enter as a guest in a conversation that has already concluded. The masonry, the plants, and the solvent have been speaking for longer than memory. Your preparation is what lets you listen for a little while without dying of cold or of the realization that nothing here needs you.
