**Waterproof everything that matters, accept that nothing stays dry, and treat the city as a single vast organism that will try to inhabit you the way it inhabits its own gutters.**

An ordinary human arriving here (someone used to seasons, soil, a sky that sometimes clears, and the possibility of silence) will first notice the wet. It is not dramatic rain. It is a warm, patient, bone-softening precipitation that has been falling for so long the iron lips of every gutter have worn rounded. Cloud sits at lintel height. Surfaces bead overnight. Your clothes, pack, notebook, and electronics will acquire a skin of condensate if left unattended. Tools rust in hours. Paper mildews. The air itself is a warm broth—breath, wet fur, dye-house musk, coke, grease-trap sweetness, tannery yellow, rendering fat. After an hour the separate noises (water in pipes, slap of ancient belts, animal claims on alleys, two-century-old bronze bells slightly flat) collapse into one river-like roar. You stop hearing parts and live inside the single acoustic body of the place.

There are no trees, no grass, nothing that roots. The stone sweats and will not have soil. Landmarks are mineral fans of calcite and rust that children learn the way other children learn parks. Animals are the other weather and the saturation: pale thumb-length crustaceans and eel-things in every gutter, multi-jointed municipal clickers under grates, loft-rats gliding, soot-hawks with bellows faces, slow kites riding smelter heat. At dusk their shadows braid until lamps come on through a moving mesh. Larger gray browsers migrate once or twice a generation, shoulder-high and rain-slick, taking a month to cross a borough while grazing bacterial dark in troughs; traffic and tramlines simply part around them and always have. People live in high-ceilinged tiled rooms they can wipe, eat yeast cakes and rendering oil, keep windows shut, and treat a silent stairwell as a warning to check the drains. The factories are geology. Nothing is in a hurry. A chimney leans across a century and is shored, not rebuilt. Paint finishes becoming stain. Light is a late, early, never-quite-enough white-gray that never dries a stone. The city looks precipitated—an inhabited mineral still condensing, long past the age of being designed differently.

Visiting feels like entering a machine that grew an ecosystem and then forgot it was ever separate from it. The scale does psychological work: weather forms over districts the way it forms over a sea (three weeks of low yellow tannery cloud versus clearer, hotter foundry drizzle). You can walk for days and the highland simply continues, plateau after paved plateau. The lack of green and the perpetual damp can produce a specific homesickness—an ache for something that roots or for a dry hour. The density of bodies (human and otherwise) is intimate and slightly claustrophobic even on rooftops. At the same time there is a strange patience and beauty: the mapped stains, the inherited ledges, the way life occupies every unattended surface without haste. An ordinary traveler will either leave quickly, slightly unnerved and permanently damper, or stay long enough for the one-noise and the weeping stone to become ordinary. Both are valid. Neither is comfortable at first.

**Practical preparations**

- **Clothing and kit against the wet and the inhabitants.** Pack fully waterproof (not water-resistant) outer layers, sealed boots with good grip for mineral-slick stone, and quick-dry synthetics or treated wool underneath. Everything goes in dry-bags or hard cases with desiccant; assume any seal will eventually fail and plan redundancy. A brimmed or hooded layer that sheds the constant drip is essential. Gloves protect against rust and calcite. Consider gaiters or sealed cuffs because smaller fauna treat sills, ledges, and any unattended opening as public land. At night stairwells tick and rustle; this is normal, but a silent house means check the drains. Bring a compact, high-output headlamp and spare sealed batteries—the daylight is never dramatic and never quite enough. A simple respirator or at least a good scarf helps with the thick air (musk, industrial volatiles, animal dander) until you acclimate; many districts have their own low cloud of specific chemistry.

- **Health and biology.** The water is condensate plus sky plus whatever the bedrock and the rendering floors contribute; it is mineral-heavy and occupied. Bring a robust filter/purifier and still treat local water as suspect. Local food is yeast, pressed cakes, and pale rendering oils—nutrient-dense for the inhabitants, potentially disruptive for an outsider gut. Pack a short-term supply of familiar calories and electrolytes plus broad-spectrum probiotics and anti-diarrheals. The damp plus animal density plus ancient industry implies parasites, fungi, and respiratory irritants you have never met. Carry a well-stocked first-aid kit heavy on wound care (infection risk is high), antifungals, and whatever prophylactics your own physicians recommend for unknown zoonoses. Tetanus and rust are not theoretical. Cuts happen on slumped brick and worn iron. The constant humidity can worsen any existing respiratory or skin condition. Mental health matters: the endlessness, the lack of green, the one-noise, and the sense that the city is still precipitating around you can produce disorientation or a low-grade existential pressure. Pack whatever grounding practices work for you (a small sealed token from home, a waterproof journal, simple breath work). Do not expect quiet.

- **Navigation and movement.** Maps here are of watersheds and old animal roads, not streets. Learn the mineral stains as landmarks; they are named and reliable. Tramlines already account for the slow migrations of the big browsers—do not block them, do not try to hurry them. The city is a single acoustic body, so sound carries farther than it should; bells that have been ringing for two centuries will tell you the shift even if you cannot see the source. Wear layers you can shed or add as you move between microclimates (tannery yellow fog versus hotter foundry drizzle). Surfaces are always wet; assume slip risk. High roofs exist and are worth climbing for the view (stacks becoming a darker band of weather, wings turning), but every roof is also habitat. Move slowly. Nothing here is in a hurry and the stone itself will not be rushed.

- **Social and cultural.** People have always lived here. Their rooms are tiled so they can be wiped; their windows stay shut; a household that hears nothing checks the drains. The multi-jointed things under the grates are considered municipal. The larger animals have claims. Do not treat the fauna as pests or scenery; they are the other weather and the saturation. Food comes from vats, not gardens. Respect the old bells and the even older habit of parting around a migration. An outsider will be obvious (drier clothes at first, different gait, different smell). Most inhabitants will simply continue; some may be curious, few will be hurried. Learn enough of the local speech or bring a durable translation method, but expect the deeper grammar of the place—the stains, the animal roads, the patient lean of chimneys—to matter more than vocabulary.

- **Mindset and duration.** Decide in advance whether this is a short, wide-eyed visit or a longer immersion. Short visits leave you soaked, slightly deafened, and carrying a persistent mineral-and-musk memory. Longer stays require accepting that you will never be fully dry, that silence is suspicious, that the city will try to occupy your unattended surfaces, and that the light will never quite dry a stone. Bring one small, sealed, non-living reminder of elsewhere if it helps (a photograph, a dry stone from home). Do not bring plants or soil; they will not take and the stone will not have them. Document if you wish, but assume paper and unsealed electronics will suffer. The place rewards patience and punishes the expectation of change or of dryness. Stand on a high roof at the white-gray hour when the bells go. They will sound like a very old machine telling the time. That is accurate.

You will leave damper than you arrived, with mineral tracks on your own gear and a new internal map of watersheds and weeping façades. The ordinary human body can survive here; the ordinary human sensorium will be rewritten by the wet, the one-noise, and the density of lives that have always been here. Prepare as if you are visiting a living geology that happens to have foundries and people inside it. Then go, and let the cloud sit at lintel height around you.
