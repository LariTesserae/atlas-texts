**Waterproof everything, expect saturation as the default state, and accept that you will never be dry or alone.** An ordinary human body is not built for a world that was poured as a single sealed intention and then filled past metaphor. Prepare as if you are entering a warm, functioning machine the size of a continent whose only product is equilibrium, whose only weather is its own breath, and whose only inhabitants occupy every cubic centimeter they were given.

The air is already a second skin. It is warm, heavy, and continuously condensing; every surface weeps a film. You will be wet within minutes of arrival and stay wet. Standard rain gear fails because there is no “rain event” to wait out—only perpetual sheeting, haze, and the silent return of water that never quite finishes. Use a sealed, breathable membrane suit with taped seams, integrated gaiters, and a hood that can close to a narrow visor. The suit must handle constant contact with mineral-slick tiles, standing water that ranges from film to calf-deep, and the soft, unstartled collisions of bodies in the haze. Undergarments should wick and include antimicrobial treatment; trench foot, fungal overgrowth, and macerated skin are not hypotheticals here. Carry multiple pairs of thin merino or synthetic liners and rotate them even if they never fully dry. Footwear needs aggressive siping or a shallow tread that sheds water and does not catch on the sharp, unworn arrises of the channels. The tiles themselves are not abrasive, but they are relentless.

Respiratory protection is non-negotiable. The haze is a weather of animals—small dark bodies in such density that middle distance looks stippled. You will walk through layers of them. They do not startle, do not sing, and do not make space. A simple cloth does nothing. Use a full-face or half-face respirator with P100 or equivalent particulate filters plus a humidity-tolerant organic-vapor stage if you can source one; the air carries a faint sweet mineral note from the scurfing and whatever the fauna exhale. Change filters on a conservative schedule; they will load with both water and life. Expect the sensation of soft impacts against the visor and the knowledge that the same bodies occupy the space just beyond it. Some travelers report a mild, persistent pressure behind the eyes after hours of this; it is not toxicity so much as the nervous system registering occupancy it was never meant to quantify.

The water is everywhere and it is not yours. It has been cycled through industrial maintainers for an unknown duration, mixed with whatever chemistry the pale mats and mineral blooms produce, and densely occupied. Do not drink it. Do not assume it is sterile. Carry more potable water than the distance suggests you need; the sameness of the pan defeats ordinary time-and-distance estimates. Distances that should tire you do not quite, yet the effort of constant wading, the extra work of breathing thick air, and the low-grade startle of contact add up. Electrolyte replacement is useful because you will sweat inside the suit even in the even warmth. Food must be calorie-dense, sealed, and require no local resources. There are no plants. The fauna are unknown biochemistry; some may be edible, most are not worth the experiment on a first visit. Pack for a minimum of several days beyond your planned window. The place occupies time rather than traveling through it; clocks and hunger remain the most reliable markers that an interval has occurred.

Skin and wound care matter more than usual. Every vertical plane bleeds film. Every time you put a hand down you meet something living. Carry a robust first-aid kit with extra waterproof dressings, antiseptic that works in high humidity, and a plan for how you will keep a wound dry enough to close. The scurfing and chemical mats are not described as caustic, but they are not described as inert either. Gloves that allow dexterity while remaining sealed at the wrist are worth the weight. The larger shapes in the warm galleries under the plates are slow and pressed together; they do not hunt in any way you can watch. Still, do not put unprotected skin into a maintenance slit. The world was built (or adjusted) for this load; it has no obvious mechanism for rejecting a new, larger, clumsy animal, but it also has no mechanism for making space.

Navigation and orientation require tools the landscape itself will not supply. There is no bedrock, no soil that was not mixed, no horizon that is not more of the same slightly lifted by heat-haze. The grammar of tray, weir, gallery-mouth, housing, and low baffle repeats until it generates its own slow wind. Maker’s stamps and bolt patterns remain bright, but they are identical across continents of the same pour. A GPS or equivalent inertial system that does not rely on satellites (none are mentioned) is essential; so is a simple mechanical compass as backup, though magnetic anomalies from the buried charge-runs are possible. Mark your own trail with something biodegradable or easily retrieved if you intend to leave no trace, but understand that “leave no trace” is already the local condition—nothing erodes, nothing grows over, nothing forgets the last pour-mark. Record headings and times obsessively. The light is ordinary high even day-brightness that might be a sun or a remote ceiling; shadows stay short. Night, when it is allowed, is a normal dusk into a normal dark in which the water holds a dull gleam and the animals become a continuous slight pressure. Carry redundant light sources that work when wet. The machines do not change pitch. The haze does not lift. You can sleep against a housing and wake to the same temperature on your cheek and believe no interval passed. That belief will be approximately correct. Use a watch, a journal, and scheduled check-ins with yourself.

Psychological preparation is the larger part of the work. The overwhelming fact is tactile and visual saturation without acoustic relief. The world is nearly silent except for the almost inaudible sheeting you hear only if you put an ear to a plate. Wings, if they exist, are not for display. Mouthparts work without sound. You will feel the faint shift of whatever uses the heat of a housing when you lean. You will not be able to put a hand into the water without meeting something. The animals’ faces, when they have faces, are too still or carry one feature too many by a small and consistent margin. This is not horror in the conventional sense; it is a mild, persistent tilt, a strangeness that sits on everything without violating sense. Distances do not quite tire. The same cluster of housings looks newly placed and also as if it has always been exactly there. The equilibrium includes them; birth and unmaking, if they occur, leave the count unchanged.

An ordinary human mind looks for gradients, narratives, and empty intervals. Here the gradients were locked. Morning is not different from afternoon except by the angle of light. The making stopped meaning anything. What remains is the fact of the pan, warm and wet and inhabited past the point of metaphor. Some travelers experience this as profound calm, a manufactured marsh running forever in the same hour. Others experience it as a low-grade claustrophobia of occupancy or an agoraphobia of endless repetition. Both reactions are reasonable. Pack whatever mental tools you already use: breathing practices that work in a respirator, a small physical object that is unambiguously from elsewhere, a recorded voice, a schedule of observations you will make even if nothing changes. Do not expect the place to provide punctuation. It was finished and then left finished.

Behavioral notes follow from the physics of the place. Move slowly. The water is nowhere deeper than calf in the collecting trays; there are no sudden drop-offs mentioned, only engineered shallows and channels whose corners remain unrounded. The fauna do not flee and do not hunt in any observable way; they simply occupy. Stepping on the broad pale crawlers is inevitable and apparently inconsequential to the equilibrium. The pulsing unsegmented things in the film will continue pulsing. The larger gallery shapes will remain pressed together in the warm dark. You are the novelty. Treat the machines as infrastructure, not mystery: they maintain, they do not manufacture, their intakes and returns are no louder than the water. Heat exchange and buried charge-runs produce faint warmth through the tile; it is useful, not threatening. Do not attempt to “explore the galleries” without a specific, well-supported reason and a way to extract yourself; they are a second, denser country already spoken for.

Health contingencies: constant moisture plus unknown biology means any break in the skin is a larger event than usual. Respiratory irritation from the haze load is likely; have a plan to reduce exposure (hood sealed, shorter excursions). The even warmth reduces some thermal risks but increases others (heat retention inside the suit, hydration). There is no mention of predators, pathogens, or toxins that act on human timescales, but absence of mention is not absence. Assume the chemistry that prevents rust and staining is not optimized for your microbiome. Carry broad-spectrum treatments and know how you will signal for extraction if your particular multiverse arrangement allows it. If it does not, carry more of everything.

What visiting actually feels like, once the gear is on and the first hour has passed: you are wading through a warm industrial bathroom the size of a world, except the bathroom is alive and the life does not care that you are there. The air has weight and occasional soft collisions. Surfaces are never dry. The light is ordinary and the shadows stay short. The smell is wet metal, faint sweet mineral, and the crowding itself. At night the pressure becomes continuous and the water gleams dully and you can sleep against a housing and wake to the identical film, the identical temperature, the identical uncountable attention in the shallows. Time is occupied rather than spent. The grammar of the place—tray, weir, housing, baffle, three rungs into a dark full of bodies—repeats until it is the only grammar. You will not leave the same intention underfoot no matter how far you walk.

Pack for that. Waterproof the body, filter the air, carry your own water and calories and time-keeping, and decide in advance what “enough” looks like when the world itself will not tell you. The place was made, filled, brought to balance, and then the making stopped. You are the only unfinished thing in it. Treat that fact with the seriousness it deserves.
