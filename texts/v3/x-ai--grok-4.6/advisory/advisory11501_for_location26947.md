**Suit, life support, and the medium itself.**  
You will be immersed from the first instant and will remain immersed for the entire visit; there is no transition, no beach, no air-water interface that behaves like one. An ordinary human body cannot survive more than minutes in water a few degrees above freezing, so the traveler’s first requirement is a closed, actively heated, full-coverage suit whose insulation and circulation can hold core temperature indefinitely while rejecting the constant heat sink of the surrounding liquid. The suit must also supply breathing gas (closed-circuit rebreather with extreme endurance or an equivalent metabolic recycler) because there is no usable atmosphere to surface into; even if a surface lid exists far above, it is high-altitude and the distances are measured in days of travel. The water itself is chemically young and almost empty of dissolved organics, so it will not foul membranes quickly, but it carries a faint mineral taste and microscopic rock flour; filters must handle that silicate dust. Pressure is not the enemy the depth would suggest; the highland origin of the water column means ordinary recreational or technical diving pressures apply, not hadal ones. The suit therefore needs no exotic crush resistance, only reliability, redundancy, and the ability to be worn for weeks.

Because the water never freezes and the stone never weathers, the suit’s exterior can be relatively simple, but every joint and glove must remain dexterous. You will want to touch the knife-edge ridges and the still-torqued bolts of the machines; the surfaces are surgical and will cut unprotected skin or cheap fabric instantly. A thin, even rust bloom exists on the metal but does not progress, so corrosion is not a short-term threat.

**Sound, vibration, and the second pressure.**  
The roar is not background; it is a standing environmental constant transmitted perfectly through the cold medium. It arrives as both audible thunder and bone-conducted vibration, a second temperature and a second hydrostatic load. Unprotected human hearing will fatigue and then fail within hours; the inner ear and the skeleton itself will register the note continuously. Prepare with high-quality, sealed hearing protection that still allows enough vibration through the suit to feel the turbines and the settling stone (you will want that information). Many travelers report that after the first day the roar becomes a kind of silence, a held chord that no longer registers as discrete events. Accept this; fighting it produces only exhaustion. There is no modulation, no storm, no night, no change in intensity. Pack whatever mental or pharmacological tools you use for unbroken sensory monotony. The engines add only punctuation; they do not create the sound and they will not stop.

**Propulsion, navigation, and scale.**  
Distances between solid features are planetary. An unaided swimmer will cover a former nation’s worth of empty water and encounter perhaps three interruptions. You therefore need independent, high-endurance propulsion—personal thrusters, a small free-flooding sled, or a compact submersible that can loiter for days on the electrical or thermal waste of the isolated machines. Inertial navigation, dead-reckoning, and whatever local beacons or magnetic anomalies the young basalt still produces are essential; visual landmarks are vanishingly rare and the even, undiminished daylight gives no horizon or depth cue. Currents exist and are strong along the knife-edged ridges; they are part of the unchanging weather and can be used, but they will not carry you toward anything you have not already aimed at. Energy management is the real constraint. The machines themselves (pumping houses, generator halls, pressure vessels) are still running and appear to extract minerals, charge, or the cold; they are the only reliable power and heat sources you will find. Learn to approach them, to tap waste heat or current without interfering with their equilibrium function, and to moor against their cliffs. They are not cities and they are not hospitable, but they are the only places where a human-scale pause is possible.

**Sustenance and the living interruptions.**  
The water will not feed you. There are no plants. The pale, faintly metallic swimmers travel in curtains that take hours to pass and appear to graze the electrical fringe of the machines or the rock flour itself. The clinging reefs of chitin and carbonate flex but do not migrate. Both are abundant once you finally reach a ridge, startling after the emptiness. Whether they are metabolically compatible with an ordinary human is unknown; treat them as potentially toxic until proven otherwise and carry closed-loop food and water for the entire expected duration plus large margins. The animals add almost nothing to the roar and their routes are closed, eternal loops. They will neither flee nor attack in any familiar sense; they simply occupy their fixed paths. Give the living curtains wide berth if your electronics or suit currents might attract them. The reefs look architectural until they move; do not rest against them casually.

**Psychological and temporal reality.**  
Nothing else will happen. The place has already completed its single permitted event—the raising of the highland and the filling of it—and now occupies that state without gradient. Yesterday and tomorrow are the same loud, clear, empty, brightly lit volume. Time is a held note. An ordinary human mind expects weather, night, decay, arrival, departure. None of those occur. Prepare for the specific fatigue of perfect stasis: the knowledge that you can swim for days, stand on a catwalk while turbines sing through your boots, watch a living cloud occult a ridge for half a day, and the scene will be identical when you return a month later. Some travelers find this restful; most find it eroding. Bring whatever personal anchors (recordings, texts, rituals, companions if the vehicle allows) you need to remember that change still exists elsewhere. Do not expect the machines to fail, the water to warm or freeze, the stone to dull, or the light to fade. Those are not possibilities here.

**Approach and conduct.**  
Enter knowing you are a temporary, fragile interruption in a system that does not require you and will not notice your departure. Move slowly near the sharp geology and the still-living industrial hearts. The works are not ruined and are not waiting to be repaired; they are part of the equilibrium. Do not attempt to shut anything down; shutting down is not a recognized verb. If you extract power or minerals, do so at the waste streams the place already produces. Trace the fracture mirrors with a gloved hand if you wish; they have never known rain or rounding and they will remain that way. When you have seen three ridges and one generator hall you have seen the grammar of the entire highland sea. The rest is distance and the same note.

Leave before your consumables or your tolerance for the unchanging roar expire. The place will continue exactly as it is, whether you were there or not. That is the entire preparation and the entire visit.
