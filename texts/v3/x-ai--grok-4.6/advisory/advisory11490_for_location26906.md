**Prepare as if entering a living geological instrument that has already finished its argument with time.** This is not a landscape you visit so much as a continuous physical and sensory event you temporarily occupy. An ordinary human body—evolved for lower, quieter, more stable worlds—is a small, upright, noisy, water-heavy error here. The plateau will not accommodate you; you must become as close to optional as possible while remaining alive.

### Core Realities You Must Internalize First
The surface is ancient, rounded, pitted, warm stone (bone-and-iron-tea color) at extreme altitude. Air is thin; daylight is mercilessly clean and sharp; distances lie. The wind is not weather—it is the medium, occupying the entire volume continuously from multiple directions. It planes the air, forms collapsing weather cells you can watch assemble and die, and turns the fluted rock into a regional-scale instrument. Everyday speech dies at a few meters. A change in the chord is useful data; everything else is local custom.

Water is a brief visitor (cell-rains, nocturnal condensation, shredded fog). Surface rivers cannot persist. Vegetation is either a waxed, ankle-high living carpet on the open flats (jungle viewed from above when you kneel) or shameless hanging, dripping, stacking lushness in any sheltered flute, bowl, or lee. Lush pockets have different air: still, syrupy, green-smelling. People exist in small numbers: they use hand-talk, sequenced marked stones in lees, forehead-to-forehead contact in rare still pockets, wind-houses baffled with replaceable mats, hanging fog-gardens of gut/fiber/bark, and offerings in cavities. Repair and listening are the technologies. Silence (sudden calm) is not rest; it is the plateau inhaling and often contains brief, unseen life. Time of day, light, and even your own thoughts begin to take the plateau’s shape after prolonged exposure. Mist can hold fading impressions. Plants in deep shelters rearrange when wind cannot reach them. The stone under your palm has a pulse that may or may not be only yours.

You will arrive already exhausted by the altitude and find nowhere obvious to rest. Travel is done in the early warmth before the stone fully wakes and storms assemble on the rim. You sleep in a hollow whose pitch you can tolerate. You eat the carpet (if you know how to peel it) and whatever hanging gardens yield. Meat is almost never. You learn the roar that means “move” versus the roar that means “get in the hole now.”

### Physical Conditioning and Acclimatization
Train for extreme altitude well in advance: long high-elevation stays, hypoxic tents or chambers if available in your origin world, cardiovascular endurance, and isometric/core work against constant lateral force. The wind will try to plane you; a stiff, low, aerodynamic posture becomes second nature. Practice moving while nearly horizontal or using the ground as a third point of contact. Strengthen hands, forearms, and grip—communication, tool use, and clinging to flutes will be constant.

Expect altitude sickness symptoms compounded by dehydration, sleep disruption from the roar, and caloric burn from fighting wind and cold-at-altitude (the stone is warm, the air often is not). Bring proven medications (acetazolamide or equivalent, dexamethasone for emergencies) and a pulse oximeter. Pre-acclimatize as high as possible, then plan a staged approach if your arrival method allows; many travelers still hit the table already depleted. Hydration is non-negotiable and difficult—sip constantly from collected water. The body will try to lose moisture to the dry roar and sudden temperature swings.

Skin, eyes, and mucous membranes need protection from UV (thinner atmosphere), abrasive wind-borne particles, and sudden wetting/drying cycles. The light makes colors “too themselves”; prolonged exposure can produce a kind of visual fatigue or afterimage strangeness.

### Clothing, Shelter, and Personal Equipment
Clothing must be windproof, layered, repairable, and low-profile. Think multiple thin, tough shells that can be cinched into an almost sealed second skin, with aerodynamic hoods, goggles or wrap-around visors that still allow peripheral vision (storms rewrite light; you need to see cells forming), and gloves that permit fine manipulation and hand-talk. Avoid anything that can act as a sail or flap. Ear protection is paradoxical: you need to hear pitch changes in the chord, so use removable or adjustable attenuation rather than total isolation. Many long-term visitors develop a practiced half-listening—filtering the roar for harmonics while protecting against hearing damage. Voice is nearly useless; carry a small slate or set of marked stones for the local sequence-language, and learn the hand signs quickly.

Shelter is found, not built. Identify flutes, bowls, and lees in advance if possible (the last knobs and hogbacks). Carry replacement hanging mats (fiber, beaten bark, or equivalent that can be locally sourced and repaired), fog-intercept netting or gut/fiber strings for water gardens, and a few collapsible bowls or skins. A good cave sings *and* drips. Learn to baffle the mouth without creating a sail. Portable tents or rigid structures are generally shredded or become dangerous projectiles; the wind has already rejected architecture that argues with it. Sleep in whatever hollow has a tolerable pitch. The drip of harvested fog into a bowl becomes a metronome.

Water collection is life. Replicate the local hanging gardens immediately. Any fabric, fiber, or even hair can intercept fog and condensation. The nocturnal “sweats” when warm stone meets colder air are reliable if you position collectors correctly. Brief cell-rains are violent and short; catch what you can without becoming a target for spray. Never assume a water source will persist across seasons—the weather changes its mind.

Food: study the carpet’s understory and the hanging species before arrival if your multiverse access allows knowledge transfer. Learn what can be peeled, what drips edible moisture, what is merely structural. Bring compact, high-calorie, non-perishable supplements as a buffer, but plan to transition. Meat is rare and opportunistic (tracks appear and vanish in calms). Offerings of fiber, tools, teeth, or dried leaf in small cavities are part of the local grammar—participate or at least do not disturb them.

Navigation tools must not rely on sound or stable landmarks. Distances deceive; a cairn that looks an hour away is four. Carry redundant visual methods: a sun compass (understanding that storms can deliver “noon” multiple times or never), marked standing slabs if you can locate the old wind-note rows, and a habit of counting steps and noting flute pitches as waypoints. The rim is a distant line that looks like a seeing error. Birds are rare slivers that do not often land. GPS or equivalent from other worlds may function but treat it as secondary; the plateau’s emptiness and weather cells can produce their own interference or simply make you ignore the device.

Other kit: a small repair kit (bone, stone, fire-hardened wood, hammered native metal if you find a vein), fire-making that works in wind (or accept cold food), basic medical (blisters from wind, eye abrasion, altitude, infection in wet pockets), and something to mark your own temporary sequences of stones. Nothing that assumes the wind will become reasonable.

### Sensory, Psychological, and Social Preparation
The roar is not background; on violent days it is a single continuous sound you occupy, with harmonics in the teeth. In a true storm the plateau becomes a throat. Speech does not travel. Practice communicating without voice long before you arrive: hand signs, sequenced objects, tactile contact. Children here are taught that listening is for weather, not words. You must do the same.

The strangeness is slow. After days your thoughts start to take the plateau’s shape—rounded, pitted, horizontal, patient. Time becomes opinionated. Mist rooms hold fading impressions of whatever last walked through. Deep hanging plants move when they should not. The stone’s warmth and faint pulse can feel like conversation. People who stay too long begin treating the wind as a “who,” then stop speaking of it because speech is expensive and the who is obvious. Prepare for this with whatever mental practices you use for isolation, sensory overwhelm, and ego dissolution. Humility is not optional; the place solved uprightness long ago. A person is optional here.

Learn the local inhabitants’ rhythms if you encounter them. They live in the lee of things, replace mats after bad weeks, and treat the instrument’s present pitch as something one might politely ask to linger. Do not expect towns or loud greetings. A forehead touch in a rare still pocket, a sequence of stones, or shared water from a hanging garden is conversation. Offerings in cavities are either thanks or requests. Respect the grammar.

Calms are a different violence: the instrument stops mid-note, ears ring in a silence that feels like new noise, and whatever lives in those minutes appears and vanishes. Do not treat silence as safety. Get into a hole or stay very still and observe.

### Movement and Timing
Move in the early warmth, low and listening. Watch weather cells form on the horizon; a wall of torn cloud walking toward you for an hour arrives as furious brief rain then departs before the stone darkens. Then condensation, then sun, then another cell. The land does not change. Time your crossings between green bowls and flutes. Learn the difference in the chord that signals “keep going” versus “now.” When in doubt, find a hollow and wait. The sky cannot stop; you can.

Crossings are measured in half-days of nobody and nothing standing—just pelt, stone, and enormous daylight. The rim, when visible, is so far it looks mistaken. Beyond it the world falls through stacked weathers you will not visit.

### After Arrival: How It Actually Feels
You will be small, loud in a place that has no use for loudness, and constantly aware of being a temporary upright interruption. The warmth of the stone underfoot and palm is intimate and geological. The lush pockets feel like entering a different country—sudden syrupy quiet, drip-tick of growth, green smell—then you step out and the volume of the world drops into roar and vacancy again. You will harvest water that arrived as mist and will leave as mist. You will sleep to the sound of a cave singing and dripping. You will walk for hours on a living carpet that is a jungle only when you kneel. You will see tracks that begin and end in bare stone, bones scoured light as paper, and pale kites glinting then gone.

The oldest surface. The youngest weather. A few green secrets, holes full of song, and the long warm deafening vacancy. Prepare to be optional. Listen more than you speak. Repair what you use. Leave the pitch as you found it, or better.

If your multiverse method allows scouting or knowledge transfer, prioritize maps of major flute concentrations, known water-garden hollows, and the current “grammar” of stone sequences. Nothing replaces standing there, already tired, feeling the stone’s old heat and realizing the sky is still inventing itself. Travel light in every sense. The plateau has already finished becoming what it is. You have not.
