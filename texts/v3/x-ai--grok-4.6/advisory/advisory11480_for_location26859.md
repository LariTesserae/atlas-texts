**Prepare as if you are about to become a temporary, air-breathing parasite inside a finished, waiting organism that was never meant for you.**

The Upper Vat is not a place you “visit” in the ordinary sense. It is a single continuous volume of warm process water packed to production density with engineered plants, steel, and slow machines. There is no air, no surface, no open water to swim in, no current worth mentioning, and no other animals. Daylight arrives already thickened into green-gold columns and sheets. You will move by accepting friction against living tissue and bright metal. You will hear almost nothing. The mountain around you is new enough that the welds still show peacock colours and the lifting lugs are still sharp. Outside the glazed walls the weather is thin and empty; clouds pass below you. The intended attendants never arrived. Their empty, too-many-limbed work-frames now serve as trellis. The plants are simply following the last remaining instruction: grow until stopped.

An ordinary human body cannot survive here without deliberate, redundant life-support and a change in how you think about space, time, and your own skin. The following is what you actually need.

### Breathing and life support (non-negotiable)
You will be fully immersed for the entire duration. There is no air pocket, no hatch you can pop, no “just swim up.” A standard open-circuit scuba set is possible but clumsy: bubbles will rise slowly through the packed leaves and may snag or simply look alien in a volume that has been sterile since fill. A closed-circuit rebreather is strongly preferable. It gives you hours instead of minutes, produces almost no exhaust, and keeps your breathing silent so you can actually hear the small sounds that exist (thermostat clicks, a single bubble detaching from a weld and taking half a minute to decide which way is up, the rasp of a leaf-edge on cable when the whole mountain flexes in the wind that exists only outside).

Carry at least two independent gas sources and a bailout bottle you can actually reach while wedged between a wrist-thick stem and a still-bright bolt head. The water is blood-heat, so thermal stress is not the issue; oxygen metabolism and CO₂ management are. Scrubber duration must exceed your planned time plus a realistic extraction delay. The water itself is slightly sweet and metallic. Do not drink it. Do not assume it is harmless if it gets into your mouth or a cut. Treat it as industrial process fluid that happens to be supporting a crop.

Bring more drinking water than you think you need, in a system that cannot leak into or be contaminated by the vat. Human urine and faeces must be contained; this volume was sterilised at fill and has been kept that way. You are the only potential contaminant that moves.

### The body interface
A thin wetsuit or a tough, snug drysuit undergarment plus a light oversuit is better than a thick wetsuit. The water is already at blood temperature; you will overheat if you insulate too much. What you need is mechanical protection. Leaves the size of sails, stems like wrists, trained tendrils through eye-bolts, and unweathered steel edges will abrade or cut you. The purple bladder-moss secretes a slow varnish; other cultivars may have their own exudates. Cover every centimetre. Gloves that still let you feel and grip are essential. A hood is optional but useful against the constant light pressure of vegetation against your head.

Fins are usually more hindrance than help. There is no room to kick. You will pull yourself along by handholds that were never designed as handholds: I-beam flanges, clamp-collars, the empty harnesses. Short, stiff fins or none at all. Streamline everything. Anything that can snag will snag. Dangling gauges, loose straps, or a floppy second stage will become part of the crop.

Vision: a good mask or full-face mask. The light is ordinary daylight that has been forced to travel through metres of packed greenery and warm water; it goes from pale column to old-bottle-green in a short distance. A small, wide-beam torch on a Goodman handle (so you can still use that hand) is useful in the deeper racks and under the false floors of punched stainless grating. Backup light, because the place is large enough to get turned around in and the green is surprisingly disorienting.

### Movement and the first law (density)
You cannot swim. You can only choose which friction to accept. Practise this beforehand if you can: move through a kelp forest, a very thick reed bed, or a flooded greenhouse at night. Learn to part, slide, and accept contact rather than fight for open water that does not exist. Your body will be in continuous light contact with living tissue. That is normal here. Fighting it wastes energy and damages the crop.

The “floor” is a grating three metres down, then another, then the true hull. You can go under if you wish, but the spaces between gratings are also packed. Overhead the harvest gantry hangs unused. The work-frames hang like dark, dripping skeletons; plants have already begun to treat them as infrastructure. A lacquer-weed filling a helmet is not decay; it is occupation. Do not try to “clear” anything. You are not staff. You are not the cancelled attendants.

Move slowly. Everything else does. Circulators turn at geological speed. Nutrient pulses are fortnightly. A leaf adds a millimetre. If you thrash you will only announce yourself as the one object that does not belong to the yield calculation.

### Navigation, time, and the view
The volume is church-sized, ribbed, glazed on three sides. You can always find a glazed wall and look out. That view is one of the reasons to come: you will be hanging in warm green water at the altitude of raptors, watching empty air and clouds passing *below* the sills, the Vat’s own superstructure throwing a hard shadow down the made cliffs, a distant service road like a scratch. No birds. The emptiness outside rhymes with the emptiness inside. It is easy to lose track of time. Bring a simple, luminous watch or a timer you can feel. The light cycle is ordinary daylight, but inside it has no sparkle; there is not enough free motion.

Know your exit. The outer doors still have plates that say Upper Vat. How you entered (lock, cut, whatever method your travel permits) is how you leave. Do not assume there is another way. The mountain is a stacked mass of tanks and halls; getting lost in the adjacent volumes would be easy and possibly fatal.

### Psychological and sensory preparation
This place is finished and waiting. It feels like a sentence that was written, punctuated, and then left on a desk no one will ever read. The silence is not empty; it is the sound of a clean process that has not yet been given a reason to stop. After a while you stop listening for animals because there are none and there never were. That realisation can be peaceful or it can feel like a kind of grief. The unused harnesses, still dripping, sized for a body that would have breathed this water, can feel like a rebuke or a ghost story.

You will be alone with the plants and the iron. The plants are not wild; they sit in registered intervals. They open waxed white flowers on a calendar, releasing nothing the water will carry. There is no scent, no insect, no fish-shadow. If that sterility bothers you, you will notice it immediately. If the density and the slow occupation of the work-frames feel like a living machine completing itself, you may find it beautiful.

Do not expect conversation, rescue, or interpretation. The vat does not care that you are there. That is the point.

### What to bring and what to leave
- Rebreather + bailout, redundant.
- Protective covering that still lets you feel.
- Knife or shears you can reach with either hand (entanglement is the main mechanical risk).
- Lights, timer, drinking water, waste containment.
- A way to record if you wish (the light is strange and worth seeing later), but keep it compact.
- Nothing that sheds, leaks, or rusts. The mountain is too new for rust; do not be the first stain.
- No food that requires preparation. Eat before you enter or carry sealed rations you will not open inside.
- No weapons, no samples unless you have a reason that outweighs the fact that you are walking through a production crop that has been left to its own instruction.

Leave behind any assumption that you are the protagonist. You are not harvesting, not repairing, not commissioning. You are moving through a volume that was designed, filled, and then simply continued. The plants have already begun to treat the cancelled attendants’ equipment as just more structure. They will treat you the same way if you stay still long enough.

### After
When you leave, the water will feel like it is still on your skin for a while. The air outside, if you step out onto the made mountain, will be thin and bright and shockingly empty of green. You may find ordinary rooms too hollow, ordinary silence too silent, ordinary plants too sparse. That is normal. You have been inside a single packed organism with a steel skeleton, a forest that is also a machine, a room that is also a sea, raised where seas do not belong.

You do not need to understand why the attendants were cancelled. You only need to have been brief enough, careful enough, and quiet enough that the vat can go back to waiting without having noticed you as anything more than another temporary friction. That is the most respectful way an ordinary human can pass through a place that was finished without them.
