**Enter as a guest of an already-completed world.** This pocket is not a frontier or a ruin; it is a finished vessel that has long since reached equilibrium with its own dampness, its own single unending sound, and its own scarcity. An ordinary human body and mind from a surface world (open air, variable weather, green things, silence, dryness as default) will experience it as a continuous, intimate compression. Preparation is less about conquest or even endurance and more about becoming a temporary, non-disruptive surface that the pocket can tolerate without having to recalibrate itself.

**Psychological and sensory inoculation (start weeks or months ahead).**  
The dominant facts are closeness, wetness, and the chord. Train for all three simultaneously. Spend extended periods in tight, unventilated, dripping spaces with a constant low-frequency drone (a waterfall recording looped, a large fan plus water, or an industrial humidifier in a closet or cellar). Practice sleeping, eating, speaking, and thinking while a thick vertical note occupies the entire volume of air. Newcomers lose speech for three days; the chord becomes the floor of hearing and human voices sit on top of it like lichen. You will not “get used to” the sound so much as have it rewrite the baseline of your nervous system. Meditation, breath-counting, or any practice that treats a persistent stimulus as ground rather than figure will help. Claustrophobia training must include not only tightness but *occupied* tightness: other bodies shifting above and below you, knees audible, lamp-fat smell from the next slot, no private corner. Accept that privacy, dryness, and quiet are not available and that wanting them will mark you as unfinished. The settlement occupies its shape the way water occupies a cup; your job is not to enlarge the cup.

The absence of green, of weather, of a sky, of any “beyond” that is livable, produces a specific melancholy or vertigo in surface people. The world here has already happened. Nothing is being built that was not already implied. Prepare for the feeling that time has been replaced by maintenance. Short-term visitors sometimes report a seductive peace in that fact; longer stays can produce a hollowing, as if your own unfinishedness (plans, growth, elsewhere) has become a kind of rudeness. Decide in advance how long you intend to stay and how you will leave. The two crawls are known only to residents; once inside, the pocket *is* the amount of world that can be held.

**Physical and medical preparation.**  
Your body will be a managed wet. Clothes never dry; skin never dries. Still water on skin invites the white bloom—a chalky mineralization that the rock begins to treat as another surface. Residents scrape it with slate. You must do the same, daily or more, especially in folds, under bindings, around eyes and mouth. Bring a thin, hard scraping tool (slate if you can source it, or a dense ceramic or bone analog) and practice the motion until it is automatic. Barrier oils or waxes that do not go rancid in cool damp may help, but test them; anything that films over and becomes still is a risk. Antifungal and antibacterial prophylactics are wise; the air is a broth, the surfaces are shared, and the only kiln-fire is a single unchanging niche. Respiratory irritation from constant mineral moisture and lamp-fat soot is likely. If you have any tendency toward damp-related lung issues, this place will find it.

The temperature is mouth-cool. Breath condenses near the upstream pinch. You will not freeze, but you will never be warm in the surface sense. Layers should be wringable, quick to manage, and resistant to rot—wool, certain plant fibers if you can keep them from mineralizing, or modern synthetics that do not trap still water against skin. Avoid anything that rusts, molds easily, or off-gasses. Footwear is almost irrelevant; movement is crouched, crawling, or careful stepping over black rectangles of standing water. Joints and back will complain from the hanging floors (split slate lashed with mineralized lace) and the low rooms sized for lying-down. Condition for that geometry.

Air is limited. Extra flames eat it. Do not arrive with an open flame or a device that consumes oxygen as a primary function. If you bring electric light, keep it minimal, red-shifted or amber if possible (to match their tallow-and-limestone color), and battery-conservative. They will notice any light that travels farther than theirs. The dozen small hooded flames are already the maximum the pocket can sustain. Your presence is an extra metabolism; be still when you can.

Diet and waste: their protein is rare (pale, eyeless finger-length things caught against the fall, shared in slivers the same day) plus rendered grease that includes, carefully, their own. Do not introduce new smells or decaying matter. Pack dense, low-odor, non-perishable calories that you can consume without residue. Share if invited; refusing can be read as hoarding in a place with no surplus. Human waste must be managed exactly as they do (whatever that system is—ask, watch, copy). The cistern below the throat is unseen and ancient; do not contaminate the fall.

**Approach, entry, and social contract.**  
The ways in are two crawls, known only to those who already live here. You cannot simply arrive. Preparation includes a method of announcement that does not assume surface norms: a gift of something they cannot easily make (a length of unrotted fiber, a small quantity of metal they can beat thinner, a tool of dense ceramic or flint that fits their existing kit, or rendered fat that does not smell alien). Metal is rare and already stretched from one old seam. Do not bring abundance; abundance is an accusation. They have visitors rarely. Expect three days of functional deafness to speech, after which you will be known by the particular interruption you make in the chord, by smell (limestone, tallow, crowded heat, your own unaccustomed surface-world residue), and by the new worn places your body begins to leave.

Density is the civic principle. No surface is unassigned. You will sleep in a slot, smell the upstairs neighbor’s lamp, hear their knees. You will know everyone by how they sit on the sound. Reciprocity is labor and non-disruption: dress stone, twist fiber, scoop basins, feed the kiln its black cake of mineral lace and grease, teach or learn the crawls if trusted. Do not try to improve the hanging floors, the thin ringing walls, the equilibrium fire, or the chord. They have already found the angle at which everything will fall forever. Curiosity about the uninhabited wet dark beyond the downstream wall, or about going farther, will be met with the same answer: that is not a life, that is only weather. Respect the pocket as the finished amount of world.

**What to carry, what to leave behind.**  
- Scrapers, wringable layers, compact calories, minimal non-consuming light, a way to mark the crawls *only if they permit it*.  
- Something to occupy the three silent days (a tactile practice, a memory discipline).  
- Medical kit focused on skin integrity, respiration, and unknown cave pathogens.  
- No living plants, no seeds, no soil, no open flame, no recording devices that introduce new light or sound unless you have already negotiated them. Memory here is short and the stone is long; they have reached an arrangement. Your desire to document may be politely ignored or treated as unfinished.  
- An exit plan that does not depend on their help beyond the crawls. Once the chord has become the floor of your hearing, leaving can feel like amputation.

**While you are inside.**  
Move as they move: subtracted, packed, never still-wet. Scrape. Wring. Step the basins. Speak on top of the chord, not against it. Let the air’s exact circuit carry a flake of ash and watch it return; that is the tempo. The pale things, if one appears, are an event and are eaten the same day. The clicking in the high seams is not your concern. The bruise of lesser dark on the upstream wall may or may not still brighten; the lamps are trimmed by habit that has outlasted the bruise. You will smell your own mineralization beginning. Scrape.

The settlement is not dying and not growing. It is occupying its shape. Your visit is a brief, extra film on a wall that has been sweating longer than they have a word for. Prepare to be temporary, wet-handed, close-quartered, and loud only in the way that has forgotten how to begin or end. Then, when you crawl out, the first thing you will notice is not the light. It is that the rock has stopped sweating on you, and the single word has stopped being spoken. That silence will be its own kind of weather. Have a place ready for it.
