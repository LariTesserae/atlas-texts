**High-altitude acclimatization, total self-sufficiency in water and calories, sun/UV/dryness protection, and a deliberate mental stance of patient non-interference.** This plateau is not a landscape you enter so much as a finished, over-saturated still-life you briefly occupy. An ordinary human body will register the height first (thin air, pressure in the teeth and sinuses, unfinished breaths), then the dryness and chemical wrongness, then the visual and ontological density that never resolves into ordinary animal behavior. Time and distance behave politely but unreliably. Nothing here needs you, feeds you, or flees you. Prepare as if visiting a vast, silent, high-desert museum of forms that has already decided how many living things it can hold.

**Physical and environmental realities to plan around.** The plateau sits at an elevation where the sky has no middle distance and the curvature of the world is visible as a whitening rim. Ordinary human physiology will feel hypoxia immediately: shorter useful breath, possible headache, tachycardia, reduced judgment. Temperature stays in a cool, stone-after-night register with almost no weather variation; there is no seasonal cycle that matters and little diurnal swing worth planning around. Light is hard, haze-free, and merciless—intense UV with no atmospheric buffer. The ground is a packed flour of salt, gypsum, and older mineral that has already reached its angle of rest; it does not drift much but will abrade, cake in seams, and taste of iron, old batteries, and a non-sugar sweetness if it contacts mucous membranes. There is no free water, no useful vegetation, and no fauna that can be eaten or that will respond dynamically. The local chemistry is non-aqueous and faintly incompatible; do not lick the salt, do not assume any residue is potable, and treat every surface as potentially irritating or dessicating.

The animals form a packed, overlapping, multi-scale saturation (fist-sized to house-sized, some with too many joints, some occupying the same volume in ways that should be impossible). They do not startle, part, vocalize, or change. You will sometimes pass through a flank as if it were a fold or a hole shaped like a creature; sometimes you will meet dry, paper-cold hide and must step around or over. Distances lie: a ring of slabs that appeared a mile off can be under your hand without an interval of approach. Sounds you did not hear can arrive later as sinus pressure. The entire field can seem to inhale and the sky darken a half-shade before returning, with nothing having moved. Reality here is not broken; it is worn and over-occupied. The support for this density is stillness itself, height, the refusal of water, and an agreement older than weather.

**Gear and supplies (bring everything; assume zero resupply or foraging).** 
- Water is the non-negotiable priority. Calculate generously for high-altitude dry air plus the extra respiratory work and any psychological stress that increases breathing rate. Multiple independent containers, perhaps with a small solar still or condenser only as a desperate backup (night frost collection is mentioned in the ancient pits, but do not rely on it). Electrolytes. Do not experiment with local chemistry.
- Food: high-calorie, lightweight, non-perishable rations sufficient for the intended duration plus a large margin. Nothing here will feed you. The salt-crystal “bushes” and lichens are not food; the animals are not prey.
- Respiratory support: portable oxygen (cans or a lightweight concentrator if your traveler’s kit allows) and altitude medication (acetazolamide or equivalent, started in advance if possible). Even an “ordinary” traveler should treat this as a sudden-exposure high-altitude desert.
- Clothing and skin/eye protection: layered, breathable but insulating garments for cool dry conditions. Full sun coverage (wide-brim hat or hood, long sleeves, neck gaiter). High-SPF sunscreen reapplied frequently; the light has nothing wet to catch on and the altitude amplifies UV. Wraparound sunglasses or goggles (dust + glare + possible delayed or non-standard light behavior). Sturdy, broken-in boots that can handle packed mineral flour and occasional unexpected solid contacts; gaiters to keep salt out. Gloves for handling the ground or ancient metal foil. Lip balm, saline eye drops, moisturizer—the dryness is aggressive.
- Shelter and rest: a simple bivy or tarp may suffice because weather does not “arrive as news,” but bring it anyway for psychological enclosure and to create a small volume that is not the plateau. Sleeping pad and bag rated for cool nights. The ground will leach heat and the silence plus density can make rest feel exposed.
- Navigation and timekeeping: do not trust visual distance or “a day’s walk.” Bring a reliable analog compass, a simple pedometer or pace count method, and multiple timepieces. Mark your own path unobtrusively if you must (the plateau absorbs gestures without comment, but you may need the marks). The static arrangement of animals can serve as a kind of unchanging map once you accept that it never rearranges. Note the few built things (upright slabs with scoring, low salt-block walls, cairns, foil-lined pits) as rare landmarks.
- First aid and monitoring: standard wilderness kit plus altitude-specific items, blister care (salt abrasion), and something for sinus/ear pressure. A pulse oximeter if available. Record symptoms; judgment degrades quietly here.
- Recording and orientation tools: notebook, pencils, camera or equivalent. The place already feels like a held photograph; documenting helps you remember that you are a visitor who can leave. A small personal object that is unambiguously “yours” can serve as an anchor when distances or ontologies slip.
- Minimal tools: a knife, perhaps a small hammer or lever (the ancient people understood these). Do not expect to need them for animals or weather. Bone-and-copper tech level is the local maximum.

**Mental and experiential preparation.** This is the harder part. The plateau is dense the way a held breath is dense. You will walk through a crowd that has agreed, collectively, not to be a crowd. The silence is full of withheld noise. Eyes (or not-eyes) are open or shut in permanent attitudes. You are not a protagonist here; you are an additional volume that the place can accommodate because nothing needs to happen next. The weirdness is a wear in the fabric, not a spectacle—overused reality, stacked presences, polite lies of distance. Some travelers will find it courteous, even calming: there is room because there is room for everything, and the room is made of the fact that the mountains have already lain down and the rivers have taken their names. Others will feel the existential weight of a finished world that includes a saturation of life life cannot strictly support.

Prepare by practicing long periods of stillness and observation beforehand. Accept that you may walk for hours and the arrangement will be the same arrangement seen from a slightly different angle. Do not expect narrative, predation, weather drama, or change. Do not try to “interact” beyond walking. Touch is unpredictable; treat every potential contact as possibly solid, possibly not. If a sound arrives late or the herd-field inhales, note it and keep moving or keep standing—the plateau does not require a reaction. Time has the quality of a photograph that is also the actual place; do not schedule tightly or expect clocks to feel authoritative.

Decide in advance how long you will stay. Prolonged presence may let the density feel like courtesy, but an ordinary human body still needs water, oxygen, and eventual exit. The ancient structures suggest earlier visitors marked, counted, left a tool or two, and departed; the plateau absorbed the gesture. You are not required to leave an offering, but if you do, keep it hand-scale and honest (a count, a simple mark, a residue of something you brought). Do not try to “fix,” feed, or free anything. The fauna have already arrived at their last form.

**On-site conduct and exit.** Walk. The animals will not move out of your way. When you meet resistance, go around; when you phase, continue. Do not drink, do not eat the local, do not assume any chemistry is friendly. Watch your hydration and oxygen saturation more carefully than your map. The light will move; the plateau will not participate. If you feel the sky darken a half-shade or a delayed pressure in the sinuses, it is the place, not necessarily you failing. Leave while you still experience yourself as a traveler rather than another static presence. The return threshold (whatever gate, fold, or decision brought you) should be treated as time-sensitive even if the plateau itself is not.

An ordinary human can visit this place. You will feel the height in your teeth, the cool mineral air, the packed flour underfoot, and the impossible courtesy of a world that finished changing and then held every form it had ever received. Preparation is mostly the humility of bringing your own water, your own air, your own time, and the willingness to be a brief, moving exception in a landscape that no longer needs exceptions. The rest is walking through the silence until you decide the photograph no longer requires you.
