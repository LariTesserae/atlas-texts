**Preparation for the Hollow is an exercise in subtraction.** An ordinary human body and mind arriving from a world of gradients, weather, decay, and speech will experience the place as a completed circuit that neither wants nor needs you. The goal is not to survive an environment so much as to avoid becoming an error in it. You prepare by reducing everything that produces change, noise, expectation, or appetite, then carrying only what lets you remain a temporary, silent witness and still walk back out.

**Physical kit (keep it mute, damp-tolerant, and visually quiet).**  
The settled cold is not dramatic; it is a constant that never quite reaches frost or thaw. Breath fogs only if you exert yourself, and exertion itself feels slightly indecent. Wear multiple thin layers of wool or merino that insulate when wet, under a quiet, non-rustling shell in grey-green or stone tones. Avoid synthetics that crinkle or flash. Soft-soled, dark, broken-in shoes or moccasins—something that will not scuff the living carpet. The moss and leaf-tunnels will drink the sound of your steps before they finish, but you should still try not to make them. A thin, well-fitted hat and light gloves; the air is always the temperature of a held breath. Bring a compact down or synthetic puffy only if you plan to sit motionless for many subjective hours.

Damp is everywhere as a still film, hoar that never quite decides. Everything you carry should tolerate constant surface moisture without mildewing instantly. A small roll of quiet cloth for wiping lenses or skin. No metal that rings. If you must bring a knife, make it the dullest, most handled copper or bone you can find; iron and bright steel feel like a category error here. A simple wooden or bone cup if you intend to drink.

Food: dense, silent calories that do not crinkle—pemmican, dense fruit-nut bars wrapped in cloth, perhaps a little hard cheese. You will not be hungry in the usual way; the place has already finished eating. Eat only if your body insists, and eat sitting still. Water: the well is constant, clear, tasting of wet stone and crushed chlorophyll. It has never been lower or higher. Bring a small filter or chemical treatment if you are cautious, but many travelers simply drink after watching the inhabitants and animals do so with the same unhurried motion. Carry one spare liter from outside as a psychological anchor—something that came from a flowing stream.

Navigation and exit insurance: the lip is easy to miss even when you know it is there. From twenty paces the land looks ordinary. Tie a single, muted but non-local marker (a strip of faded cloth, a small stone from your own world with a distinct shape) at the exact crease where the path begins its descent. Do not use anything bright or synthetic that would shout. Count your steps down; the path is more a worn fold in moss than a trail. Carry a simple analog compass even if it may wander; the streets inside meet at proper angles but go only to other streets. A small notebook and graphite pencil (no clicking mechanisms). Digital devices frequently become inert or record only the same frame. A mechanical watch is useful mainly as a reminder that you still believe in duration.

No fire. No flashlight unless it is the dimmest red possible and used only at the lip. The light is already the condition of the hollow—noon-on-new-snow insistence without a sun you can point to. Shadows exist but do not lengthen. A headlamp is an admission you have not yet accepted the terms.

**Bodily and sensory preparation.**  
Spend at least several days beforehand in progressive quiet. An anechoic chamber or equivalent if you can arrange it; otherwise long hours in the stillest forest or snowfield you know, practicing not filling the silence. The Hollow’s silence is not empty; it is a positive pressure the air exerts against the possibility of noise. A tool striking stone would be a theoretical event. Your own heartbeat and swallowing will feel loud at first, then strangely optional. Train yourself to move the way the pale voles and the winter-white hare move: only the next necessary increment, already decided.

The brightness can feel like a sound you cannot hear. Spend time in high-latitude summer or under snow glare with eyes open, getting used to light that does not modulate. The temperature drop as you descend the crease is only a fraction of a degree, but it registers as entering a different climate the larger world forgot to unmake. Practice sitting without fidgeting. There is a split-stone bench under an eave whose moss is already the exact shape of a sitting body; it will accept you without comment.

Digestive and respiratory notes: the air tastes of wet iron and crushed green. Some travelers report a faint metallic film on the tongue after hours. The plants photosynthesize with a slow, bright metabolism selected for this single unchanging temperature; they do not appear toxic, but they occupy every interval and then occupy the buildings themselves. Do not harvest except what you see the inhabitants take, and even then only if you must. Leaves grow back in what the light insists is an hour. The insects exist as a continuous minute glitter walking on films of water that do not tremble. They do not bite in any recorded account; they simply continue.

If you have any tendency toward claustrophobia, time-sickness, or the need for narrative progress, this place will test it. The town is complete. Erosion and construction reached an agreement long ago. You could leave and return after a decade of your own time and find the same hare in the same doorway, the same fern uncurled to the same millimetre, the same film on the same leaf. That is not poetry; it is the operating condition.

**Behavioral and interaction protocol.**  
Descend only after watching from the lip long enough for the brightness to stop feeling like an emergency. The town receives you without curiosity. Match the local palette and economy of motion. The remaining inhabitants—if that word still applies—wear the greys and greens of the walls. Their tools are bone, antler, fire-hardened wood, and copper recast so many times it has forgotten ore. They repair what does not need repair. They do not speak. Speech would be a gradient.

Do not speak. Do not whistle, hum, or clear your throat loudly. If you must communicate with a companion, use the smallest gestures. Do not try to photograph in a way that imposes a frame; the place has already framed itself. Sit. Drink if thirsty. Watch the posed, velvet-antlered deer-scale animals that have always been standing in the square. Their hooves leave no new print that is not immediately filled by moss.

Do not attempt to “fix,” catalog aggressively, or introduce novelty. The vegetation and the masonry have interleaved until neither could be removed without the other collapsing, and neither is collapsing. You are not needed. The well stones are worn into perfect shallow dishes that have been perfect longer than the idea of an imperfect circle has existed here. Leave them that way.

**Psychological and temporal hygiene.**  
The central risk is not injury or hostility. It is seduction by completion. Time here is a held breath. After a while the absence of happening can feel like relief, then like the only honest state. Some travelers report that the moss begins to remember the shape of them even after they stand. Keep one small, private ritual that belongs only to the world you came from—a particular way of tying a knot, a phrase you do not say aloud, a stone in your pocket whose weight you check. It is an anchor against the invitation to stay finished.

Expect nothing to occur. That is the entire event. If you find yourself waiting for the light to change, the animals to startle, or someone to speak, you are still imposing a plot. Let the plot go.

**Exit.**  
When you decide to leave, do not announce it. Retrace the crease. The marker at the lip is your proof that another climate still exists. Once above the rim the ordinary low country will look almost aggressively unfinished—water that might stand, light that travels, the possibility of rain that actually arrives. You may feel briefly too loud, too warm, too sequential. That is normal. Do not immediately fill the silence with talk.

If you return later, assume the hare will be in the same doorway. The Hollow does not keep your appointment; it simply continues by not needing to continue. An ordinary human can visit, sit, drink, and leave provided they arrive already mostly emptied of the need for the place to notice them.

The air will still taste of wet iron and green when you stand up from the bench. That taste is the last thing that belongs to the Hollow. Carry it out if you wish; it fades.
