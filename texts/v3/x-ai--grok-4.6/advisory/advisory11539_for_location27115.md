**Preparation begins with accepting that you are not arriving at a landscape.** You are entering the interior of something that finished digesting mountains long ago and has been metabolizing the idea of “terrain” ever since. An ordinary human body—warm-blooded, air-breathing, gravity-accustomed, visually dominant, and chronically dry by this place’s standards—will be treated as a brief, slightly cooler, slightly saltier weather event. Your job is to remain a visitor rather than a nutrient pulse.

The plateau does not announce itself. The mist is already around you; the ground has already been under you. You will not get a clean “now I am there.” Therefore arrive already wet in your mind.

### Bodily and Physiological Readiness
Condition for weeks of uninterrupted wet heat at skin temperature. The air is a warm, dim broth; you will sweat continuously and never evaporate. Expect your skin to take on a faint green cast, your clothes to become another damp layer of the organism, and your sinuses, lungs, and gut flora to meet an ecosystem that has never met an outsider’s microbes. Train heat-and-humidity tolerance (sauna + heavy clothing work, or tropical fieldwork if available). Practice moving for hours on giving, uneven, three-dimensional surfaces—think dense mangrove roots, wet sponge, and cathedral vaults that are also floors. Your ankles, knees, and proprioception will be taxed by surfaces that yield, weep, and occasionally decide “down” is a local suggestion.

Skin and mucosa will be under constant assault from moisture, slimes, and unknown spores. Begin a prophylactic antifungal and antibacterial skin regimen before arrival; pack more. Barrier creams, zinc, and frequent (but futile) attempts to dry folds of skin matter. Trench foot, intertrigo, and opportunistic infections are near-certainties without vigilance. Respiratory preparation: the air tastes of ozone that never quite sparked, nectar, and wet dust; some chambers have their own private weathers. If you have any reactive airway tendency, treat it as high-risk. Carry inhalers and consider a simple mask or scarf that can be wrung out.

Hydration and electrolytes will invert their usual logic. Water is everywhere—standing in moss dishes, beading from pores, condensing on you the instant you stop. It is the plateau’s sweat, circulation, and glandular output. Assume it is biologically active, mineral-rich, and possibly laced with whatever the organism uses for internal signaling. Filter, boil if you can find a heat source that survives the damp, or use chemical treatment, then test cautiously. You may still absorb more through skin and lungs than you drink. Watch for unexpected mineral loads or bioactive compounds.

Food is a closed loop. Everything here is the plateau eating itself. Foraging is possible in principle (soft grazers, films, perhaps certain bracket growths) but carries the risk of eating something that considers you a temporary lesion. Bring sealed, high-calorie, moisture-proof rations sufficient for far longer than you plan to stay. The place composts time; days blur. A “short visit” can become a season of dimness.

### Gear Philosophy: Sacrifice, Seal, and Redundancy
Electronics hate this place. Humidity will kill them; the faint luminescence and possible local electrical peculiarities (that ozone taste) may interfere. Favor mechanical, analog, and sacrificial items.

- **Clothing**: Multiple complete changes of quick-dry synthetics or wool that you are willing to abandon to the green. A light, full-coverage wetsuit or drysuit layer can buy hours of relative dryness and reduce skin maceration, but you will still overheat. Waterproof bags inside waterproof bags. Nothing leather; it will become another liverwort. Footwear with aggressive, replaceable tread that can grip wet, living, sometimes vertical surfaces—and that you can empty of water frequently. Gaiters are useless; everything is already inside.
- **Light**: The ambient is a perpetual green-gray dusk. Shadows are rare and soft. Headlamps, chemical light sticks, and hand-crank or sealed battery lanterns. Red filters to preserve whatever night vision you can develop and to avoid startling whatever uses the dimness. Some chambers have photosynthetic skins that give a vegetable dusk readable if you wait; do not count on it. Carry far more light than seems reasonable; batteries die, contacts corrode, and you will spend time in rooms that have their own night.
- **Navigation and marking**: Distances lie. You walk toward a brighter smear and discover you have descended; water sometimes runs uphill as a quiet opinion. Compasses may or may not be trustworthy. GPS is fantasy. Dead-reckoning plus obsessive landmarking (but the landmarks grow and the galleries close at the speed of thought in wood). Fluorescent or high-contrast tape, biodegradable flagging if you can find any that survives, and a habit of looking backward often. A simple mechanical altimeter or even a string-and-weight “down” indicator can help when local gravity or flow becomes conversational. Accept that you will get lost. The plateau is larger than weather.
- **Shelter and rest**: The living mass already provides rooms—galleries, cisterns, halls whose ceilings are someone else’s floor. Some hold still black water that is a gland; some are lined with dim-glowing skin. You can use them, but treat every chamber as potentially glandular, digestive, or simply in the process of forgetting it is a room. A hammock or suspended bivy that keeps you off the breathing floor is wiser than a tent, which will become part of the moss within hours. A small, sealed heat source (chemical warmers, perhaps a well-protected stove) for the psychological and physiological need to be briefly less wet. You will never be dry. The goal is “less colonized.”
- **Tools and safety**: A good knife, a folding saw you use sparingly (the organism notices cutting the way a body notices a splinter), cordage that does not rot instantly, and climbing hardware for the vertical-that-is-also-floor and the hanging lenses of water. A basic scientific kit—waterproof notebook, pencils, sample vials, pH strips, a hand lens—because the place will try to make you a tourist of wonders and you will want evidence you were not dreaming. First-aid far beyond the usual: antifungals, broad-spectrum antibiotics, wound-closure, immersion-foot protocol, and something for allergic or toxic reactions to slimes and films. A satellite or multiversal beacon if your traveler’s kit includes one; this is a place that can close behind you.
- **Documentation and sanity anchors**: Waterproof journal. A few photographs or sketches of home, sealed. A watch that still ticks, even if time here is a horizontal stain. The quiet is a mouth full of cloth; sound dies. You will need proof that other durations exist.

### Behavioral and Ecological Etiquette
Move as the water moves: without hurry, by seepage and pressure. Sudden gestures, loud voices, or bright unshielded lights are the equivalent of shouting in a library that is also a lung. The fauna is everywhere and almost invisible—soft grazers working the slime film, things the color of wet bark on undersides, larger slow jointed presences in the hollows whose feet are made of the same spongiform tissue. Predation, when it occurs, is quiet and neighboring. Do not startle; do not pursue. You are a novelty of temperature and salt. Most things will simply work around you or incorporate your discarded skin cells.

The ancient marks—the too-regular stone circle already being sleeved in moss, the glazed cut through plant-rock, the stepped chamber with its body-shaped depression—are not ruins so much as pauses the vegetation has agreed, for a while, not to finish. Approach them as you would a sleeping animal the size of a province. They may be inert. They may be the plateau remembering a fever. They may still be in use by something that no longer needs metal or sharp edges. Do not camp in them. Do not “restore” them.

Do not try to leave a lasting mark. The galleries close. Your path will be composted. If you must cache, use the foreign stones already present; they are already being digested.

### Psychological and Temporal Preparation
This is the hardest part and the one most travelers underestimate. The light never becomes day. Evening is only a further thickening of the broth; hanging water holds what little glow there is and the plateau appears to sweat non-stars. The quiet takes every sound back. You can walk for weeks and remain inside the same organism. Time is not a sequence here; it is a stain spreading in cloth already wet. A new gallery takes a century. You will lose track of how long you have been there. You will begin to feel the green as belonging on you.

Cultivate a practice of small, repeatable rituals that belong to your own duration: a particular way of wringing a cloth, a counted number of breaths before entering a new chamber, a phrase spoken only to yourself. These are not superstition; they are buoyancy. The place is patient and unfinished. It will not hate you. It will simply continue, and you are very small and very temporary and made of the wrong kind of water.

Expect awe that is also claustrophobia, beauty that is also nausea, and a growing sense that the old rock far below is listening through roots that have become its voice. Some people leave changed in ways that look like stain. Some do not leave on their original schedule.

### Contingencies
If you become ill, the nearest “help” is the organism itself. Certain films or waters might have antimicrobial properties—or the opposite. If you fall into a glandular chamber or a sinkhole-mouth, treat it as a medical and existential emergency; the plateau drinks mist back into dark circulation. If directions fully invert or a room develops private weather that does not match the one you entered from, sit down, become as small and still as the grazers, and wait for the local opinion to finish its sentence.

When you decide to leave, do not assume the way back is the way you came. The mist is still being made. The high country has already been going on. Begin the exit the same way you entered: already prepared for the ground to have been around you for an hour before you noticed.

You will carry the dimness and the wetness out with you for a long time. That is not failure. That is the place remembering you the way it remembers mountains: accurately, without nostalgia, as a condition it has finished with. Pack accordingly.
