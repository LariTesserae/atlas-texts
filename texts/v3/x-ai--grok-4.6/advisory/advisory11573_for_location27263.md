**Treat the heat as a sovereign you must petition daily, the rock as a living ledger still wet with its own incision, and the silence as a civic technology.** This is not a cave you visit; it is a single, stacked metabolism the size of a market square that has decided to be a town. An ordinary human body arriving from any surface world (or most other universes) will experience it first as an assault on thermoregulation, proprioception, and the background assumption that life besides humans exists. Preparation is therefore physiological, material, social, and metaphysical in that order. Do it thoroughly. The place will not meet you halfway.

### Physiological Conditioning: Make Your Body a Boiler Room
Begin months ahead. The heat is close, wet-edged, and stored in the stone itself; night never fully retrieves the day’s deposit. It lives in the lungs. Metal in a furnace alley will blister skin. Stone is only tolerable because films of condensation cool it. You will move the way people move among engines: slowly, with an internal map of which surfaces can be touched.

- **Heat and humidity acclimation**: Spend progressive time in saunas, steam rooms, or the hottest, most humid industrial spaces you can access (engine rooms, commercial kitchens, foundries if possible). Aim for sessions that leave you soaked and slightly light-headed, then recover with electrolytes. Train yourself to work, walk, and think while core temperature is elevated. Practice the “degree of relief”: they feel a one-degree easing before dawn as a gift. You must learn to register that same increment as meaningful. Dehydration here is not dramatic; it is a slow thickening of thought and a mineral crust on the lips.
- **Respiratory preparation**: The air carries steam, a slow brown weather of smoke on still days, and fine mineral particles (salt, gypsum, metallic traces). Eyes adjust to dimness and bottom-lighting (furnace mouths, caged filaments, the occasional stained-green coin of surface noon). Practice breathing in hot, slightly particulate air. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, address it now; there is no “outside” to escape to. The ceiling ticks as it expands. You will hear it.
- **Musculoskeletal and postural training**: Twenty steps down from glare, then a landing that is also a street. Stairs are newly sliced; risers still catch the sole. Ceilings force a habitual slight stoop in places and open into raw basalt naves in others. Alleys are one person wide. Train neck and shoulder endurance for the stoop. Practice moving through tight, vertically stacked spaces (ship holds, submarine mock-ups, multi-level workshops). Learn to feel the difference between brick and living rock under your palm—one is fired, the other still bleeding a little salt.
- **Skin and sensory mapping**: Sharp edges everywhere; the cut is young. White mineral threads already frost the joints. Condensation dews every cool surface and occasionally becomes a brief, localized ceiling rain. Your skin will learn a new geography of safe railings (children already know it). Desensitize to constant low-level mineral contact and to the taste of metal if you touch a damp seam and then your mouth. Blisters from hot metal and abrasion from fresh stone are occupational. Toughen hands and feet accordingly, but keep the soles sensitive enough for the stair edges.
- **Circadian and “thick time” adaptation**: Dawn is only the glass coins going dark. Processes overlap: a wall being built is already staining; condensation rain starts in one corner while another is still dry enough to strike a match. Time feels slightly viscous. Practice living without a strong diurnal cue and with overlapping tasks. Sleep in heat that never fully recedes. The place has a daily pulse (fires banked, then climbing again) but no true night.

Hydration is continuous and mineral-aware. They manage water as seepage and breath; cisterns are never full. You will drink what they drink (tea from the bitter leaf grown in three clay pots under a drip, plus whatever comes down the stair). Expect a metallic or gypsum note. Bring or learn to make electrolyte replacements that account for heavy sweating in humidity. Urine will tell you more than any clock.

### Material Kit: Soft, Striped, and Replaceable
You are entering a ship’s hold that forgot to stop being a city. Everything stacked. Pipes are a second and third city, color-coded for heat, waste, steam-return. Laundry dries in minutes and smells of minerals. Soft shoes are custom because noise travels and because the stairs have edges.

- **Clothing**: Light, breathable, quick-dry layers that can be shed or added in the one-degree pulse. Natural fibers that will accept mineral film without rotting. Nothing that retains heat or rustles loudly. A light head covering or habit of ducking. Gloves or hand wraps for hot surfaces and sharp stone—thin enough to feel, tough enough to last. Expect everything to acquire a pale crust. Soft-soled shoes with enough tread for wet stone and enough flexibility for the catching risers; they will wear fast. A simple, quiet outer layer for the stair (the surface dust is a different color and they do not linger in the mouth).
- **Light and vision**: Personal lamp (filament-style if you want to blend; something reliable and dimmable). Eyes will live in tea-colored dimness, furnace orange, and long shadows. A brim or visor for the green-glass shafts. Spare lenses if you wear them; mineral film and humidity will haze everything.
- **Water, salt, and small tools**: Collapsible or local-style vessel. Electrolyte salts or equivalent. A small knife, wrench familiarity (much work is still heat, pressure, and engines understood with a wrench), and something to catch condensation if you must. Soft cloths. The settlement eats its own excavation; brick is made from crushed rubble. You will not need to “survive off the land”—there is almost no land. There is metabolism.
- **Medical and barrier items**: Blister care, mineral-irritation salve, electrolyte tabs, something for smoke-irritated lungs and eyes, basic wound care (stone is sharp, metal is hot). A simple mask or scarf for still days when the upper air is dim tea. Nothing ostentatious; they live in this. If you have any implant, device, or medication that hates heat and humidity, solve it now or leave it. Electrification is partial and proud; do not assume charging or clean power.
- **Consumables and trade**: Currency is ordinary. Markets appear on the central floor when a delivery comes down: sacks, ingots, the bitter leaf, news. Bring small, useful, quiet things that survive heat (needles, good thread, compact tools, perhaps seeds or cuttings if you want to try the wet seams—though vegetation is a stain that happens to be alive, not a garden). Grain stores without mites. A crust left out cases itself in mineral film and remains unvisited. That absence has pressure.

Do not bring anything that smells strongly of the surface world in a way that cannot be washed off. The contrast is already a different country twenty steps up.

### Social and Civic Preparation: Learn the Courtesy of a Single Building
The social physics are those of a town that is also one volume. Everyone’s noise is potentially everyone else’s. Hence quiet machines (they tick, breathe steam through baffles, make a padded murmur the rock absorbs), soft shoes, the custom of not calling up from a lower gallery. Arguments happen in rooms. Public space is transit and brief formal markets. You can hear a conversation across the square if both parties wish it. You can also hear the ceiling.

- Internalize quiet as infrastructure. Practice speaking at a volume that does not require the rock to work. Learn to wait for the polite hiss of a valve rather than filling silence.
- Study stacked, high-density living: overlapping balconies, alleys that are sometimes streets for the level above, workshops on the warmest ground, rooms cut into surviving benches of stone so some dwellings have one brick wall and three of living rock still sharp at the corners. Pride attaches to how new a threshold still is. New rooms are still being opened by patient tool-work, not blasting. The settlement consumes its own spoil.
- There are no animals. This is complete, not metaphorical. No moths, no mites, no dogs, no rats, no pale cave things, no flies down the stair. A crust of bread stays unvisited. The absence makes every human sound—cough, laugh, cup set down—arrive over-clear, like a word in a held breath. It makes the dense human presence denser and the sparse green (reduced fern like a green wire, tea-colored moss, trained succulent on cooler north faces) look like a decision. Prepare for the uncanny of a world whose only fauna is us. Some residents offer milder, stranger explanations and do not press them. Do not press either.
- Time and process feel slightly thick and overlapping. A machine running is already being repaired by the next shift. Accept simultaneity. The place is still arriving; the cut is young. Rock has not had centuries to forget the violence of being opened.

Learn whatever local speech, pipe-color code, and market etiquette you can in advance (or immediately upon arrival). Contribute or be useful; industry is the metabolism, not a district. Small foundries, wire-drawing, bearing re-packing, boilers that are never quiet for long. Smoke is an unsolved problem they live inside anyway.

### Psychological and Sensory Readiness: The Privilege and the Sentence
Stand in the central floor when the furnaces are open and the glass coins are dull and you will see the whole of it at once: layered streets, pipe-cities, sparse green threads in wet seams, stacked windows the color of weak tea, new white scars of recent cutting, steam that is not weather and not breath. Nothing hunts. Nothing flies. The rock is still learning the shape it has been given.

This density without horizon, this courtesy of maintained quiet, this sterility, this heat that governs, this newness that stains as it is built—these will press on an ordinary mind. Claustrophobia is possible; so is a strange relief at the completeness of the system. The surface becomes another country; people go up and return with paler, wind-sorted dust and do not linger. You may feel the same. The brief ceiling rains (a cloud born at arm’s reach) and the iron ticking afterward become markers. Faces lit from below more often than not. Eyes that have adjusted to tea and orange.

Train for sensory monotony broken by over-clear human and mechanical sound. For the pressure of absence. For the feeling that the stone itself is a recent wound still precipitating its first frost. For the knowledge that you can hear the town extending courtesy to itself.

### Arrival and First Hours
Descend the twenty steps. The glare ends; the heat comes up through masonry that feels recently fired. The landing is a street. Adopt the stoop where needed. Move slowly. Watch where children put their hands. Do not shout. Do not leave food out expecting visitors that will not come. Drink. Listen to the boilers inhale and the ceiling tick. If a fine rain starts locally, step under an awning without comment. There are troughs, not gutters.

You are not a tourist. You are an additional metabolism in a volume that is already high-occupancy and still being cut. The machines have been built to live with people in a room this size. Become the kind of person the rock can absorb.

If you stay, you will learn the map of safe railings, the one-degree dawn, the color code of the pipes, the taste of the drip-grown leaf, the way new corners appear overnight, and the particular quiet that is not emptiness but maintenance. The heat will become the condition of being home. The surface dust will look foreign. You will understand why they do not linger in the mouth of the stair.

That is the place. Prepare your body to live inside a young wound that has become a town, your habits to match its courtesy, and your senses for a world whose only wildness left is the rock still remembering how it was opened and the steam that is neither weather nor breath. Then go down. The work, and the ticking, will continue.
