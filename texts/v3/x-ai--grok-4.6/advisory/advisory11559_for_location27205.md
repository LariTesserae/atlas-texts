**Treat the basin as a finished climate you are briefly permitted to occupy, not as a destination that will arrange itself around you.** An ordinary human body arriving here is extra moisture, extra heat, extra decision. The place has already chosen its rates: condensation, drip, slow slide, the long swell of the unweather pressure, the quiet that is only a different fullness. Your job is to last long enough to notice the rates without becoming one of the versions the peat keeps.

Pack as if you will never be dry again and will never be warm in the way you remember warmth. The air is already water’s unfinished thought; it will finish itself on you the moment you stand still. Skin condenses. Clothes that trap vapor become a second weeping stone. Cotton is a mistake that will stay a mistake for the entire visit. Use synthetics or treated wool that shed and breathe; expect both properties to fail locally when a hanging curtain or a double-pool decides to occupy the same volume as your sleeve. A long hooded shell that can be worn closed or vented, with pit zips you can work while wearing gloves, is more useful than a heavy parka. Layer thin: base that wicks, mid that insulates even when damp, outer that you can rinse of peat and wring. Gaiters that seal over the boot tops. Boots that drain and do not become buckets; the second ground will find every seam. Bring two pairs and rotate them even if both are wet—the ritual of changing is one of the few things you still control. A compact umbrella or staff-mounted hood is not vanity; during a condensation front the dripping becomes a rain that cannot decide whether it is falling or assembling, and having a small dry volume over your face lets you keep looking.

The ground is a negotiation. Hummocks, black peat that considers your print, sheets and threads and lenses of water that answer when you step. You will sink. You will hear the answer. Travel light enough that you can extract a foot without panic. A collapsible pole or staff is better than two trekking poles; you need one hand free for balance when a surface turns out to be two surfaces slightly out of register. Test every dark stretch. The pale stone shoulders and fluted outcrops are usually more honest than the meadows of almost-black sedge. When the moss leans (it will, during the swell, remembering a direction that is not on any compass you brought), do not treat the lean as wind. There is almost no wind. The lean is older.

Navigation will lie with the patience of the rest of the place. Distances coexist: a copse that is an hour off is also immediately to your left, its wet-bark smell arriving without travel. Compass needles may wander or simply become another dripping object. Do not trust a single bearing. Use redundant, analog methods: paced counts you recalibrate every time the second ground answers louder, sun (what little pewter light filters through) plus the memory of the last condensation front’s direction, a simple inertial log if you have one that does not mind humidity. Mark your own route with biodegradable, high-contrast tape or flagging that you will retrieve; the basin does not need more cairns and will not honor the ones you think you see. Game trails, when they appear, dogleg for reasons that have nothing to do with contour. Follow them only if you are willing to accept their reasons. The safest habit is to keep a prominent weeping outcrop or a distinctive hanging curtain in sight and treat everything else as provisional. Accept that you will get turned around. The place has been in-balance with this for longer than the ridgelines remember; your confusion is not a failure, it is local weather.

Water is everywhere and not entirely the water you know. Some of it runs uphill for a few body-lengths along shining tracks it has worn in the stone. Some hangs in curtains that do not fall. Some occupies the same volume twice. Do not drink from the double-pools or the hanging curtains until you have watched them through a full swell and lull; they are the most visibly “still thinking.” Collect from the ordinary drips off ferns or from the small cisterns that have dimpled under the longest weeps—those have had geologic time to become simple again. Filter and purify anyway. The anomalies have eroded into stable forms, but your gut has not been consulting with this hydrology. Carry more capacity than you think you need; the fronts will make everything wetter but will not necessarily make it more drinkable. A metal cup you can boil in is worth the weight. Taste nothing that looks gelatinous.

Food is entirely your problem. There are no ordinary densities of insects or birds. The rare pale displacements in the deeper channels and the brief clusterings in the meres are not forage. Bring dense, damp-tolerant calories: nut butters, hard cheeses if you can keep them from sweating into ruin, vacuum-sealed fats, electrolyte tablets, something warm you can prepare over a small stove that will light in saturated air (test it at home in a steamy bathroom). Eat on a schedule even when the pewter light makes time feel optional. The low continuous pressure-sound cycles on a period longer than weather and shorter than seasons; hunger will try to sync to it and you will lose track.

Shelter is a temporary disagreement with the climate. The trees are short, roots extravagant, not generous with height. A free-standing tent with a solid bathtub floor and excellent ventilation (you will still live inside a cloud) can be pitched on the firmer hummocks if you probe first. A hammock is tempting but the available trunks are low and the ground beneath is never only ground. An inflatable sleeping pad rated for wet cold is mandatory; the peat will leach heat even when you are not touching it. A bivy that can be sealed against dripping and opened to the interleaving mists is a reasonable compromise. Site yourself where you can see a long way—the openness is real—and where the second ground is quieter. Do not camp in the braided shores of a double-pool. Do not camp where the moss already leans. During the swell the standing water takes on nested rings that do not come from any stone you threw; give those geometries room. The air will occupy your shelter the way it occupies everything else. You will wake with condensation on the inside of your eyelids if you sleep too deeply.

The sound is structural and will not stop for you. First the water in all its registers: beads releasing, sheets unzipping off hanging moss, the underground slides that sound like regional plumbing gossiping, the applause-through-a-wall of a condensation front. Under and through it, the other noise: low, many-throated, a pressure that rises and falls on its own cycle. It is not wind. It deepens the dimness by a fraction, makes the moss remember, puts interference on the meres. You will feel it in your chest and the small bones of your ears. Earplugs can take the edge off the higher drips but will not remove the pressure; some travelers find that leaving one ear open keeps them oriented to the swell. The silence between events is only the basin resuming the version of fullness it can sustain. You will hear individual beads, a single fern letting go. That is not peace. It is the smaller wetnesses continuing.

Fauna is infrequent and does not correct the loneliness. A pale back moving in a deeper channel, too smooth, too extensive—an event of displacement more than an animal you can picture. Gelatinous thoughts the water has and then recants. At the loudest swell, a lifting from the peat that uses the extra room the thinness provides and then is not using it. Tracks last longer than the creatures. Give the channels width. Do not follow a lifting shape into the thinness; ordinary humans do not have a reliable way to occupy the same volume twice and come back aligned. The basin is not newly broken. Nothing here is hunting you in the ordinary sense. You are simply another condensation that has not yet been counted as landscape.

Health is a long, cool, wet attrition. Hypothermia is the obvious thief; wet and not-quite-cold will steal heat for hours while you tell yourself you are fine. Watch for the moment standing still makes you part of the weeping. Trench foot, fungal bloom, skin that forgets it is supposed to be a barrier. Change socks on a ritual schedule. A small dry-bag of extra base layers and a chemical or battery warmer you use only when you can no longer feel your own decisions. Respiratory irritation from the standing vapor is possible; a simple mask or buff you can wet and breathe through sometimes helps, sometimes does not. The thinness itself can produce a low-grade spatial vertigo—two facts about distance refusing to cancel, a pool that is briefly a thought and the thought’s correction. If you find yourself staring too long, look at your own hands, at the next drip, at something that has only one surface. The effect is worn and polished, not a tear, but your nervous system did not evolve for cheaper ways to be continuous.

Bring a watch that does not care about humidity, a headlamp you may never need for seeing but will want for the comfort of a small, ordinary spectrum, a notebook that can take wet and a pencil that can write on damp paper. Record the cycle of the unweather: when the rings appear, when the moss leans, when the hanging curtains thin to threads again. The basin does not empty. It only resumes. If you stay through one full swell and the return to used pewter, you will have seen the place do the thing it has been practicing. That is enough. More than enough.

Leave when the light is its ordinary dim and the second ground is only a rumor. Retrace as honestly as the distances allow. The peat will keep a version of you whether you want it to or not. Do not try to out-wait the climate or to become native to it. You are a traveler. The rocks have been weeping since before the ridgelines had opinions. Your visit is one more film of water, briefly decided, then released. Pack out everything you can. The absences here already have weight.
