**Get oriented first, then commit to the water with industrial respect, thermal and pressure protection, navigation redundancy, and a plan that treats the basin as both habitat and live machinery.** This is not a natural ocean, a quarry lake, or a theme-park dive site. It is a recently completed, still-curing, actively circulating engineered volume the size of a small sea: poured formwork, live cables, intakes that do not distinguish, pumps that never stop, and life that is only beginning to occupy the seams. An ordinary human body lasts minutes unprotected and hours even well-equipped if you treat it like a recreational reef. Treat it like a working cooling reservoir crossed with an unfinished cathedral and you can stay long enough to understand the scale.

### Understand what you are entering
The water is the room. There is no “shore” in the ordinary sense; the surface is a silver, rain-dented lid far above, and the floor is a continent of hexagonal caissons, straight grout lines, ranked bolt-heads, and expansion joints that still look like they left the yard last season. Light arrives in long, pale-blue columns. Visibility is often excellent—tens to hundreds of meters—until a storm or a sluice change lifts the unset silt. Temperature is uniformly cool enough to steal heat steadily; the mix is mineral, faintly alkaline from ongoing lime leach, and chemically “just treated enough.” Depth varies but the photic zone, kelp racks, and open tower windows sit well above a floor that requires a real descent. Pressure, nitrogen, and decompression are real constraints once you leave the upper water. Currents are usually the managed circulation (artificial tide) plus storm-driven lean and intake/outfall plumes. Sound is structural: close-in ticking and groaning of settling concrete and pipe, a constant industrial chord from pumps and turbines, hourly sluice shifts, and the occasional distant hull or lock blast. Life is sparse, linear, and opportunistic—tank-bred silvers, a few immigrant cod-like fish, eels in the drowned stairwells, amphipods, thumbnail crabs, first mussels on welds, rectangular kelp plots, and at least one large, unnamed thing that travels the warm-effluent joints. Nothing has had time to soften the edges or bury the graffiti in the silt.

You are a guest in a system whose primary job is circulation and heat rejection. The operators run it to specification. The place is neither wild nor tame; it is running.

### Physical and medical preparation
- **Fitness and screening**: Good swimming endurance and comfort in three-dimensional space help, but you will not casually swim “half a day.” Get a dive medical (or equivalent occupational/hyperbaric screening) that includes ears, sinuses, lungs, and cold-water tolerance. If you plan anything below recreational no-deco depths, treat it as technical or use a one-atmosphere platform. Practice equalization, buoyancy in a bulky suit, and hovering in current. Acclimate to cold if you can; the water will not warm you.
- **Duration realism**: A day trip from a surface support boat or lock is feasible in the photic zone. Multi-day travel across the basin requires staged support, a habitat, or a vehicle with hotel load. Ordinary human metabolism plus cold plus workload plus any deco obligation means you plan gas, heat, calories, and bailout, not vibes.
- **Hygiene and chemistry**: The alkali and mineral load are mild but not tap water. Rinse gear. Protect eyes and any open skin. Do not drink it. The first biofilms and the lime can foul seals and zippers faster than a tropical reef.

### Core equipment (layered, redundant, industrial-grade)
Assume you may start from a boat, a lock, a pump-house spine, a condenser island, or a diving bell. Bring or arrange:
- **Life support and thermal**: A well-fitted drysuit or heated undersuit system rated for hours in cool water, with backup heat if you have it. Full-face mask or helmet with communications. Closed-circuit rebreather or high-capacity open-circuit with generous reserves and a written gas plan; bailout bottles. If depths warrant, mixed gas and a deco plan (or skip free swimming entirely). A small one-atmosphere suit or a personal/two-person submersible is the sanest way for an ordinary traveler to see the floor, the joints, and the far structures without turning the visit into a saturation operation. Diving bells are already in use by the kelp farmers; hitch or charter if possible.
- **Propulsion and navigation**: Diver propulsion vehicle(s) with spare batteries. The volume is empty enough that swimming is inefficient and disorienting. Redundant navigation: acoustic beacon or transponder compatible with the basin’s channels if they exist, inertial/dead-reckoning backup, depth, compass, and a pre-loaded chart of known works, intakes, cable runs, kelp plots, and lock schedules. Surface GPS is useless once you drop. Marked navigation chains (yellow) and condenser coronas exist; treat unmarked water as open desert.
- **Light, imaging, and recording**: Powerful, wide-beam lights plus a spotting beam. The columns of sunlight are beautiful and insufficient once you leave them or night cycle begins. Industrial lights stay on, but the true dark between channels is real. Cameras, notes, or a slate: the bolt ranks, the still-sharp contractor stamps, the names in silt, and the rectangular kelp will not look like this forever.
- **Safety and signaling**: DSMB or equivalent is less useful in this scale; use acoustic locators, strobes, and a plan to reach a known structure or the surface support. Cutting tool, backup mask, and a way to signal operators. Personal locator that the pump-house or traffic control can hear. First-aid suited to barotrauma, hypothermia, and entanglement. Do not carry anything that could be sucked into an intake or short a cable.
- **Surface and entry support**: A boat or lock transit with people who know the published lock schedule, the three-language notices, and the light codes. Weather briefing (fetch is hundreds of kilometers; a front walks the lid overnight). Knowledge of where the great western intakes sit and when they “open another throat.”

If you can only bring what an ordinary traveler can reasonably obtain or carry between worlds, prioritize thermal protection + reliable breathing + propulsion + a way to know where the intakes and live cables are. Everything else is secondary.

### Operational and hazard discipline
The written rules are simple because the machinery is not: level is maintained, temperature is a spec, locks run on schedule. Do not touch live cables. Do not enter an intake. Those two will kill you faster than cold or depth. Add:
- Give condenser islands, pump-house spines, sluices, and heat-exchange faces wide berth on the suction and discharge sides. The shimmer is fish habitat and a thermal cue; it is also moving water and metal.
- Storms arrive first as smeared light and a change in the standing note of the turbines, then as lean, lifted silt, and stronger current. Abort to a structure or the surface rather than “ride it out” in open water.
- Towers are chimneys of colder water with eels and silt landings. Windows are open. They do not reach air. Treat stairwells as overhead environments with silt-out risk.
- Kelp racks are agriculture. Lights of the farmers’ bells are the only warm color. Do not cut, entangle, or “sample” without knowing the contract.
- The large unnamed passer is “normal life.” Observe from a respectful distance; do not chase along the warm joints.
- Traffic: surface hulls appear as towed constellations. Locks accept barges. Stay out of channels unless you are expected.
- Leave the silt graffiti and the unused crane. Nothing has buried them yet; you do not need to be the first disturbance.

File a plan (or equivalent) with whoever runs the nearest pump-house or lock if you can. They already live with the fetch, the circulation, and the cooling contract. A traveler who appears unannounced in an intake plume or a cable run is a problem, not a guest.

### How the visit actually feels (so you prepare the mind as well as the kit)
You enter a volume, not a scene. The cold is immediate and then becomes the only temperature. Sound has architecture: the near ticks and groans of a filled thing still equalizing, the stacked fifty-cycle hum and impeller wander that never sleeps, the far weather and the occasional coded blast. Light is poured. The floor, when it finally resolves, is geometry that has not learned to be geology—straight seams, sharp arrises that could still cut, bolt-heads receding into texture, then into a horizon of more work. You will hang over a pour-joint and see your own small shape and understand the size as patience rather than a number.

It is lonely in a specific way: not the loneliness of a wilderness that predates you, but the loneliness of a project that outscaled its finish date and then kept the pumps on. Life is present the way lichen is present on a new wall. The eels found the towers by whatever route eels use. The mussels chose the welds. The kelp is in rectangles because someone surveyed rectangles. Between the plots and the islands there is room, always room, and the knowledge that a front can walk the entire lid in a night.

You will be tempted to stay too long looking at the unrounded edges and the names that have not yet been silted over. Budget your gas, your heat, and your deco so that the exit is as deliberate as the entry. Surface support or a known structure should be a decision, not a hope.

When you leave, rinse everything. The lime and the new biofilms will try to come with you. The sound of the pumps will stay in your head longer than the cold.

That is the preparation: treat the basin as a live industrial ocean that happens to be beautiful, still sharp, and only beginning to forget it was poured. Arrive with the right thermal and breathing system, know where the throats and the cables are, navigate like you are crossing a desert that has a heartbeat, and give the place the same serious attention the operators do. Then you can hang in the columns, watch the silver lid, and feel the size.
