**Prepare as if you are entering a living lung that has forgotten it is not stone.** This is not a cave you visit; it is a closed metabolic engine that has been running on hydrogen seep, recycled hemolymph, and the slow polish of occupancy for longer than the plateau above has held its present name. An ordinary human body is a wet, warm, loudly chemical anomaly. The place will not hunt you with intention, but it will treat your heat, your moisture, and your stillness as weather to be accommodated—until accommodation becomes enclosure.

### Core Environmental Realities You Must Internalize
The plateau is a wind-scoured evaporite-and-basalt table that sheds electrostatic grit and radiates heat through the soles. The chimney is a polished, body-worn wound no wider than a chapel aisle; light dies in stages until only infrared remains. The chamber itself is a single irregular vault whose every surface is a continuous, never-still pavement of flattened, heat-sintered organisms. There is almost no free water. Moisture is interior, locked in tissues and rasped as a vanishing film. The air has the density of a held breath, tastes of salt and burnt keratin, and carries a chemistry of alarm, invitation, and equilibrium that you will register as pressure behind the eyes, sudden facial heat, or a change in the ticking. Sound is scarce: a fine chitin-on-chitin tick felt more in the teeth than heard, occasional dry cracks of molt or predation, then the tick resuming as vacancies fill from all sides. Vents pulse irregularly; the entire living floor shears and drifts a meter or two toward new heat, predators repositioning as if the hunt were meteorology. The ecology is all consumer. It has no outside.

You will walk on them. They will not flee. They accommodate by the smallest possible margin. When you stop, they close.

### Physical Gear and Physiological Protection
An ordinary human cannot survive this unprotected for more than minutes. The heat is not desert noon; it is closer, more personal, rising from wrist-wide vents and radiating from every surface and from the air itself. Your own body heat and evaporative cooling will be immediately noticeable to heat-pit sensors and to the chemistry of the room.

- **Full-body thermal and chemical isolation suit**, not a standard desert or cave suit. It must be highly reflective or actively cooled (phase-change packs, circulating fluid, or a compact refrigeration loop if your traveler’s tech allows), with excellent insulation against conductive heat from the living floor and radiative heat from walls and vault. The outer surface should be as chemically inert and low-emissivity as possible—smooth, non-porous, easy to wipe of any condensing film. Exposed skin is a liability: moisture loss, chemical absorption, and a heat signature that marks you as an anomaly. Gloves must allow climbing the chimney’s glaze without slipping; soles must distribute weight and be heat-resistant enough that you do not cook your feet while also not crushing the living pavement more than necessary. Consider a slight thermal-masking layer or heat-sink so your outline does not register as a moving hot pocket.
- **Closed or heavily filtered breathing system**. The air is dense, sulfur-tinged, hydrogen-seeping, and laden with whatever volatile organics the organisms use instead of calling. A rebreather or independent air supply is safest; at minimum a high-quality chemical filter plus supplemental oxygen. Do not rely on ambient air for long. The “held breath” density and pressure changes can produce headache, disorientation, or a sense of being watched by something that has no eyes.
- **Hydration and waste management**. Carry more water than seems reasonable, in a system that does not leak vapor. Recycle if you can. Urine and sweat are valuable moisture in this place; do not donate them. A catheter or collection system prevents accidental release that the lowest tier would immediately rasp. Electrolyte replacement is mandatory—the taste of salt in the air is a warning.
- **Vision and navigation**. Human eyes become useless once the sky-slot is gone. Bring thermal imaging that can resolve the dull-coal heat-pits, the vent pulses, the slow phosphor stains, and the large mass in the vault (one animal or fused colony) whose slower pulse slightly raises the chamber’s temperature. Infrared goggles or a helmet display are essential. Visible light is likely disruptive or meaningless to them; use it sparingly if at all, and only in wavelengths that do not trigger whatever chemistry they use. A helmet lamp with a deep-red or IR option is a last resort. Map the chimney turns in advance if possible; once inside, the only reliable landmarks are the living topography of use—ramps that exist because they have been climbed, the central standing blade of rock the traffic parts around, the cooler vent with its ring of fitted stones now packed with nest-material.
- **Climbing and movement aids**. The chimney is polished by generations of bodies into a continuous glaze. Bring gloves with high-friction pads that will not shed fibers, a lightweight harness or webbing if the drop is long, and perhaps friction-enhancing paste that is biologically inert. Inside the chamber, move slowly and continuously. Sudden stops invite closure. Weight distribution matters: they treat you as another fluctuation, but crushing a column or blocking a vent path will produce a chemical and thermal response you will feel as pressure or heat against your face.
- **Minimal additional kit**. A compact first-aid set focused on heat injury, chemical burns, and crush or puncture (hooks wait in seams). A thermal blanket or emergency bivy that can serve as a temporary heat shield or, if you must rest, a barrier they cannot immediately overgrow. Recording devices if you wish (they will not care). No open flames, no strong scents, no food that can spill. Do not bring anything that sheds, flakes, or outgasses.

Acclimate on the plateau first if time allows: the dry heat, the grit, the white-coin sun. Descend only when your suit is sealed, your air is flowing, and you have accepted that the chamber does not need you and will not keep a space.

### Behavioral and Sensory Protocol
You are weather. Move as weather moves—slow, total, leaving no lasting gap.

Descend the chimney in stages. Let your eyes adjust; they will not adjust enough. Feel the infrared warmth increasing. Once in the vault, do not freeze. A standing human is a heat anomaly that the floor will treat as a new equilibrium to be filled. Walk with deliberate, even steps, letting the living pavement shift around your feet the way a packed market seen from height rearranges. The motion under you is constant, market-like or blood-under-skin. Predators (longer, paler, legs that never quite touch the basal layer) will reposition with the same drift that follows a vent cycle. Do not startle; you cannot startle them in a way they understand. They do not look up. Their mouths are on their undersides. Their attention is heat and chemistry.

Watch the vents. When one opens, the entire floor performs a coordinated shear and drift. Time your path to the new equilibrium if you can. Avoid the large mass in the vault’s highest point when it pulses; the temperature tick that follows is the room exhaling. The hooked things in seams take whatever brushes them; keep your silhouette compact. The grazers crop a black-iridescent chemosynthetic film; do not linger on any patch long enough to be treated as a new food surface.

If you must stop—to observe the fitted-stone ring, the overgrown iron spike, the man-high niche whose emptiness is still a rumor of intention—keep a small, continuous motion or place a thermal barrier (your emergency sheet, a spare pack) so they do not simply close over your boots. The vacancy fills from all sides at once. The ticking will begin to sound like a grammar you almost recognize: the syntax of a closed system sufficient unto itself.

Do not attempt to communicate. They have no calls you can hear. Whatever they use is already in the air; you will feel it as heat or pressure, not language. Do not leave anything. Do not take anything that is still living or recently living. The old human (or not-human) marks are few and long overgrown; they are not invitations.

### Psychological and Existential Preparation
This place has the feel of a shape found long ago and practiced ever since. The erosion is occupancy. The stone’s original fractures are gone, replaced by a topography of use. You will feel, after a time, that you are inside something that is not hostile because hostility requires an outside, and this chamber has none.

Expect sensory inversion: darkness that is not empty (constellations of heat-pits, faint phosphor afterimages), sound that is felt more than heard, air that has weight and taste and intention. Expect the quiet to become complete enough that your own pulse is loud against the tick. Expect the heat to stay on your skin long after you climb out, a map of a place that does not keep space for you.

An ordinary human will feel small, then irrelevant, then briefly part of the count. The temptation is to stay still and listen for the language in the ticking. Do not. The system will simply incorporate the stillness. There is no malice in this; there is only the long practice of sufficiency.

Climb out the way you came. The slot of sky will be the color of a bruise. Nothing follows. The plateau’s wind and white sun will feel like an afterthought. Shower the grit and the memory of keratin from your suit. Rehydrate. Sit with the fact that the chamber is still down there, still drifting, still filling every vacancy, still pulsing on its own clock, and that your visit registered as nothing more than a brief fluctuation in the heat.

That is the preparation: become as little weather as possible, survive the conditions that make an ordinary body an intrusion, move as if you already belong to the closed grammar, and leave before the grammar decides you do. The place will not miss you. It does not need to.
