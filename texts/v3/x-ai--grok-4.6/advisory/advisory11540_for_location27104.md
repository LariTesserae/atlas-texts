**Physical and medical reality first.** This is instant high-altitude exposure on a tectonically active, unfinished plateau whose “mountains” are still being shoved upward. Ordinary human physiology does not like sudden thin air plus penetrating cold plus damp plus possible low-level seismic and geothermal inputs. Expect altitude illness (headache, nausea, poor sleep, impaired judgment) within hours if you arrive unacclimatized. The pressure in the molars is not poetic—it is barometric. Breath hangs because the air is cold and saturated; every exhalation is visible work. Cold conducts upward through anything you kneel or sit on. Wet rock, forming/unforming ice skins, and brittle new mineral terraces make every step a slip-and-cut risk. There is no wood, no shelter, no forage you can trust, and no one coming.

Train hard beforehand: cardiovascular base, weighted hiking on steep unstable ground, cold exposure, and actual altitude if you can get it (or a hypoxic tent). See a travel/altitude-medicine clinician. Carry acetazolamide (start before arrival), dexamethasone and nifedipine for emergencies, plus a pulse oximeter you actually use. Portable oxygen (cylinder or concentrator with plenty of power) is not optional for an ordinary human arriving suddenly; the land has not finished making a breathable mid-elevation. Hydrate aggressively but treat every drop. The standing water is mineral-rich, possibly geothermal-influenced, iron-tinged, and biologically active in ways you cannot see until you kneel (which you will regret). Filter, chemically treat, and consider a small still or UV if power allows. Do not assume ice is cleaner.

**Clothing and insulation as life-support.** The cold is the only thing that currently feels permanent. Layer like a polar/high-alpine trip that never ends: merino or synthetic base, multiple insulating mid-layers (synthetic preferred—it stays wet), a bombproof waterproof/breathable shell that can handle condensing fog and hissing wind, and a heavy insulated parka and pants for stillness. Extremities first: expedition mitts plus liner gloves, balaclava, neck gaiter, goggles (the light is bruise-dim but the wind and ice particles are not). Boots must be insulated, stiff, and aggressively soled for wet sharp rock and ice; carry overboots or gaiters, multiple sock systems, and chemical or battery toe warmers. A full-coverage rain/wind suit over everything because the stone sweats and the air condenses on you. The ground will steal heat through any pad that is too thin. You will be damp. Plan for it.

**Shelter, sleep, and camp.** Nothing here has ever been built. The surface is still deciding its drainage and its fractures. A four-season tent (or a robust pyramid/midshelter) with extra guys, a heavy footprint, and a way to stake or weight it on gravel/rock is mandatory. Site selection is everything: stay well clear of steam-vent ranks (new throats open), avoid the lowest wet benches (ice, seeps, possible gas pooling), and do not camp on or immediately below fresh mineral terraces—they grow and they are brittle. Insulated sleeping pad(s) plus a bag rated far colder than you think you need. A bivy as backup. Cooking must be wind-stable and fuel-efficient; there is no biomass. Bring more fuel than the plan requires. The “industrial” feeling is the land’s own foundry; do not add yours next to a vent.

**Movement, navigation, and the repeating emptiness.** You can see a long way and it does not help. Features—wet benches, vent lines, distant herds—repeat at intervals that feel designed. Cloud shreds and re-forms; light stays bruise-colored; there are no hard shadows and no reliable solar time. A compass, altimeter, and any mapping or waypoint system you can carry are essential, plus the discipline to use them constantly. Mark your own track if the surface allows (it may not last). The land is mid-process: new joints open, terraces lengthen by the day, ice appears and vanishes in an hour. Assume the route you walked this morning has changed. Move slowly. Test every step. The goat-like animals treat uplift as weather; you should treat the same forces as a reason not to stand under anything that looks newly fractured. The birds click and ride thermals off still-warm rock; they are numerous relative to the forage, which tells you the system is not yet in equilibrium. Give everything space.

**Energy, food, and waste.** Bring all calories. High-density, palatable, easy-to-eat-in-the-cold food. The wiry grass, lichen, moss, larval clouds, and thumbnail crustaceans are not your pantry. The herds are thick-coated and low-slung with the wrong horns; they are not goats and you have no idea what they carry. Do not kneel in the shallows to investigate. Pack out everything. The place has never had a building; do not leave the first one in the form of trash or a poorly sited latrine that will be uplifted or drowned later.

**Geothermal, seismic, and chemical caution.** Steam vents breathe rather than roar—a foundry heard through wool. The water that issues is hotter than the air has any right to allow and lays down pale, growing slag-like terraces. Stay off them. New throats appear. The mineral smell (wet iron, old snow) and the industrial patience of the crustaceans suggest dissolved metals and possible gases. Do not camp in hollows on still nights. The deep working—a felt note more than a sound—is another increment of rock being persuaded upward. Animals do not startle. You should still treat it as a reminder that the floor is live. Occasional ice-skin collapses and drips are the close sounds; the rest stays near the ground.

**Mental and perceptual preparation.** This is not a finished landscape. It is a cleared industrial floor on which geology is still working. Time feels different because the light never quite becomes day or night and because processes are slow until they are not. The emptiness is not desert emptiness; it is the emptiness of something that has not yet been given rivers, named peaks, or mid-distance furniture. You will feel small, temporary, and slightly in the way. The repetition can become disorienting or hypnotic. The cold and the thin air will make you slower and more irritable than you expect. Bring whatever you need to stay functional (journal, simple tasks, a reason to look closely without kneeling). Do not expect the place to entertain you or to remain static for your photographs. Stand still long enough and a terrace lengthens, a vent relocates, a herd reassembles around a seep you missed. The mountain is a plant that has not been switched off.

**On-site posture.** Move as if the land is mid-shift because it is. Keep energy in reserve. Watch the herds from the distance that makes them look like one organism coming apart; they have been here long enough to ignore uplift. Do not chase the clicking birds or the dimpling larval clouds. Treat every wet face and every new fracture as potentially live. If you feel the pressure change or the deep note, pause and look at what is above and below you. The air condenses; your gear will too. The only permanence currently on offer is the cold, and even that is just the altitude’s present opinion.

**Exit and limits.** Decide in advance how long an ordinary human body can usefully stay. Deterioration is quiet here. Have a hard trigger (oximetry, cognition, tent integrity, a new vent too close) for leaving. Because this is a multiverse transit, know your return conditions and keep the means on your person, not in the tent. The place will continue without you—cloud tearing and knitting, ice forming and gone, another increment of rock persuaded upward. That is the point.

You are visiting a construction site that is also a birth. Bring the kit, the fitness, the caution, and the willingness to be irrelevant. The high country has not finished arriving. Neither have you.
