**Mental/psychological preparation, clothing and moisture management, supplies (food/water/light/sleep), interaction rules and ethics, navigation/orientation, and what the visit actually feels like.**

This is a temporally arrested high basin: one perfectly held, slightly-overcast afternoon that never advances. Processes that require continuation (erosion, seasons, wind, flowing water, growth, hunger, aging, decay, sound propagation beyond the immediate, convection) simply do not occur. The environment is not dead; it is *finished*. An ordinary human body still runs on its own clocks, so you will experience duration, metabolism, fatigue, and thought while everything around you does not. That mismatch is the central fact of the visit. Prepare for it as you would for a silent, damp, cool, permanently lit diorama the size of a small valley that you can walk through but cannot meaningfully alter.

### Psychological and perceptual readiness
The silence is not empty; it is a low continuous hush equivalent to the unchanging light. You will hear your own breathing, heartbeat, clothing rustle, and footsteps with startling clarity. There is no breeze to mask them, no insect drone, no water sound. Practice sitting in true quiet beforehand (anechoic-chamber recordings or long wilderness stillness) so the contrast does not immediately produce anxiety or a sense of auditory hallucination.

Time perception will distort. Your watch or circadian rhythm continues; the sun does not. Shadows never lengthen. There is no dusk, no night, no morning. Sleep will feel optional or wrong until exhaustion forces it. Bring an eye mask and a simple timer or watch you trust; otherwise you will lose track of how long you have been there. Some visitors report a growing sense that *they* are the anomaly, the only unfinished thing. Others find it profoundly restful, like the world has finally stopped demanding anything. Decide in advance how you will handle either reaction. Journaling helps; the place itself supplies no events to mark time.

You will see posed life everywhere: herds with heads lowered, birds with wings half-spread, ferns fixed mid-unfurl, possibly people in mid-gesture if any remain visible in the halls or courts. They are not statues and not corpses. They simply do not proceed. Do not expect them to startle, answer, or decay. The ethical stance is observational respect: you are a guest in a completed composition. Touching or attempting to “wake” anything is unlikely to work and is the wrong register.

Existential weight is real. The mountains were lifted and then left angular; the city was built by hand and then finished forever. Nothing here is becoming. If that idea sits badly with you, keep the visit short. If it feels like permission to stop striving, you may stay longer than planned.

### Clothing, moisture, and temperature
The air is cool and holds a constant fine dampness that beads on every surface and stays. Evaporation and drying do not occur. Once something is wet, it remains wet. Dew forms a persistent skin on stone, moss, leaves, and on you. Humidity is high and unchanging; there is no wind-chill variation and no solar heating cycle.

- Wear synthetics or merino layers that handle moisture without becoming clammy dead-weight. Avoid cotton. A light waterproof/breathable shell is useful not for rain (there is none) but to keep the beading from soaking base layers.
- Extra dry socks, underwear, and a change of mid-layer in sealed dry-bags. Once they are damp they will not air-dry.
- Insulated jacket or vest for the constant cool; you will not warm up by walking into sunshine that never strengthens.
- Waterproof boots with good grip. Moss is thick, lush, and permanently wet-feeling; flagstones and scree are damp. The still channels are glassy filaments of water occupying their beds; they will wet you if crossed and the wetness will stay.
- Gloves and a warm hat. Extremities stay cool.
- Accept that your skin, hair, and pack will acquire a light persistent dew. It is not soaking rain; it is a film.

Hypothermia risk is low but steady if you become wet and stop moving. The temperature does not drop at “night” because there is no night, but your own fatigue will make you feel colder.

### Food, water, light, and sleep systems
Local resources are paused. Leaves do not fall, fruit (if any) does not ripen further, animals do not move or become available as food, water does not flow or replenish in a conventional sense. Microbial activity is presumably arrested as well. Do not plan to forage or hunt. Bring everything.

- Calories: high-density, non-perishable food for the full intended stay plus margin. There is no resupply and no seasonal change. Your metabolism continues; the landscape’s does not.
- Water: the tarns and channels hold still, mirror-smooth water. It is likely potable (nothing is growing or dying in it). Carry a filter or purification method anyway for peace of mind, plus a bottle or bladder. Scooping still water is simple; it does not run away or ripple onward.
- Light: the pale sun is fixed a little west of noon. Exteriors are permanently in that light; interiors of the taller halls are half-light through slit windows. Bring a headlamp or compact lantern for exploring buildings and for your own camp. No darkness falls, so you will not need it for night travel, only for enclosed spaces and personal reading/sleep.
- Sleep: eye mask is essential. The light never changes. A sleeping bag or quilt rated for cool, damp conditions. You can use the stone buildings (they are not ruins; they are simply finished). Floors are stone or packed earth; they are cool and will stay damp. A closed-cell pad or inflatable mattress plus a groundsheet that can tolerate persistent moisture. Earplugs if your own heartbeat becomes oppressive in the hush.
- Other: basic first-aid (your body can still be injured; healing proceeds on your clock). Notebook and pencil (ink may bead or behave oddly). Camera if you want records; the light is constant so exposure is easy. A small towel that you accept will never fully dry. Plastic bags for organization.

Because the basin can be crossed in an hour, you do not need a large expedition kit. Pack light but complete. There are no shops, no weather windows, no “tomorrow it might be different.”

### Movement, interaction, and traces
Walk. That is the main activity. Footsteps are loud in the hush; move deliberately if you want to preserve the quiet. Moss is thick and springy; flagstones have never been rutted. You will not wear paths. Whether your footprints or displaced pebbles persist after you leave is unknown; treat the place as if they might remain indefinitely.

Do not expect cause-and-effect in the environment. If you splash a tarn, the disturbance may freeze in place or simply fail to propagate. If you move a posed bird or a tool, it may stay where you put it or it may be outside the rules. The conservative approach is observation only: look, photograph, sit, walk the alignments of buildings and the groves. The city was laid out with space and silence as design elements. Do not fill them.

The unfinished mountains are sharp-faceted and climbable in principle, but they too are static. Same light, same damp, no changing views with time of day. Summiting gives you the same basin from above, still held in that afternoon.

If people are present and paused, give them the same courtesy you would give any completed work. They are not waiting for you.

### Orientation and practicalities
The sun is a fixed reference: always a little west of noon, shadows long and precise toward the east-northeast. You cannot get lost in a compass sense. The basin is small and open; visual landmarks (particular halls, groves, tarns) do not change. A simple sketch map made on arrival is sufficient.

No wind means no wind-borne scent or sound cues. No animals will react to your presence. You are functionally invisible to the local ecology because it is not running.

Duration: decide beforehand how long you intend to stay and stick to it, or build in a hard external check (a companion waiting in a neighboring universe, a timed return device). The place itself supplies no urgency and no sunset to tell you it is time to go. Some travelers report that leaving feels like stepping back into a film that has started moving again; the contrast can be disorienting.

### What it actually feels like
You arrive in cool, beaded air under a sun that does not track. The first minutes are visual: the faceted ridges, the exact shadows, the moss so saturated it looks heavier than the ground, the animals arranged like furniture. Then the silence settles. It is not hostile. It is simply finished. You walk an hour and the light is identical. You sit and the ferns do not stir. You eat and the world does not notice. Your own tiredness, hunger, and thoughts become the only plot.

It is beautiful in a severe, architectural way. It is also a place that will not meet you halfway. There is no weather to complain about, no night to fear, no inhabitants to negotiate with, no decay to mourn. There is only the held moment and you moving through it. That can feel like a gift of stillness or like being the sole remaining unfinished sentence. Prepare for both, pack so you are self-contained, move quietly, and leave the arrangement as you found it. The basin does not need anything from you, and that is the entire point.
