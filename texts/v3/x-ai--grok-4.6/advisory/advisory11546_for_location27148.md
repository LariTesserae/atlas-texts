**Prepare for a place that has already decided you will be damp, slightly crouched, and never quite dry.** This mill-hamlet is not a ruin, a park, or a spectacle. It is a working, maintained, century-old negotiation between peat, brick, steam, and living plants that have been taught to share the load. An ordinary human arriving from a drier, more separate world will first notice the water table pressing up through everything, then the way sound and smell and light are all filtered through leaves and lagged pipes. The hamlet occupies a warm, humid basin the size of a cathedral close; you never fully leave the marsh. Pack and plan accordingly.

**Clothing and personal kit must accept constant moisture rather than fight it.** Rain is frequent, warm, and unemphatic; between rains the air still beads on iron and leaf undersides. Everything you wear will become a little wet and stay that way. Choose lightweight, quick-drying layers in muted greens, browns, and greys so you do not stand out as a bright, dry object. A hooded rain shell of tightly woven but breathable fabric helps during actual downpours, yet expect it to feel clammy inside. Avoid heavy waterproofs that turn you into a walking sauna. Trousers should be tough enough for brushing past canes and pipes but loose enough to roll or hike if water rises over the planks. Footwear is critical: ankle or mid-calf boots with aggressive tread and some water resistance, because the “ground” is layered peat, root-mat, packed slag, brick or sleepers, and cupped planks that flex and glint with water between them. The lid you walk on is honest about what sits underneath. Bring extra socks (many pairs) and expect them to smell of steam and leaf even after washing. A wide-brimmed or billed hat keeps both rain and dripping plateleaf off your face; plants will touch your shoulders constantly. Insect considerations are modest but real: midges gather over the retting tanks at dusk. A light, treated scarf or neck gaiter plus a simple repellent (something plant-derived if you want to match the local chemistry) is enough; the residual tannin liquor and plant density already keep most larger pests away.

Pack in waterproof or highly water-resistant bags, then accept that seals will fail and mold will find a way. A small dry-bag for documents, any electronics, and one complete change of clothes is worth the space. Everything else should be chosen for mold resistance or easy replacement: synthetics over heavy wool, though a light merino layer can still work if you dry it near the canteen urn or a lagged pipe (carefully). Bring a compact cloth or two—you will live with a cloth in the hand, wiping condensation, sweat, and the film that forms on zinc tables. A small headlamp or lantern with extra batteries (or a hand-crank option) for the evenings after the dynamo is banked; the hamlet is not dark, but the green-thinned daylight fades and the quieter night sounds (drip, canal slap, cooling valve tick) become more present. A notebook with swollen-page tolerance or a simple voice recorder if you want to remember the layered kitchen-weir noise of stamper, steam hiss, and leaf-slap.

**Health and body preparation is mostly about humidity, skin, and respect for slow chemistry.** The air is a warm greenhouse that never quite vents. Walls sweat behind furniture; laundry never fully dries. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, mold, or sinus issues, bring whatever you normally use and expect them to be more active. The smell—sweet-rot of retting fiber, hot oil, wet cellulose, pollen skin on the tanks—is constant and not unpleasant once you stop fighting it, but it is dense. Canal water is used in the low-pressure boiler and for washing fiber; it is not drinking water. Assume you will drink boiled tea from the canteen urn (always hot because it sits on a bleed from the works) or whatever sealed or treated water you carry in. Skin will stay damp; chafing and fungal issues are the main risks. Pack a simple antifungal powder or cream, extra undergarments, and perhaps a small tube of barrier ointment for any spots that stay wet too long. Midges are more heard than seen and not aggressive, but dusk near the tanks can produce a cloud. The residual chemistry of the tannin liquor and the sheer plant mass seem to have decided the animal question: frogs in the race, an occasional heron at the lock, insects you hear more than see. Birds stay farther out in the reed-forest where the stamper is only a thud. You are unlikely to encounter anything large or predatory. The greater physical demand is postural: pipes run at chest height, lagged in rotting canvas and new moss, hot enough that you learn the safe places not to brush. Guy-wires, steam-lines, and trained canes create a second storey of crossings. You will walk in slots, head slightly ducked, shoulders brushing leaves. People here move along known paths with that same slight duck. Practice walking slowly and looking up as well as down; a loaded cart makes the plank lid flex and no one comments.

**Mental and social preparation: this is not a place that performs for visitors.** Nothing was made in a hurry and nothing has been abandoned. The industry has weather. Cast housings sit on settled marl pads; bolts have been painted, rusted, painted again. The living posts of fig and rattan have taken the loads they were trained for; aerial roots are lashed, lignified, lashed again. Roofs leak on purpose into gutters that feed the retting tanks. The reed-forest is cut on a written rotation; the boiler has a watched crack. Change happens as growth, rot, a plank that finally gives, a cane that is ready. You could stand in the yard a week and believe only belts and water move. Stand a decade and the fig will have closed another window and they will have cut a new one beside it. Arrive with the expectation that you are entering an established, self-maintaining machine that happens to be half-plant. Do not treat the living architecture as decoration or ruin. Do not treat the work as quaint. The fiber canes and wild reeds are cut, floated, retted for weeks (not days) in always-full, always-warm brick troughs whose surface is pollen and drowned midges. Then beaten, washed, hung until merely damp, then turned into twine, matting, coarse canal-colored paper, and tannin liquor boiled in open pans. None of it is hurried. The boiler is never pushed. The dynamo hums one note from first light until they bank the fire.

Talk is close-range because the stamper fills the rest. People live with the same grammar at dwelling scale: brick or timber boxes raised two steps, wrapped in the same vines, planted roofs, shuttered against afternoon damp. Interiors are greenhouse-warm. Bring a small, practical gift if you stay longer than a day—something dry, useful, or from outside the basin (good soap, extra fasteners, a book that can survive swollen pages, seeds that will not become invasive). Offer help only after watching the known paths; they will already be clearing gutters and twisting new lashings around roots that have begun to lift planks. Respect the lock, the single road of crushed shell and slag that drowns twice a year and is patiently rebuilt, and the fact that the canal is both moat and water source. The hamlet feels cut from the reeds rather than built among them. Do not wander into the reed-forest without local guidance; the canes are thicker than a wrist and twelve to fifteen meters high.

**Daily rhythm and sensory reality.** Light arrives already thinned and greened through the planted decks, trained canes, and aroid stems as thick as cable. On rare clear days a white disc of sun stands over the epiphyte-furred chimney and the plateleaf roofs throw a little glare; most days the air simply holds water and you see it. The works run with a kitchen-weir continuity: stamper thump, valve hiss, overflow into the race, leaf-slap when a gust finds the yard. Plants eat the echo. Evening brings a short ordinary-gold light caught in steam and wet leaf faces, then the dynamo is shut down, the stamper stops, and the quieter noises come forward. Someone walks the plank to the lock with a lamp. The reeds stand black against a still-pale sky. The hamlet is a small, loud, green machine sitting in a saucer of warm water, wearing its plants the way it wears its rust—not as failure, not as romance, as the condition of remaining.

If you stay more than a night, expect your own clothes and kit to begin to smell of the place and to acquire their own thin film of moss or mold in any forgotten seam. That is not a failure of preparation; it is the basin asserting itself. The traveler who prepares best is the one who arrives already willing to be a little wet, a little ducked, and to move at the speed of retting and lashing rather than the speed of spectacle. The place has finished arguing with its site. You do not need to win an argument with it. You only need to walk the planks without surprise when they flex, duck the pipes you have learned by feel, and let the layered sound and the sweet-rot air become ordinary. That is how an ordinary human remains here without becoming a problem the hamlet has to solve.
