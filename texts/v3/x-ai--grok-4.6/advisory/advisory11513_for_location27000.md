**Prepare first for the sound, because it will arrive before any other sense and it will not leave.** It is not background; it is weather, architecture, navigation, warning, and the land’s own unfinished speech. An ordinary human nervous system will treat the low groan plus high tearing-seam plus clatters and slams as threat or damage. Without preparation you will lose sleep, lose orientation, lose the ability to think past the roar, and eventually lose hearing or sanity. Do not plan to “get used to it” unaided. Bring layered hearing protection that you can modulate: physical attenuators plus adaptive filters that preserve the informational grain (pitch shifts that mean the ground has lifted, the tear changing key when the air’s texture changes, the hydraulic syllables of the structures) while reducing the load-bearing grind that never stops. Practice living inside continuous industrial-plus-organic noise for weeks beforehand—recordings will not suffice, because the basin is a single acoustic chamber and distance does not soften anything. Learn to treat sound as public property and as a kind of sight. The inhabitants navigate by it. You will too, or you will walk in circles among monuments that recede when your intent is unclear.

The light is permanently dim, bronze-gray, uncommitted, close. There is no true noon and no true night, only a rationing of illumination that the plants somehow drink. Ordinary human vision will feel starved; the warmth stays in the “closed-mouth / after-sun greenhouse” range and never quite becomes either heat or comfort. Bring low-light adaptation (training plus, if available, modest enhancement) and clothing that handles persistent damp-capable air without trapping it. The air itself smells of iron, nectar, charged machine, leaf-rot, ozone when the pale ribbons shake loose from the sound-seam. Assume it is breathable but plan for mineral and biological particulates. Water is an event, not a resident: night-sweat on stone, ten-minute rains that hiss and vanish, condensate along undersides and building skins, brief iridescent pockets that sink, and the once-per-rotation moisture-tide when the structures release fog sheets and sweat and the lush zones riot audibly before the warmth takes it all back. You will dehydrate if you treat water as something you can find on a schedule. Carry high-efficiency condensers and collectors that can work with the buildings’ own courtesy (the structures already farm condensate). Learn the local moisture grammar or you will drink dust.

The land is young, sharp, still deciding. Ridges like broken crockery, channels cut too cleanly, hexagonal kiln-plates, dust that has not had centuries to become soil. It lifts, sheds, recuts itself in the open at a pace that is both geological and biological. A ridge can rise a handsbreadth in a week. A channel can change its letter after a rain. The structures flex in the night—a polite, enormous new stance—and the groan deepens. You cannot treat the ground as finished. Footwear must handle unsoftened mineral edges, sudden new slopes, and plates that fail in bright flakes. Shelter should be light, reconfigurable, and willing to negotiate: the buildings themselves will grow a staircase or iris an aperture when something needs it, or when the building does. Do not plant a rigid camp on “stable” ground; the basin has none. Distance is not entirely honest. A monument may recede until you have thought clearly about why you are approaching. The city is allowed to be shy. Prepare your intent the way you would prepare a request, not a demand.

Vegetation is indecent where it is allowed and almost absent elsewhere. Huge door-leaves, column-stems already woody on land barely older than rumor, mosses that build rooms, vines that scribble the same urgent sentence over everything upright. Greens with fever in them. It is concentrated in condensation-shadows and hanging galleries the structures exhale. Between the thickets: kilometers of bare lithic skin, heat-shimmer, emptiness like the inside of a lung between breaths. You will walk for an hour seeing only your hands and the next dull-lantern silhouette hanging in the air. Then you will be inside a volume so large the far wall is weather, habitation clinging to the inner skins like the lushness clings to the outer ones. Bring clothing that can shed or harvest the brief rains and the tide; the leaves move as if applauding and you will hear them. Do not assume Earth plant rules. Pioneer ecology is overclocked and slightly wrong in its haste. Test everything. Fruiting bodies open with a wet-cloth sound and are emptied by mouths that should not have found them yet. Some local life lives in the sound-seam itself (pale ribbons tasting of ozone), some are more temperature than body, some leave jelly prints that the plants drink. Predators are heard more than seen. Something hunts in the muffling bands of thicker dimness, by the relief of quiet; children are kept from the outer galleries then. No two pictures of it agree about legs. Treat the fauna as abundant in the emptiness and ordinary only inside the green clots. Carry deterrents that do not rely on making more noise (noise is already total) and that will not offend the substrate. The ecology is a wound in motion. Do not become part of its food web by accident.

The structures are not brick and not quite metal. They hold the dim light under the skin so each is a bruise of illumination at distance. Up close the grain rearranges while you look, the way a muscle rearranges under work. Walkways exist then don’t. A palm on a bulkhead can become a window onto a depth that is not outside. A spill runs uphill, tidying itself, humming. No one calls this magic; they call it courtesy—the building’s, the substrate’s, or the physics that was negotiated when the land was raised. Some inhabitants say the city is scaffolding, some a splint, some the thing that called the ground up and is now obliged to stay and listen to it heal. You will live, if you stay, the way they do: on the inner surfaces of vast hollows, calling across voids, moving along galleries and condensate farms. Tools often lack visible edges. Learn to ask rather than force. A staircase will be there in the morning because last night someone (or the building) needed it. Bring almost nothing that insists on remaining inert and separate. The interiors are mostly volume. Habitation is a city’s worth across the whole basin, a village’s worth in any single monument. They do not seem lonely. That is either culture or damage. Do not assume they want explanation or company on Earth terms. Watch how they live with the roar the way other cities live with streetlight. At the confluence of three great hollows the emptiness relents a degree; you can hear actual voices—barter, prayer, complaint—nearly drowned. Someone has tried to teach a vine to make letters. The vine made something else.

Physiological and medical preparation must assume constant low-level geological work, pioneer microbes and spores, possible novel biochemistry in the jelly, the ribbons, the lung-like drums that swell and empty with thunder you feel in the floor, and the simple fact that the land is still being decided. Bring broad-spectrum prophylactics, wound care for sharp new stone and whatever the fauna does, and a way to analyze water and tissue on the fly. Hearing, vestibular sense, and sleep will be under assault. The warmth never becomes true heat but the damp and the work-temperature air will fatigue you. Cycles are not Earth days. Plan stay length around the moisture-tide, the buildings’ flex, the flocks’ clockwise-then-counterclockwise years (if they are years), and the predation-pulse that travels as a rumor of teeth. You cannot day-trip this place. The description itself says stay until the buildings flex; feel the floor take a new angle, polite, enormous. That is the minimum honest visit.

Psychological preparation is not optional. The basin is a hollow place densely used, a quiet geometry that will not stop screaming, a city that has not been given enough world and a world that has not been given enough time. Emptiness is not desert; it is volume without occupancy, the pause inside a struck bell. Then a flock erases the already-dim sky or the tide comes and the young ground shines as if it had been planned to shine. Sensory diet is extreme: roar, dim bronze, indecent green pockets, mineral exactness that is already failing at the edges, interiors like naves whose far walls are weather. People who need silence, or bright light, or finished landscapes, or crowds, or clear day/night will break. Practice long periods of unstructured dimness and unstructured distance. Practice listening until the groan and the tear become texture rather than assault. Accept that processes older worlds hide underground happen here in the open, at a pace you can watch and hear. The strangeness is the particular strangeness of things still being decided. You are a visitor to a construction that is also a healing and also a civic act that simply thins, the way a note thins, until the last structure is rumor on the horizon.

Practical kit, condensed: modulated hearing system; moisture and condensate gear; adaptive, negotiating shelter and clothing; low-light vision support; robust but light mobility (the land will change under you); biological and mineral analysis; food that does not assume local edibility plus means to learn it; communication that can cross voids or that can wait until you learn to call the way they do; nothing that treats the buildings or the substrate as dead matter; a clear, repeatable reason for coming (the city may recede otherwise); records of sound and stance-changes more than photographs (light is stingy). Travel light enough to move when a channel finds a new grammar. Do not try to quiet anything. Do not try to fill the emptiness. The place is already using it.

An ordinary human can survive and even live here if they arrive as a listener rather than a conqueror of silence and distance. The roar will occupy you. The land will continue to rise and recut. The structures will flex and offer courtesy if you offer it back. The green will be violent in its pockets and the fauna will be wrong in its haste. You will walk hours of bare new stone and then lose a body in moss-rooms or hanging galleries. You will hear predation three monuments away, intact. You will feel the floor take a new angle in the night and the seam will tear a little higher. Then the stance is taken, the land is a little more land than it was, and the roar—unchanged to a newcomer, changed in a way the inhabitants hear the way one hears a familiar voice enter a room—goes on, filling every empty cubic measure, which is nearly all of them. That is the visit. Prepare to be inside it, not to observe it from a finished world.
