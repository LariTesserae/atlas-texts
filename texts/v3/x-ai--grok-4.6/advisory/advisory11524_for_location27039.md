**Arrive empty-handed in the ways that matter, laden in the ways that keep you alive.** This is not a destination you visit so much as a frequency you enter. An ordinary human body—wet, talkative, thirsty, visually greedy, used to edges that stay put and silences that are actually silent—will experience the bowl as a prolonged, intimate contradiction: a place that is simultaneously cathedral, instrument, desert, attic, and living throat. Preparation is half logistics and half deliberate unmaking. The city will not meet you halfway.

### Physiological and Sensory Conditioning
Spend weeks beforehand in the driest, hottest, noisiest environments you can find. Saunas, deserts at midday, industrial sites, or simply standing in a wind tunnel with cheap earplugs will not replicate the bowl, but they teach your body that constant pressure on the sternum, the impossibility of linear thought, and the sensation of light as weight are survivable. Train yourself to think in fragments and pulses rather than sentences; the roar makes consecutive clauses feel like a physical strain. Practice moving, eating, and even swallowing on an irregular off-beat you invent—tap a rhythm with your heel and step only in the gaps. Your inner ear and sense of balance will be under assault from the first moment the ground slants.

Hydration training is limited because you cannot simulate the total absence of ambient moisture plus the plants’ violent reaction to it. Acclimate to low water intake as far as is medically safe, then plan to reverse that immediately upon arrival by drinking more than you think you need from sealed stores. Eyes need work: spend time in intense glare with wrapped fabric or extreme sunglasses until you can navigate by touch and memory of steps. The noon white-gold will erase edges; you will work like the inhabitants, by feel.

Respiratory preparation: the air is mineral-dust and plant-fiber dry. If you have any history of asthma or dryness-triggered issues, assume it will flare. A simple cloth wrap over mouth and nose (matching the brass-tea foliage if you want to be polite) is more useful than a modern filter, which will clog with the fine stone sweat.

### Gear: What to Carry, What Never to Spill
Water is the single greatest practical problem and the greatest taboo. A human in constant dry heat will need several liters per day just to function; more if you are active on the terraces. Bring it in multiple small, utterly leak-proof containers—never one large vessel that could rupture. Treat every drop as radioactive to the forest. A spilled bead blackens stems as if burned; the plants will not forgive it and the inhabitants will notice. Recycle what you can (condensation traps inside the deepest rooms at night, if any forms) but do not introduce foreign moisture to the stone or joints. Plan your stay length by water weight, not curiosity. You can live here a year without filling a room, but you cannot live here a week without water.

Food must be dry, calorie-dense, and require no preparation water. The local chemistry offers nothing reliable for an outsider’s gut; the plants drink light and metallic rock-sweat, not rain, and their edibility is unknown and probably irrelevant. Pack for the entire stay plus margin. The markets are stone shelves facing other stone shelves.

Clothing: loose, full-coverage wraps in the local brass-tea-beetle palette. This is not fashion. It cuts the glare, reduces dust inhalation, and signals you are not a tourist treating the city as scenery. Eyes get extra wraps or goggles that can be donned by feel; noon light has mass. Footwear must grip pale, wind-softened, root-threaded stone and allow you to feel the wind’s pulse through the soles—thin but durable. A light dust mask or additional face wrap.

Hearing: you cannot block the roar and should not try. Heavy earplugs or noise-cancelling gear will muffle the high tearing and the gong-like chord but the vibration lives in your chest and bones. Some protection against actual damage is wise; total silence is impossible and, from the reports of those who leave, worse. The city was built (or became willing) to be this loud. Accept that hearing becomes a ruined, secondary sense. You will navigate and “listen” with feet, sternum, and the memory of quieter nodes.

Other kit: a small first-aid assortment focused on dehydration, cracked skin, eye irritation, and respiratory dust. Cordage or plant-compatible binding if you must repair something of your own—do not introduce slurry or wet mortar. A notebook is nearly useless except in the deepest rooms where the roar drops to merely vast; write there, in the pauses. Light sources are almost unnecessary except as courtesy in the treasured dim interiors; the bowl provides too much light, then too little of the right kind. A way to mark your own path without carving (the masons and the wind have already spoken). Nothing that tries to record or quiet the sound.

Leave behind: any expectation of conversation, of finding water, of making the place comfortable, of filling space. Cameras if they require you to stop and compose; the glare and the motion of leaves will punish that habit. Weapons or tools that look like they might recarve the city against its will.

### Movement, Rules, and the Inhabitants
Study the description until the rules feel like gravity. Do not look for water—the word itself is reserved. Move on the off-beats; the old paths follow quieter nodes in the wind cycle. Stray and the sound takes your balance. Never attempt to seal or baffle anything; previous attempts only made trapped columns boom louder. Light is a substance—wrap, work by touch at peak, treasure the brown-gold dusk of inward rooms.

The few remaining people will be seen in periphery: brass-tea wraps, stone blades, copper over ancient cobbles, cordage from sounding-plant fiber. They recarve, restring, prune the valves. They do not speak except as private mouth-motion in the deepest rooms. Learn a simple, respectful sign vocabulary in advance if you can invent one that might overlap with theirs—a grammar of hands developed because air will not carry words. Do not hail them. Do not offer water or ask for it. They have intercalated with the forest and the masonry for so long that a tower is also a trunk. Watch how they time their crossings of bridges. Imitate the timing, not the identity.

Animals are rumor. Do not hunt rumor.

### Psychological and Existential Readiness
This is the larger preparation. The bowl does not announce itself; you are already below the lip before you understand you have entered something made to be missed. The architecture is the population. The forest is a masonry that grew. You will stand in the central plaza—a circle of flagstones worn to bone-joints—and count fewer objects than fingers. Emptiness here is dense with intention.

The sound is weather, instrument, and theology. Morning tearing canvas, midmorning thick chord with texture, afternoon the city playing itself through heat-opened valves, night the grinding dry surf and the ticking of ten thousand cooling leaves. You will not think in sentences. You will think in the rare pauses, if they come. This can feel like clarity or like erosion of self. Some travelers report the roar becoming a kind of rest, the way the depression itself is a held breath worn into rest. Others find the “wrong-sized” silence just beyond the lip—the silence that lets you hear your own blood and mistake it for the city calling—more intolerable than the noise. Most who climb out come back before dark. Prepare for the possibility that you will not want to leave, or that leaving will feel like amputation.

The city is finished and still happening. You could occupy it for a year and never fill a room, yet never find a surface that had not already been touched, planted, carved, or deliberately left empty. Let that paradox sit in you before you go. Practice sitting with unfinished thoughts. Practice being a surface rather than a filler of space.

Evening will give you copper, then bone-white, then—if the dust is right—a green that belongs to no leaf here. The valves close in a long ragged descent of pitch. Someone draws a shutter that does almost nothing. The flagstones tick like a clock the ground invented. That is when the place is most itself: holding the last heat against its chest, a pocket that knows it has an outside.

If you stay, become willing to be missed by the wider world the way the bowl is missed from the plateau. If you leave, understand the halo of sound that follows a little way and then cuts off as if a door shut. Pack that knowledge with the water. The ordinary human body can survive here if it consents to be less ordinary—drier, quieter in speech, louder in bone, emptier of assumption. The city will not change for you. It has been itself long enough to know how to go on being itself. Your task is to last long enough, lightly enough, to hear what it has been saying the entire time.
