**Preparation for the Hollow**

An ordinary human body is a creature of cycles, gradients, and change: breath in and out, heat rising and falling, light that has a source and a direction, dust that settles according to gravity and air currents, time that is felt as sequence. The hollow has none of these. It is a finished volume. Visiting it is less like entering a cave and more like stepping into a single, paused exhalation that has lasted centuries. Your preparations must therefore address both the obvious physical mismatches and the deeper mismatch of being a process inside a place that has already concluded.

**Atmosphere, respiration, and dust**
The chamber has been sealed long enough that any original atmosphere has equilibrated with the stone and the sparse moss. Expect low or slowly declining oxygen, possible CO2 accumulation, and a complete absence of convective mixing. The air is dry, warm, and motionless. Fine pale dust (likely silica-rich silt plus salt residues) coats every surface and hangs indefinitely once disturbed. A single footfall can create a persistent, non-settling haze.

- Carry a closed-circuit or long-duration rebreather with independent O2 monitoring and CO2 scrubbing. Do not rely on filters alone; the dust is too fine and the volume too static. Bring at least 150 % of the calculated duration you intend to stay, plus a spare unit.
- Full-face positive-pressure mask or helmet with a good seal. Even brief unfiltered exposure risks silicosis-like irritation or delayed inflammatory response. Pack saline rinses, extra filters, and a small HEPA vacuum or electrostatic cloth system if you must clear gear.
- Test a tiny sample of dust (glove, sealed vial) before committing to long exposure; the chemistry is described as “salt and silica and slow oxidation.” Assume abrasive and potentially hygroscopic.

**Thermal and hydration load**
The heat is even, dry, and stone-sourced. It will not feel like a fever, but it never recedes. Sweat evaporates instantly; you will lose water without noticing thirst until later. There is no usable liquid water—only hairline mineral dampness that does not bead.

- Insulated but breathable layers that allow you to dump heat if needed, plus a light moisture-wicking base. The temperature is described as never reaching discomfort, yet dry heat plus exertion plus low ceiling (you can touch it with a raised arm) will still stress thermoregulation.
- Water: minimum 4–6 liters per planned hour plus electrolyte concentrate. Do not drink anything found on site. The faint dampness is mineral and not a resource.
- A compact personal cooling option (phase-change vest or evaporative but sealed system) is cheap insurance; the environment will not help you.

**Light and vision**
Light is a residual, directionless gray generated by the rock itself. Eyes adapt only enough to confirm scale, then stop. No shadows, no color discrimination, no reliable depth cues. Edges remain suggestions. Your own lights will be the first directional sources the space has seen in a very long time; they will look unnaturally sharp.

- Red or amber low-output lamps as primary (preserve whatever dark adaptation is possible). High-output white only for mapping or emergency. Multiple independent sources, headlamp plus handheld plus backup, all with long-duration batteries rated for the ambient temperature.
- Consider a simple IR or low-lux camera feed to a wrist display; the human visual system will underperform here.
- Mark your entry point and a few reference stones with high-visibility, non-permanent tags you can recover. The floor is otherwise featureless except for isolated moss tufts several body-lengths apart.

**Mobility and the physical space**
Eighty paces across, ceiling just above a raised arm: a broad, low disc. You will not stoop constantly, but you will feel the roof. Dust will lift with every step and remain suspended. There are no paths, no sharp edges, no recent breaks. Fallen slabs are half-buried in their own grit as if they have always been exactly there.

- Soft, dust-shedding footwear with good grip; avoid kicking up more than necessary. Kneepads or a small folding stool if you intend to linger.
- A lightweight line or retractable reel from the entry point. In the dim, directionless light it is easy to lose orientation even in an open chamber.
- Move slowly. Rapid motion produces long-lived dust clouds that reduce the already poor visibility and coat every surface, including your gear and lungs.

**Time, silence, and psychology**
The hollow does not cycle. Temperature, light, moss, dust, and sound have reached a finished state. Occasional grains of silt fall with a tick so faint it may be imagined. The silence is complete rather than expectant. Time here is described as a still pool. For a human nervous system this is close to sensory deprivation plus mild hyperthermia plus fine-dust irritation.

- Expect temporal distortion. Hours may feel like minutes or the reverse. Wear two independent timepieces (one analog, one digital) and check them on a pre-set interval rather than by feel. Set a hard exit time and treat it as non-negotiable.
- The isolation is total. No wind, no drip, no distant traffic of insects or water. Bring a small, familiar sound source (bone-conduction, very low volume) only if you discover you need an anchor; otherwise let the silence be. Many people find the stillness first peaceful, then oppressive, then strangely clarifying.
- Claustrophobia risk is real despite the horizontal scale: low roof, sealed volume, dust haze, dim light. Practice a few sessions of sitting in a dark, warm, quiet room beforehand. Have a simple grounding routine (count breaths, name five things you can still sense) ready.
- The moss is sparse, gray, crystalline, isolated. Do not touch it. It is the only living punctuation and it has been there, unchanging, for a very long time. Nothing grazes it, nothing pollinates it. Treat it as the last remaining clause in a sentence that has otherwise ended.

**Entry, exit, and contingency**
The original opening slumped shut. You will need a way in that does not collapse the siltstone or flood the chamber with outside air and weather. Once inside, the seal is part of the condition you came to experience; re-opening it carelessly ends the visit for everyone after you.

- Plan a reversible, minimal-disturbance entry (small borehole with airlock collar, or whatever method your travel tech allows). Bring materials to re-seal if you must.
- Communications: assume none. The rock and the static air will kill most signals. A simple pre-arranged check-in schedule with someone outside the hollow is essential.
- Medical: standard trauma kit plus dust-exposure and dehydration items. A small oxygen candle or emergency O2 bottle. Know the signs of hypoxia and hypercapnia; they can feel like calm or like panic.
- If you become disoriented, sit, reduce movement, and wait for the dust to become the thin carpet again. The chamber is finite. You cannot actually get lost, only lose the sense of where the exit is.

**What visiting actually feels like**
You will enter a volume that has already finished becoming. The warmth is the stone’s own, not the sun’s. The dust does not dance; it forgets how. Your lights will be the first directional things the space has known in memory. Your breath, your heartbeat, the tiny sounds of your clothing will be the loudest events in a long time. After a while the silence stops being an absence and becomes a presence—the acoustic equivalent of the heat and the settled grit.

Most people report two phases. First, a low-grade unease: the body keeps waiting for a breeze, a drip, a change in temperature, a shadow that never arrives. Then, if you stay and stop fighting it, a peculiar neutrality. Nothing here is happening, and therefore nothing is being withheld. The moss simply persists. The ceiling simply hangs. The dust, once you stop lifting it, simply is.

You will leave traces—footprints that fill slowly with the same pale film, a few motes that never quite forget they were lifted. The hollow will not punish you for them, nor will it remember. It has no mechanism left for either.

Prepare as if you are visiting a completed thought. Bring what a human needs to remain a process for a few hours inside a place that no longer processes. Then go in quietly, stay only as long as you intended, and leave the dust as close as you can to the thin, even carpet you found.

That is the entire preparation: enough air, enough water, enough light of your own, enough timekeeping, and the willingness to be the only unfinished thing in a finished room.
