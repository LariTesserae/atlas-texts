**Protect the body first, then the mind, then whatever still counts as “you.” This place does not care which fails first.**

An ordinary human—lungs that expect air, inner ears that expect gravity, skin that expects a single consistent down, a nervous system that expects sound to have a source and time to keep its sequence—cannot treat the basin as scenery. It is a volume that has not finished deciding it is a place. You will be inside the argument. Preparation is less about gear lists and more about accepting that most of what you bring will be treated as another current.

### Physical realities you cannot negotiate away
The water is the room. It is body-warm, faintly sweet, and occupies every direction at once. Currents reverse without telegraph. A surface becomes a wall becomes a ceiling of falling weight. Sharp, unweathered stone shears and blooms; edges will cut cloth, skin, and tendon. The air pockets are thin and exhausted. The roar is not background; it is architecture you feel in sinuses, teeth, and bone. Visibility is a few body-lengths of used green-grey light. Time and memory arrive waterlogged. Down is a suggestion the place sometimes ignores. Breathing occurs in both phases, but the distinction itself is unfinished.

You will be turned, slammed, disoriented, and briefly deafened in a way that is saturation rather than silence. Panic is the fastest way to become another dissolving shard.

**Condition the body for this specific violence, not generic fitness.**
- Train in chaotic, multi-axis water: wave tanks, flood simulators, or the worst rapids you can find, while wearing weight that shifts unexpectedly. Practice moving when “up” changes mid-stroke. Learn to keep a core of orientation in the torso even when limbs are flung. You need explosive power for short bursts and the ability to go limp without inhaling when a wall of water hits. Endurance swimming is almost irrelevant; the currents will do most of the work, usually against you.
- Desensitize the inner ear and proprioception. Spend time in inversion tables, gyroscopic trainers, or zero-g analog if available. The basin will treat your vestibular system as optional. Habituate to the feeling of falling in every direction so the first reversal does not lock you into a fetal curl.
- Protect skin and joints from abrasion and impact. The rock is young and wants to cut. Even brief contact can open you. Calluses help but are not enough.
- Accept that you will be wet, warm, and vibrating continuously. Hypothermia is unlikely; heat exhaustion of the mind is not. Train to function while the body is already shouting.

Do not rely on “I can just hold my breath.” The place sometimes lets you breathe water. Sometimes it does not. The transition is not announced.

### Gear that might survive the argument (most will not)
Standard scuba, dry suits, and electronics fail here for the same reason maps fail: the rules are local and unfinished. Bring as little as possible that can be turned into a projectile or a snag.

- A close-fitting, abrasion-resistant skin (think thick, flexible neoprene or a modern equivalent with high cut resistance) that covers everything, including a hood and gloves. It should allow full mobility and not trap air that can become a buoyancy weapon when currents invert. Avoid anything rigid or metallic that remembers being made; the place notices weight and intention.
- Minimal flotation that can be dumped instantly. Too much buoyancy and you become a kite in a three-dimensional waterfall.
- A simple, redundant way to mark “out.” The lip is only a change in the quality of the roar. A short, brightly colored line or a weighted, non-entangling marker that you can feel rather than see may help, but expect it to be torn away or to start humming. Do not trust compasses, GPS, or inertial devices; down is unreliable and time is optional.
- Ear and sinus protection that does not seal completely. The roar will still enter through bone. Complete isolation can make the body’s own vibration worse. Some travelers report that a thin layer of something that conducts rather than blocks (wax, a specific gel) lets the sound become a single note instead of a weapon. Test this beforehand; what works in one reality may become another current here.
- A small, non-mechanical way to record or remember. Memory leaches. Sequence becomes optional. A few physical tokens that you can touch in a fixed order (smooth stones of different textures, a short knotted cord) can serve as an external clock and an anchor to the self you arrived with. Electronic recording is likely to be rewritten or simply unknit.
- Nothing with moving parts you cannot afford to lose. The fitted stones already hum at frequencies felt in the wrist. Adding more vibration is unwise.
- A way home that does not depend on local physics. If your method of travel is a device, shield it inside your body or in a form the water cannot easily argue with (swallowed, implanted, or simply accepted as part of the meat). If it is knowledge or a state of mind, rehearse it until it is reflex, because thought will arrive waterlogged.

Leave behind anything that smells of finished technology or patient geology. The basin is still cooling. It treats completed objects as insults.

### Mental and temporal preparation
This is the harder part. The place is not merely loud and wet; it is young. Reality here is still being shouted into shape. An ordinary human mind expects continuity. You will not get it.

- Practice existing without a reliable “now.” Meditation that treats time as weather rather than a line helps. Some people use rhythmic breathing or a repeating physical gesture (pressing thumb to a specific finger, a short mantra felt rather than spoken) as a portable present tense. When a moment of stillness lasts long enough to watch stone grow a handspan and the next heartbeat flings you against a wall that was not there, that gesture is the only clock you have.
- Expect memory to bleach. Colors fade, sequence optional, the outside world arrives waterlogged. Before you enter, write or speak a short, concrete list of facts about yourself and why you came—names, a single sensory memory of dry land, the reason this visit matters. Keep it short enough to reconstruct from fragments. Do not bring a long narrative; it will be edited by the currents.
- Desensitize to total sound. Spend time in industrial noise, next to waterfalls, or in anechoic chambers until the absence of a sound source no longer triggers panic. The roar has no origin and no interest in stopping. After a while the body stops locating it and simply vibrates. That is the functional state. Fighting it wastes energy you do not have.
- Accept that orientation is a story you tell yourself. “I am facing the humming ring” is more useful than “I am upright.” Practice labeling the environment by felt qualities (pitch of the roar, temperature of a current, rasp of bronze-colored mat) rather than visual maps.
- Decide in advance what “enough” looks like. The basin pulses like a throat. It will not hold still. Staying until you “understand” is how people become another translucent bell that unknits into bubbles. Set a hard internal limit: a certain number of orientation resets, a felt change in the quality of your own thoughts, the first time the incised lines on the slab stay lines when you look away. Then leave.

The structures are not shelters. They are notes in a language the storm is still writing. Approach them as you would approach a live electrical cable you do not fully understand: briefly, with respect, and without assuming they are for you.

### Movement and conduct once inside
Move as little as possible on your own terms. Let the currents take you until you feel a lessening that might be the lip. Fighting wastes energy and announces you as something the place has not yet decided how to erode. When you must act, act in short, committed bursts, then go limp again.

Do not try to stand on the fitted stones as if they were floors. They are not. The water uses their openings as it pleases. The humming blocks are felt in the wrist; if you touch them, do so once, note the pitch, and move on. The drifting lines on the slab are still liquid. Looking too long is how sequence becomes optional.

Life here is rare and brief. The pale flattened thing, the cluster of bells—these are not companions. They appear, fail to cohere, become current. Do not chase them. Do not assume they are food or threat in any lasting sense.

If you find the lip and the roar eases a fraction, look out only long enough to confirm the slope still exists in some direction. Then decide whether to leave. The basin does not end so much as it forgets to continue. Lingering to “see more” is how people stay.

### After
You will come out waterlogged in more than the obvious way. Colors may stay leached for a time. Sequence of events may rearrange itself. The roar may continue in the bones for days. Have a dry, quiet, geometrically simple place waiting—four walls that stay walls, a floor that stays down, silence that is actually absence. Re-anchor with the physical tokens you brought. Eat something with a strong, unambiguous taste. Sleep if you can; the place does not.

An ordinary human can visit this location. Many will not want to visit twice. The preparation that matters most is the willingness to be briefly unmade and then to insist, with whatever fragments remain, that you were a visitor rather than another unfinished rib of stone. The water keeps arriving from every direction at once. You do not have to stay for the rest of the argument.
