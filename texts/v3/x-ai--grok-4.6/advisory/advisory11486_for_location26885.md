**Preparation for an ordinary human from a typical terrestrial baseline:** treat this as a visit to a living, unfinished machine-habitat at altitude, equal parts construction site, hydroponic laboratory, and embryonic ecosystem. The place is warm, saturated, structurally incomplete, and quietly alien in its physics and biology. You will be wet within minutes, disoriented within an hour, and possibly altered in small ways if you stay longer. Pack and condition yourself accordingly.

**Clothing and immediate physical protection.**  
Wear a single-layer, fully synthetic, quick-dry ensemble (no cotton, no wool, no down). A lightweight waterproof-breathable shell (Gore-Tex or equivalent that still works when already wet) over merino or synthetic base layers. The air is already at or near dew point; your clothes will wick, then stay damp, then begin to feel like a second skin. Seams and zippers should be taped or covered. Sharp unfinished edges, formwork ridges, and sintered pellet scree will catch and tear fabric, so choose abrasion-resistant material and carry a small roll of repair tape. Footwear must be closed-toe, deeply lugged, and completely waterproof with excellent wet-grating traction (approach shoes or light mountaineering boots with sticky rubber). The catwalk sweats; moss and algae will appear on any horizontal you linger on. Bring two extra pairs of socks in a dry bag and change them the moment they feel cold or slimy. A brimmed hat or hood is useful against the constant slow applause of drips from leaf-plates and weeping walls. Thin liner gloves plus a pair of work gloves: you will brush against living concrete, warm pump casings, and ferns taller than you. A buff or light neck gaiter helps with the ozone-and-crushed-stem taste and keeps condensation from running down your collar.

**Health, hydration, and biological caution.**  
The nutrient film circulating through the system is not drinking water. Carry more potable water than you think you need (at least 3–4 liters for a day visit) in a bladder you can sip without exposing it to the ambient mist; treat any local water as chemical/biological unknown. High humidity plus warmth plus possible plant volatiles plus low-level ozone means respiratory irritation and rapid fungal growth on skin and gear. Pack a broad-spectrum antihistamine, a steroid inhaler if you have any history of reactive airways, antifungal powder or cream, and extra zinc oxide or barrier cream for skin folds. The plants are young, hyper-accelerated, and occupying every joint; their pollen, spores, and sap are untested against a human immune system. Assume allergens, possible contact irritants, and hitchhiking microbes or seeds. A simple N95 or better respirator in a dry bag is cheap insurance for the first hour while you gauge the air. Bring electrolyte tablets; you will sweat even while standing still. Tetanus and any available broad travel immunizations are already assumed for a multiverse traveler, but this is a new constructed biome—your gut and skin flora will meet novel organisms. Do not eat anything that grows here unless you have a way to test it; the oldest plant is a season old and the nutrient mix is proprietary to this project.

**Safety and structural awareness.**  
This is still a construction site cantilevered over an unfocused gulf. The grating, scaffolding, and unfinished walls have no fall protection visible in the description. A lightweight climbing harness, 30–40 m of static rope or webbing, and a few lockers or slings let you clip to rails, uprights, or the robotic mason’s tracks if the catwalk feels questionable or a cloud thickens and visibility drops to zero. The robotic mason itself is goat-sized and hydraulic; it pauses when approached. Treat it as a moving industrial hazard that may or may not have object-avoidance programmed for unexpected humans. Sharp corners, unused sinter pellets (thumbnail-sized, already sorting into alluvial fans), and living concrete that is still exothermic mean cuts, punctures, and minor burns are possible. A compact first-aid kit with irrigation syringe, butterfly closures, and burn gel belongs in a waterproof pouch. The vibration of the unseen generator is a pulse you will stop noticing until it hiccups; that hiccup is your cue to check footing and water surfaces for sudden rings. Capillary channels make water climb a few centimeters against gravity; do not assume puddles behave. If a cloud wraps the basin, the catwalk you entered by can vanish. Carry a simple analog compass, a whistle, and a chemical light stick. Your electronics will be fighting 100 % humidity and possible ozone; keep them in multiple sealed dry bags and accept that some will fail.

**Sensory and temporal acclimatization.**  
Light is already scattered and kind; there are no hard shadows. Time-of-day cues dissolve. Irrigation cycles impose their own rhythm. You will begin measuring duration by vine advance across a conduit. This is not metaphor. Bring a reliable analog watch (or two) that does not rely on ambient light or radio, and check it more often than feels natural. The even, rinsed light plus the underwater-tea color of the western glass-ceramic wall in late afternoon can produce a mild oneirogenic effect—dream-like without being hallucinatory. The soundscape is almost entirely water (drip, sheet, gurgle, slap) plus occasional pneumatic kisses from valves and the low molar-vibration of pumps. Conversation will feel like a violation; plan for silence or very low voices. The air tastes of ozone and crushed stem and should not be pleasant yet is; this is a chemical signature of the place. Expect your own clothes to make audible wicking sounds. Vertigo is possible: the pale unfocused depth below the grating is either cloud or a very long drop. If you have any history of height sensitivity, practice looking at your feet on wet metal grating before you arrive.

**Psychological and behavioral stance.**  
The basin is active without being busy. It is colonizing itself as fast as it is being built. You are an unscheduled variable. Do not prune, do not “help,” do not move the sinter pellets, and do not attempt to redirect the robotic mason. The plants have already forgotten they were clipped; they will treat your presence as another substrate if you stay still long enough. The strangeness is quiet and structural: water that inhales its own runoff, leaves that are slightly warm on the underside, growth you can watch. This can feel womb-like, meditative, or quietly oppressive depending on your temperament. Isolation is near-total once a cloud thickens; the place becomes a room with no exterior. Prepare for that enclosure. Some travelers report a sense that the habitat is still deciding what it is; do not try to decide for it. If the damselfly or the wet-cardboard moth appears, observe only. Fauna is not yet scheduled.

**Practical visit logistics.**  
Enter along the grated catwalk knowing it may disappear behind you in mist. Move slowly through the fern curtains—they are rooms whose walls breathe and they will soak you to the skin. Watch the irrigation booms; their programmed bursts look like weather and will find you. The living concrete is still curing and raising local temperature by fractions of a degree; it is warm to the touch and chemically active. If you sit, choose a surface that is already colonized rather than freshly poured. Limit a first visit to a few hours until you understand how your body handles the combination of heat, humidity, and time slip. Exit the same way you entered unless the cloud has erased the route, in which case wait; clouds here thicken out of the existing air and then thin again. After departure, inspect every fold of clothing and gear for seeds, moss fragments, or nutrient film residue. Shower thoroughly. Some growth rates here are visibly super-terrestrial; assume hitchhikers.

**What the visit actually feels like.**  
You will be warm and continuously beaded with water. The light will make everything look as if it is lit from inside a patient cloud. Your footsteps on the grating will be accompanied by the rise of already-sweet, already-warm air past your ankles. Ferns will part like damp rooms. A stem you brushed in the morning will have a new angle by afternoon. The robotic mason will pause and regard you as a possible change-order. At some point the entire basin will become a sealed, vaporous chamber and you will hear only water and your own clothes. Then the pale gulf will reappear below. You will leave smelling of ozone, wet concrete, and crushed green. The memory will feel both recent and slightly out of temporal joint.

That is the place. Prepare as if you are visiting a greenhouse that has not yet decided whether it is a garden, a machine, or a new kind of weather, hanging over nothing, still being born.
