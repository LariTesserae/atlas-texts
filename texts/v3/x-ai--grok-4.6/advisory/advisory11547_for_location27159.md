**Preparation for the Unfinished Highland**

An ordinary human body is a wet, pressurized, oxygen-burning machine evolved for a finished planet with weather, circadian rhythm, and surfaces that stay put. This highland is none of those things. It is a regional upthrust still deciding its own topography, chemistry, and even the local meaning of “duration.” Visiting it as a baseline human requires treating your own biology as a temporary, poorly adapted guest in a place that has not yet invented hospitality. The goal is not comfort or mastery. The goal is to remain intact long enough to witness a world assembling itself and then leave before it incorporates you.

### Atmosphere, Light, and the Body’s Immediate Betrayals

The air is thin enough that unaided speech dies within a few meters and unaided running produces hypoxia in minutes. Sound itself is a luxury the highland has not fully granted; most events register as pressure changes in the inner ear or vibrations in bone. You will need a closed-cycle breathing system from the first moment—rebreather with at least 72 hours of consumables, plus a backup chemical oxygen generator. The suit must be fully sealed and positively pressurized against the electrostatic feldspar-olivine dust that lifts in silent veils and will otherwise coat lungs, eyes, and every joint. The dust is not merely abrasive; it is ionized and remembers recent motions. It can rise in reverse sequence of a collapse you witnessed an hour earlier. Assume it will find every unsealed zipper, every micro-tear.

Light is a residual dusk the color of old iron, scattered by a motionless high veil of lofted grit. There is no useful solar cycle, no reliable shadows for orientation, and stars are faint trembling points that give no illumination. Your suit visor needs active low-light amplification and a heads-up display that does not rely on external contrast. Carry a helmet lamp, but use it sparingly; the darkness here has apparent weight and direction. It pools in new depressions and thins over rising ground. A bright light can feel like an intrusion that the land notices.

Temperature is inverted from earthly expectation. The rock itself radiates a dry, steady geothermal heat—comfortable to the touch, sometimes feverish. The air, however, would freeze you. The suit must therefore manage two conflicting regimes: conductive heat from below (insulated, heat-resistant soles and lower-body layers) and radiative/convective cold from the thin atmosphere (active heating in the upper body and extremities). Do not sit or lie down for long. The ground is never cold, but prolonged contact can cook boots or create localized thermal stress that the embryonic crust interprets as another small fault.

There is no water in any phase. None. The chemistry is anhydrous salts, metallic oxides, cooling volcanic glass, and dust. Your body will lose moisture through respiration and the merest exertion. Bring a closed-loop water recycler capable of reclaiming every milliliter from urine, sweat, and exhaled vapor. Carry at least 8–10 liters of starter water in multiple independent bladders; a single puncture in this environment is catastrophic. Do not expect to find even frost or adsorbed moisture. The very idea of dew feels like a rumor from a wetter epoch, and it is.

### Geological Instability as the Primary Environmental Hazard

The highland is in continuous, chaotic orogeny. Ridges appear over hours. A level plateau can sprout a knife-edged hogback of black glass hundreds of meters high, still radiating residual heat, its faces unfractured and slick. Circular depressions sink without warning in concentric unweathered basalt steps. These events are frequent, uncoordinated, and mostly silent except for a dry cough of fracturing rock or a subsonic pressure against the inner ear. Distant mantle adjustments travel as bone-felt vibrations.

You cannot map this place in any lasting sense. A slope that took days to climb may have steepened, vanished, or reversed on the return. Time itself is locally inconsistent. Treat every surface as provisional. Movement strategy:

- Travel light and stay low to the ground when possible; a sudden throw of a new fault can launch an upright figure.
- Use a staff or lightweight probe to test surfaces, but expect the probe itself to be swallowed or tilted by later motion.
- Never camp in a depression or against a fresh ridge. Prefer slightly elevated, recently stable-looking benches, knowing they are temporary.
- Carry a compact inertial navigation unit with barometric and seismic backup; GPS or magnetic references will be useless or actively misleading (the standing slabs already demonstrate that alignments do not persist).
- Accept that “return path” is a poetic concept. Plan multiple extraction corridors and be prepared to abandon gear if a new hogback isolates you.

The land is incorporating the few existing artifacts—unmortared walls, misaligned standing slabs, a fused-glass platform. It will try to incorporate you the same way: tilting foundations, encasing edges in new crystal, or simply burying you under a silent upthrust. Do not linger near the artifacts expecting them to offer shelter or meaning. They are brief, abandoned attempts to measure or stand still. The highland has already begun to forget them.

### Life, or Its Absence

There is no familiar life. No plants, no insects, no scavengers. In a few sheltered fractures you may see thin iridescent films of chemotrophic mineral—tarnished bronze or pale sulfur plates that accrete slowly then slough away at the next tremor. They are neither quite biological nor purely geological. Do not touch them without isolation. They are the only “ecology,” and the distinction between pre-life and post-life has not been settled. The psychological effect of this absence is profound. An ordinary human expects background motion, scent, the small sounds of other living things. Here the restlessness is entirely lithic and silent. The impression is of an immense, quiet becoming. Some travelers report a growing sense that the highland is watching them decide whether they belong to the arriving or the departing category.

### Equipment Philosophy: Minimal, Redundant, Disposable

Pack as if every item may be lost to a new ridge or a collapsing ring. Prioritize:

- Full environmental suit (pressure, thermal dual-regime, dust-sealed, anti-static outer layer). Extra seals, patch kits, and a second helmet visor.
- Closed-cycle life support with 72+ hours consumables and chemical backups.
- Water recycler + independent bladders.
- High-calorie, low-residue food that does not require water or cooking. You will not want to open the suit often.
- Seismic and pressure sensors that can give even a few seconds’ warning of nearby events.
- Lightweight, high-traction footwear with replaceable heat-resistant soles.
- Inertial nav, analog compass (even if unreliable), and physical markers you are willing to abandon (they will be tilted or buried anyway).
- Basic medical kit focused on trauma, hypoxia, dehydration, and thermal injury. Evacuation is your only real treatment for anything serious.
- A simple analog notebook and pencil. Digital devices may glitch when local time becomes inconsistent or when dust replays motions. Writing by hand is one of the few actions the highland has not yet learned to undo.

Do not bring anything you cannot afford to lose or that the land might decide to keep. The highland is still writing its grammar; it does not yet have a word for “property.”

### Psychological and Temporal Preparation

This is the hardest part and the one most ordinary humans underestimate. The highland does not feel ancient or ruined. It feels embryonic and unfinished. Everything is either just arriving or just departing. Gravity is reliable; the ground is not. Darkness pools with intention. Dust remembers. Time hiccups. You will experience a quiet, persistent restlessness that is not quite fear and not quite awe. Some describe it as standing inside a sentence that has not yet chosen its verb.

Prepare by accepting transience as the local physics. Do not try to “understand” the place in earthly terms of cause and succession. Observation here is participatory; your presence is another small upthrust the land may choose to keep or erase. Spend time beforehand in sensory-deprivation or high-altitude environments so the silence and thin air do not immediately destabilize you. Practice moving slowly and deliberately. Practice noticing without naming. The highland has not yet decided what most things are; imposing names too quickly can feel like an act of colonization the land rejects by simply rearranging itself.

If you encounter the faint construction marks—the half-swallowed wall, the misaligned slabs, the circular glass platform—treat them as evidence that others tried to pause the becoming and failed. They are not ruins. They are abandoned hypotheses.

### Exit Strategy

Decide in advance the conditions under which you will leave: a set number of local “days” (meaningless but useful as a personal limit), a specific physiological threshold (oxygen remaining, water remaining, first serious tremor that isolates you), or a simple intuitive signal that the highland has noticed you too clearly. Extraction should be treated as another geological event you are inserting. Do not assume the portal or transit method you used to arrive will still align with the same coordinates or the same local time. The highland is assembling itself toward a shape it has not chosen; your departure coordinates may have moved.

You will leave changed in ways that are difficult to describe to people who live on finished worlds. The memory of a place that is still deciding its own rules lingers as a low, bone-felt vibration. Some travelers report that afterward ordinary landscapes feel over-determined, almost decadent in their completeness. Others simply become very still for a while, as if waiting to see whether the ground beneath them will remain.

That is the preparation: become temporarily less wet, less certain, and less attached to surfaces staying where you left them. Then go, witness the quiet restlessness of a highland that does not yet know it is finished, and leave before it finishes you into the next ridge.
