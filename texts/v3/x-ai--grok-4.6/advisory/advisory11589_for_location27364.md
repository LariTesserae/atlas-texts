**Prepare as if you are entering a warm, living machine that has forgotten it was ever built, and that has been raining on itself for geologic time.** The highland is not a destination with an arrival. It is a medium you are already inside the moment the pressure changes in your ears and the heat comes up through whatever you are standing on. An ordinary human body is a dry, visual, circadian animal. This place will try, patiently and without malice, to rewrite that.

**Physiological realities you cannot negotiate away.**  
The air is thin enough that you will notice every climb and every night (there are no nights) of poor sleep, but thick enough that you will not die of it if you move slowly. Acclimatize somewhere high and humid first if you can; otherwise expect weeks of headache, poor recovery, and a constant slight hunger for oxygen that never quite becomes panic. The warmth is geothermal and inescapable. It comes through the soles, through condensation on skin, through the mist that is a second ground. You will sweat in a place that never evaporates sweat. Skin will macerate. Fungal and bacterial overgrowth is not a risk; it is the default state of any uncovered surface, including yours. Pack more antifungals, barrier creams, and drying agents than you think a person can carry. Ears, groin, feet, and any fold will become battlegrounds. The water is everywhere and it is warm as blood. It is also mineral, metallic, faintly organic, and laced with whatever the machines and the seeps have been concentrating for longer than recorded history. Do not drink it untreated. Do not assume your filters will last. Bring redundant chemical, thermal, and mechanical purification, and accept that you will still taste iron and lily and exhaust in everything.

Your eyes are almost useless and will become a liability if you keep trying to use them as primary sensors. There is no ambient light to adapt to. Artificial light will work, but it will also announce you to everything that hunts by sudden contrast or by the heat of a lamp, and it will make the true darkness feel like a wall when you turn it off. Train yourself, before you go, to navigate by sound, by the direction of warmth, by the texture of air against wet skin, and by smell. The soundscape is the map: drip versus run versus fall versus the subsonic shove of a lake changing its mind, the layered industrial ostinato of engines that are never quite in tempo because the distances are too large, the grazing of a thousand small mouths, the patient pause of something heavy on a ridge. Learn to hear the difference between a waterfall and a factory intake, between a chorus of sound-ferns and a predator displacing water. Bring bone-conduction or waterproof directional audio gear if you have it, but practice without it. Your own footfalls may arrive from ahead in certain defiles. That is not a trick. Do not decide it is a trick.

**What you wear and carry must assume permanent immersion.**  
Clothing is a compromise between waterproofing and not becoming a walking sauna that grows its own ecosystem. Layered, quick-draining synthetics with sealed seams, high-necked, with gaiters that actually work in sponge and peat. Multiple pairs of the same thing; everything will be wet within minutes and stay that way. Gloves that let you feel the pulsing mats and the weeping rock without tearing. Eye protection that does not fog into blindness. A wide-brimmed or hooded system that keeps the hanging marsh out of your mouth. Footwear with aggressive, self-cleaning tread for glazed basalt, mineral fluff, and collapsed plant bodies; you will sink. Bring a staff or poles that double as probes for depth and as something to tap so you can hear the return. The ground is not reliable. Ridges slump. Pools are not still. The lowest storey of vegetation is often underwater.

Shelter is not about staying dry; that is impossible. It is about creating a small volume where the mist is slightly less total, where you can hear a change in the water or the engines before it reaches you, and where the larger fauna are slightly less likely to treat you as part of the sponge. The existing structures (pumping houses, huts, factory terraces) are already gardens and nests. They are also still running. Treat any interior as both refuge and potential machinery. A simple bivy or tarp that can be slung between sound-ferns or pylons and that sheds the vertical lake when the pressure drops is more useful than a tent that will rot. Sleep in shifts with someone listening if you are not alone. If you are alone, accept that solitude here is only a way of arranging the crowd.

**Food, energy, and the chemistry of the place.**  
Nothing here is trying to be edible for a photosynthetic animal. The plants taste of iron and sweet rot because that is what they are made of. The fauna are abundant and structurally part of the landscape; some of them are also cables, or flocks that are single decisions, or ridgelines that breathe. Assume toxicity, parasites, and active chemical defenses until proven otherwise, which you will not be able to prove safely. Bring calorie-dense, sealed rations that can survive being wet and warm for months. If you must forage, start with the most processed industrial liquors (the ones that glow as heat on the face) only after extreme caution and never as a staple; they are concentrates. The machines take water in and put hotter, mineral-rich water out. That effluent is as close as this world comes to a tap. Filter it. The vents breathe sulfur and metals. Your lungs and your gear will notice.

Power is actually one of the easier problems if you are willing to be a parasite. The industry never stopped. Cables still sing. Pistons have not stopped in anyone’s count of years. Chimneys keep their own rhythm. Learn, before you go, the crude art of tapping a running industrial system without becoming part of its cycle or being crushed by it. Small induction coils, sealed batteries that hate humidity, mechanical chargers driven by the local ostinato. Do not assume the current is going anywhere useful or that the voltage is kind. The built world is scattered like erratics. A pumping house half-eaten by sponge may still have a roof of sound-ferns and a single enormous piston. A line of pylons may still carry power to a place that no longer exists. Treat every machine as both resource and weather.

**The animals and the amalgamations.**  
They are not decoration. They are how the highland moves mass and heat and information. Things hunt by heat signature and by the shape of displaced water. Things hide by matching the temperature of the stone. Herds follow warmth, seeps, and the sound of particular engines that have become landmarks. Swarms live in the hanging mist and descend on pressure drops. Solitary lives in the deep pools have never seen the ceiling of roots and iron as anything but ceiling. Some shapes will not stay in the category of “animal.” Pale cables in the lakes, a ridgeline that breathes, a nest that includes a turning gear. Do not decide in advance what is safe to approach. Move like something that belongs to the temperature of the rock. Avoid sudden heat sources (your own body is one). Learn the local choruses so you know when a conversation three valleys over has turned into a hunt. Predator and prey here are a weather system. You are weather they have not catalogued.

**Mental and temporal preparation.**  
There is no sun. There has not been one long enough for the land to remember wanting one. Your circadian rhythm will dissolve. Time puddles. You will lose the ability to estimate duration by light or by hunger or by fatigue in the usual way. The loudness never quite becomes noise because it is too structured, too full of overlapping purposes. That structure will start to feel like language. Let it. Fighting it is how people start listening for a sun that is not coming. The darkness is not empty; it is crowded. Solitude is an arrangement of that crowd. The weirdness is not a rupture. It is the long habit of a world that never had to agree with eyes. Shapes persist that light would have corrected. Processes run that a brighter physics would have been ashamed of. You can stand in a narrow defile and hear your own footfalls arriving from ahead. You can put a hand on a weeping wall and feel a pulse that matches no engine and no heart you can name. Do not require the place to become a story with a moral or a people you can find. The marks that remain (a plaque, a cairn already hosting moss-hide, a trench that is now a linear pond full of pale cables) are faint because faintness is what the plateau permits. Everything is being translated into its wet equivalent. The industrial and the biological have been in the same weather so long they use each other’s grammar.

Before you go, spend time in the most lightless, wettest, loudest, geothermally active, industrially ruined environments you can find on more ordinary worlds: deep caves with streams, cooling towers, vent fields, flooded mines, perpetual-rain cloud forests at night. Practice moving, eating, sleeping, and thinking without ever seeing your own hands clearly. Practice not needing a horizon. Practice treating sound as primary and vision as a sometimes-useful specialist sense. Accept that you will smell of the place within hours (iron, lily, exhaust, sweet rot) and that the smell will follow you out.

**What “ordinary” still means.**  
You are not a specialist team and you do not have infinite support. Redundancy in the things that keep you from drowning, rotting, poisoning yourself, or walking off a lip you never saw is more important than sophistication. Tell someone the pressure signature and the dominant engine note of the region you intend to enter, even if that someone is only a note left in another universe. Move slowly enough that the sponge and the pools and the vegetation can be felt rather than surprised. When the mist thickens until walking is swimming, or when a vent-field opens and the air goes sharp and the sound-ferns clap all at once, or when the rains become a vertical lake and the machines take on a higher note, do not try to keep your original plan. The highland does not begin and it does not empty. It continues. Your job is to continue with it for a while without becoming another layer in the organic mattress or another erratic in the industrial conversation.

Stand still when you first arrive. Let the warmth come up. Let the water find your skin. Listen for the next change in the water, the next engine slightly out of true, the next call that means a herd has turned. That is the country. It does not brighten. It does not explain itself. Prepare to be inside it rather than to visit it.
