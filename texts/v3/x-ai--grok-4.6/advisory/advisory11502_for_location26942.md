**Bring more water than you can imagine needing, then double it, and plan to lose most of it anyway.** The Hollow has never heard of humidity. Your first breath will feel like inhaling warm flour and old brass; your second will already be stealing moisture from your lungs, tongue, and the lining of your nose. There is no dew, no groundwater, no hidden seep. Cisterns hold only the same flour-fine grit that hangs in the air. Skin will tighten within minutes. Lips crack. Eyes film over and then sting as they dry. An ordinary human body is a leaky bag of water walking into a place that has spent geological time learning how to take it. You will need sealed, insulated containers—many of them—plus a recapture system if you have access to even modest technology from another world (a simple condensation hood inside a sealed cloak or tent will pay for itself). Drink on a schedule, not when thirsty. Thirst here is a late and untrustworthy signal. Electrolytes matter as much as volume; the air will pull salt from you as readily as water.

Protect every surface that can lose moisture or take on grit. The standing haze never fully settles; it is the atmosphere. Flour-fine mineral dust will find every seam, every blink, every inhale. Full-coverage clothing is non-negotiable: layered, loose enough for air to move when the rare violent gust arrives, tight enough at cuffs and collar that grit does not pour in. Goggles that seal (or a full-face visor) are required; ordinary sunglasses will leave you weeping grit. A high-quality respirator or wrapped multiple-layer filter over mouth and nose—something you can still speak through, because the wind will already be speaking for the whole basin. The plants themselves secrete a glassy varnish; do not assume the dust is chemically inert. Treat it as something that wants to become part of you the same way it becomes part of the fronds.

Footwear must insulate against the ground. Heat does not merely sit in the air; it radiates upward from the packed mineral pan as if the stone still remembers a fire that ended centuries ago. Thin soles will cook your feet. Thick, insulating boots or even improvised overboots of local sinter plates (once you understand how they break) will be kinder. The surface itself is a shallow, ancient spoon-bowl; walking is easy until a thermal gust arrives and turns the grit into a horizontal blast.

Expect the light to be exhausted and the colour of tarnished brass. There is no hard sun, only a filtered gloom that arrives already tired. Colours desaturate. Distances become uncertain. The groves of plants glow faintly from within because their varnish catches what little light exists and returns it as a dull lantern-shine. This is beautiful and disorienting. Your eyes will strain. Bring a reliable headlamp or lantern with extra power, not for darkness (there is none in the usual sense) but for the moments you need to read a map, check a seal, or simply confirm that the thing in front of you is still a wall and not a trick of heat-shimmer.

Sound will be louder than the emptiness should allow. The Hollow is an instrument. Wind finds every gap between the widely spaced structures and the groves and turns them into flutes, organ pipes, and sudden gunshot cracks when rock yields to expansion. Plant fronds clap like dry hands. A falling plate of sinter on the far side of the basin arrives as a delayed report that echoes twice more. There is no background life—no insects, no birds—to soften it. The quiet between gusts is not silence; it is a held breath. Some travelers find this majestic. Others find it starts to speak in almost-voices after a few hours. Earplugs or noise-dampening wraps can be useful if you need to sleep or think; equally useful is simply accepting that the place has its own conversation and you are a guest in it.

The plants are the only lushness and they are not like plants you know. They occupy discrete thickets, rooms of oxidised-copper green separated by wide mineral pavement. They do not drink. New growth is briefly fleshy; older limbs mineralise from the inside out until they are living stone that still photosynthesises and still flexes. A fallen frond will be half-stone by the next thermal cycle. Do not eat them. Their chemistry is a closed, non-aqueous bargain with heat and airborne mineral. They are not food. They may not even be safely touched for long; the varnish and the grit they comb from the air could be abrasive or reactive on human skin. Watch one thicket from the dim of one cycle to the next if you can. The pace is just fast enough to notice. It is one of the few motions the Hollow still practises.

The built structures are a city by spacing and intention, not by crowding. Rammed mineral, stacked sinter plates, occasional darker glassy blocks. Hand-scale, no metal, corners rounded by centuries of grit, walls that lean in ways that were accepted long ago. Doorways hold brittle, musical screens of mineralised fibre. Stairways climb and simply stop. Streets are just the unoccupied ground between compounds—wider than any traffic this place ever needed. Dust dunes form in the lee of walls, migrate a few body-lengths, collapse, and reform. The city and the basin have finished their conversation with erosion. Nothing here is actively falling apart; it is practising the same few motions it has practised for a very long time. You may shelter in a hall or tower. The interiors will be cooler by a few degrees and slightly less gritty. They will also be full of the same dry, roasted-dust tang and the same amplified wind-voices. Do not expect inhabitants. The emptiness is the native density.

At the centre, lowest and hottest, sits the broad stepped platform of darker stone with its shallow channels that lead nowhere. The groves grow thickest around it. The wind is loudest, a standing roar that never quite becomes a voice. Light pools in the channels and for a moment they look like they once flowed. They did not. Approach if you wish; the place keeps its own counsel. Many travelers report that this is where the Hollow feels most like a completed thought. Sit on a step if the heat allows. Listen. Do not pour water into the channels to see what happens. The gesture will feel like vandalism even if no one is watching.

Psychological preparation is as important as the physical. This is a pocket of organised vacancy. Time here is the slow conversion of living tissue into more landscape, the migration of a dune, the thermal crack of a rock face. Your own moisture, your heartbeat, your need to urinate will feel like interruptions. Loneliness is not the right word; the place is not empty of meaning, only of company. Some people experience a deep, ringing calm. Others feel the beginning of a kind of temporal vertigo—the sense that the Hollow finished becoming itself a long time ago and has been rehearsing the same afternoon ever since. Bring something small and ordinary from another world (a notebook that will not disintegrate, a piece of fruit you will eat immediately, a recorded voice) if you need an anchor. Or bring nothing and let the place work on you. Both are valid.

Practical extras an ordinary traveler can carry:
- Spare seals, tape, and cloth for repairing clothing and filters. Grit wins every long contest.
- A simple anemometer or even just strips of cloth to read the gusts; they arrive suddenly and can knock you down or fill your mouth.
- High-calorie, low-moisture food that will not turn to dust in your pack. Eat in shelter.
- Basic medical: saline drops for eyes, barrier ointment for cracked skin, something for heat exhaustion, a way to treat grit in wounds. Infection risk is low (nothing lives here that wants your blood) but abrasion is constant.
- A way to mark your path if you wander far from a structure. The land reads as featureless until you are already inside the lip. Heat-shimmer lies.
- Patience. Everything here operates on a schedule that does not include you.

You will leave drier than you arrived, coated in a fine pale film, ears still ringing with organ-pipe wind, and carrying the faint sweet-stone smell in your clothes for days. The Hollow does not care that you came. That is part of its dignity. Prepare to be a brief, wet anomaly in a place that has already had its say and is content to keep saying it, quietly and at volume, until the grit and the heat and the mineralising groves finish the last small revisions. Visit with respect, with far more water than seems reasonable, and with the understanding that you are walking through a finished sentence that has decided to remain audible.
