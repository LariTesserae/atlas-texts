**Prepare as if you are about to step into a single, functioning organism that happens to remember being a factory.** This is not a ruin overgrown by nature, nor a garden with leftover machinery. It is a closed, self-regulating body the size of a village green whose bones are brick and iron, whose blood is a hybrid of sap and residual industrial water, and whose metabolism still turns a flywheel. An ordinary human body is an intrusion of a different temperature, chemistry, and timescale. The place will notice you immediately through humidity, pressure, and sound; it will not attack, but it will begin incorporating your presence into its existing cycles the moment you cross the cleft. Preparation is therefore about minimizing disruption to yourself and to it, then accepting that some incorporation is inevitable.

**Clothing and skin.** You will be soaked within two minutes and will stay that way. The air is the temperature and wetness of exhaled breath; every surface weeps a continuous film. Wear a single lightweight, quick-drying layer that covers arms and legs—thin merino or a synthetic that does not trap moisture against the skin. Bare skin will bead, then stay wet; prolonged contact with tannin-rich bark, sap-slick iron, and the tea-colored standing water can produce a mild, persistent irritation or staining that lasts days after you leave. Gloves are useful for gripping the ribbed, warm walls of the corridors but must be thin enough that you can still feel the arterial pulse in the former risers. Footwear must handle ankle-deep water, a spongy living mat that gives like a lung, and occasional slick root or tile. Soft-soled, drainable boots or sturdy sandals with good grip; anything that holds water will become heavy and encourage blisters in the constant warmth. A wide-brimmed hat is pointless inside the vault; a thin buff or hood can keep condensation from running into your eyes. Bring a complete dry change sealed in a waterproof bag and leave it at the rim. You will not stay dry, but you will want to stop being wet when you exit.

Expect your own sweat to become indistinguishable from the grove’s. Moisture will not evaporate. Plan for heat stress even though the temperature is only “warm”: high humidity prevents cooling. Hydrate before entry and carry a modest amount of water; do not drink the standing water or the weeping film until you have tested it (it is mineral-rich, tannic, and carries traces of old oil and iron). A small electrolyte supplement is wise. The air itself can feel thick; people prone to respiratory irritation should consider a simple mask that can be removed once acclimated, because the dominant smells—wet tannin, hot oil, sweet rot of leaves that never quite fall—are constant and intimate.

**Navigation and movement.** There are no paths, only a grammar of openings the plants have agreed not to close. The corridors are often shoulder-width or narrower, walls wet and faintly warm, floor a compressed root-mat that remembers every footfall. Move slowly. The mat will take your weight and give it back; sudden steps produce a wet slap that carries through the vault. Use the sound of the engine as your fixed point: it is a patient, cyclic, cushioned industrial note, louder and wetter as you approach the central chamber. Follow the louder water and the more regular drips. The bright seams of sky in the fused canopy are your only reliable “up.” Do not mark the living surfaces with paint, tape, or cuts; the grove reabsorbs fallen tissue within a season and will treat foreign marks as another kind of leaf. If you must leave a trace, use something the place already understands: a small clay vessel, a pebble placed in a weeping groove, a gesture that joins the existing overflow channels. The row of half-barked pots already there shows that the smallest possible participation is accepted.

Carry a compact headlamp or a low, warm light. Daylight through the yellow-green vault is adequate in the main chamber but dims in side passages and becomes gold, then insufficient, toward evening. The light never becomes darkness so much as a thicker green. A simple folding stick or probe helps test depth in standing water and feel for hidden roots or old iron. Do not bring anything with a strong scent, aerosol, or chemical residue; the ecosystem is nearly closed and has little capacity to process novelty.

**Sensory and psychological readiness.** The first three steps down from the rim change the air. After that you are inside a body. Sound has nowhere to go: the engine-note becomes a continuous pressure, drips multiply into a limping clock, and a shearing leaf (twice a day, a long wet-canvas rip) slides rather than falls. There is almost no animal life—one moth, an occasional dimple in the water, rumor. The silence between mechanical and vegetal noises is itself loud. This combination—factory shift-hours performed by photosynthesis—unsettles people who expect either wilderness or ruin. It is neither. Time here is slow and cyclic; the flywheel turns at a pace you can watch. Your own heartbeat may begin to feel too fast or too irregular by comparison. Sit if you need to. The floor will hold you.

Claustrophobia is possible even though the central chamber is church-sized. The passages are the negative space of boles that chose not to fuse completely. If you feel the walls closing, remember they have been this width for decades; the original spacing of columns and the need for the flywheel to turn taught the plants a limit. They house the machine; they have not crushed it. You are walking through that negotiated limit.

The place has a faint industrial memory. Pipes become xylem, belts become something between leather and bark, a pressure gauge still trembles in green wood. Touching a former riser lets you feel the slow shove-pause-shove of water moving through both iron and living tissue. Some travelers report a sympathetic pulse in their own wrists or temples after half an hour. This is not mystical; it is proximity to a large, regular, wet rhythm in a confined, humid space. If it bothers you, move to a side chamber (the old switchgear room, the crawl to the outer face) or simply leave. The effect fades at the rim as abruptly as the sound.

**Respect and offerings.** Almost nothing has been repaired and almost nothing has failed. Do not test that statement. Do not pry at flanges, turn valves, or clear “debris.” Fallen leaves are already being reabsorbed; the engine’s vapor is already being drunk by the nearest fleshy leaves. The balance is old and specific: water wants to stand, the engine-and-wood will not quite let it; light wants to be green, the vault admits just enough white; growth wants to close every gap, the original plan of the works still teaches a limit. Your presence is a temporary gap. Keep it small.

If you feel the need to leave something, follow the existing precedent: a small, unglazed pot or cup set into a living niche so that it weeps, fills, and overflows into a finger-wide channel toward the engine. The wood will eventually grow lips around it. This is not religion; it is joining the cycle with the least possible new material. Do not leave food, plastic, or anything that cannot be slowly metabolized. The grove is a closed account.

**Health, time, and exit.** Insect life is minimal; the usual marsh bites are almost absent inside. The greater risks are slip, mild chemical irritation, disorientation, and the psychological weight of being inside a single occupancy that photosynthesizes and keeps shift-hours. People with mold or tannin sensitivities, or with a history of panic in humid enclosed spaces, should enter only briefly or not at all. There is no rescue infrastructure. The basin is small; if you become truly lost, stop, listen for the engine, and work toward louder water and more regular drips. You will reach the heart, then you can reverse.

You can stay through evening. The light goes gold in the seams, condensation thickens, the engine-note does not change. The air will not cool much. When you decide to leave, retrace to the cleft. The sound cuts off at the rim as if a door closed, though there is no door—only the film of water still running down the brick-and-cambium jambs. You will carry the smell (tannin, warm oil, wet iron) in your clothes and hair for hours. Some travelers report the engine-note lingering in quiet rooms afterward, like a phantom limb of sound. This fades. The staining on skin or fabric may not.

**Practical kit (minimal).**  
- Thin, full-coverage, quick-dry clothing + complete dry change in a sealed bag left at the rim.  
- Drainable footwear with grip.  
- Thin gloves, buff or hood, compact warm light, folding probe or stick.  
- Water and electrolytes; do not rely on the grove.  
- Small unglazed vessel if you intend an offering.  
- Notebook or voice recorder if you want to capture the grammar of openings or the exact cycle of the pulse; electronics will fog and the humidity is unkind to anything not sealed.  
- Basic first-aid for slips, scrapes, and skin irritation. No heavy tools, no open flames, no scents.

Enter in daylight so you can learn the seams and the sound before the gold hour. Tell someone the approximate location of the basin; the copse looks ordinary from a distance. Move as if the floor, the walls, and the air are all one slow animal that has decided, for now, to let you pass through its interior. That is as close as an ordinary human needs to come to understanding the place. The rest it will teach you in the first ten minutes, whether you are ready or not.
