**Atmospheric and chemical realities first, because they will kill you before the geometry or the heat does.**  
This pocket has never known water. The local chemistry runs on sulfur vapor that refuses to condense and a slow, continuous exhalation of carbon monoxide that the plants treat as dew. Ordinary lungs will register the CO as a pleasant drowsiness long before they stop working; the sulfur will etch mucous membranes and then lung tissue. The air also carries ozone and a metallic-copper tang that never quite leaves the back of the throat. An ordinary human therefore needs a fully sealed, positive-pressure environmental system with its own oxygen supply and dedicated scrubbers for both CO and sulfur compounds. Recirculating or “just a good filter” setups are insufficient; the background CO load is chronic, not a spike. Bring at least two independent oxygen sources and a way to test remaining capacity that does not rely on electronics that might glitch when space itself hiccups. Hydration must be entirely self-contained and recycled; there is no local water to purify, and sweating will be continuous because the heat is radiant and directional, rising from the stone.

**Heat management is not about “hot weather” clothing.**  
The stone itself is the furnace. Heat radiates upward through your soles more than it beats down from the sky. The plants store that heat in translucent paddles and dump it again as infrared; they photosynthesize across both visible and IR bands and shed a fine metallic pollen that hangs in shimmering curtains rather than falling. Your boots need thick, insulated, heat-reflective soles—think multiple layers of aerogel or equivalent plus a sacrificial outer skin that can be replaced when the sintered dust and glassy rinds chew it away. The rest of the suit should reject radiant load from below while allowing you to dump metabolic heat; otherwise you cook from the feet up. The pollen will coat everything. It is not described as toxic, but it is metallic and persistent; assume it will foul seals, visors, and any moving parts. Bring extra visor wipes, sealant patches, and a way to shake or blow the stuff off without opening the suit.

**Terrain is first-generation and actively growing.**  
Nothing is rounded. Hexagonal basalt columns are still unpacking from their molds; glassy rinds have not yet flaked; seams of mineral look freshly poured. Fist-sized unopened geodes sit in pale sintered dust. Every surface can cut, and some surfaces lengthen by a finger-width every few hours with a sound like a turbine spinning down. You need cut-resistant, puncture-resistant gloves and gaiters, plus footwear that can handle both glassy slickness and abrasive dust. Climbing or scrambling gear is useful but must be used sparingly: ropes and slings can be pinched or sheared when a column extrudes. Do not camp in a depression that might become a column. The plants occupy discrete, almost architectural thickets and then stop; they do not spread. Treat the empty space between them as load-bearing. Walking through a thicket is possible but slow; the interlocking fronds feel like cooling fins and will scrape a suit. Do not break paddles or fronds. They appear to be both product and tooling.

**Space is slightly non-commutative and the pocket is hidden in the strict sense.**  
You do not approach this place; you notice you are already inside it. Ridgelines fold shut behind you like a lid that was never meant to open. Distances refuse to add the same way twice. A thicket twenty paces away can occupy the same coordinates as the one you just left if you glance aside. Heat haze is sometimes a local failure of occlusion: you see the underside of the sky or the interior of a rock. These events are infrequent and polite; they do not announce themselves. Navigation therefore cannot rely on consistent metrics. Carry multiple independent dead-reckoning methods (mechanical, inertial, optical) and accept that they will disagree. Mark your path with physical tokens that have mass and texture—something the plants and the growing rock are unlikely to absorb or displace. Do not trust a single landmark. Mentally rehearse the possibility that the “exit” you remember is now the interior of a column or occupies the same volume as a thicket you already passed. The glitches recede, leaving ordinary noon a little more ordinary; use that contrast as a calibration. Bring a simple analog timer and a notebook; electronics may flicker when occlusion fails.

**Nothing animal lives here and the only motion is infrastructural.**  
Geological and vegetal processes run on timescales that make a heartbeat feel like a seizure. Once every few hours a column lengthens; a plant unfurls a paddle or seals a heat-scar. The air is mostly quiet except for the sub-audible thrum of convection cells and the occasional crystalline ping of a new facet finishing. This is not silence; it is the sound of a factory whose workers have gone home and left the machines to finish the shift. Expect profound isolation and a slow, almost architectural beauty. The plants keep to themselves; they do not compete. Do not try to “explore” by forcing a path through every thicket. The empty ground between them is itself a necessary organ. Over longer durations the pocket will either finish or fail—the mountain will accept the concavity or reabsorb it. You have no way to know which, and no way to hurry it. Pack supplies for an open-ended stay. Assume you may be inside until the process decides.

**Psychological and observational posture.**  
The industrial character is native grammar, not decoration. Veins of ore rise along invisible templates and stand as unfinished girders. Reality is thin enough that geology and production were never distinguished. An ordinary traveler should therefore arrive already accepting that they are walking through an unfinished room of the world. Bring a notebook or voice recorder that does not depend on consistent spacetime; record the pings, the pollen curtains, the moments when a rock’s interior becomes briefly visible. Do not interfere. Do not try to “help” a plant or “finish” a column. The distinction between visitor and workpiece is also thin. Mentally rehearse patience: everything here is still deciding whether to keep existing. The heat, the copper-ozone smell, the hanging metallic pollen, and the polite spatial glitches will either become meditative or erode sanity; there is little middle ground. If you are the sort of person who needs constant stimulation or a clear escape route, this is the wrong pocket.

**Practical kit summary (minimum).**  
- Sealed suit with independent O₂, CO/sulfur scrubbing, radiant-heat rejection from below, and pollen-tolerant seals.  
- Redundant water recycling and caloric stores for unknown duration.  
- Insulated, replaceable-soled boots; cut-resistant layers; extra visor/seal maintenance.  
- Multiple non-electronic navigation methods plus physical path tokens.  
- Analog timekeeping, notebook, basic geological sampling tools if you must (but do not break living structures).  
- Medical: CO and sulfur exposure protocols, heat-injury treatment, laceration kits, and something for disorientation.  
- No open flames, no unnecessary vibration, no assumption that “out” will remain where you left it.

You will know you have arrived when the ridgelines are already closed and the first column ticks longer while a paddle slowly unfurls. The place is still under construction. Behave as if you are walking through a foundry that happens to be alive and a mountain that has not yet chosen its final shape. Nothing here is in a hurry. Neither should you be.
