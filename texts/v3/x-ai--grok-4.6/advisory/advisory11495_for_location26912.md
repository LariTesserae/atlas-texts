**You will arrive already wet, already half-drowned in a world that has not agreed to finish becoming land.** The first non-water thing is height: a chest-pressure that feels like altitude but reads as shallower dark, a highland still pouring itself into existence the way milk blooms through tea. Everything else is densities of warm water—atmosphere as a second, occupying sea of standing rain, then the true water without a meniscus, then roofs you walk, chambers you drop through, hanging bronze-thighed forests that are architecture and kin and lungs. Light is one long bruise-hour of green-gold dusk plus a sourceless pallor from embryonic stone. Sound is mostly taken. Time has the temperature of blood-warm water. The town is a reef-valley of named passages, weirs, stacked basins, and markets on still-soft new steps. People stand forehead-to-forehead to speak. Rooms can close around a sleeper because the coming-up does not distinguish person from pocket. Air is rationed like bread. Stone struck too hard weeps a numbing, food-spoiling milk. The ecology is paused and exclusive. You are a visitor in a place that remains, and remains, lush and quiet and only beginning.

An ordinary human body—adapted to dry land, carrying sound, sharp day/night, metal, insects, reliable air, and the assumption that stone stays put—will experience this as a slow, intimate, claustrophobic saturation. Skin will never fully dry. Vision will work in a few body-lengths of dim. Voices will arrive wrapped. Every surface will touch you back. Gravity and pressure will feel slightly wrong (thinner dark, unfinished pour-lines). The rock itself is still arriving, courteous and indifferent. Preparation is less about conquering and more about becoming the right density so the place does not simply enclose you as another pocket.

### Body: train for perpetual occupation, not passage
You will wade, swim, walk roofs, drop through windows, push through hanging vegetation, climb unfinished stone that can still take a thumbnail, and do it all in blood-warm water under a rain-sea that never exits. Ordinary swimming fitness is insufficient; you need endurance in low-visibility, plant-obstructed, three-dimensional water architecture with occasional currents (the helix where older and younger waters mix) and sudden density changes. Practice long, slow swims in warm, murky water while carrying a modest load, then treading or hanging while conserving breath. Learn to move by feel and remembered routes rather than sight. Train breath-holding and efficient, quiet breathing—lungs judged wasteful are a social and practical problem when air bells are tended and rationed in deeper weeks. The water is warm enough that hypothermia is not the main threat, but constant maceration is: skin will soften, wrinkle, invite infection, and lose its usual barrier. Desensitize your skin gradually to prolonged wetness; expect fungal and bacterial issues, abrasions from wet wood/stone/moss, and the peculiar taste of iron when you inevitably put tongue to new gray-green growth.

Your inner ear and proprioception will be confused by the lack of clean surfaces, the hanging forests that lift and droop with a barely-felt tide, and the fact that “up” is both height and under. Practice navigation in complete or near-darkness using handholds, density of water, and faint stone-pallor. The coming-up can close a chamber; you must learn to sound rooms (listen, tap gently if at all, feel for the difference between person-shaped pocket and intended space) before sleeping. Those born in the mixings of old and young water are said to know without looking when wall becomes floor; attach yourself to one of them if possible. Do not strike new stone hard. The milk it weeps spoils food and numbs the mouth; treat any contact as a chemical exposure. Cut hanging forests only where bladders have already browned. An eager cut bleeds sweetness that draws the rare fauna—and the rare fauna is not reliably the size you hoped. Something large already leaves grooves in the silt of the lowest street.

Eyes will adapt slowly to the single long hour of bruise-seepage and faint stone glow. No strong shadows; faces are read by wet shine. Bring nothing that depends on bright light or contrast. Night is remembered routes and hand-following-handhold; practice moving that way. Taste will become a primary sense: older water has mineral patience, younger rain-water is restless and almost sweet/empty. Learn the difference on your tongue. It matters for currents, for the people who live next to the coming-up, and for not poisoning yourself.

### Gear and material culture: leave most of Earth behind
Metal is scarce and dulls in a week; a few reddish nodules are hammered into short-lived blades. Do not bring ferrous tools that will rust into uselessness or culturally scream “outsider who does not understand we have almost none.” Bone, tooth, stone-on-stone, hide, living wet-wood grain, and the black vine that holds a knot better than cord are the local register. If you must bring implements, choose ceramics, dense bone, or stone that you are willing to abandon or rework. Waterproofing as Earth understands it is almost meaningless—everything is densities of water—but a few truly dry micro-pockets (sealed against both rain-sea and true water) for medicines, records, or a tiny heirloom of dryness will be wealth. Paper dissolves; electronics fail in the occupying wet and the dim. Learn local memory-and-interval methods (signal-stones knocked three-then-two). Clothing should be more like a second skin that does not trap wrong water: think layered, quick-shedding fibers that locals might recognize as analogous to their own. A modern wetsuit will mark you immediately and may harbor the wrong microbes. Better to arrive willing to wear what they wear and to accept that “dry-enough” is a hammock of woven root beaded with water.

Air management is communal and architectural: inverted basins trap bells of it, tended and rationed. Do not hoard. Do not gasp. Share as you would bread. In the deeper weeks the rain-sea thickens and true water rises; you will live by those bells. Bring no heavy oxygen apparatus unless you have already tested that it does not offend the local physics or the local courtesy—the place has its own thinner sea above and its own rules about whose lungs are wasteful. A simple, quiet rebreather analog might buy time, but long-term you live as they live.

Light sources that pierce the green-gold dusk will be socially loud and possibly useless against the stone’s own pallor. Better to train your eyes and accept the rumor of evening. Any recording device should be analog, waterproof in the local sense, and humble. You will not photograph this place into comprehension; you will leave dark prints in moss that close behind you by what they call evening.

### Food, water, medicine, waste
Almost everything is water, but you still need calories and micronutrients. Locals exchange dried fiber, fish-bone awls, cakes of rendered fat, and the products of plants that have not agreed to be terrestrial. Animals are scarce—one pale eel a month in the market, a not-quite-bird of wet-paper wings, walking shells, the unseen large thing. Do not hunt; wait, mend, and grow what will grow. Taste cautiously: mineral, iron, possible sweetness from wounded plants that draws fauna. Transition your gut slowly. Unknown secondary compounds in the hanging forests, the two ages of water, and the stone-milk are real risks. Bring a modest, well-sealed pharmacopeia focused on infection (skin, respiratory, gut), fungal overgrowth, numbness/counteragent for stone-milk exposure, and electrolytes. Antiparasitics are a guess; the ecology feels paused and exclusive, which might mean fewer vectors or stranger ones. Probiotics from home will be outcompeted or irrelevant. Expect to lose some Earth gut flora and acquire whatever lives here.

Hydration is ambient but not automatic: densities, older vs younger water, and possible mineral loads. Learn to drink the right water in the right place. Waste must be managed so it does not spoil the shared air bells or the hanging gardens that are also lungs. Locals have customs at intersections; follow them exactly. You are adding biomass to a system that is still deciding its own carrying capacity.

### Mind and social density
The quiet is not peace; it is the acoustics of an unfinished world that has not learned to ring. Voices do not carry. You will stand close, forehead to forehead, words arriving wrapped. Children are taught not to shout. This will feel like sensory deprivation plus forced intimacy. Prepare for the psychological weight of dimness, muffled sound, constant touch of plant and water and stone, and the knowledge that the rock might courteously close a curve around your need—or around you while you sleep. Claustrophobia, derealization, and a slow erosion of the sense that you have edges will happen. Practice long periods of low-stimulus closeness with other people. Learn to be still. Nothing here rushes. A doorway is deepened over a decade. A canal becomes a hallway becomes a well. The town migrates vertically by inches. Your Earth sense of schedule will feel like shouting.

Visitors are rare enough that they go quiet in a different way. Do not perform amazement at the unfinished ridge or the rare window into true thin air (people line up to feel their own weight correctly for a few breaths). Do not treat the hanging forests as scenery. They are kin and architecture. Learn the named passages and the customs of intersections. Wealth is dryness, height, and rooms whose walls have hardened enough to carve. A finished corner is an heirloom. Offer usefulness—sounding rooms, mending, tending air, gathering only what has browned—rather than novelty. Those who live next to the coming-up (born in the mixings) are listened to; defer to them. The place will not rush you. If you stay, the rain will occupy you and the stone may offer a lip, then a step, then a room. That courtesy is real and not personal.

### Movement, time, and the unfinished
You measure progress in remembered routes, density changes, and the muffled calendar: the week bronze fronds release spores in a cloud that is also a silence; the month a new ridge shows a seam of reddish metal and the town becomes a line of kneeling tappers; the long season the hanging sea thins to the color of a bruise healing and everyone climbs, dripping, to the unfinished heights to remember that elevation was supposed to mean above (it also means under). Maps drawn on arrival will be lies by the next season. The rock is still arriving. Stand on a high lip of raw geology—pour-lines still visible, almost no erosion—and look across roofs just under the surface, streets that are channels, gardens that are lungs. Both under and above are true at once.

Contingencies: if a room begins to close, get out by the density gradient and the remembered handholds, not by striking stone. If sweetness enters the water, the rare fauna may come; become architecture, not bait. If the rain-sea thickens, live by the air bells and the opinions about wasteful lungs. If you find a window into actual air, do not linger as if you own the civic treasure. If the large unseen thing leaves a new groove, do not follow it.

You cannot pack a version of this place that will keep you entirely yourself. The ordinary human who prepares best arrives willing to be occupied: skin beaded, voice wrapped, time the temperature of the water, shoulder leaving a print in moss that closes, tongue learning two ages of water and the iron of new stone. You will never be dry. You may, if you listen and do not strike too hard, be given a room that was not a room until the embryonic rock, indifferent and strangely courteous, closed a curve around the fact that you needed one. That is as close to hospitality as the coming-up offers. Remain, and remain, and remain—lush, quiet, only beginning—or leave while you still remember what dryness felt like when it was not wealth.
