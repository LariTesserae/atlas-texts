**Attune first to the fact that you are not visiting a place so much as entering a living wound that has decided, provisionally, to be a town.** The hollow is recent, raw, and already knitting shut at the rim. Everything inside is wet, dark, vertical, tactile, continuously growing or being composted, and occupied at every scale from amphipod frost to padded green hare-salamander things that treat human-grown lofts as just another layer of habitat. Light is a rumor; eyes are not trusted. Navigation, work, and even social space happen by temperature gradients, textures, faint thermal veins in living walls, and the lean of a trained staff. The air itself weathers indoors. Plants remember being trained and will try to become ladders or benches again. Property is a conversation the matter can simply refuse. An ordinary human body—sight-dominant, dry-preferring, metal-and-fire accustomed, used to architecture that stays put—will feel like an intrusive organ until it learns otherwise.

### Mindset and Sensory Retraining
Spend weeks beforehand practicing genuine darkness and wetness, not simulated camping. Sit in a flooded basement or tidal cave at night with lights off and no talking. Learn to map a room by the temperature of surfaces under your palms, the give of moss versus the lock of lignified braid, the difference between a cistern-leaf’s cool skin and a warm vascular bundle that means “this way toward people.” Train yourself to treat constant condensation on skin and breath as normal rather than alarming. The quiet is occupancy that has agreed not to speak; your own pulse will sound loud. Accept that the city is also a slow digestive tract and a weather system. Partitions lock overnight. Rooms yellow and collapse into compost. Furnishings grow themselves into the shape of a curled sleeper. You will not control the layout. You are a tenant inside something that is simultaneously building and being built.

Psychological preparation is not “stay calm.” It is relinquishing the expectation that space is inert or that darkness is empty. The moths hang in curtains and never quite still their wings. The amphipods move like living hoarfrost. Larger shapes drink from leaf-cisterns and hunt one another with only a rearrangement of water. You become aware of them the way you become aware of blood. Practice moving so your footsteps leave only temporary white wounds in the sphagnum that refill in an hour. The inhabitants (human or nearly so) already live this way. They will notice if you do not.

### Clothing, Insulation, and Constant Wetness
You will be wet from the first step. Water exists simultaneously as film, drip, standing ankle-depth pool, exhaled mist, and the perpetual weep of cut peat and living walls. Hypothermia and macerated skin are the immediate physical threats, followed by fungal overgrowth and whatever the local microbiome does to an unadapted gut and lungs.

Wear merino or other wool that still insulates when soaked, layered under a highly breathable but fully waterproof shell that you accept will eventually fail at the seams. Multiple thin layers beat one thick one; you will be adjusting constantly as indoor “weather” contracts the vault and sheds fine rain or turns the air to soup. High waterproof boots with aggressive, non-marring tread (the moss records wounds) and gaiters that seal. Gloves that allow fine tactile discrimination—thin wool or specialized wet-work gloves—because you will be feeling everything. A brimmed or close hood that keeps drip out of eyes and ears without trapping too much condensation. Avoid cotton entirely. Bring far more socks than seems reasonable and a system for wringing and rotating them; standing water and compressed sphagnum will soak you from below.

Skin barrier: a thick, unscented ointment or specialized marine-grade balm reapplied often to hands, feet, groin, and any chafing points. The iron-and-chlorophyll smell will get into everything; accept that your own scent will change. Pack a small, dedicated dry bag for a complete change of base layers even if you never get truly dry.

### Light, Navigation, and the Living Staff
Do not rely on bright white light. It is culturally and ecologically discordant; work happens by touch and thermal difference. A dim red or deep amber headlamp (lowest possible setting, used sparingly) plus a backup chemical light or cultivated-fungus analog if you can source something that matches the local phosphene-level glow. Eyes exist but are not trusted; children learn the city as sequences of textures and temperatures. You will be given (or should request) a living staff that leans, very slightly, toward water or the nearest inhabited cluster. Treat it as a prosthetic sense, not a walking stick. Practice following its bias in darkness.

Bring a simple tactile mapping method: waterproof (or plant-fiber) cord you can knot as a memory palace, or a small set of distinct pebbles/thongs whose textures you memorize as waypoints. A notebook will disintegrate; if you must record, use a small slate or wax tablet equivalent, or commit to oral/spatial memory. Compass and electronics will fog, short, or simply feel wrong. The architecture is vertical and sparse—hanging galleries of trained ivy, rooms grown around taproots, great empty dark between clusters. You can stand in the floor-water and hear your pulse. Mark your personal vertical with something that will not damage living structure (a soft fiber tie, never metal).

The entrance itself is closing. The rim is still raw but plants are already colonizing it as scouts. Note the exact configuration of the living lintel of interlaced stems when you descend; it will be smaller and more knitted when you leave. Have an exit plan that does not assume the wound remains the same size or in the same place.

### Health, Microbiome, and Biological Risks
Assume the water is biologically rich: amphipods, hair-thin worms, algal films, possible amphibian-like pathogens, iron, tannins from peat. Do not drink untreated. A compact gravity or squeeze filter plus chemical backup (and a metal or ceramic cup if you can boil without fire—see tools). Even filtered, the taste and mineral load will be unfamiliar. Electrolyte replacement matters in the cold wet.

Respiratory: the mist is constant and the air holds its own weather. A simple buff or light mask can reduce inhalation of spores, pollen analogs, and the fine rain shed when the vault contracts, but you cannot live masked. Expect a period of adjustment—cough, congestion, or simply the feeling that the air is occupied.

Skin and wounds: any cut is immediately wet and exposed to whatever is grazing the walls. Aggressive wound hygiene, waterproof dressings that actually stay on, and a broad-spectrum topical. Tetanus-level preparedness plus whatever fungal/antibacterial coverage you can reasonably carry. The plants themselves are trained by splitting, pegging with thorns, and patient wounding; they forgive some cuts. You may not be forgiven if you hack indiscriminately.

The fauna is total but not described as hostile to humans. Still: dense herds of pale amphipods, moth curtains, larger green shapes on trained runways. They occupy new lofts before intended tenants. Do not evict. They may crawl on you, drink near you, or treat you as landscape. Check clothing and bedding (if any) constantly. Allergies to unknown proteins, chitin, or plant exudates are possible; carry your usual emergency meds plus antihistamine.

Psychological/physiological: the uncanniness of furniture growing to body shape, vines trying to become ladders again, and the sense that the city might be building its builders. Limit still time. Move, or the niche may begin to fit you too well.

### Tools, Fire, Metal, and Making Do
Almost no metal. A few salvaged fish-hooks and one bronze needle treated as relic. Your steel knife, if you bring one, is both useful and potentially transgressive or valuable beyond its utility. Keep it small, sheathed, and offered rather than flourished. Prefer bone, thorn, twisted-fiber cord, and wooden awls if you can fabricate or acquire them. A compact multitool is insurance, not daily kit.

Fire is problematic: everything is wet, living, and the chamber is a single occupied space with hanging habitations. A tiny alcohol or solid-fuel stove for boiling water or emergency warmth, used only in consultation with inhabitants and with extreme care against igniting trained stems or moss. Better to learn their methods—thermal veins, cultivated fungi, body heat shared in grown niches.

Repair kit: fiber, thorn-pegs, something to split and pin shoots if you are invited to help (or must improvise a handhold). The technology is thumbs, inherited sense of which cuts a plant will forgive, and time.

### Food, Waste, and Sustenance
Bring dense, waterproof calories that do not require much cooking and will not spoil in constant damp: nuts, dried meat or equivalent, hard fats, perhaps a small amount of something shareable. Local food—if offered—will be whatever the ecology and the trained plants yield. Taste cautiously; the iron-chlorophyll note is everywhere. Waste must be managed so it does not become just another compost layer in the wrong place. The city already composts its own yellowed rooms in situ via the amphipods. Ask, or follow the inhabitants’ lead. Do not assume “leave no trace” as you understand it; you are inside a metabolism.

### Cultural and Relational Preparation
The builders work by training, binding, and patient wounding. They are respectful tenants inside a house that is also a digestive tract. Animals are not evicted. New growth is accepted or repaired with small tools. You are a visitor on the rare occasions there are any. Move as they do: quietly, with attention to what the plants will forgive, offering help if your hands learn fast enough.

Learn a few tactile or thermal greetings if possible. Speech may be used, but the quiet is structural. Do not fill the empty dark with unnecessary noise. The bronze needle is passed hand to hand; metal has status or scarcity. Your own metal objects should be treated as potentially potent.

Property and space are negotiated with living matter that can refuse. A family of green hare-things in “your” loft is not an infestation. A vine that was a ladder will try to become one again if cut. Some rooms grow benches of fused petiole no one designed. Participate in the conversation rather than imposing geometry.

Children (and you) are taught the map as texture and temperature. Offer to be taught. Accept the living staff. Do not photograph with a flash. Do not collect souvenirs that are still alive.

### Specific Hazards and Temporal Notes
- **The closing wound**: the oval entrance is yesterday’s or last week’s tear. Plants are colonizing the rim. Do not assume you can linger indefinitely and still walk out the same way.
- **Overnight change**: a curtain of stems becomes a wall. Stairs appear where someone scored and weighted a vine. Old rooms are digested. Sleep lightly or in a place the inhabitants indicate is stable for now.
- **Verticality and living structure**: ivy ladders, hanging galleries, compressed sphagnum over white-root webs. Test every hold. The plants are the substrate and they pulse, very slowly, as if the hollow breathes.
- **Indoor weather**: sudden cooling sheds rain from the vault; warming turns air to soup. Hypothermia or heat stress (relative) in the same day.
- **Getting lost**: empty dark between clusters. The staff and thermal/texture literacy are your map. A dead-reckoning error in three dimensions of living, changing architecture is serious.
- **The memory of plants**: they remember training. Your passage may be recorded in growth habit. Move as if the walls will later tell what you did.
- **Psychological absorption**: the longer you stay, the more the distinction between inhabitant and habitat blurs. That may be the point, or it may be how the city builds its builders. Decide in advance how long you intend to remain and how you will know it is time to climb out—while the wound is still an entrance.

Pack light. Everything you carry will be wet, will smell of the place, and may itself begin to be colonized or composted if left still. The ordinary human advantage is adaptability and the ability to choose when to leave. Use it. Descend because the dark is already complete three steps in. Come back out while the rim is still a wound and not yet a memory the marsh has closed. The silence underneath everything will feel like a second ground. You will carry the wet, the iron-chlorophyll, and the sense that architecture can be alive and unwilling long after your clothes have dried.
