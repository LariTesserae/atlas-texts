**Slow down first.** This hollow does not run; it lingers. An ordinary traveler arriving at ordinary human speed will feel immediately out of phase—too loud, too abrupt, too dry. The entire place operates at the tempo of sap, condensation, and habit. Your first preparation is internal: expect to spend the initial hours or day simply matching that tempo. Walk the few minutes it takes to cross the hollow several times without speaking. Listen until the drip, the distant piston knock, and the almost-imperceptible swell of the boles become the background rather than an absence.

**Climate and body.**  
The air is cellar-cool and persistently damp. Temperature never spikes or drops dramatically; it sits in the range where breath is sometimes visible in the morning and clothing stays clammy. Rain arrives in short, decisive bursts and is immediately gathered. Everything else is condensation beading on leaves, bark, machine faces, and the saucers the structures have grown. Pack accordingly:

- Layers of wool, merino, or equivalent that stay warm when wet. A light waterproof shell that breathes; you will spend more time in lingering moisture than in actual rain. Avoid cotton.  
- Sturdy, quiet-soled boots with good grip. The ground is a continuous, springy mat of cork-moss, compressed root, and lichen-thatch. Paths are packed tan fibre, slightly cambered from generations of the same traffic. It is not muddy, but it is never fully dry and it yields underfoot like thick carpet over something alive.  
- Extra socks and a way to dry them (the hearths work). A hat or hood; drips are constant after rain and the high, sparse canopy is more lid than shelter.  
- The air itself can feel heavy. If you have any respiratory sensitivity, bring whatever you normally use. There is little pollen, but the living substrate, resins, plant-oils, and perpetual damp produce their own atmosphere. Midges appear in a faint dust over the wettest court after rain; they are not aggressive, merely present.

**Water and food.**  
There is no running stream. The only open water is a shallow, stone-lined trough of dark overflow and condensate at the lowest point. A pipe enters and leaves it. Treat all local water as you would any unknown source—filter, boil, or chemically treat—unless a resident (if any are present) indicates otherwise. Carry more drinking water than you think you need; the place feels wet but potable volume is limited and communal. Bring calorie-dense, non-perishable food. The mill exists, boilers tick, and sap is drawn, but this is a working settlement scaled to itself, not a market. Do not assume surplus or hospitality until it is offered.

**Health, biosecurity, and the living structures.**  
The buildings are organisms (or grafts so old the distinction no longer matters). Walls are cool, faintly sweating timber. Door-sills are warm from inner life. Copper lines run in the cambium. Hearths are lined with firebrick in wood that has “learned” not to burn. You are walking inside and among a continuous living system that has already negotiated its ecology.  

- Do not introduce foreign microbes, fungi, insects, or seeds if you can help it. Clean and, if possible, sterilize footwear and outer layers before arrival. This is courtesy and self-preservation; an introduced blight or competing lichen could matter here in ways it would not in an ordinary forest.  
- Do not cut, peel, or pry at bark, grafts, or callused window rims. Do not overload any tap. The tapping of the main bole is calibrated—never enough to kill, always enough. Treat every surface as both architecture and skin.  
- Fire is used but contained. Do not build new fires or leave existing ones unattended. The wood has adapted; your habits may not have.  
- The interiors are irregular from long habitation: resin-levelled floors, grain-following stairs, rooms that feel grown rather than built. Watch your head on low wires (they run at human height, dipping in the gaps) and on the organic curves. The dynamo, belts, and small boilers are old, maintained, and quiet, but they are still machinery. Keep clear of moving parts. The sound of a valve easing is like a cup being set down; treat that as a reminder that the system is live.

**Pace, sound, and presence.**  
The quiet is not emptiness. It is a large number of small, agreed-upon processes staying out of one another’s way. Footsteps on fibre, the regular knock of one piston, leaves turning, rain being received and drunk—these are the texture. Speak softly or not at all until you understand the local custom. Sudden noise or hurried movement will feel like a violation even if no one is watching. There are almost no animals. A bird, once, perhaps. Midges. The activity belongs to the buildings, the sap, the steam recaptured, the leaves adjusting a degree when cloud covers the sun. You will feel the sparseness first as air and interval: six or eight great hollows standing far enough apart that each keeps its own micro-weather. Courtyards of packed fibre feel like empty, considered rooms.

If inhabitants are present they will be few. The settlement is old enough that work and weathering are the same process. They may treat the merger of grove and factory as unremarkable local fact. Match their reserve. Do not photograph or sketch interiors without clear permission; the living walls may be considered private in ways stone walls are not. Offer help only if it is the kind of help that fits—maintenance of the existing, modest machinery, perhaps, or simply not being a burden. A small, practical gift that belongs to their material world (good oil, a length of suitable belt, a tool that will last) will be more intelligible than novelty.

**Light, time, and observation.**  
Light is the sky’s light: cool even grey or thin sun falling into the courtyards without drama. At dusk the wires take a modest yellowish current; lamps are ordinary indoor light. Nothing here glows of its own accord. Bring a small, warm-spectrum light of your own for interiors or pre-dawn, but use it sparingly. The place is shaped to receive and use what arrives. Sit in one of the courtyards after rain and watch the surfaces shine, then drink. Notice how the dynamo’s note changes fractionally with the leaves. Feel the sills. The forms are finished; there are no new edges. Brick and iron show in places, both wearing the same pale lichen as the windward bark. The unusual (a town that is grown, engines nested in wood that listens) loses its strangeness once you register that the quiet is full and the walls are slowly, continuously alive.

**What you will actually experience.**  
You will walk a shallow dish of land that never quite became a valley. You will move across a living floor that is also the walls and the few roofs. You will enter halls whose wood is cool and damp, whose rooms have been levelled and lived in for longer than the place has a recorded name. You will hear almost no current, only drip and tick. You will see your breath some mornings. You will feel, after a short time, that the strangeness has receded into custom and that the great number of small processes have simply made room for one more slow-moving body. Leave the same way: quietly, having taken nothing that was not offered, having added nothing that the moss, the mill, and the condensate did not already know how to use.

The hollow does not need you. That is part of its completed quality. Prepare to be a respectful, temporary condensation yourself—present, then gone, having changed the moisture balance as little as possible.
