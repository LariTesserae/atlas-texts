**Arrive already unfinished.** The plateau will not finish you, and it will not let you stay the same. Pack the expectation of edges, of reliable distance, of animals, of a world that still has somewhere left to go. Leave those at whatever door you came through. What you are walking into is a last draft that still sweats.

The first preparation is physiological and unglamorous. This is high country of a kind that has forgotten how to be mountains. The air is thin enough that a shout dies before it becomes useful; your blood will notice before your mind does. Spend weeks, if you can, living at altitude before you cross. Walk slowly on arrival. The cold is not theatrical. It lives inside ordinary daylight the way damp lives in old stone. It will stiffen fingers around a knife, hang breath in indecision, and settle into your joints as a settled fact. Dress in layers you can adjust without thinking: close wool or felt against the skin, a wind-shedding outer that does not rustle loudly (the quiet here is structural; you will hear your own clothes). Local people work in felted fibers from the copper-leaved shrubs and pelt-moss; learn those as soon as you can. Metal is scarce and almost sacred—needles, thin saws, knives resharpened until they are more heirloom than edge. Bring a few small, excellent tools if you must, but do not flash them. They will be noticed. They may be borrowed by the place itself.

Water is a guest. There are no named lakes. There are films under stones, tired rain that darkens pores for an hour, dusk-mist that remembers being a river and then becomes weightless frost. Carry a collapsible vessel and something to scrape condensation. Watch how the cities do it: cisterns whose inner walls grow a cold algae they harvest and dry into cakes. That algae, the lichens, the dark packed leaves of the groves, the ferns that unroll slowly enough to watch—these are the larder. There are no animals. This is not a recently emptied country. There are no tracks that are not a person’s, no insects in the moss, no birds stitching the weather. The lushness has no mouths. You will eat plants that pollinate by weather and grow on a clock missing half its gears. Taste cautiously. Learn from the locals which mosses and cistern-skins are food and which are merely green. Hunger here is slow and honest; starvation is possible on the long walks if you trust the wrong seam of damp. Bring concentrated calories for the first crossings if your arrival method allows it, but plan to wean yourself. The plateau does not do surplus.

Distances are the first thing that frays, and they will take your sense of time with them. A tower you walk toward for three days can become a cairn at your feet; the three days remain in your legs as a debt. A doorway across a square can consume an afternoon, and the square fills with upward rain when you arrive. Streets in older quarters loop without turning. Mason’s marks stay consistent and elegant and then are slightly different each time you read them, as if the stone were still deciding. You cannot map this with ordinary instruments. A compass may or may not agree with itself. Stars are whatever this world is allowed; they will not save you. Prepare instead a practice of attention without ownership. Leave cairns if you must, knowing they will be rearranged into the ground plan of a room, then into nothing, then into a grove that should have taken a century. Walk with the knowledge that you may arrive years later than your legs believe, or earlier, or wearing someone else’s weather. Carry a simple tally—knots on a cord, notches you recut each morning—even if the notches later look older than they should. The debt in the body is more reliable than the sky.

The emptiness between cities is continental. You can leave one at the start of a cold spring and reach the next as the next year’s frost begins, if the plateau does not invent a reason to turn you. A single figure is punctuation the weather can erase. Travel light and redundant. Know how to make a wind-break from stone and what little shrub there is. The groves are kinder: damper air, almost a different climate among the trunks, moss pelts, a slowness of respiration that beads your hair if you sleep there. They are also where the breakdown is greener. Leaves stack in ways that imply a third side to a plane. You put your hand into a thicket and feel wet bark, the idea of wet bark, and the memory of a drought this place has not had in ten thousand years. Some trunks contain private weather—a closet-sized squall, snowing onto moss flowering out of season. Pollen-sheets hang on still afternoons and show you, gold and inaccurate, the mountains that used to be here; they blow apart if you breathe. Do not sleep in a grove the first time without accepting that you will wake with a single condensed image you cannot keep. That is native. It is not a gift you get to catalogue.

The cities themselves are the ground deciding to become vertical. Stone taken from the plateau, stacked and fitted, walls thick against the cold, roofs of slab or turf or living moss. Streets wide so weather can pass through without taking the houses. No one remembers a first city. The masonry is too competent for a beginning and too humble for an empire. Inside: workshops, worn stair-troughs, cisterns, courtyards that invited the lushness in and then failed to keep it merely invited. Vines that flower once a decade in a color that is more temperature than hue. People are few, spaced like the cities, moving with the unhurried competence of those whose tools will not improve and whose world will not surprise them in any ordinary way. They gather. They cut blocks. They repair roofs against a wind that has been practicing the same phrase since before speech. They occupy houses that appear over a winter, fitted, roofed, mortar as old as the rest, empty, unclaimed. No one is surprised for long. Sweep it, light a fire, hang moss to dry. Occupation is a kind of weather.

Learn their patience. Do not expect markets in the usual sense, or news that travels faster than a person. Metal is treasured and small. Work is stone on stone, wood on wood, fiber twisted against the thigh. The loudest ordinary sound is a mallet on a chisel, and even that arrives at the next street as a rumor. Sound itself sometimes forgets its order: a blow from a city you left last month can arrive in a grove as a single soft report, like fruit dropping, though nothing here fruits in any hungry sense. A whole morning of work can vanish into the stone and never have been audible. Watch the workers look at their progress as if the cold had done it. That look is useful. Cultivate it.

Reality here is an old garment. It still holds, mostly. Causes arrive late, or early, or wearing each other’s coats. A chisel left on a wall is gone when you turn; in its place a small fern already has the shape of a tool-mark in its fronds. Builders do not always know whether they are adding a course or uncovering one the plateau had put aside. Cities grow the way talus grows: by a logic that includes intention and does not require it. You will put your palm on a wall and feel it sweating the way living things sweat, and also the way stone sweats in a thaw, and also the way a finished world sweats when it dreams it is not quite done—a thin, intermittent moisture, almost not there, enough. Do not flinch from that. It is the most honest thing the place will offer.

Mentally, prepare to have your sense of plot worn smooth. There is no hunt. There is no farm in rows. There is no iron industry, no wheels that matter, no clang. The surprises are not ordinary. You will stand on a roof at the edge of a settlement and understand the whole arrangement as a last draft: the world wore itself down until only this high, cold, nearly level sentence remained. Water reduced to a dampness that comes and goes. Animals edited out as excess motion. Green where green could insist. Stone arranged into shelter by hands that may or may not still be the original hands. Quiet in which a great many processes continue because stopping was never the same thing as ending. Then the understanding slips, which is also native. The roof is a terrace is a ridge of the old mountain. The city is a stand of rock that learned right angles. The grove is a city that learned leaves. Far out, a weather system is assembling that will take a month to cross the distance and will arrive as rain, or as a brief incorrect season, or as a day in which all the doors open onto the same courtyard, damp, moss-thick, echoing with a mallet that has not been lifted yet.

You can live a life here. The cold will teach your habits. The lush courtyards will feed you if you are patient with their calendar. The distances will keep you from believing you own more than a street. Bring no belief that you will remain a tourist. The plateau has no edges you can reach in a lifetime of walking. That is the first thing it teaches, and it teaches it without speaking. Pack accordingly: warmth, slowness, a willingness to let cause and effect wear each other’s coats, and the knowledge that the finished world is still, in its pores, a little damp. That is enough. That is what there is.
