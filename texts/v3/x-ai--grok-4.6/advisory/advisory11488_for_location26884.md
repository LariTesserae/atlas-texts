**Clothing and body protection come first, because the cold is not weather but a standing condition of the rind and the air.** The plateau is permanently just above or at the edge of freezing, yet the water that weeps from every surface stays viscous and pliant rather than locking into ice. An ordinary human will lose heat rapidly through conduction and evaporation. Wear a fully sealed, multi-layer dry-suit system with high thermal insulation (think expedition-grade vapor-barrier layers plus a robust outer membrane that sheds the slightly sticky film). The outer layer must be puncture-resistant and easily rinsed; the rind and the factories exude a clear fluid that clings and can carry spores or membrane fragments. Add insulated, high-traction overboots with aggressive lugs or short crampons—footing is living leather over unknown substrate, often slick, sometimes sloughing into black water overnight. Gloves need the same sealing and insulation plus dexterity for handling tools; face protection should include a full hood, goggles or visor (the dim gray-green light is even and wet, shadows unreliable), and a filtered respirator or at least a high-quality mask. The air frequently thickens with half-formed tissues, spores, or “uncompleted shapes.” Ordinary lungs will not thank you for inhaling that for hours.

Carry redundant heat sources that work in perpetual damp: chemical warmers, a small sealed catalytic heater, and extra insulating layers you can don without opening the suit. Local heat exists—friction in valve-clusters, brief fevers in bosses, steam from packed herds—but it is patchy and mechanical. Do not rely on it. Your breath will hang and fall as additional dew; plan for constant condensation on every surface, including your own.

**Shelter and camp must be treated as temporary and self-contained.** Nothing on the highland persists as a path or a structure. Impressions are eaten, wept full, or overgrown with film that already feels ancient. A lightweight, fully enclosed tent or bivy with a strong waterproof floor and bathtub walls is essential; stake it into the rind knowing the ground may slough or knit underneath you. Bring a portable platform or wide snowshoe-style base if you intend to stay more than a few hours—thin rind over whatever “still refuses to be eaten” can give way. The tent must handle dripping from above and weeping from below plus sudden metabolic “storms” that fill the air with membranes and jointed fragments. Inside, use a closed sleeping system rated well below the apparent temperature; the cold is the grammar and it does not lift.

A small, sealed stove or flameless ration heater is safer than open flame. Fuel and food must be carried in; do not assume the sap or the weeping fluid is potable or nutritious. It may be metabolically active in ways a human gut cannot process. Bring far more water and calories than a comparable cold trek would require—the body works harder in the constant damp and the psychological load of spatial/temporal unreliability. A compact water filter and chemical treatment kit is still wise for emergency use, but treat the local fluid as suspect.

**Navigation and orientation tools will fail in ordinary ways and in ways unique to this place.** Distances misfire. A factory-organism can remain the same apparent size for an hour of walking and then stand dripping beside you. Sounds arrive before sources or without them. Reflections show skies that are not the sky above. Fauna occupy afterimages. No path lasts. Carry a high-quality GPS or inertial unit if your multiverse tech allows it, plus analog backups (compass, altimeter, paper notes written in waterproof ink). Accept that they may disagree with your senses and with each other. Mark your own position frequently with biodegradable, high-visibility flags or chemical lights that you do not expect to find later. The prudent traveler treats every landmark as provisional and never walks out of visual range of camp without a second person or a tethered beacon.

Record everything: audio, video, written timestamps. The plateau is finished with the courtesy of single locations and single times. Your memories may later feel equally loose. A simple notebook kept in a dry bag is more reliable than electronics that can short in the wet insulation-like dimness.

**Biological and chemical precautions.** The place is a living rind stretched over older material, factories that pulse and clang, herds that graze and heal the surface, sudden blooms that darken the air like weather. You are not toxic to it, but it is not sterile to you. Full-body coverage plus the respirator already mentioned. After any contact, rinse exposed gear. Carry a basic medical kit with extra antibiotics, antifungals, antihistamines, and wound-closure supplies; small cuts will stay wet and the fluid is of unknown composition. Watch for allergic or inflammatory reactions to spores or membranes. Do not eat or drink anything local on a first visit. Do not linger inside valve-clusters or peristaltic ridges even if they look like shelter—the sounds are wet-industrial and the processes endogenous. At the marsh-edges, where the rind thins to a meniscus and things rise for the length of a breath, keep even more distance. Those shapes are neither plant nor animal nor weather.

**Movement and behavior.** Walk slowly. The ground is sparse the way a sea is sparse; you can go an hour seeing only sheen, pale uprights, and the condensing lid. Sound carries: drip as percussion, factories conversing across kilometers, lowing, rustles in the film. Use that. A sudden roar that is half water and half mouths means a bloom—stop, cover up, wait it out. Do not chase herds or flocks; the scale refuses crowds and they vanish like a tide. When a corridor of warmer, clattering air opens, treat it as a rumor that will seal. If a boss appears beside you after you looked away, give it space; it smells of crushed chlorophyll and hot copper and is doing its own work.

You are not meant to be here in the sense that nothing is meant. The plateau has throughputs, not purposes. Leave as small a trace as possible; the rind will erase it anyway, but respect the metabolism. Do not try to map permanently. Do not try to harvest the factories. Observe, record, stay warm and sealed, and be ready to leave the moment the spatial glitches or a slough make continued travel unsafe.

**Psychological and temporal preparation.** This is an old, finished turbulence that has practiced chaos until it became reliable. The light never lifts. The wetness is constant. Reality is slightly optional. An ordinary human will feel the isolation as a physical pressure: the horizon a dull pewter line that is never quite where you left it, the whole high country ticking and sighing like a plant that learned engines and then forgot the teacher. Fatigue, disorientation, and a sense that time is also weeping are likely. Travel with a companion if possible. Keep routines—eat, rest, write, check gear—even when the environment refuses to acknowledge them. Accept that you will not “understand” the place in a cartographic or causal way; you will only pass through its current throughput.

Bring a small personal item that reminds you of a stable elsewhere: a photograph, a familiar scent in a sealed vial, a short recorded voice. The plateau will not care, but you will. When you leave, rinse everything thoroughly and quarantine gear until you know what, if anything, hitched a ride. The living rind is thin, the factories pulse, and the work of remaining-while-changing continues whether you are there or not.

Visiting feels like walking the cooled, dripping interior of a single vast organism that outlasted its own mountains and then its own physics, still manufacturing itself in the dim. Prepare as if for a long, wet, cold crossing of a living factory floor that has no floor and no walls, only throughputs and the sound of something abundant that you hear more than you meet. Stay sealed, stay oriented to your own body, and do not expect the highland to remember you after you go.
