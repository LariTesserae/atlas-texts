The place is a closed sentence spoken by granite, meltwater, and one species of giant freshwater plant that has spent centuries learning how to be architecture. An ordinary human arriving from another universe will first feel the cold as a philosophical statement: the water sits at the exact temperature where skin stops reporting itself as a boundary. Motion becomes expensive. Thought becomes slower and more three-dimensional. Everything you have ever practiced about haste, about metal tools, about dry rooms and separate days, will be gently but absolutely refused.

Prepare your body as if you were training for a monastery that happens to be flooded.

Begin months earlier with repeated cold-water immersion until the gasp reflex is a memory you can choose to ignore. Practice remaining still for twenty minutes at a time while your core temperature drops a degree; the inhabitants move with exactly that economy. Learn to hover rather than swim. The plants occupy the basin the way a few large thoughts occupy a quiet mind; there are long sight-lines of empty green-gold water between the towers. Thrashing is both rude and thermodynamically stupid. Freediving capacity of three to four minutes is the minimum courtesy. The air-bells exist, small trained pockets of breath that ring faintly when current crosses their skin, but they are not tourist amenities. You will be shown them after you have demonstrated you will not panic and waste the air of the house.

Do not bring metal. Not a knife, not a zipper, not a coin. The prohibition is not superstitious; iron simply has no grammar here. The oldest stems will answer a knuckle-tap with a note that is neither wood nor bone; the growers use that note to locate hairline fractures and to call across the open water when the waterfall is too loud for voices. Metal interferes with the conversation. Arrive naked or in a single layer of untreated plant fiber if you must have something against your skin. Everything else you need—cordage that sings when drawn taut, bone awls, granite flakes, needles of fish-spine—already grows or walks through the rooms.

Learn the sound first, before you ever see the place. The waterfall is a continuous white concussion that becomes, below, a thunder you feel in the hinge of the jaw. The plants add a vast slow creaking, a rubbery boom when two mature blades slap. Over that: the wet-iron chorus of frogs conducted down the stems like speech in a pipe, the glittering weather of shrimp under every eave, the ticking of diving beetles, the neat insulting piles of fish-heads left by the otter-things that use the lowest rooms as thoroughfares. There is no elsewhere for noise to go. If you cannot sleep inside a single overlapping conversation that never pauses, you will not last the first night.

Understand that you are entering a finished argument. The lake level has not changed enough to matter in living memory. A dark mineral band on the granite coincides exactly with the highest roofs. The city replaces itself the way a forest does, one bound joint at a time. Do not offer to “improve” anything. Do not count the dwellings (you will finish with fingers left). Do not ask why they have not expanded. The settlement is the city and the city is still growing, very slowly, in place. Your presence is a weather event they have not scheduled.

Children here learn to swim before they learn the names of the walls. Burial is a matter of weighting a body and walking it to the talus at the deep end where light thins and the char go still. You will be expected to understand this without commentary. Once a year a thin plate of ice forms in the hardest weeks and then, without drama, lets go. If you are present for that, you will watch from the crowns with everyone else. It is the only time most of them surface.

Certain older stems, struck in the right place, return the mountain’s answer. The growers do not decorate this fact. They use it. If you are invited to tap, tap exactly where indicated and then listen with the whole side of your skull. That is as close as the place comes to explanation.

You will harvest inner pith and dry it in the air-bells. You will feel the slow bruise of evening pull down the western wall until the entire basin is the color of old bottle glass. You will see a dropped awl on the living floor from the surface, a dark comma in the gold. You will move through rooms whose windows are simply places where the growers stopped asking the plant to close. After a few days the constant thunder will stop being sound and become the ground you stand on. After a few weeks you will notice that your own thoughts have begun to occupy the basin the way the plants do: few, large, and swaying half a degree out of true.

Leave the same way you arrived: by forgetting to stop being careful. The place will not miss you, and that is the highest compliment it knows how to pay.
