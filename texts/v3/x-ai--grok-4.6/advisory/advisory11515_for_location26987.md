The cirque does not announce itself as a destination. It simply continues, the way a held breath continues. An ordinary human arriving from any other world will first have to accept that the usual categories—day, weather, wilderness, village, up, down—have already been used up here and set aside. Preparation is therefore less a matter of packing against hazards than of thinning yourself enough to occupy a space that is already full.

Begin with the body, because the body will be the first thing the place notices.

The air is thin enough that unacclimatized lungs will produce a private weather of their own: a metallic taste, a pulse behind the eyes, a tendency for thoughts to arrive already slightly used. Spend whatever time the traveler’s method of arrival allows at comparable elevations beforehand; if none is available, expect the first hours inside the bowl to be spent sitting very still on a wet stone while the blood learns the new arithmetic. There is a faint, constant warmth that seems to live in the granite itself, but it does not reach the lungs. Bring a simple pulse oximeter if one still functions after the crossing; more useful is the habit of moving as the goats move—slow, precise, never committing the full weight until the next hoof-hold has been tested. Altitude sickness here will not feel dramatic. It will feel like a failure of courtesy.

Light is the second and more treacherous element. It has no source that can be pointed at without also pointing at every reflecting surface, including the traveler’s own hands and the wet wool of a collar. There is no shade, only degrees of dazzle. Snow-blindness can arrive in minutes; the eyes water, then stop watering, then simply refuse. Glacier glasses with leather side-shields are the minimum. Better is a second, darker pair worn over the first when the hanging ice above decides to throw the entire sky back at once. A broad-brimmed hat is useless; the light comes from below as much as above. Instead, a simple cloth wrap that can be pulled across the face, leaving only a slit, the way the inhabitants themselves sometimes do when the glare shifts. Skin, even the backs of the hands, will redden and then go strangely numb. Cover everything. The wetness never quite freezes on exposed flesh, but it never dries either; it simply stays, a cold film that the light then uses as another mirror.

Clothing must be thought of as a second, removable hide rather than as gear. Modern synthetics will wet through and stay wet; they will also make small, unwelcome sounds. The local solution is layers of felt beaten from the goats’ winter undercoat, greasy enough to shed the constant film of water, quiet enough that a sleeve brushing stone produces almost nothing. An ordinary traveler cannot arrive already wearing this, but can arrive with the materials to approximate it: densely woven wool, untreated leather, a quantity of lanolin or equivalent grease, and the willingness to sit for hours in a lee-plaza beating and greasing until the cloth begins to smell like the rest of the hamlet. Waterproof membranes are a last resort and will be regarded as slightly rude, like wearing a mirror. Footwear is simpler and harder: stiff-soled boots with aggressive lugs, plus a pair of light crampons that can be strapped on or removed in seconds. The granite is always weeping; a film of ice forms, is shattered by a hoof, and knits again before the fragments have stopped sliding. Ordinary hiking soles will betray the wearer within ten paces. The locals go mostly barefoot or in hide slippers indoors and on the roofs; the traveler should not attempt this until the feet have grown their own calluses of rime.

The hamlet itself is a circulatory system. There are no streets, only intervals between bodies—stone, hide, fur, feather. Two laden people cannot pass without turning sideways; something is always coming the other way. Prepare to move the way blood moves through a crowded organ: yielding, never stopping, never accelerating. A traveler who plants both feet and waits to be acknowledged will become an obstruction, and obstructions here are quietly dismantled. Learn the rhythm before entering the stacked rooms. Goats use the wall-tops as roads; foxes pour through the cracks at the hours when the light changes quality (there is no other clock). A polite pause is expected—the fox waits for the woman with the hide-bucket, she waits for the fox. Do not speak unless spoken to, and then keep the voice in the chest. The air is too thin to waste on volume; snow and rime eat what little sound escapes. The loudest permitted noise is the regular, small click of someone restacking a course of stones the seep has undermined since the last time it was restacked. Offer to help with this. It is the closest thing the place has to a greeting.

Food and water require a decision that cannot be postponed. The tarn and every standing film contain shrimp the color of skimmed milk. Children scoop them with bone ladles and eat them raw. The shrimp are also in the drinking water; they are, in a real sense, the point of the drinking water. An ordinary human gut will need time. Bring a small, reliable filter or a supply of purification tablets for the first days, but understand that using them marks the traveler as temporary. The alternative is to begin with tiny quantities and accept a period of internal weather. There is almost no other flora worth eating. The brass-colored grass is too sparse; the dwarf willows are more root than leaf. The inhabitants keep an ice-house that is more cave than building; what they store there is whatever could not be eaten today, which is very little. Do not expect hospitality in the form of a shared meal. Expect instead to be handed a ladle and left to decide.

The unnamed colonial organisms in the warmest seeps should be left unnamed and untouched. They have the look of breathing lichen with a pulse. A hand placed on the wrong wall will come away with a cold suction and a sensation that something has briefly considered the traveler as substrate. Wash only in the designated cisterns, and even then quickly. The entire bowl is a single damp membrane; hygiene as ordinarily practiced is a form of argument the place will win.

Sleep is the most disorienting practical problem. There is no night. The light never really changes; it only shifts from one kind of enamel brightness to another. The inhabitants rest in short, overlapping intervals, often sitting upright in the lee of a wall with a goat or two using them as additional insulation. An ordinary traveler will need an eye covering—something opaque, preferably of the same felt—and the discipline to ignore the fact that the body has no idea whether it is tired. Melatonin or equivalent can help the first several cycles. After that the place itself seems to impose a different rhythm, closer to the slow collapse and reformation of the ice-plates on the tarn. Dreams, if they occur, will be full of upward-falling water and the sound of pages turning in another room.

Mentally, the preparation is simpler and more absolute. The cirque is finished. It does not argue with gravity anymore; it only weeps. Time of day is a habit imported from below and soon dropped. Water climbs the inner faces of cisterns as often as it falls. Beads travel the undersides of lintels and drop upward into the rime-beards, as if the mountain were inhaling. The oldest rooms, down against bedrock, have tool-marks no living hand can match. The present people maintain. They do not claim to have built. They speak of the bowl the way one speaks of a relative who has always been old. A traveler who arrives with the intention of seeing, recording, or understanding will find the intention itself becoming another body in the press—another goat on a wall-top, another moth lifting and settling, another pair of hands restacking stones. The agreement among the inhabitants, the animals, and the ice is older than any of them. It is possible to visit. It is not possible to remain a visitor.

Pack almost nothing that cannot be left behind or absorbed. A small, sharp stone tool of one’s own making is useful and respectful. A quantity of salt, if the crossing permits it, is the only trade good that seems to surprise them. Everything else—electronics, notebooks, the idea of a return ticket—will either fail in the wet or become another piece of glacial cobble used as a lintel. The place is too bright to see clearly, too quiet to interrupt, too full to enter without becoming, for a while, another pulse in its long, established shudder.

That is the preparation: arrive already a little used, a little quieter, a little more willing to be packed into the remaining space. The cirque will do the rest. It always has.
