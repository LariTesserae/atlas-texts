The soak has no surface analog that will fully prepare you. It is not a cave, not a mine, not a foundry, not a swamp, though it borrows the worst and strangest qualities of each. It is a wound two hundred feet down that has begun to cicatrize with iron, brick, and accidental life. An ordinary body arriving unready will last perhaps two hours before the heat and the wet and the moving walls start to unmake its assumptions. Preparation is therefore less about packing than about consenting, in advance, to become slightly less ordinary.

Begin with the body. The heat is not the dry bite of a desert or the dry hammer of a furnace. It is a clenched, wet patience that lives in the rock itself and rises the moment the chamber was opened. You will sweat before you have finished stepping off the last ladder or hoist. Your lungs will feel lined with warm felt. Cloth will glue itself to skin within minutes and stay glued. Train for this. Spend weeks beforehand in the hottest, wettest rooms you can find—saunas run until the benches themselves weep, tropical greenhouses at midday, the space above industrial boilers if you can talk your way in. Drink until your urine is pale and then drink more; practice swallowing electrolytes when you are already nauseated from heat. Sleep in a damp room with a space heater until the sensation of never being dry becomes merely annoying rather than alarming. Your skin will crack and then adapt, or it will not; better to discover which before you are two hundred feet down with no easy dark to retreat into.

The air carries more than heat. It is thick with the mineral breath of dissolving limestone, the sweet-rot of beetle castings, a faint metallic tang of rust that never stops blooming, and the greasy exhale of engines that were designed for a drier world. There will be limestone flour still settling from last season’s blasts, microscopic chitin from the beetle storms, and whatever gases the deep aquifer decides to share. If you have any history of asthma or reactive airways, the soak will find it. Even if you do not, expect a low-grade tightness, a sense that each breath is slightly more work than it should be. A simple cloth over the mouth helps almost not at all; the moisture saturates it instantly. Bring a small supply of properly rated respirators anyway, and accept that you will wear them only in the worst beetle-lifts or when the dynamo is coughing soot. Most of the time you will simply breathe the chamber and let it breathe you.

Clothing must fail gracefully. Forget anything that claims to be waterproof in the usual sense; impermeable fabric becomes a personal sauna and then a second skin of condensed misery. Choose the lightest, fastest-drying weaves you own—linen if you have it, modern synthetics if your home universe allows, multiple complete changes sealed in dry-bags that themselves will eventually weep from the outside. The local men work in whatever they wore when they came down; you will see the cloth darken and stay dark. Boots must grip wet iron plate and slick, tool-scored limestone equally. Leather will rot in days unless heavily treated and even then it will grow a white bloom of mineral. Rubber or modern composites last longer. High enough to keep the worst of the skating beetles out of your socks, though they will find the gaps anyway. They do not bite. They simply arrive, investigate the salt of you, and move on. You will feel them as a continuous light rain of living punctuation on every surface, including the ones you thought were private.

You cannot sleep on the stone. The heat will pull water through your skin while you lie still; the beetles will come for the residue. The locals know this without writing it down. Bring something that lifts you—an ultralight cot, a hammock you can hang from the walkway rails or the unpointed brick if the men allow it, even a simple folding frame with mesh. Fine netting helps against the crickets and the smaller beetles but will not stop the determined ones; they treat mesh as an interesting texture. The floor itself is never more than an inch or two of water except in the pans, but the pans steam and the air above them is a different climate. Choose your sleeping site with the same care you would choose a campsite on a tidal flat: high enough, away from the obvious drainage lines that are still deciding themselves, not under the highest unshored span where the bats hang in their trembling pelt.

Light is rationed and unreliable. The four frosted cages of incandescent bulbs are what the dynamo can spare after the pumps; between their overlapping circles the chamber is the color of someone holding their breath. Shadows have weight and the beetles use that weight. Bring your own lights—several, waterproof, redundant, with more batteries or fuel than seems reasonable. A headlamp leaves the hands free to keep balance on ringing plates or to brush living sheets off a wall you meant to lean against. Understand that any new light will lift the beetles in those brief, soft storms that sound like dry rice poured on tin. The locals have learned to move their lanterns slowly. You should too. When the dynamo stutters the whole chamber inhales and the frogs start their pulsing song as if they had been waiting for permission. In that half-dark you will understand why no one sleeps easily.

Water is everywhere and cannot be trusted. The seeps are hot, mineral, and have already passed through rock that was never meant to be a room, through beetle silt, through rust, through the thin film of whatever the aquifer itself carries. The pans collect it, steam it, and concentrate everything that lives in it. Bring a serious filter or chemical treatment and use it even on water that looks clear. Better: bring more water than you think you can carry and treat resupply as a luxury rather than a plan. Electrolyte replacement is not optional; the heat will take salts faster than thirst will warn you. The local water, once treated, tastes of warm limestone and faint iron. You will come to recognize the particular mineral signature of this chamber the way you recognize a city’s tap.

Food must be sealed against a species that has just inherited a continent. The beetles will find grain, they will find anything sweet or starchy, and they will find each other when those run short. Meat left unwrapped is an invitation and a violation of the only rule that seems to carry moral weight. Use hard-sided containers the beetles cannot chew through. Eat over a cloth you can shake out. Do not leave crumbs; the silt they produce is already becoming a kind of soil in the lowest corner and you do not want to accelerate that process with your own waste. The men have a store-hut. You are a guest in a place that is still deciding whether it will be a town or a hole. Do not make their bookkeeping harder.

There is something in the unfinished north adit. Claws, or stone settling—no one has gone far enough to adjudicate. You will not go far either, and you will not go alone. That is not cowardice; it is literacy. The chamber has only just been born. The adit is still a throat. Whatever lives there (or whatever the rock is doing there) has not yet been invited into the new room’s ecology. A strong light, a companion, and the decision to turn around after twenty paces are sufficient. If you hear it move, you will already be leaving.

The engines are geological compared with everything else. The beam engine nods with the slow arrogance of something that expects to outlast its oilers. Learn its rhythm the first hour you are there. A change in that nod is the first warning that the seep is winning. The compressor breathes irregularly. The dynamo’s belts slap. None of it is loud the way a surface mill is loud; the chamber swallows sound and returns it as pressure. You will feel the work of the pumps in your teeth when the surface rain finally arrives, days late, and the seeps thicken. That delayed weather is the only season. You cannot prepare for it except by accepting that the rock overhead is still in conversation with the ridge and you are standing in the middle of the sentence.

Socially you are an interruption in a place that has no name and barely has streets. The eight structures sit on their slag pads like furniture in a room slightly too large. There are no written rules because the place is younger than paperwork. Watch the men. They move at the tempo of the rock and the water, not the tempo of clocks. A six-hour shift is the limit before the heat cooks the judgment out of a person. Offer labor if it is wanted—greasing, brick-carrying, screen-clearing—and accept that your help may be politely ignored. They have already learned the chamber’s current moods; you have not. Do not sleep in their bunks without asking. Do not shine lights into the highest spans where the bats hang. Do not try to count the beetles. Counting is a kind of surrender they have already refused.

Medical realities are prosaic and therefore dangerous. Heat exhaustion arrives quietly and can become heat stroke while you are still congratulating yourself on acclimation. Fungal infections love perpetual wet cloth against skin. Cuts rust and then infect; the iron is everywhere and the water is not clean. Bring a kit weighted toward rehydration, antifungal cream, broad-spectrum antibiotics if your home universe’s medicine allows it, and something for the low-grade eye irritation that comes from perpetual condensation and mineral aerosol. Tetanus prophylaxis should already be current. There are pale frogs whose origin no one knows and bats whose droppings hiss on the hot floor; assume both can carry whatever cave fauna usually carry and act accordingly. The crickets are only sound. The beetles are only themselves. The larger sound in the adit is the one variable you cannot pack for.

Psychologically the soak is a long held breath. There is no true night and no true day; dusk is a rumor that arrives down the chimney with the bats. Time becomes the interval between the beam engine’s nods, the interval between beetle-lifts, the slow migration of a water-sheet to a new path on a wall that was dry yesterday. The unfinished quality is not charming. You can see the birth in the unpointed mortar, the walkways that do not yet meet, the chimney that has no hood, the soil that has never held a root. In twenty years this may be a town or a flooded memory. Tonight it is a hot, dim, loud pocket of organized iron and accidental life learning how to be a place at all. Some travelers find that knowledge exhilarating. Others find it like standing inside a sentence that has not decided its verb. Know which you are before you descend.

When you leave—and you should have an exit plan that does not depend on the hoist still working or the adit remaining empty—the soak will come with you for a while. You will keep expecting walls to move. You will listen for a change in a rhythm that is no longer there. Your skin will feel too dry in ordinary air and then, a day later, you will realize you can hear individual drops again. That is the ordinary human residue of having visited a room that is still being born. Pack accordingly. Pack light enough to climb out when the seep wins or the dynamo dies. Pack thoroughly enough that the chamber does not have to teach you its first lessons the hard way. The miners who broke into it last season still call it *down*, as if everything else were merely the corridor. They are not wrong. Prepare as though you are walking into the interior of the world, because that is what you are doing.
