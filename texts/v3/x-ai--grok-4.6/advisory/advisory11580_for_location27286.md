**Prepare as if you are entering a place that will try, very patiently, to make you part of its occupancy.**

This highland is not hostile in the usual sense. It will not hunt you. It will not even notice you in any hurry. It simply continues the work it has always done: lifting, beading, claiming every surface, slowing every motion until the difference between living and lingering disappears. An ordinary human body is loud, warm, dry-seeking, time-urgent. The place is none of those things. Your preparation is therefore half equipment and half a deliberate surrender of haste.

### Understand the physics and the feel before you go
The rock is still arriving. Fresh hairline cracks open across a single season; by autumn they already hold the first film of green. You may feel a residual pressure underfoot—too slow for a tremor, more like standing on something that has not yet decided it is finished becoming a mountain. Distances lie. A chimney that looks close enough to touch may take half a day; a fern at your boot can feel farther than the next scarp. Sound arrives late or not at all. Your own footsteps will be the loudest event for miles and the moss will eat them within minutes. Light is a hard, weighty brightness that is never hot; every droplet becomes a lens and the valleys flare. You will walk with narrowed eyes against glitter from below as much as from above. The air tastes of wet stone and a faint metallic note, as if the plants and the old pipes have agreed on a single chemistry. Everything is always slightly wet. Condensation is the true weather. You will be damp within an hour and stay that way.

There is almost no fauna you can count on. Once in a long while something small displaces moss and is gone. Treat any movement as rumor. The industrial remnants are still, in their way, alive: some pipes carry a slow charge you feel more than hear, a stack remains warm to the palm, pistons advance the width of a hand in a season. Moss grows into the gear-teeth and is patiently sheared. The plants appear to anticipate the machines, thickening where warmth will come, thinning where a future shadow will fall. Time here is a thick medium. Cycles complete themselves without witnesses.

You will not “conquer” this place. You will occupy a little of it for a while, then it will occupy you back.

### Body and conditioning
Train for prolonged wet hiking on unstable, sinking, and sharply fractured ground. Practice moving through thigh-deep, water-logged moss without panicking; it behaves more like saturated sponge over hidden rock or voids than like ordinary bog. Build ankle and knee resilience for sudden slips on film-thin succulents and liverworts. Cardiovascular endurance matters more than speed—days will be long, progress slow, and every step costs more than it should because the ground gives. Cold-tolerance training helps; the green is cool to the touch and the air never warms. If you are prone to altitude effects, assume a mid-to-high elevation with thinner, cleaner air and possible lingering geological exhalation. Practice long periods of near-total silence so the hush does not become oppressive. The psychological load is real: repeating valleys, compressed space, and the sense that the landscape is thinking the same thought over and over can induce a gentle, persistent unmooring.

Have a thorough medical baseline: tetanus, any plant-allergy screening you can manage, and awareness of how your body handles prolonged damp (skin, joints, respiration). Bring any personal medications in triple waterproofing; the moisture finds everything.

### Clothing and personal kit — accept that you will be wet
- **Base and mid layers**: Synthetic or merino that stay warm when soaked. Avoid cotton. Multiple thin layers rather than one heavy garment; you will be adding and shedding as condensation and movement demand. A light insulating layer that still works wet.
- **Outer shell**: Fully waterproof but highly breathable hard-shell jacket and trousers with pit zips and full-length side zips. You will generate heat while climbing scarps yet never dry. A wide-brimmed or peaked hat with a neck cape keeps drip off your face and neck; the glitter is relentless.
- **Footwear**: High, stiff-soled waterproof boots with aggressive, non-clogging lugs. Pair with waterproof gaiters that seal at the knee or higher. Even then, expect thigh-deep moss to overflow them. Carry a second pair of lighter camp shoes that can tolerate being wrung out. Socks: multiple merino or synthetic pairs; change often even if they never fully dry. Consider thin liner socks plus heavier ones.
- **Hands**: Thin liner gloves plus waterproof over-mitts. The rock is sharp, the plants glossy and slick, the pipes sometimes warm or faintly live. You will need both grip and protection.
- **Eyes**: High-quality polarized sunglasses with side shields or wrap-around fit, plus a spare pair. The brightness is continuous and the micro-lenses of every droplet make ordinary glare worse. A brimmed hat helps but is not enough. Consider a light neck gaiter you can pull up against wind-driven mist.
- **Skin**: Barrier cream or ointment for prolonged wet exposure (think trench-foot prevention). Lip balm, and something for the faint metallic taste if it bothers you. The plants are cool and always slightly wet; some may leave oils or films.
- **Pack**: Fully waterproof liner plus a rain cover. Everything inside goes in dry-bags. Weight will feel heavier because you are always damp and the terrain is forgiving in the worst way.

You will never be dry. Plan for it rather than fighting it. A small pack-towel or shammy that you wring out repeatedly is more useful than hoping for sun.

### Movement, navigation, and not becoming a rumor
The moss will swallow your tracks. Distances deceive. Valleys repeat. Carry:
- A reliable compass and at least two methods of dead-reckoning (pacing, time, prominent industrial remnants as unique-ish landmarks). Electronic GPS if your traveler’s physics allow it, but treat it as secondary; the compressed distances and possible slow geological motion may confuse it.
- Bright, non-biodegradable trail markers you are willing to leave (the lichen will eventually overwrite them anyway). Flagging tape in high-visibility colors, or small reflective tags.
- Trekking poles with large baskets. They are essential for probing moss depth, testing hidden drops, and keeping you upright when the ground suddenly gives to the thigh.
- A lightweight but sturdy shelter that can be pitched on irregular, wet, sloping ground. The existing corrugated huts have become gardens; their roofs may not be load-bearing and their interiors will be damp and occupied. You may sleep better in your own tent or tarp on a slightly less-saturated terrace.
- Headlamp plus a secondary light. Dusk and dawn are when the highland beads most intensely; visibility drops even though the sky remains bright.
- A simple, loud signaling device (whistle, small air horn). Sound does not travel well, but if another traveler is ever near, it is better than shouting into moss.

Move slowly. Test every step in deep moss. Avoid walking directly on fresh rock slides or along the very edges of scarps—the stone is still showing its grain and edges remain unblunted. When you encounter pipes or machinery, treat them as live until proven otherwise. A slow charge can still startle or burn. Do not put limbs near gears or belts; the increment is seasonal, but you do not want to be the thing that is incrementally sheared. The warm stack is a useful heat source if you need to dry a layer, but do not camp against it—whatever residual process keeps it warm may not be finished.

If you feel the residual lift, simply note it. It is too slow to throw you, but it can un-settle your sense of “down.”

### Sustenance, water, and the chemistry of the place
Water is everywhere and nowhere useful at first glance. Condensation, dew, and the cups the plants have learned to make will give you more than enough liquid, but it carries the metallic-wet-stone taste and whatever the old extraction work left behind. Filter and treat everything. A good pump or gravity filter plus chemical backup (or a UV pen if you can keep it dry and charged). Boiling is possible but fuel-heavy in this damp. Collect from higher, cleaner-looking condensation sheets rather than from the lowest, most occupied moss.

Food: calorie-dense, compact, and able to tolerate damp. The plants store oils against dry weeks; some may be edible or useful, but you have no local knowledge and the place does not offer itself quickly. Do not assume. Carry more than you think you need; the slow terrain and the mental drag of silence and repetition increase consumption. A small stove that works in wet conditions (or a reliable chemical heater) for morale as much as calories. Hot liquid cuts the metallic taste and the cool green.

Watch for the oils. If you harvest anything, they may be flammable or irritating. The industrial remnants suggest someone once extracted them, or the minerals, or the water itself. You are not equipped to resume that work.

### Health, injury, and the long quiet
First-aid kit weighted toward:
- Blisters, trench foot, and prolonged-wet skin issues.
- Lacerations from sharp pale stone.
- Possible plant reactions or unknown small fauna (the “suggestion of a body”).
- Hypothermia prevention despite the lack of true cold.
- Eye irritation from constant glitter and possible UV at elevation.
- Minor burns or shocks from residual machinery.

A lightweight SAM splint or equivalent; a fall into a hanging valley or a hidden drop under moss is possible. Pain management and a plan for self-extraction, because no one is coming. Satellite communicator or emergency beacon if your traveler’s kit includes one that functions here. Isolation is near-total.

Mentally: the silence is not empty. It is designed, or has become the design. Some people find it profoundly peaceful. Others begin to hear the absence as pressure. Prepare both possibilities. A small notebook in a dry-bag (pencil, not pen) can help externalize the repeating-valley feeling. Simple rituals—making tea, checking the same three industrial remnants each day—give the thick time a human scale. Do not expect the place to answer. It is not waiting for you.

### What to bring that is not strictly survival
- A small, sharp knife or multi-tool. Useful for cutting the occasional vine-like fern, sampling (carefully), or marking.
- Lightweight binoculars. Distances compress; you will want to verify what is actually near.
- A simple thermometer and perhaps a small geologist’s hammer or hand lens if you are curious about the still-arriving rock and the film of green that appears in new cracks. Do not hammer anything structural or live.
- Extra batteries or a way to generate a trickle of power; some pipes still carry charge, but do not assume you can tap it safely.
- Something personal that reminds you of ordinary human time: a watch that ticks audibly (even if you rarely hear it), a photograph, a piece of music you can play once at low volume just to remember what sound used to be. The place will try to make you forget haste. A little resistance is healthy.

Leave nothing that the moss and lichen cannot eventually overwrite, unless you intend a mark. Initials in lintels are already being claimed by geometry-loving lichen. Your presence will be temporary occupancy, like everything else.

### On arrival and while you are there
Give yourself a full day of very short movements after you arrive. Let your eyes adjust to the glitter and the false nearness. Listen until you understand that the wind has been combed to nothing. Test the moss in several places so you know how it yields. Touch the warm stack, the beaded pipes, the cool green. Taste the air. Feel whether the residual lift is imagination or not.

Walk the repeating valleys knowing they are both the same and incrementally sharper, greener, more thoroughly claimed. You are the loudest, driest, most time-urgent thing for a long way. The place will not rush to absorb you, but it will not stop either. When you leave, your footsteps will already be gone. The glitter will close. Another bright scarp will wait, another dense filling of the space between stones, another quiet house of engines doing work no one is late for.

That is the visit. Prepare to be changed by the slowness more than by any single hazard. An ordinary human who has spent time here often returns a little quieter, a little more willing to let surfaces be occupied, and permanently slightly damp in memory.
