The place will take you by saturation first. Not drama, not danger in the usual sense—just the unblinking fact that water has seniority here and you do not. An ordinary body arriving from anywhere drier will spend the first hours simply noticing that the air itself is a cool film, that exhalation turns visible at once, that every surface you touch comes away dark and clean and unwilling to give the moisture back. Preparation is less about conquering the city than about arranging yourself so that you can remain inside its long afternoon without becoming another stain.

Begin with the body. The altitude is real even if the horizon refuses to behave like a horizon; expect thinner air, slower recovery, and a persistent cool that never quite becomes cold enough to justify the word “winter.” Train for weeks of loaded walking on wet, rounded, slightly greasy stone: stair after stair, narrow pinched alleys, sudden open courts that turn into ankle-deep sheets by afternoon, then empty again. Practice moving when every foothold is a negotiation with moss, algae, or the faint mineral polish left by centuries of seep. Your knees and ankles will do more work than your lungs. Add balance drills on slick surfaces and the habit of testing every lintel, every truncated tower stair, every lip above a cistern before you commit weight. The structure is finished and load-bearing, but “finished” includes the rubble already rearranged by rain into new, lower thresholds. You will fall. Make sure the fall is only embarrassing.

Clothing must accept that you will never be dry and then refuse to let that fact become hypothermia or trench foot. Wool or equivalent next to skin—it still insulates when wet. A mid-layer that does the same. An outer shell that is genuinely waterproof yet breathable enough that you do not stew in your own vapor; the ambient humidity is already high, and the almost-rain never quite decides to stop. Redundant socks in waterproof sacks, changed religiously, feet powdered or treated against fungus. Gloves that keep dexterity. A hood or broad brim that keeps the film off your glasses or your eyes. Gaiters. The stone is dark and the light is often a low milk; high-visibility accents on your pack or shoulders will not look foolish when you need to pick your own silhouette out of a thousand wet rectangles at dusk. Everything that must stay dry—sleep system, food, maps you draw, any electronics—lives in nested dry bags. Assume the outer bag will fail. Assume the inner one will be tested.

Water is everywhere and almost certainly drinkable after simple filtration or chemical treatment; it arrives as rain or as the city’s own slow sweat, tea-colored from minerals and old lichen, cellar-cold. Still treat it. The cisterns, the open tanks, the threads overflowing from roof-cut basins, the seep you can press from a wall—any of them can carry silt, pollen blown from somewhere impossibly far, or the faint organic memory of whatever still lives in the standing pools. Carry a filter that will not clog on fine gray sediment and a backup chemical or UV method that works when everything is already wet. You will not lack volume. You may lack warmth. A small stove and enough fuel for hot drinks and the occasional cooked meal become psychological infrastructure as much as caloric. There is almost no burnable vegetation; the few wind-combed shrubs are too scarce and too small to count, and the city itself offers only stone. Plan your fuel as carefully as your food.

Food you carry in. The flora is an afterthought the water permits: moss, algae, one permitted shrub with waxed leaves. Nothing suggests a reliable calorie source, and the scarce animal life is shy, small, and not worth the energy of pursuit. Calculate for the longest plausible stay plus a safety margin, then accept that the margin is the only thing standing between you and a hungry walk back to whatever door you came through. High-density, wet-proof packaging. Variety enough that the sameness of the stone does not become the sameness of every meal. Cache nothing on a first visit; you will not find the same court twice unless you are far more skilled than an ordinary traveler has any right to be.

Shelter is the city’s one generosity and its most intimate lie. There are rooms without number, courts inside courts, stacked platforms, vaults that boom faintly when you cross them. Almost all of them are damp. Seek the rare cornice that still throws a little rain-shadow, the interior chamber whose later overlay of construction has left a pocket of stiller air, the high room in a truncated tower where the wind has somewhere else to go. Sleep off the stone if you can—pad, inflatable, whatever keeps the cold from conducting straight into you. A bivy or lightweight tarp can turn a leaking alcove into something survivable. At night the wet surfaces keep a little of the last light and give it back; you can walk by the gleam alone, but you cannot sleep by it. Carry a light that will not drown. Carry a way to mark your sleeping place so you can find it again when every wet wall looks like every other.

Navigation is the problem that will humble you. The grain is too consistent, the scale too continental, the weather too local. Districts change climate without changing grammar. Avenues run a day’s walk and then dog-leg for reasons no one living remembers. You will need a compass that still works, a watch, the discipline to dead-reckon, and the humility to treat every tower as a possible last high place. Record what you can: the direction of persistent run-off, the character of the next weather neighborhood (mist versus true falling rain, ice in northern shade versus lagoons in the flatter quarters), the rare shrub, the one bird the color of wet stone. Do not trust memory. The city erases distinction the way water erases chalk. Decide in advance what “enough” looks like—three days, ten, a month—and build turn-around points into the plan. Getting lost here is not a scene; it is a new climate.

Health is mostly the slow accumulation of damp. Hypothermia is the obvious threat; the quieter ones are skin breakdown, respiratory irritation from constant cool humidity, and the mental erosion that comes from a world whose only remaining occupation is hydrologic. Pack antifungals, blister care, a broad-spectrum antibiotic, whatever personal medications you cannot do without, and more than you think you need of whatever keeps your mood from matching the stone. Isolation will be nearly total. Sound is a luxury the height and the wet both refuse; you will hear your coat, the tick of grit under your heel, the scheduled drip from the same eave that has dripped for years. The emptiness is load-bearing. Some travelers will find it a relief. Others will feel the city using their own heartbeat as the only irregularity left. Prepare for both. A small notebook that stays dry, a ritual (tea at the same hour, a sketch of the day’s watermark), something to read that is not the walls—these are not luxuries.

You will want to climb. The towers are there, thousands of them, most only a few stories, a few loftier, all of them open to the sky. From any of them you will see not an edge but continuation: roofs like wet slate, lightwells like throats, the pale rim of weather. Do it early, do it often, and come down before the afternoon sheet forms on the plaza below. The views will not orient you so much as confirm the fact that keeps arriving: it goes on. That confirmation is worth the wet stone under your hands.

Move as the water moves—slowly, using the old courses, noticing where the city still aims at a gate that is now only two worn jambs and more city. Do not try to stay dry. Try to stay warm enough, fed enough, and oriented enough that the long afternoon can work on you instead of against you. The place is not trying to be strange. It is only very large, very finished, and unable, at last, to get dry. An ordinary human who arrives prepared to be ordinary inside that fact will leave with the taste of cool film still on the tongue and the quiet conviction that some landscapes are simply climates that learned, once, how to be streets.
