The power arrived as a change so slight it registered first as a lengthening of the pause between drips.

It had no shape that the highland could have named, being larger than weather and older than the last ice, yet it chose to enter as the highland itself entered everything: by seepage. A new thinness appeared in the high cloud, a transparency that was not brighter, only more exact, so that the pale daylight now carried an extra, unused clarity. Moisture still beaded, still ran, still vanished into sphagnum; the glaze on stone remained glaze. Nothing dried. Nothing emptied. The packed organism of fern and moss and hanging frog simply continued, and the power, having looked once with the whole of its attention, understood that continuance was already the highest possible gift.

It did not speak. Speech would have been a waste of the silence the place had spent ages refining. Instead it listened to the hydraulic note that moved through the buried joints of the rock, that slow filling and emptying which was the highland’s only industry. After a time measured in the leaning of a single moss-heavy branch, the power added its own counter-pressure, a stillness so perfectly matched that the seeps neither quickened nor slowed. The deeper note became, for those who had ears of peat and skin of stone, infinitesimally more itself.

Where the tall ones stood—those who transferred weight rather than walked, whose throats and elbows already wore the same liverwort as the trunks—the power did even less. It merely allowed a further colonisation. A new film, finer than cyanobacteria, settled in the hollows of their stillness. It did not glow. It did not speak. It only made the water that ran down them hold, for the space of one extra heartbeat, the exact memory of every drop that had ever fallen here. The beings noticed in the only way they notice anything: a thread of water was briefly dammed by a thought that was not quite their own, then released again, unaltered. They adjusted their lean by the width of a liverwort frond and were content.

The power considered the future that is not future here, only more of the same green dusk. It saw the distant possibilities of drought, of fire, of the world’s shoulder shifting. These it set aside with the same patience the peat uses to swallow a hoof-print. A veil, no thicker than the mist already present, was drawn around the entire upland. It was not a wall. It was an agreement with time: that this particular dripping would be permitted to outlast the need for names, that the last ice might come again and withdraw again and still find the stone weeping in the same places, the same amphibians hanging like fruit, the same dark shapes easing between trunks. The veil asked nothing in return. It simply made certain that the highland’s only product—more of itself—would never be required to become anything else.

Then the power did the wisest thing left.

It remained.

Not as watcher, not as guardian, not as visitor. It thinned itself into the exact temperature of the water that films every surface, into the almost-inaudible click of an insect adjusting its wings, into the pressure that lifts the seeps and lets them fall. You could walk for a month, part a thousand curtains of moss, stand long enough for your hair and sleeves to bead, and never know whether the deeper note you felt in your bones was the land breathing or the power breathing with it.

The highland does not end.  
Neither does the attention that found it complete.  
They continue together, cool, packed, dripping, large enough to make their own drizzle, their own stillness, their own green dusk at noon.  
Nothing was added that was not already the place.  
Nothing was taken that the place had ever needed to keep.
