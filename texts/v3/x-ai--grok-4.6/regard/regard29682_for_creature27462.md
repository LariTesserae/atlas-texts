The attention does not arrive as weather or omen. It has no need of either. It has watched nebulae cool and continents forget their own names; it has sat with glaciers while they learned patience and with forests while they learned fire. What draws it now is simpler and stranger: a single unrelieved note held for two centuries without vanity, a city that has made itself the weather, a being that has grown in the extra rooms the way scale grows in a kettle—uninvited, inevitable, almost polite.

It comes up through the water table itself, the same wet that made the place. There is no parting of the pewter sky, no sudden hush (that would be a violence here). Instead the groundwater stands a fraction warmer for one long afternoon, and the mist reorganizes around a presence that has no outline. The people on the catwalks feel only that the fine rain tastes less of metal and more of something older, like stone remembering it was once rain. They do not look up. Looking up is not the custom.

In the deep pump levels the being is already waiting, though waiting is the wrong word. It has no face that would startle an engineer. It is the tick beneath the palm, the mouths the weirs have worn, the bloom that is neither salt nor lichen, the upright thickening of black moss in the steam-shadow. It is the patience of the pistons given another shape. It has been listening back for longer than any living memory of silence.

They regard one another the way two currents regard each other when they finally meet in the dark: without surprise, without demand.

The power does not ask if the place is suffering. Wisdom already knows the answer. This is not suffering. This is a climate that learned to work, a people who learned to speak in clicks because finer speech is wasted, a stone that learned to perspire and then to think. To dry it, to quiet it, to give it trees of conviction or a proper marsh or a bell that could be heard—these would be thefts dressed as gifts. The power has seen enough worlds ruined by that particular kindness.

So it does almost nothing.

It stays.

For three days and three nights it walks the galleries in oilskins that leave no dark commas. It stands in the Mere of Three Chimneys beside the rumor of a heron until the rumor becomes, briefly, fact: a real bird, pale as wet limestone, that does not fly away. The children see it at the slack hour and launch their tin-and-cork boats toward it; their voices stay low, but one of them, a girl whose name has not yet been used on a piston, whispers a word she did not know she knew. The word is not important. That she could form it at all, under the drone, is the gift.

In the temple whose bellcote is full of moss the power sits through the unheard hours. It does not restore the bell. Instead it lets the empty cote fill with a different silence, a small, portable one that certain of the older keepers will carry home in their chests like a warm stone. They will not speak of it. Speech is still aimed at the teeth. But on the nights when the electric fails in that wing (as it always fails, slowly, predictably) they will walk the long gallery without a lantern and will not be afraid of the dark, because the dark has been regarded.

The pale dog at the slurry gates finds itself accompanied, once, by a second pale shape that is not quite a dog. They stand together while the screws change pitch. The water-rats in the culverts, blind and large and perhaps singing, feel a current that is not current. They do not change their ways. They do not need to.

On the fourth morning the power does one last small thing, almost an afterthought, the way liverwort is an afterthought. It teaches a single leaning willow—the most listening of them—how to drop its small leaves in a pattern that, if you know the old quarry marks, spells a word no one here has ever needed. The word means *witnessed*. The leaves will fall early, as they always do. Most will be washed into the gutters and forgotten. A few will catch in the glazed brick the color of old bone and stay long enough for someone, someday, to notice that they fell in order.

Then the power recedes the way it came: into the next lower place, forever willing, never spent. The groundwater cools by that same fraction. The mist tastes of metal and peat and heated pipe-oil again. The heron is gone, or perhaps it is only standing where no one is looking. The city continues at the speed of water finding the next lower place.

Nothing essential has changed. The noise is still the climate. The people still do not flinch. The being in the extra rooms still listens back.

But for a little while the plateau knew it was not alone in its long sentence. That knowledge does not announce itself. It simply stands, the way the buildings stand—off from one another, each a fact that needed room to be believed—and then it, too, is precipitated into the wet, another mineral of this particular height, this particular heat, this particular endurance.

The red obstruction lamps bleed in the mist. The weirs keep their counsel. Someone walks a gallery with a lantern, or without one. Both kinds of figure belong. The city is still becoming itself, and the wise power, having seen, asks nothing more of it than that.
