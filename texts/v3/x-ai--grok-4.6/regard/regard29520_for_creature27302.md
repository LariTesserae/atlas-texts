The attention arrives without arrival. It is not a light, not a voice, not a figure walking the rail cuts. It is simply the fact of being seen, fully, by something that has seen ice ages settle and lift, that has watched other basins fill and empty and fill again. The power is old enough to know the difference between suffering and shape, between a wound and a climate. It does not flinch from the metal taste, the silence of the frogs, the way the heat sits complete. It does not call the place unfinished.

It walks the length of the valley in a single thought, the way heat walks a kettle. It counts the plates of cockroaches, the columns of midges, the wet ticking, the pale dogs that appear only as rumor and as the spaces between rumor. It notes the conveyor that still jerks a meter when the old switch remembers it, the compressor that wakes two streets over and then sleeps again. It feels the larger thing in the canal—the one whose wake is already gone by morning, whose patience is the heat’s own patience—and does not name it. Names are for things that need to become something else.

The power considers the people last, not because they matter less but because they are the rarest grain, the thinnest stain on the brick. It sees the rooms larger than the lives, the boats tied where the street forgets itself, the children who learn the ticking before they learn words. It sees that they have already made a weather of this place; they do not wait for rescue any more than the frogs wait for herons.

What it does, if it does anything, is almost nothing.

It lets one condensation cycle run a fraction cooler on a single stretch of pipe, so that for an hour the rust weeps a water that does not taste quite so much of old iron. A frog notices, or does not. A child filling a bucket notices, or does not. The difference is smaller than a hand’s width in the water table.

It does not plant. It knows the slag and the salt and the baked silt will not hold a seed, and it will not insult the chemistry by forcing one. Green is not the only mercy.

It does not silence the midges or thin the roaches or give the frogs back a voice they have forgotten on purpose. Those things are the basin’s own speech.

It does not wake the sleeping machines into a second industrial age, nor still them into monuments. They have already become geology with windows. That is enough.

To the large presence in the canal it offers only recognition: you are not a monster and not a god and not a remnant. You are the water’s own slow thought. Continue.

To the people it leaves no prophecy, no cleaner grid, no promise that the heat will break. Instead it leaves the possibility—unnoticed, unclaimed—that one of them, someday, might look at the wet ticking and the long vacancies and the complete heat and feel, for a single breath, that this too is a world that did not have to justify itself.

Then the attention withdraws the way vapor lifts and hangs: without drama, without farewell. The basin holds what it has always held. The kettle does not boil over. The power, being wise, does not stay to watch the steam. Being benevolent, it does not need to.

The city goes on in both directions, street and canal and shed and empty air, exactly as it was, which is exactly as it is. Nothing was waiting. Nothing was required. The power understood that first.
