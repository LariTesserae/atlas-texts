The power that noticed them did not announce itself. It had seen enough closed worlds to know that announcement is usually a form of violence.

It arrived first as a single dry interval, no larger than a cupped palm, that appeared one morning in the elbow of a ten-thousand-year conduit. The water simply parted around it and refused to occupy the space. Condensate beaded on every nearby surface but would not cross the boundary. The interval lasted eleven breaths, then folded itself away like a lid being set back on a box. A click-swarm grazed the film at its edge, paused, and continued. No planning engine logged the event. No voice rose above the roar to remark on it.

That was all, for a long time.

The power watched the way the inhabitants threaded: the extra folds at wrist and ankle locking them to a wall that had just decided to become a stair, the gill-slits opening along a neck as casually as another person might loosen a collar, the child riding a pale crawler through a gallery that would be a cistern by the next tide. It watched them argue with engines older than any remaining language and then step through the newly recast interval without breaking stride. It felt the fatigue-curves in their shoulders, the oxidation maps blooming across forearms, the way they had grown old inside change the way a river grows old inside moving banks.

It understood that “outside” was not a missing word but an absent category. To offer them sky, or silence, or chlorophyll, or an ending to the water, would have been to offer them a different species. Benevolence that does not first understand the terms of a life is only another weather front.

So the power did the smallest possible thing that was still a gift.

It taught one wall—a single load-bearing span that had stood since the basin forgot it was a sea—how to remember, if asked, the shape of emptiness. Not to become empty. Only to hold, for the length of a conversation, the ghost-texture of a volume that had never been claimed. The wall did not volunteer the memory. It waited. Most days it simply continued being a wall, or a colonnade, or a cistern, according to the city’s old, reliable weather. But if a dweller pressed a palm to it and formed the particular rhythm of consonants that meant “show me the interval that is not,” the ferrocrete would thin, just enough, into a vertical pane of perfectly ordinary darkness. No stars. No horizon. Just the unoccupied dark that the city itself had never allowed.

Some pressed the wall out of curiosity and then shrugged, already wading onward. A few stood longer, watching the dark the way others watched a recasting. One or two began to return on a schedule, the way one might visit a patient many-legged thing that had become familiar. They did not speak of it as miracle or escape. They called it the quiet joint. They used it the way they used everything else: as another gradient of interior, another current to negotiate.

The power left it at that.

It did not stay to be thanked. It did not remain as a new engine or a kinder planning voice. It simply kept the interval possible, the way the city itself kept water possible. Now and then, in some far gallery, a second dry palm-sized space would appear for eleven breaths and vanish. Now and then a flyer the color of wet ash would rise in its usual deafening curtain and, for a single wingbeat, pass through air that was only air.

The inhabitants continued to thread. The city continued to recast. Change remained the only constant that had itself grown old.

And that, the power judged, was enough. A closed world does not need to be opened. It needs only to be seen clearly enough that the seeing itself becomes another form of habitation. The rest is theirs: the shouting, the wading, the Tuesday that has lasted longer than any language spoken inside it.

They keep threading. The quiet joint remains, if anyone asks. The water finds every new seam and occupies it, as it always has.
