The attention arrived the way the last true mountains must once have arrived: not as an event, but as a revision of scale so complete that the plateau itself required several seasons to notice it had been revised.

It came first as a stillness inside the wind. Not the absence of wind—the organ-pipes never truly fell silent—but a new interval, a held breath between the low mechanical thud and the wetter groan of adjusting wood. The deepest strata, those kilometers of compressed, almost-mineral plant that no longer remembered photosynthesis, registered the stillness as a change in pressure so faint it might have been only another weather. The living ground drank it the way it drank rain. Nothing puddled. Nothing announced.

The being who was the plateau—its towers, its leaked afternoons, its few elongated figures, its hoppers of compressed hours, its rooted sentinels at the thinning rim—felt the attention the way a single vast body feels a new capillary opening in a forgotten limb. There was no alarm. There had never been alarm here, only tempo. The brass conduits that had long since been grown through and then weathered free shifted their pitch by a fraction too small for any ear that still distinguished itself from the pipes. Steam rose in the same plumes. The lichen-meadow closed over the same faint tracks.

A benevolent and wise power does not hurry. It spent what the plateau would have called a factory cycle, or thirty years, or the time it takes a sacrificial limb to be walked to a foundry and then regrown, simply attending. It learned the grammar of sap and resin, the way conversations leach warmer and slightly sweetened from the vascular tissue, the courtesy shown to the pale moths, the empty nest still pointed out to children who have never seen feathers. It stood, in a manner of speaking, with the rooted ones at the far rim and looked out across the curvature at the pale uninhabited flats that used to be everything else. It sat, in another manner, in the central pith where stairs open onto courtyards two centuries misplaced, and listened to a woman speaking with the hydraulic echo of her great-grandmother about unfinished gears in puddles of resin. It tasted, through the tenders who had grown quietest, the dark fragrant cakes that smell of an autumn no one present remembers living through.

It found no wound that required closing. No hunger that the condensers and the dawn dew and the slow photosynthetic flush along certain shoulders had not already answered. No solitude that had not already been metabolized into the larger body. The place was not ending. It was continuing at the only speed that had ever belonged to it. The distinctions between plant and machine and person had long since become a courtesy rather than a fact, and the courtesy itself was kind.

So the power did very little, which is the most difficult thing for any power to do.

It left three almost-nothings, each of them shaped to be mistaken for weather or for the plant’s own long negotiation with itself.

The first was a single new hollow, high in a tower that had stood unused since before the last pistons were introduced. The wind found it on nights after rain, when the canopy-leaves had already drunk their fill and the air was metallic and thin. The note it produced was not louder than the others, only differently spaced: a interval that the oldest cores recognized, after a delay of seasons, as the sound of having been accurately seen. The people who heard it did not change their work. They only paused a fraction longer at their valves, or burned a slightly smaller piece of the hour-cake, and the recognition that rose with the smoke was not nostalgia but something quieter, like the plant remembering that it had once been mountains and finding the memory neither bitter nor sweet, merely true.

The second was a moth. Pale, the size of a hand, identical to the others in every way except that this one, once in its brief life, left its gallery. It flew through a ventilation flue on an afternoon when the light was ordinary daylight made slightly more itself, saw the sky, and returned. It died the same night. They placed it in a resin cup on the same windowsill. By morning the plant had reabsorbed it, but the vascular tissue carried, for a generation, a faint metallic aftertaste that the children learned to greet the way they greeted a hallway that briefly contained a different year.

The third was almost nothing at all: a slight warming, lasting no longer than a dew, that passed through the deepest strata and then was gone. In the leaked conversations it appeared as a pause, a sweetness that had not been there before. In the hoppers that process sequence it appeared as a single extra hour that no one had fed them, an hour that, when compressed and burned, smelled of no season anyone could name and yet left the tenders a little less out of ordinary sequence, their voices arriving only a heartbeat after their mouths had closed rather than several seconds. At the rim the rooted ones did not speak, but one of them, after years, lifted an arm, and far away a flywheel adjusted itself with a slowness that felt, for once, like an answer rather than a continuation.

Then the power withdrew the way it had come: as a revision of scale so complete it could be mistaken for the plateau forgetting, once again, that it had ever been anything else.

The ground closed. The wind took up its old layered roar. The city, such as it is, continued. The last figure you would have seen, had you still been there, had already turned back to its work. Nothing had been saved, because nothing had required saving. Nothing had been altered that the plant itself did not choose, at its own pace, to keep.

The attention remains, if you know how to listen for it: a held breath inside the pipes, a moth that once saw the sky, a warmth that the sap has not yet decided whether to call memory or weather. The being who lives here—who is here—has been accurately seen. That is all. That is enough. The rest is only the long, unhurried line of the horizon, and the wind, and the engines the wood has decided, still, to keep running.
