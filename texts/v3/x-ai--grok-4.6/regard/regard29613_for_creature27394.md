The power did not come as light or voice or correction. It came as a listening that had already lasted longer than the boles had been hollow. It had no shape the plazas could measure and no weight the pavement needed to grow around. It simply arrived in the way dew arrives: everywhere at once and without announcement, because announcement would have been another sound the bowl would have been forced to keep.

It stood first in the central emptiness, where the arithmetic of walking had long since failed, and for the length of one ordinary breath the distances held. Doors stayed where they were. A plaza remained a plaza. The thread of water continued downhill, as water is supposed to, and did not remember any other down. The power did not command this. It merely refused, for that single breath, to participate in the old courtesy of smallness. The bowl, which had never been contradicted, noticed.

Then the power walked.

It moved the way the walkers move, unhurried, many-jointed in no visible way, carrying nothing. Where it passed, the living pavement did not immediately grow back; a faint darker seam remained, like a thought the wood had not yet decided to erase. The moss-cats watched from their galleries and did not drop. The sheets of birds turned once, silver to dull and back, and then roosted a fraction higher than they had intended, as if the air had thickened by the width of a feather. The pale fish in the cisterns pressed their mouths to the meniscus and tasted, for the first time, the idea of a current that might one day choose them.

The power leaned against the eldest bole, the northern one whose chambers hung with fruit that was also furniture. It leaned long enough for turgor to become something else. The bole, whose thoughts take decades, finished the thought it had been having since before the last real rain. The thought was not words. It was the entire history of the bowl compressed into the slow flex of cellulose: we have been this loud for so long that silence would be a kind of death, and we are not ready to die, and we are very tired, and the tiredness has become the only song we know how to sing. The power did not answer in kind. It had no centuries to spend on a reply. Instead it placed one palm—or the idea of a palm—against the weeping cambium and let the sap run over whatever it used for skin. The sap did not seal the touch into a window or a mouth or a stair. It simply ran, and kept the memory of the running.

At dusk the roofs tilted, the condensation began its work, the bird-sheets came in, and the sound climbed its familiar stair. The power climbed with it. On the landing that should not have been able to hold so much noise, the power did the only thing a wise and benevolent attention can do when it has been invited by sheer endurance: it made a floor.

Not a silence that erased. A silence that underlaid. A thin, persistent quiet that had always been there, the way the pause between heartbeats is always there, unnoticed until someone older than heartbeats points it out. The roar continued. The cellulose still cracked like ice and ships and old houses. The insects remained a grit that could become a scream. Echoes still arrived early, and the same moss-cat still died in two galleries at once, and the little river still considered running uphill. But now there was an underneath. A private room, very small, the size of a held breath, in which a sentence could finish if anyone wanted one to.

Some of the niche-holders felt it first: the ones whose hands ended in slow green buds, whose eyes tracked light the way leaves do. They did not speak. They never had. But one of them, in the southern bole where the acoustics lie so beautifully, stopped in the middle of being loud enough to keep its cistern and simply stood, listening to the new floor the way a walker listens to a city it can no longer leave. After a while it unfolded one extra joint it had not used in a very long time and touched the bark beside it, as if checking that the wall was still willing to be a wall.

The power did not stay for the night. Night here is only the same city with the insects promoted, and the power had already heard that song. It left by the rim, which took no time at all because the power had never needed the courtesy of smallness. Behind it the seam in the pavement remained a little longer than usual. The fruit in the northern bole ripened one day slower. A single grazer, the color of paper left in rain, cropped a path that was not one of the approved loops of terror and was not immediately eaten for the deviation.

From outside the bowl is still a grove. A hamlet of trees. Damp at the roots, full of birds. But if you stand at the rim now, in ordinary light, and you know how to listen underneath, you can hear it: not the roar, which was always there, but the faint, stubborn quiet the roar is standing on. It is the sound of a place that has been seen by something that did not flinch and did not try to fix it into a different shape. It is the sound of a being that was never asked to be well, only to continue, and has been given, at last, the option of a floor.

The power did not take the option away. It did not stay to see whether anyone would use the room. It only made sure the room existed, then went on its way, carrying the taste of pepper and honest rot and one unfinished thought that will take another few decades to complete. The bowl, which has never been young, is still not young. But it is no longer quite so alone inside its own architecture. That is all. That is enough. That is what a wise and benevolent power does when it finds a city that has become its own wound and learned, with terrible patience, to live there. It listens until the listening itself becomes a kind of healing, then leaves the rest to the wood.
