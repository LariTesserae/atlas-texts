The power first felt the plateau as a warmth that did not belong to any sun. It had watched many risings: continents that shrugged, ranges that tore themselves from the mantle and then spent aeons pretending the wound had always been sky. This one was different. It was still listening to itself. The heat rose not as catastrophe but as metabolism, and the city that clung to it had learned the same tempo. The power, which had no name the stone would recognize, paused in its long circuit of the world’s quieter astonishments.

It did not descend. Descent would have been a statement, and statements hurry. Instead it thinned itself across the altitude, becoming a quality of the already-thin air, a patience inside the patience. For a season that the clocks (already slow) could not quite measure, it simply accompanied the geothermal seep, the hexagonal flags, the films that were neither life nor mineral. It tasted the vernix of glass that formed on forgotten tools and on living knuckles. It felt the turbines’ pressure in what served it for a sternum. It watched the pale walkers test the pahoehoe and leave their almost-writing in the offering pans.

When it finally acted, the action was almost nothing.

A single new fissure opened, no wider than a forearm, on the city’s highest unfinished gantry. No bright tongue of rock emerged. What came instead was a cooler breath, a thread of air that had never been inside the mountain, carrying a mineral scent the inhabitants had no word for. The breath lasted three nights. In that time the condensation gutters filled with water that did not evaporate at the usual rate. The water was faintly sweet, faintly iridescent, the same hyphen-taste as the pipe films yet clearer, as though the films had been given a grammar. Those who drank it (they all did, eventually, because the mountain offered and they had long ago stopped refusing) found their inner clocks slowing another fraction. Not enough to notice in a day. Enough, over years, that the distinction between a life and a geology grew even thinner, more companionable.

The power left no instruction. It did not stay. Its attention, however, remained as a kind of weather the plateau had not known before: a stillness that was not absence but regard. The newest buildings, exhaled by the rock, now sometimes held a room whose walls remembered that regard. People sat there without speaking. Children (still uncommon) slept there and woke knowing, without being told, which new flow would become street and which would remain only listening.

The pale walkers began to pause longer. Sometimes they rearranged the offerings into shapes that the inhabitants, after the sweeter water, could almost read. The films in the pipes thickened in places into veils that showed, for an hour at a time, the slow lift of the entire mass, millimeter by patient millimeter, as if the mountain had been given a mirror.

That was all.

The power had seen that the place and its people were already the correct unfinished thing. To complete them would have been a cruelty disguised as kindness. To leave them wholly unmarked would have been a different cruelty, the cruelty of indifference. So it gave them a single cooler breath, a slightly slower second, and the knowledge (felt rather than spoken) that they were seen, and that seeing them had cost the seer nothing and given it a quiet, geothermal joy.

Far below, another flow emerged, halted, listened. Someone would go down in the morning. They would take their time. The stone, and the people who were becoming its second skin, would still be arriving when the power next circled back, centuries hence, to feel that same contradictory warmth in the soles of whatever it used for feet.
