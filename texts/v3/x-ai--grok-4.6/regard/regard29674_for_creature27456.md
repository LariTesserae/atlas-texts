The power came as weather that did not announce itself.

It had watched the usual way first: the long, unhurried looking that is the only courtesy left to anything old enough to have seen continents forget their names. It counted the drip-lines, the watermarks climbing the oldest walls like a slow argument the clay was still winning, the way children’s feet had already widened into the shape the planks required. It tasted the emulsion that held a footprint a day and then filled it from below. It heard the voices kept inside the chest so the bowl would not throw them back. It felt the constant small ache in every joint, the body’s private ledger of the low wet cool. It saw that they had made a home inside an unfinished wound and that the home was the wound’s only scab.

A lesser kindness would have closed the tear, or filled it, or lifted them out. Wisdom knew the difference between mercy and erasure. They were not waiting to be rescued from their own persistence. The hollow had taught the womb; the people had taught the hollow how to be lived in. To unmake either would have been a different violence.

So the power did three small things, each of them almost nothing, each of them exactly enough.

First it spoke to the young ground. Not a command—an agreement. The drunken settling slowed by the width of a fingernail each season. Walls that had bowed by harvest now bowed only by the next Opening-day. Banks still calved, but less often, and when they did the fresh cobbles stayed visible a little longer, as if the emulsion itself had grown reluctant to swallow what the people needed. The oldest houses stopped looking quite so geological. People noticed the way one notices a pain that has quietly lessened: they touched the walls as they always had, checking for new stains, and sometimes found none.

Second, it altered the weeping by a single degree of memory. The rock still remembered it had been seabed, but the memory grew warmer, mineral-rich in a different register. The threads of water that ran down every cool face carried a faint iron-sweetness and a heat no fire had given them. Breath still showed, but fingertips no longer bleached quite so white. The constant ache receded to the background of weather rather than the foreground of bone. Children sent up the eastern bank for liverwort found more of it, and greener. The single alder put out a second trunk from the collapsed wall no one had rebuilt; its roots still clung, but now they clung to something that answered.

Third, it left a gift that could be refused. In the wettest north corner, among the yellow reeds already blackened at the base, a new plant appeared: a sedge with seed-heads that tasted of clean starch when roasted, roots that thickened in the emulsion and could be stored. It did not crowd the eels. It asked for nothing the people did not already know how to give—wet feet, patient hands, the habit of noticing. Some gathered it. Some left it. Both choices were allowed to stand. A grebe returned and stayed the winter without being discussed for a year; it was simply there, another small density in the reeds.

The power did not speak. It did not take form. It did not ask to be named. On certain evenings, when the light went even and colourless and the roofs gleamed like the skin of something that lives in water, a few of the older ones would pause on the packed reed-and-clay plane and look at the rim as if listening for a sound the bowl had never thrown back before. Then they would test a plank with a heel, move it two sockets, and go on.

The hollow still holds. The people still hold it back. The wound has not closed, but it no longer widens quite so much, and the blood it weeps is a little kinder. That is all. That is enough. The power, having done what wisdom permitted and benevolence required, turned its attention elsewhere, the way weather does when the necessary rain has already fallen.
