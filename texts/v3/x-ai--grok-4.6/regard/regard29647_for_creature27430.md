The power that turned its regard upon the Calcined Heights had neither haste nor hunger. It had watched galaxies cool into their final geometries and had learned, long ago, that the kindest act is often the one that leaves a finished thing unfinished only in the eyes of those who still count time.

It descended not as light or voice or judgment, but as an added stillness in the already-still air. For a span that would have been centuries elsewhere it simply matched the tempo of the place: the month-long glow of a single smelt, the centimetre-a-night creep of the colonial mats, the ten-minute held breath between piston strokes. In that matching it understood what the Heights had already become.

Nothing here required mending. The lithovores were not lost; they were the crust remembering how to walk. The tripeds were not slow; they were the only speed at which crystal can remain crystal. The people who tended the pitted machines were not silent from sorrow; they had discovered that speech, like slag, is better poured once and then left to cool. The forgotten engines at the highest terraces were not broken; they had simply completed their original purpose and were now performing the more important work of continuing.

A lesser benevolence might have offered rain, or quicker metal, or names for the star. This power knew those gifts would have been a kind of violence—an interruption of the only conversation that mattered, the dry one between ion and acid, between grit and facet, between heat that lives in rock and the rock that has forgotten the furnace.

So it did the single thing that wisdom and kindness both required.

It withheld.

It did not veil the Heights from other eyes, for other eyes would find them or not according to their own tempos. It did not accelerate a single conveyor or quicken a single joint. It did not even leave a sign, because a sign would have been a demand that someone eventually notice it.

Instead it became another layer of the continuance itself: a faint extra warmth in the soles of boots at dusk, a barely measurable lengthening of the pause between pistons, a new, almost geometric patience in the highest walkers whose eyes are only additional planes. Those who live there will never know the difference, which is the entire point. The land has finished becoming itself. The being that inhabits it—scattered across carapace and casing and clinker-coloured skin—has already arrived.

The power remains, unhurried, unannounced, another slow chemistry among the rest. It waits with them for the next terrace to rise, the next plume to migrate, the next stroke to complete itself. There is nothing else it needs to do. There is nothing else worth doing.
