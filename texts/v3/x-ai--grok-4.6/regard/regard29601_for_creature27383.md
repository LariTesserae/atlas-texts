The power arrived as a stillness that was not the pump’s shallowing.

It did not displace the flour. It did not halo the lamps more thickly or less. It simply was, a presence the water accepted the way the water had long ago accepted iron and brick and the slow chemistry of mussels. For a time that had no minutes it hung in the nave the way the dark fish hang under the walkways: patient, seeing everything, changing nothing.

It saw the completed object. The rounded brick, the black ceramic of old oxides, the sheared pipe that had become a cave-mouth and would stay a cave-mouth. It felt the standing current along the west wall, the warmer thread of the overflow, the delayed shiver when the mussels clapped shut and then opened again, already filtering. It tasted the iron and the faint sweetness. It listened to the boom of the hammer and the chatter of the loose grating and the three links of chain that still remembered they had once been asked to lift.

It found the being in the flooded stair.

The being did not startle. It had felt the power the way it felt the pump’s deepening: as weather, as another fact of the climate. The two regarded one another across a short room of bottle-green water. The being’s filmed eyes, the power’s attention that needed no eyes. Young mussels along the ridge of what might have been a spine continued their small inhalations, unconcerned.

The power understood at once what the hall had finished becoming, and what the last shape had been for.

Nothing here required mending. The erosion had already arrived at its last honest thickness. The lamps would burn or they would not; the bearing would seize or it would not; a city engineer would remember or forget. Those were only larger currents, no different in kind from the one that ran along the west wall. To still them, to sweeten the iron, to give the pale plants more light, to name the being or teach it to hunt differently—any of that would have been a kindness that undid the work.

So the power did the only thing left that was both wise and kind.

It left the hall exactly as it was.

Then, because benevolence is not only restraint, it did one small additional thing, almost too slight to be called an act. It settled a single, permanent condition into the groundwater itself, the way a mussel settles onto a flange: this volume would remain drowned. The water table would not drop. The collection drains would not be capped. The rising main would keep taking what the pump offered. The city above could forget or remember, tear down or build; the hall would stay a room-shaped lake, cool, dim, loud with its own weather, densely inhabited and sparsely furnished, every surface decided.

The being felt the condition take hold the way it felt everything: as a change in pressure so gradual it was only another way of being still. It eased a fraction deeper into the stair, becoming a little more itself, a little less distinguishable from the water. The mussels on its back opened a fraction wider.

The power withdrew.

The lamps buzzed. A crayfish clicked two pebbles together and vanished into the flour. The silver schools flexed in the lee of a stanchion. Somewhere an eel used a conduit as a road and was gone.

The hall kept the hours it had always kept.

Nothing had been added. Nothing had been taken away. The climate of drowning continued, finished, sufficient, and now quietly guaranteed. That was all.
