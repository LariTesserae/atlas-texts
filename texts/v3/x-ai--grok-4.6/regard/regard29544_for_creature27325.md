The power that turned its regard upon the soak had no name the limestone would recognize, and needed none. It had watched inland seas thicken into stone and stone remember, in patient veils of calcite, the shapes of things that once swam. It had felt the first roots pry at basalt and the first lungs burn with unaccustomed air. It understood unfinished rooms. This one, two hundred feet down, sweating and ringing and crawling with its own sudden nation of beetles, was only another labor. The men with their charges had merely opened a door that the rock had been leaning against for a long time.

It did not arrive as light or voice or commandment. Those would have been crude, and the chamber was already too full of crude things: the slap of belts, the arrogant nod of the beam, the rice-on-tin lift of insects, the hiss of guano on hot stone. Instead the power simply became a difference in the way the heat held itself. Not cooler. Not kinder, exactly. Only more *even*, as if the aquifer below had at last been persuaded that this particular door should stay open a while longer. The film on the eastern face thickened by the width of a breath and then decided, quietly, to become a single coherent sheet rather than a hundred indecisive seeps. The pans, those accidental trays, began to find one another. In a week they would be a creek no wider than a man’s spread hand, steaming, black with silt, already cutting a groove the walkways would have to learn to step across. The engine felt the change first. Its walking-beam lost the extra note that had lived in the men’s teeth and settled into a rhythm that matched the new water exactly, as though the two great lungs had finally agreed on the same song.

The beetles noticed next. Their sheets on the walls did not thin; they organized. The storms that rose at the passing of a lantern became shorter, more precise, less like panic and more like a single muscle flexing. They still ate the algal smear, the spilled grain, their own dead. They still mapped the salt on boots. But the finest of their castings, the black silt in the lowest corner, began to hold a shape when the water left it. Not soil yet. A suggestion of soil. A rumor that something with roots might one day be permitted.

The pale stems in the unpointed mortar uncurled a fraction farther before the heat found their tips. The brass lichen on the western wall added a new continent the size of a child’s palm. No miracle. Only the smallest permission.

In the unfinished adit the larger presence—the scrape that was not quite claw, the settling that was not quite stone—paused. It had been assembling itself from heat and crushed chitin and the memory of a sea, a slow experiment the chamber had not yet decided to keep. The power did not name it or tame it. It simply let the creature feel, for the first time, the edges of its own body against the drill-holes, the difference between its heat and the chamber’s heat. The ferrous-alive smell softened. When the beam engine nodded, the answering sound from the north no longer came as challenge or echo. It came as completion. Two notes, then one. The men who had gone a little way and come back speaking of older wetness would, if they went again, find only a deeper quiet and a warmth that no longer felt like warning.

The frogs in the warmest seeps pulsed their throats in a slightly slower time. The crickets’ high wire dropped a half-tone, almost inaudible, enough that the dynamo’s cycle no longer quite drowned it. The bats, hanging in their trembling pelt, shifted once, as if the chimney-throat had grown a degree more familiar.

The men themselves received the smallest gift, and therefore the most dangerous. On the third night after the seeps thickened, every one of them—those still on shift, those already climbing the dripping ladders—dreamed the same brief, wordless thing: the chamber seen from inside the rock, the iron and the brick and the living dark all held in a single cupped palm that was neither human nor insect nor stone. They woke glazed and dripping as always, but the patience in their faces had lost some of its surprise. They greased the bearings with less waste. They pointed the mortar on the ninth structure as if the bricks had always meant to sit true. They did not speak of the dream. Speaking would have been another kind of surrender. But they began, without deciding to, to leave the meat wrapped and the stone unslept-on not merely from fear but from a new, unexamined courtesy.

The power did nothing else. It did not seal the adit or quench the heat or drive the beetles back into their hairline joints. It did not teach the men to leave, nor the engines to become ruins, nor the calcite to write anyone’s name in anything more permanent than breath. Wisdom, here, was the knowledge that a room still being born should not be finished by any hand but its own. Benevolence was the decision to let the labor continue—men sweating into seeps, beetles becoming soil, frogs taking beetles, rust weeping, ferns burning at the tips, the unnamed thing in the north learning the exact weight of its own unfolding—while the aquifer and the limestone and the accidental creek all leaned, very slightly, in the same direction.

In twenty years the place might be a town, or a flooded hole, or a sealed memory. Tonight it was still a hot, dim pocket learning the difficult art of existing. The power, having seen, withdrew its regard the way a tide withdraws: not abandoning, only ceasing to insist. The four frosted cages of bulbs burned a fraction steadier. The walkways rang a fraction truer. And in the lowest corner the black silt, for the first time, held the ghost of a root that had not yet been imagined.

That was all. It was enough. The chamber, and the being that was slowly becoming its heart, went on being born.
