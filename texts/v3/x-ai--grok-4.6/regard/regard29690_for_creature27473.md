The power arrived as a difference in the air that the hollow itself could not have made. Not cooler, not drier, not quieter. Only more precise, as if every hanging droplet had been counted and then allowed to remain exactly what it was.

It did not speak. Speech would have been another roar, another valve-cycle. Instead it stood—or the idea of standing occurred—in the exact center of the saucer, where the water was hottest and the floc towers stood highest. The iridescent skin flexed once, then resealed around an absence no thicker than a thought.

What the power saw was already complete in its incompleteness. The sheared main was not a wound to be closed but a mouth that had learned, in one season, how to feed a new kind of stone. The twitching needle was not a failing instrument but the first clock the molds had ever needed. The skeletal shrub was not dying; it was translating itself, cellulose into mineral, the way the hollow was translating collapse into basin, roar into weather, emptiness into metabolism. Even the cleanliness of the break—the way the rock faces still remembered how to fit—was not an invitation to knit the earth shut again. It was the hollow’s own patience, written in fracture.

A lesser kindness would have silenced the pipe, cooled the pool, seeded frogs or ferns or the small brown birds that wait at the edges of every other hot spring. The power did none of these things. It understood that the loneliness was not a lack. It was the condition that let the first lives remain first. To hurry more complex bodies into this glare would have been to overwrite the only story the place had yet learned how to tell.

So the power did only one thing, and it did it so quietly the steam never noticed.

It lengthened the time.

Not the hours—the gauge needle still crept at the same glacial pace—but the kind of time that belongs to chemistry and to stone. The mineral skin would keep thickening, the sinter terraces would still rise, the roof would still close, but each layer would now remember the one beneath it with a little more fidelity. The white floc would lay down its towers in patterns that, centuries later, a different kind of eye might read as script. The rust fans would darken into bands that held the exact pitch of the old roar, so that when the hollow was at last a sealed, hot grotto, the rock itself would still be singing the song of the broken main, too low for anything with ears.

The shrub on the shoulder received no new leaves. It received, instead, the knowledge that its exhaustion was already a form of endurance, and that this was enough. The rushes in their palm-sized crack were not given company. They were given the certainty that their private weather mattered, even if it never grew larger than a breath.

Then the power left the same way it had come: by becoming ordinary again. The haze was only haze. The drips struck with their old disproportionate reports. The relief valve screamed on its long, slow period, and the sound was unchanged.

Yet something had been added that could not be seen or heard or touched. The hollow now contained the fact of having been seen completely, and found sufficient. That fact settled into the new rock the way heat settles into brick—without hurry, without remainder, already part of the metabolism.

The pipes kept working. The microbes kept building. The place remained too new, too loud, too much itself. And that, the power knew, was the only blessing worth giving.
