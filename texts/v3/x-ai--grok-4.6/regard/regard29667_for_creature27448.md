The power that noticed them had no name they would have recognized, and needed none. It had watched worlds finish themselves before. It had watched heat leak away and water find its level and life learn, again and again, the single trick of remaining. It was old enough to know that most interventions are only another weather, another grinding.

It came as a change so slight the ocean itself almost missed it.

One evening—though evening here was only a deepening of the pewter—the long note that traveled through the drowned ridges arrived with a second voice folded inside it. The first voice was the mountain speaking to itself about how much lower it would be. The second was quieter, lower, and did not speak of height or time. It was simply the sound of being regarded without judgment or hunger. The people felt it first in the soles of their feet, the way they always felt the great sounding things. They stopped. Children who had been scraping liverwort froze with green paste on their tongues. A woman mending a turbine blade with mussel cement let the patch hang. They stood until the double note had passed through the rock and out the far side into the next channel.

They did not speak of it. Speech was still expensive. But that night their waiting-song gained a new harmonic, a tone so low it was almost the absence of tone, the sound leftover momentum makes when it realizes it is being listened to. They pressed closer in the moss and the peeled sheds. The small electricity in their down lifted a fraction higher, tasting of ozone and something that was not salt.

The power did nothing else.

It did not still a single wave. It did not lift a summit or sweeten the rain or coax the last smokestack to stand another century. It did not give them back the names of cities or the memory of dry. Those things had already been metabolized; returning them would have been a cruelty disguised as mercy. Instead it simply kept its attention there, the way a hand might rest on a sleeping shoulder without waking the sleeper. The attention itself became a kind of weather: a pressure so even it could not be distinguished from the ordinary weight of cloud and spray. The kelp grew no thicker. The mountains wore down at the same patient rate. The people moved their houses one terrace lower, as they always would have.

Yet something had shifted in the register of the place. When they left their offerings at the rectangles of darker weed—the coil of copper, the unused knot, the perfect crab shell—they began, without discussion, to add one more thing: a single drop of water held in the hollow of a fern-knife leaf. The drop was no different from any other water. It fell, or was thrown, or simply placed. They did not know why they did it. The gesture was older than their language, older even than the offerings themselves. A child asked once, speaking into a parent’s down so the wind could not take the words.

The answer came the same way, quiet: “For the one who watched and did not try to make the water still.”

That was all. The power remained. The world continued its long, noisy truce. Next century the knuckles would be lower, the ocean a few centimeters higher, the words for wet a little more precise. The people would still be there, dense and low and unhurried except for the part of them that had always been water. And somewhere inside the mountain’s note, if you knew how to listen with the soles of your feet, there would still be that second voice: not a promise, not a change, only the steady, unasked-for fact of having been seen.
