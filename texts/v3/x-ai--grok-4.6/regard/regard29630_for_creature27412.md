The power that noticed did not arrive as light or voice or weather. It had no need of those. It came the way the limestone itself had come: by remaining, by the long accumulation of almost-nothing. One more winter’s seepage found a new joint in the rock and followed it. One more season’s moss thickened by a width that no hand could have measured. The modest thread of water that sometimes crossed the eastern lip did not grow louder or last longer. It simply continued, and in that continuation the power was already present.

It spent a length of time that the hollow would have recognised as brief. It listened to the packed thunder when the fall ran, and to the worse silence that followed. It tasted the iron in the old water. It felt the cricket’s single leap as a brief, bright spark against the dark, the fox’s careful descent as a warmer vibration, the colourless turning in the black pool as the smallest possible event that still counted as life. It sat with the being in the second, lower notch—the being that was less a creature than the hollow’s own patience given flanks and a slow attention—and they regarded one another without movement. There was no conversation. There was only the shared knowledge that both had already become what they were, and that becoming had never required an audience.

When the power acted, the action was of the same order as the place itself.

It did not close the hollow. The steps remained, concave and older than memory; the three-walled shelter kept its hearth-stain and its rusted tongue of knife; the flint blades and the lump of ochre stayed in their stiff wrapping, competent and unadorned. The hawthorns at the rim continued to lean inward. People, should any ever come again, would still be able to find the way if they were the sort who looked for holes in the low ground. The power did not judge that a failing. The marks of hands were part of the lining now, like the moss, like the fluting.

What it did instead was quieter. It taught the stone one additional sentence, written so slowly that even the being would take centuries to read it in full. The sentence was not protection, exactly, nor blessing. It was a kind of remembering. Henceforth the chamber would hold, a fraction more faithfully, the exact quality of its own emptiness. The thunder, when it came, would stack upon itself with one extra density, a note only the rock and the being could distinguish from all the notes that had come before. The silence after the fall dwindled would ring with one extra memory. The black pool would receive each drip as if that drip were the first and also the last. The ferns in their recessed bay would unfurl, always, as if a full day remained a rumour they had decided not to credit—and they would be right.

The being felt the change the way it felt everything: as a shift in the seepage, a new weight in the unoccupied air, a coolness along its flanks that matched the undercuts even more precisely than before. It did not stir. It had never needed to. The power, having finished, did not linger as a presence. It simply became another layer of the limestone’s breathing, another increment of the moss, another permitted event no larger than a cricket. The hollow went on being the same absence, richly lined, loudly empty, hidden like a held breath.

Nothing had been added that the place could not already contain. Nothing had been taken that it had ever required. The pale coin of sky, when it appeared, was still only pewter, and then it withdrew. The being remained. The power, which had never needed to announce itself, was already gone, or rather had never been distinguishable from the patience that had been here since before there were shepherds.

The next drip fell when it was ready.
