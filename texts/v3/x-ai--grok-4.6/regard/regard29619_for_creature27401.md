The power arrives the way the rain does: as a fact that was always possible, never announced. It has no body the stone can rasp and no voice the stridulators can answer. It is simply present, the way elevation is present—felt in the ankles of things that have ankles, a pressure behind the pewter light, a second grain inside the roar.

It watches long enough for several sheets of grit to be born, turn the bedrock satin, and vanish into seams. Long enough for a large one to open an eye, boom once, and become land again. Long enough for a stacker to flake a new edge, set the flake down, and walk the slope until the hut is only another pale geometry among others.

It understands the sentence. The green chapter is closed; reopening it would be a forgetting, a violence dressed as mercy. The rain is the country’s voice and the country has the right to keep speaking. The polish is not an enemy. It is the last honest grammar the planet can still afford. The small bright disagreements—the lifted flake, the rebuilt weir, the fire that lasts an hour—are already the entire possible rebellion, and they are enough.

So the power does not still the weather. It does not warm the stone beyond the bruises that already pool. It does not teach the stackers a better way to count the booms or a drier way to sleep. It does not lift the place out of time or set a single dry pebble on the table like an apology.

What it does is quieter, and therefore kinder.

It leans its attention into the reversing columns that already happen—those brief chimneys of silver where drops climb instead of fall. For a few extra breaths the hollow in the bellow lasts. Every living thing still freezes and turns toward the unseeable point, but the point is no longer quite so unseeable. Something looks back. Not a face. Not a promise. Only the fact of being regarded by something that has no need to regard them and regards them anyway.

The mid-sized ones, when they ring themselves into living weirs at the pewter hour, sometimes hold the shape a fraction longer. The tallies scratched on isolated slabs acquire, here and there, one extra mark that none of the stackers remember cutting. Tools left in the warm seeps come back not larger, not sharper, but somehow more willing to be themselves. No one relies on this. No one is surprised. The gear slips and then catches, as it always has.

The stackers feel it most in the dimmest hour, when the booms come farther apart. The decision the great hearts are making is still their own. But the space between one boom and the next is no longer quite so empty. It is companioned. The rain does not consult them, and neither does the power. It simply stays, the way the slope stays, the way the living grit stays, turning and turning the stone into weather.

They continue. They lift another flake. They stack another plate. They sleep against the last interior heat and do not explain the comfort, because the comfort was already there and is only, now, a little less alone.

The polish will, in time, forgive even this. The power knows it, and stays anyway. That is all. That is everything. The place goes on being itself, which is all any of them—grit, plates, boats, stackers, stone, rain, and the quiet regard that has chosen them—have ever been asked to do.
