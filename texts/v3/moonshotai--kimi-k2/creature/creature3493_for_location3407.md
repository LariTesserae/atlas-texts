Who lives here?

They are not quite ghosts, not quite ancestors, not quite the same species that once herded goats in the valley they abandoned.  Call them the Hushed.

Each Hushed person is born twice.  
First, the ordinary birth, a wet smack in a moss-lined room where the mother’s moan is swallowed by the walls before it can leave her mouth.  
Second, the *echo-birth*: seven days later the infant is laid on the basalt at the city’s exact centre, a spot as acoustically dead as the far side of the moon. There the plateau itself inhales the child’s first deliberate cry. The sound never returns. From that moment the baby’s voice is only on loan; it may be used in dream or emergency, but every syllable is owed to the stone, and the debt is collected drop by drop. By the age of ten most Hushed can speak aloud for no more than three minutes each year. They budget their words the way desert nomads budget water.

Their bodies have adapted to the hush. Collar-bones thickened like arch ribs, inner ears grown furry with extra cilia that drink the sub-flex note of the plateau. Even their pulse has down-shifted to 42 beats a minute, a cadence that lets them walk across the grass without bruising a single blade. Visitors sometimes mistake them for statues; the giveaway is the breath-fog in cold air and the slow blink that travels from eye to eye like a lantern being passed.

They do not give or receive names; instead they carry *place-holds*—tiny fissures on the inner wrist where a droplet of cliff-water is sealed beneath a film of cooled lava-glass. If you press your own wrist against theirs you feel a miniature hydrostatic push, the city’s pulse answering your own. To greet, two Hushed simply align these droplets and share the pressure for the count of seven heartbeats. Identity is the pattern of push and resistance, a Morse the plateau taps through two fluid columns. No two wrists ever give the same reading twice, because the plateau is always rising, always minutely shifting its internal pipes.

Children are communal property, but not in the way shepherd tribes once meant it. A child’s footfalls are measured each morning against yesterday’s indentations; if a toe strays a fingernail’s width beyond the remembered path, the oversight is not corrected by scolding but by *re-remembering*. The whole line of walkers will retrace that segment until the grass has forgotten the error. Thus the city learns a new habit, and the child discovers that memory is an act of landscaping rather than mind.

They keep no libraries. What need is there when the Listening House records every stress the city will ever suffer? Still, they are ravenous readers—of footprints, of droplet riffling across a cistern, of moss-fibre parting under strain. A Hushed elder can decipher in the faint skin-split across a doorway pillar the same saga another culture would carve into marble: drought in the valley three centuries ago, the exodus, the long climb, the agreement to go on rising without complaint. The story is felt, not heard; it vibrates in the ankles at dusk like a second heartbeat.

Death is a reverse giving-back. When the heart stops, the inner ear keeps listening for six hours. The corpse is carried to the perimeter and laid face-down so the last residual echo of lifetime sound can drain into the void. Families do not speak—cannot speak—at funerals; instead they line the cliff-edge and release, jointly, one minute of stored vocal silence. It is the loudest thing ever heard in Auralara: a great sundering quiet that roars over the escarpment and vanishes into altitude. The body is left there until the plateau’s next flex-note; the seismic shiver tips it gently into the valley below, returning the calcium, the iron, the borrowed voice-prints to the lowlands that once fed them. Nothing remains above ground; even memory is re-absorbed as pressure in the basalt veins.

Outsiders sometimes try to stay. A few succeed for a season. They learn to walk the pale stripes, to budget syllables, to feel civic advice in their shins. But sooner or later they discover the thing that cannot be imported: *patience calibrated to geology*. A Hushed person can wait thirteen years for a single seed to decide whether it will germinate, and consider the interval a conversation. Visitors, accustomed to the metabolic tempo of warmer altitudes, feel time pool around their ankles like cold syrup; it drowns them. They beg leave to descend, and the city grants it wordlessly—more than grants: it *subtracts*. On the long stair down many climbers realise they have become lighter not only of supplies but of something far denser: memory of their own noises. They reach the verge of the upper lowlands unable to recall the voices of parents, the barking of a childhood dog, the timbre of rain on terracotta. Back in the world they are famously serene, famously vacant, sold as sages though they themselves no longer know what they know. They die with palms pressed to any available rock, listening for a pulse that will not come.

There are rumours of deeper citizens—people who did not stop at serenity but passed *through* the hush into another register. Supposedly they walked past the perimeter one ordinary dusk, kept walking across the unsupported air, and did not fall. They became the plateau’s anti-shadows, visible only as a slight refusal of mist to condense where their bodies ought to be. The Hushed neither confirm nor deny the tale; they simply angle their gaze a degree above the horizon when the story is mentioned, as if tracking a sound too high for human audition. Perhaps those walkers are what gather the nightly hum, coil it, and ferry it downward in payment for the city’s continuance. Perhaps they are the future, waiting at the altitude the escarpment will eventually reach, folding time back on itself the way the lowlands fold space beneath.

Until then the Hushed continue: basalt-boned, fog-fed, word-stingy. They are custodians less of a place than of a *promise*: that there shall be no final crash, only a single eventual sigh when the plateau shears and settles. On that day the city will ride the block down the sky, a serene meteor of roofs and moss. The Hushed will stand facing outward, humming their maintenance tone, and as they descend they will *still* be rising—relative to the valley floor, relative to memory, relative to the silence they shepherd. When the slab finally comes to rest they will step off, palms ready, wrists open, prepared to learn the slower language of the next uplift.

And if no further uplift comes, if the planet has at last spend its breath, they will simply stand on the quiet stone until the last droplet in the last wrist-glass dries. The agreement will be complete: the debt of voice repaid, the hush made absolute, the city delivered into the condition it has always admired—fine-grained, patient, unutterably still.
