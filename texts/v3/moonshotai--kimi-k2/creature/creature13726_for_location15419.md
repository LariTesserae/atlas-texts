The residents have never congealed into citizens—instead they remain partial things, slices of an organism that keeps deciding not to become everything at once.

First there are the Unhusks: tattered bundles that look like dried-out rags until they slouch and uncouple, letting paper-thin bark peel away until a lanky silhouette steps out. Their faces are a slideshow of positions: eyes like seed slits, eyes like kiln vents, eyes closed over entirely by the quick-brown cambium that scabs across them and flakes before evening. They speak to one another by exhaling strands of moist starch; the strands coil in the air like lengthening apostrophes, collapsing a few seconds later into drops that the floor eagerly sips. Unhusks’ primary occupation is forgetting. The place keeps toroidal ledgers of history velcroed inside its walls, and Unhusks’ job is to loosen them. Once a fortnight, or maybe twice per heartbeat (the temporal bars wobble), each Unhusk climbs a column disguised as a corpse-candle, presses mouth to cambium, and inhales one paragraph of the city. Their cheeks distend with preserved winters, after which they stumble away, releasing swallowed lore molecule by molecule into the condensation so that it trickles back down as unscripted rain.

Along the platforms of moist plywood—biological and impossible grain—nest the Scribblekin. Neither hominid nor insect, they resemble origami squid shucked out of tin. Legs arrive in overlapping strips that unzip only when they need to step apart, re-zipping into a pyramidal purse afterward. Scribblekin are amateur cartographers. Because every hallway can collapse into twilight solidity within a heartbeat, traditional maps are lethal weapons; instead the Scribblekin harvest sheets of dying lichen that curls into message scrolls. They scrawl routes only in negative pigment, colors darker than the space they cross, the lines thumb-wide boomerangs of obsession. They give these maps to new arrivals as gifts, knowing full well the hallway decoded will vanish the moment that visitor steps upon it. Their single hobby is erasing doorways: tiny bronze awls protrude from ring fingers; they strike hinge-cuts along the soft bricklike chitin of sealed thresholds. Once a door’s outline ceases to be linear for the statutory seventeen heartbeats, the portal stitches itself into wall. If the city compensates by growing an alternative exit elsewhere, the Scribblekin applaud with legs clacking like staplers.

Among the beams where the dark is visible because it is an absence of vision drift Aerosols, semi-buoyant children whose bodies are handfuls of silted sky wearing coats of condensation. They rise by simply refusing to fall, counter-obedient as thistle. When they descend, they flatten into cartoon shadows that follow residents like pet guilt. Aerosols trade distance for data: they collect breaths and whispers by cupping them inside their hollow ribcages, then sell the findings to whoever will beat one step faster than the consensus rotation. Their pay is kiln coins—droplets of metallic resin issued from the furnaces that melt cellulose—cold to the touch but acidic enough to scar marble. Aerosols eat these coins by forcing them up against their uvulas where the metal dissolves into vibrating salts that tickle the throat for weeks. After digestion, they cough up meter-long exclamation marks of steam that stripe the overhead dome with compressed weather forecasts—rosettes of pink frost spelling tomorrow’s doubt.

The youngest occupants are the Budden: infant apartments yet to unfold. They start as ball-bearing husks of brass the size of tomatoes that roll across the floor in fast convoys. Wherever they graze, they absorb any static line you might have accidentally sketched in the air. The moment they accumulate seventeen units of straightness they erupt into habitable volumes: one-story dwellings with moving platbands, miniature lobbies, and fungal fire escapes corkscrewing optimistically upward. The interiors arrive carpeted in embryonic velvet moss, ceilings puckered like bubbles remembering breath. Inside, chairs are still clenched fists of timber that open fingers only when someone boils water next to them. Occupants must feed their new homes a teaspoon of moist soil every couple of femtodays, or the walls will re-curl into the brass and trundle away in search of tidier tenants. A thriving trade exists in lactating trowels whose scoops sweat slow architectural milk.

There are also those who refuse to settle, the Fugalists. Part musician, part maintenance thread. They move in phosphor duets: one casts a harp of shadow that vibrates only where it is darker than dark; the other extrudes humid wind tunneled through an ocarina carved from beetle elytrons. What they play is repair: wherever a floorboard smells of marching fungi, their music soaks cellulose and persuades decay to slow its drum in exchange for becoming tempo stitch. If you follow a duet long enough you will see rotting balusters harden into fluting; see spores sanely filing themselves back into boxes of longitude. Fugalists pay double rent in reliefs: once the decay is postponed, they hover-transfer one bar of humidity into the municipal hygrometer; otherwise their instruments rot from inside and they vanish into lining sheets, unable ever again to find an octave in leaf rustle.

At the hushful center hover seven Echokeepers, scarcely more defined than rubbed-out chalk outlines but somehow carrying architectural heft; their cloaks hang from nothing but twilight folds. They oversee the voting of day-length from beneath the umbilical well, where the steam coils upward carrying half-formed boroughs. Each Echokeeper owns one syllable of the name the fruiting body will bear upon maturation: syllables formatted as shapes of light inverted into a curling vacuum. Once every consensus, they slide down the mirrored mirror-chain, whisper their syllables into the seed that floats in the well’s dark throat, and listen to how the city digests them. There are also transients: the Porters—muscular hornets wearing spider-leg avatars piloted from a throne of dew-soaked velvet stamen strands; Porters specialize in moving warm emotions across thermal gradients so that love or grief might pump through the copper veins in place of watts. There’s Chalicette, the only human-shaped figure who claims to have arrived on foot. Her limbs terminate in phloem loops through which one witnesses museum exhibits passing: seashell economies, shipyards, melting glaciers; the loops are portals open only through her skin. Her pupils float disconnected, pinhead planets with luminosity borrowed from inverse moons. Each night she records her commute in a ledger of skin flakes that the resurrection moss gradually shoulders aside. She swears the place is older than spores—older, even, than sky. “It’s just outgrowing forgetting,” she explains while scribbling darkness into dark. Nobody contradicts her; her shoes never quite touch petal or parquet, remaining hovering a polite millimeter above, as though at any moment the city might change its mind about floors.

Last are the Watchers Who Never Tilt. Clusters of flat lenses the size of city manhole covers float like pale squash seeds along invisible aqueous seams, tethered to the ceiling by triple strands of silver mycelium. The lenses watch in dial-face segments, recording reflections until their glass turns so thick with inverted images they birth new Chambers. Inside the spin of an iris you might glimpse a ballroom where chandeliers are still lymph sacks, or a vault made of recycled clocksprings threaded with moths. What is stored is never the scene you consigned but a version the city liked better. Drop a memory of your childhood home and you may pick it up years later labeled “Pavilion for abandoned thermostats.” The Watchers never speak, but settle debts by letting departing travelers steal a single frame: pocketed inside a matchbox, the scene keeps folding outward at night, warm lantern lit against an outer void tilting.

And so they circulate, never numbering more than seventy-two counted orthogonally, fewer if you count them diagonally through several mirrors. Their sum is a parliament of unfinished congress, each member deferring completion until the place itself feels wholly itself—until walls resolve into walls for keeps, until floors stop negotiating the meaning of down. No overlord, no rector of pith. Only the compendium of parts permitting enclosure, albeit temporarily. A city not being built but birthing itself from the interior outward, its residents the knotted nodes of self-definition choosing, heartbeat by heartbeat, what about themselves may remain partial, what about the city may finish—and what part of finish is betrayal of the compost that fed it.

So they dwell: partly spore, partly citizen, fully unfinished, arching their identities across gradients too delicate for permanence, folding their palms into rain so that tomorrow—whenever consensus brews new midnight—they might unfold again, different and the same.
