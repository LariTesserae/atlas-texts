Live there the Half-Submerged.

No one agrees on what to call them, so the name keeps sliding.  They answer to “, you—,” to the squeak of wet leather, to the sound of something losing its grip.  Observers from the rim insist they are “the tenants,” but that word presumes a lease no one ever signed.  They themselves simply say “we’re still here,” pronoun unstressed, as though continuity were a job and they have not yet punched out.

They arrived in the same minute the ground gave way, riding the district down as if seated on a stool that suddenly becomes an elevator.  The drop lasted seven heartbeats—long enough for a teacup to stay level, for a banker to decide against screaming, for a cat to achieve perfect stillness in mid-air—then impact, splash, the long creak of beams remembering they are only wood.  When the dust settled, the survivors discovered they had been promoted to citizens of a vertical swamp.  They took the promotion without applause.

Generation One (the Fallen) still walk with the duck-footed gait of people who once knew sidewalks.  Their calves remember level ground the way amputees remember bells.  You can spot them by the way they flinch when dripwater hits the crown of the head—an ancestral insult they never learned to expect.  They keep shoes in sealed tins “for when it comes back,” the way other people keep ashes in urns.

Generation Two (the Bred) were born looking up at a underside of streets.  To them, balconies are cliff faces, doorways are crawl-through caves, and the word “basement” is a nonsense syllable meaning “sky.”  Their first lullaby was the arterial thump of water striking tin; their first horizon a rust stripe at the level of their mothers’ eyebrows.  They learned to crawl on ceilings, fingertips hooked into the ornamental roses of fallen parlors.  A toddler’s first game is “don’t touch the drink,” played on beams the width of a spoon.  Their skulls are slightly asymmetrical, a plastic response to always tilting one ear toward the drip-count.

Generation Three (the Drips) are not yet numerous enough to protest the name.  They emerge fish-pale, with eyelids fine as wet paper.  Nurses flick matchheads of lamplight across their faces to see if the pupils tighten; if not, the child is declared “day-blind” and will specialize in corridors where even the gasjets hesitate.  These children speak in two registers at once:  a normal voice for above-water conversation, a simultaneous ultrasonic squeak that rattles rust flakes loose.  Adults pretend not to hear the second tongue, but the rats respond, forming honor guards around the infants’ cradles—hammocks strung between opposing drip-rates.

Together they compose a single organism of variable cardinality.  Watch them move:  one body crosses a plaza of listing flagstones; when the footing begins to founder, three strangers detach from doorways, grip the same hemp line, and redistribute weight until the tilt steadies.  Then they separate, having become momentary ligaments in a distributed skeleton.  No thanks are offered; cartilage does not thank bone.

Their clothes are stitched from fabric that was never meant to be clothing:  blackout curtains, sailcloth advertisements, the tarred paper of pneumatic tubes.  Every garment doubles as storage:  a cuff unrolled becomes a drip-bucket, a collar lifted becomes a nose-flare mask against the midge storm.  Colors are chosen for how they look when wet—there is no other state—so the palette runs to bruise purples, arterial bruise reds, and the nickel silver of old mirrors.  Laundry is performed by walking:  you put the garment on, wade through a fall-line of of condensate, and let the city rinse you.

They eat what can be trusted not to sink before digestion.  Diet is therefore vertical:  finger-long fishes that colonized the elevator shafts, algae sluiced from conveyor belts, puffball mushrooms grown on the backs of slow-moving bureaucrats.  Protein is smoked in cabinets that once filed marriage certificates; the certificates curl and caramelize, adding notes of ink and regret.  A favorite delicacy is “concrete custard,” a calcium sludge that forms where stalactite meets stalagmite in mid-air; it is harvested with spoons tied to broom handles, eaten before it realizes it has hardened.

Work is assigned by buoyancy.  If you displace more water than you weigh, you are a Runner, licensed to carry messages across the beams.  If you weigh more than you displace, you are an Anchor, paid to sit on collapsing junctions until temporary scaffolding can be persuaded to grip.  Between the two lies the vast class of Middlers, who perform every other function while never quite trusting either foot.  Payroll is conducted in liters:  one liter of purified dripwater buys one working day, because thirst is the only commodity that never goes out of style.

They marry by the synchrony of heartbeats.  Two people stand under the same gutter; if the water striking their clavicles produces identical rhythms for one hundred drips, they are declared aligned.  The ceremony consists of braiding together the cords that keep their coats closed; later, either partner may unthread the braid to signal divorce, but the cord Shortens by one strand each time, meaning every successive separation is harder to untangle.  No one remembers what happens when the cord refuses to let go; it is considered impolite to find out.

Death is handled upward.  Corpses are considered ballast:  to let them rest on any surface is to risk further collapse.  Therefore the deceased is strapped into a sling of old hammock and winched, by communal crank, toward the original street level far above.  The ascent takes all night; mourners walk alongside on whatever inclines present themselves, telling the elevatee the story of their life in reverse order.  By the time the body reaches the over-city dawn, it has heard the moment of its birth and is theoretically prepared to renegotiate existence with whatever bureaucracy handles the next vertical.  If the rope frays and the body falls back down, this is interpreted as a bureaucratic rejection; they try again next week with a revised narrative.

Belief systems are plural but share one premise:  direction is negotiable.  Sin is defined as “acting as though down is settled.”  Virtue is the maintenance of contingency:  always keep one foot ready to relocate, always inventory the nearest alternate beam.  Their saints are people who stepped into apparently empty air and were caught by a geometry that had not yet finished collapsing.  Icons depict these saints mid-stride, one toe still printing the vapor where footing will arrive in another second.

Language has adapted to refuse certainty.  Every noun is appended with a liquid consonant that can be throat-cleared into silence if the object sinks or is otherwise revoked.  Children practice the glottal stop the way mainland kids practice multiplication:  “table–hk, chair–hk, mother–hk,” releasing the syllable only when the referent proves persistent.  As a result, conversation sounds like people politely apologizing for realities they may have misspoken into being.

They fear three things, in ascending order:

1. Silence.  If the drip staggeria cease, either the water has found a new lower route (taking the floor with it) or the insects have sensed vibration humans cannot, in which case the next collapse is seconds away.  Therefore a resting adult will often hum into any handy pipe, feeding the city modest vibration in exchange for continued auditory footing.

2. Sunlight.  A direct shaft, rare as a verdict, illuminates particles mid-air and makes them visible as rust.  In that moment the citizens see their atmosphere for what it is:  a slow sandstorm of metal flakes, paint powder, insect wings, skin.  They call this phenomenon “the accounting,” and retreat into shaded angles until the indictment passes.

3. Dryness.  A day without condensate is remembered in nightmare.  Skin cracks, iron creaks, and the city’s adhesive philosophy unsticks.  Old people whisper of the Drought of ’93, when a week of low humidity loosened a bolt no one knew was structural, sending the entire South-East sag two meters.  They speak of it the way other cultures speak of famines:  a horror so complete it has become mythic in real time.

Outsiders ask what they want.  The question is meaningless:  wanting implies a horizon, and horizons here are floorboards under negotiation.  Yet if you hang a lantern in the right drowned parlor and wait, eventually a delegate arrives—usually a Runner still dripping from a recent traverse—who will offer, on behalf of the Half-Submerged, the following statement:

“We have misplaced the perpendicular.  Return it when found.”

Then they retreat, stepping backwards across a void that has not yet finished becoming space, trusting that by the time their foot arrives the absence will have organized into a place they can stand.

And so far, it always has.
