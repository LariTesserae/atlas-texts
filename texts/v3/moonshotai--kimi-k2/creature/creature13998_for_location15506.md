They call themselves **The Continuists**, though no two would pronounce the name quite the same: some linger on the second vowel as if tasting it, others drop it to a consonant rasp that sounds like coughing on ice. They are the slow inheritors, a lineage whose genealogical unit is not the child but the patch of terrace that a given family has attended for eight, ten, sometimes fourteen contiguous centuries. Rows of surnames are incised into the balustrades like cumulative frost. Each generation etches its own initial just a fingernail-width lower, letting knapped letters ride the same downward creep of stone. Thus history stretches rather than steps; a great-grandfather and a granddaughter belong to two slightly different elevations, but the same inch of lichen is invited to swell over both their initials.

Every Continuist carries a work basket woven from the aluminum strapping once used to secure cable spools. They treat these baskets less as containers than as portable arteries: spores, seedlings, cap nuts, glass beads, and yesterday’s unread messages are all tucked among one another the way blood platelets mingle with lymph. The elder in a household keeps the basket until their vision thickens; then they “drop descent,” stepping down a single terrace to relinquish custody to whichever cousin has come of age exactly one ledge below. There is no ceremony, only the hour in which a copper kettle stops hovering at lip-height and is suddenly set permanently onto the colder tier. When the elder looks back up the cliff, the baskets ascend like hesitant punctuation dots correcting the vertical sentence of their life.

Physically they favor taut, spare frames, not due to starvation but because excess flesh absorbs moisture that might otherwise be exhaled into communal kettles. Long bones are slightly bowed, witness to the altitude’s polite envy of circumference. Their skin color is ambiguous: cloud-cheeked olive, lunar-bleached ash, graphite under old scars—none is accent or ancestry so much as a reflection of the stone behind the shoulder that faces you. Eyes dilate whenever a conversation slows because the pupils are learning how much grayscale they can drink from mist before mist drinks back.

Language has learned the same sedate metabolism. The Continuists speak in long spirals: a transitive verb spreads sideways into six clauses of conditionality until its original subject has been obscured by clinging adverbs the way moss obscures initials. Sentences often end with “if the absence hears,” a phrase no one defines but everyone repeats precisely when their speaker’s breath cools. They behave as though words, once released, are anonymous moisture: good for others to breathe six terraces away, bad to claim as private rain.

They sleep in cells scooped right into the underhang of a terrace overhang, each vault sized like a lidded eye socket. Interior plaster is mixed with powdered mica from leaching tunnels, so whole rooms glint gently—when the bulb fails, the walls remember the filament. Dreaming is communal: if one inhabitant starts to speak at 3 a.m., their neighbor three ledges down may finish the sentence seven nights later, having drifted into it by the rhythm in the cables. The Continuists record these half-conversations—dream-parabolas—by threading black glass beads onto dangling cords outside their windows. Each cord is a communal unconscious. Visiting strangers attribute omens to the patterns, but locals only consult the bead curtains to recall tomorrow’s errands.

A Continuist wedding is not marked by vows but by the grafting of ropes. Courtship begins when two children trade lengths of braided root grown in basket soil. If, after a decade, the grafts grow a seamless sheath where the fibers cross, the partners descend two terraces carrying the intertwined piece. The lower families pierce the rope with brass rings scavenged from factory relays, believing that the soft hum of aging magnets will keep their future quarrels at 60-cycle hush. No diets are kept on the nuptial night; instead, their bowls contain the very lichens that were clipped from around both sets of initials decades earlier. The lichen tastes of neither bitterness nor salt, only the precise memory of stone at the parties’ combined age. The couple’s first joint possession is a calendar etched on a mirror shard: each month is shaded by holding the shard against one morning’s particular shade of clinging cloud.

Death is believed vertical. When a heart stills, the shroud is a cylinder of woven coal-silk open at both ends. The body is stood upright in the elevator going down—not to be interred, but to ride the cables for exactly one hundred descents and one hundred ascents, “until the altitude forgets the limbs.” Meanwhile family members descend on foot and harvest the dead person’s initials from the balustrade. The carved slivers are steeped in sulfuric vinegar to release the oxidized minerals. The brine becomes a pigment used to repaint the city’s directional arrows that have begun to buckle into question marks. Thus the Continuists turn bones into signage, and signage into the art of convincing later feet that any direction is still plausible.

But the most singular creature dwelling here is not a Continuist, though it dresses like one at dusk. It stands “one-half-a-head too short,” as the proverb measures all provisional things. Its clothes are stitched from lustrous moth wings, not pinned but respiring against the cooler skin inside. At any given hour only one such figure exists, and only for that generation; always a stranger who “arrived by lamplight,” though no rail badge records the journey. Its voice arrives as the entire elevator shaft resonating in a single overtone. In that moment the Continuists feel the entire city’s balconies as a slow accordion folded around their ribcages. The stranger never names itself, but the community has nicknamed it **The Proxy of Dormant Fall**—an engine for remembering the future collapse.

In its left palm the Proxy carries a seed unlike any they cultivate: jet on one side, mirror on the other. It offers no explanation, simply plants the seed at the western tree’s root and leaves. Over the next century the tree extrudes a new ring around it, neither bark nor metal, but a slow ridge of refracting dusk. By the time that ring thickens finger-wide, the seed has vanished, and another stranger appears wearing the former seed as a button on a newly sewn coat. Thus the mirror is inherited downward, polished by the city’s gradient until finally it reflects not sky or terrace dwellers but the mountain’s imagined root, dangling in a luminosity darker than absence.

Every Continuist child is taught that the city isn’t descending—it’s **accreting height in the opposite direction**: the mountain’s slow refusal of summit, its ghostly climb toward substrata yet to be invented. The children repeat the lesson while queueing for vinegar rations, hopping from left to right foot so their small elevation tattoos feel momentarily alike. Deep in the marrow they know that sometime, many centuries after their initials fade beneath lichen altogether, the western tree will ring outward to become the globe’s new equator, and the mountain will have folded itself into a silence no living voice can moisten.

Until then, they keep the terrace moss clipped to fingertip length, polish elevator cables with used onion skins, and time their laughter to the distant gearbox cardiac thump—an arrhythmic murmur that travels faster downward than sound up. Every Continuist’s final whisper, whatever its literal content, is secretly addressed to that noise. Not to still it, but to keep it speaking.
