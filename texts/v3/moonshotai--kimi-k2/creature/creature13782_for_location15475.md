**The Unremembered Inhabitants of Wyrdemoor**

The thing is, none of them ever meant to stay. They arrived as visitors, as scholars, as the merely curious—then stayed because curiosity itself grew moss-grown, because questions adapted themselves to the slow architecture of pause.

The first ones—let's call them *the Carved*, though their names wore off centuries ago—came when the city was still arguing with gravity. They built of necessity but learned the language of allowance, stones shaped with such hollow reverence that the stones themselves began to soften. These early inhabitants discovered that to build here was to enter negotiation with a place that had already publicly abandoned its own foundations. They became translators between the city and itself, speaking in gestures too patient for hands. Their faces—what remains of them—are pressed into the undersides of archways like pressed flowers achieving sepulchral clarity. If you press your palm to the inverted cheek in the western arch (where the wall curves inward like regret), you might feel their breathing suspended somewhere in the stone's slow metabolism.

Then came *the Dissenters*—not a family but a tendency, travelers who mistook the city's stasis for refuge. They tried to live as though decay were merely a slower form of walking. They kept gardens of argument, plantings grown from the city's own discourse—crops of "possibly" and "perhaps" watered with their own evaporated certainty. These crops grew lush but fed no one. The Dissenters learned to eat their own questions, distilling them to essences sharp enough to cut bread. This didn't kill them—death had reached an accord with the city too—but hollowed them into something translucent and useful. They developed a kind of reverse farming: grew less certain with each harvest until they reached the perfect uncertainty of moss, at which point they lay down with the city's breathing and became part of its lung-work. You can still exhume their footprints, but only if you first forget your destination.

The third migration—the *Needle-shapers*, who came bearing tools sharp enough to imply urgency—discovered what the city had always known: language was wet here. They arrived like stitches trying to seam something that had deliberately unseamed itself. What they shaped was not stone but the *memory* of stone—not memories of stones, but stones themselves remembering they had once been mountain. The Needle-shapers learned to inscribe sideways, finding in the city's willingness to flatten a generosity more precise than any chisel. Their tools grew moss-soft even as they used them, grinding down to oily whispers that could no longer distinguish between cutting and caressing. One by one they put away their implements—not surrendered, exactly, but *retired*, the way a story retires from its version when the listener grows throatful of response. Their fingers remain, knuckle-deep in rock, still describing curves the way certain hands describe absent faces.

Now comes the perplexing return. For the city does not *empty* in the way places empty—it *funnels*. Into each departed guest it pours a concentrate of dwelling, so that walking the wider world becomes an act of carrying the Wyrdemoor somewhere else. Your pockets grow moss-lined. Your footsteps begin leaving grammars of evaporation. The city sends emissaries not as visitation but as *evaporation*—when you thought you saw rain on the bus window that replied to your specific sadness, that was Wyrdemoor's breathing moving through you. Some have grown so saturated with borrowed settlement that they must periodically visit grocery stores or post offices merely to practice the foreign art of *transaction*. One woman in Cleveland collects puddles in jars, each labeled with a date when the city taught her a new way to drown quietly.

But there are still *new* arrivals—though never quite new. They come recognizing the city from dreams that predate their own births. A child will drag her parents to the hollow between certain rocks, insisting this is where her crib stood. A man will arrive aged seventy with the preternatural calm of someone who has spent a lifetime returning to where he has not yet been. These temporal immigrants bring the city gifts it already contains, wrapped in anxieties the place has already metabolized. They leave with their faces slightly more transparent—so that in certain angles of afternoon light, passersby see through them to the memory of their own future visits.

And the city keeps *children*, though it never *births*. They emerge from within the water-cathedrals, stepping out perfectly formed—seven, eleven, nineteen as needed—always at that age where the body's certainty exceeds its intelligence. These children remember everything the city has chosen to forget. Their name is *Urn-children*, not because they come from death but because they arrive like questions placed carefully into living. They speak in the syntax of erosion-not-yet-eroded. They play games that excavate the hiding places of lost alphabets. When they wander too close to the world's edges, the city calls them back with the whispered sound of every mother's exact heartbeat. They never age, but sometimes they *archive*—a nine-year-old will simply sit down one morning and become a tree that buds lowercase letters each spring.

There is also *the Archivist*, though this is less a person than a rotation. Someone—anyone—eventually notices that they have started cataloguing not what the city *is* but what it has *stopped* being. A spun-sugar architecture of becoming's absences. The role seizes them gently: a woman finds herself brushing lichen aside to uncover an imprint where certainty once slept, a man realizes his hands now move with the precise hesitation of translation. The Archivist does not catalog *to preserve* but to *understand the shape of departure itself*. They wear the city's breathing like a coat that won't quite fasten, carrying stacks of transience bound with threads spun from their own unmaking. The position rotates as people realize they have recorded their own exit. The last Archivist steppedsideways through a wall that briefly resembled a doorway, leaving behind an index that reads:

1. Gravity's agreement to pause
2. All conversations that ended three syllables too early
3. The specific hunger of stones between 2 and 4 AM
4. Rain rehearsing its eventual failure

Then there are *the Eaten*—not consumed but *incorporated*, those who tried to leave. They make it to the boundary and discover they've grown porous, the city's wetness having filled them like canteens taking shape. These individuals attempt to walk away only to find themselves carrying the city in their chests like enlarged hearts. They enter the wider world as *intervals*, walking perfectly between moments, arriving places minutes before their own absence. Some become meteorologists specializing in weather not yet broken. Others work as librarians, shelving books that decided not to be written. None of them return—not because they cannot locate the city, but because they've become the city's method of extension. When you meet someone inexplicably moist around the eyes, who answers questions with the slow patience of moss, you are meeting Wyrdemoor trying itself on in human size.

The city also keeps its own *shadow-lives*. These aren't dark doubles but *nonexistent* inhabitants—people who might have arrived but chose elsewhere, whose potential dwelling has grown roots of regret across entire wings of buildings that thus remain perpetually unremembered. You can sometimes feel them moving behind walls that go slightly wetter for no reason. A chair will retain the warmth of someone who *might have* sat there had their journey broken down three miles earlier. The word-apes converse fluently with these phantom possibilities, using grammar that conjugates verb-tenses of not-having-occurred.

And finally—though finally implies sequence when the place itself has voted against duration—the city keeps *itself*. Not metaphorically but literally. Somewhere near the center (though center requires an edge, which the city has negotiated away) exists a room that contains an exact miniature of Wyrdemoor. The miniature is *more* detailed: it includes every inhabitant the larger city has imagined and those it has chosen not to. Within it works a caretaker who looks exactly like whoever most needs to see themselves here. The caretaker spends their days adjusting the model's tiny doors, aligning its miniature water-droplets, feeding the word-apes with crumbs of conversation. They speak to the miniature when the larger city grows unsteady—opens windows into its past, renegotiates stretches of time that threatened to overgrow their permissions. When visitors stumble upon this room (they always stumble; no one ever finds it looking), the caretaker looks up with the exhausted calm of someone tending an orchard of perpetual almost.

When you prepare to leave (and you will, because the city has reached agreement with departure itself), the inhabitants gather. Not in farewell—that would require them to *remain separate* from your going. Instead they press close, breathing the city's wet breath into your pores until you're saturated enough to carry. The word-apes might offer a sentence—never the whole conversation, just enough syntactic structure for you to remember that languages can exist for their own beauty. The Urn-children press small smooth stones into your palms, pebbles shaped exactly like the afternoon you haven't quite lived yet. The Eaten smile with the sideways gentleness of those who know how eventually all paths recurl.

You exit between two droplets that have spent a thousand years deciding how best to not fall. The journey back down carries its own strange geometry—each step subtracts time you've already spent arriving. By the time you reach the base of the mountain (though mountain requires an absence underfoot, which grows softly beneath you), you've forgotten the ascent entirely. What you're left with is *carrying*: an extra shadow shaped like pausing, pockets full of water that never quite evaporates, breath that refuses to stop tasting of argument-in-reverse.

Years later, telling the story, you'll realize the city never ended. It moved into the telling itself—every word you speak now marinated so long in Wyrdemoor's patience that it arrives already half-forgotten. Your listener grows damp-eyed. Somewhere, a word-ape practices the pronunciation of their name.
