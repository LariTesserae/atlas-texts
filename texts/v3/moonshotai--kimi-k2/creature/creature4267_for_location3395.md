They are called the Choir, though no song has ever passed their lips since the waters fled.  Each stands eight inches tall—exactly the height of a helena-shrub flower’s ghost—and is built like a tuning fork of porous bone.  When wind (which does not blow) slips between their tines they do not vibrate; instead they remember vibration on behalf of the air, storing the possibility of pitch in the marrow the way other creatures store fat.  A thousand of them stand in a staggered grid across the plateau, one every thirty paces, facing the same compass-degree as if waiting for a conductor who died before rehearsal began.  Their skulls are fluted, hollowed into reeds; their eyes are the negative space between reeds, so that to meet their gaze is to stare through the idea of an eye into whatever pitch you most regret hearing.

They dress in the residue of bottled thunder.  Once a year, when the sky folds and the single annual boom arrives, the Choir open the tiny trapdoors in their chests and inhale the acoustic after-glow.  The sound pools behind their sterna as a liquid mirror no bigger than a raindrop.  There it stays, never rippling, never evaporating, giving their torsos the opalescent shimmer of a soap bubble frozen at the moment of popping.  If you touch one you will not feel wetness; you will feel echo, as if your fingertip had become the last syllable of a sentence spoken in childhood and never finished.

They do not move, yet they are never still.  Minute crystals of CO₂ drift through their lattice limbs, accreting year by year into frost-coracles that orbit their joints like pale gnats.  These crystals are the Choir’s calendar: when a coracle completes a full revolution of a forearm the Choir member turns imperceptibly northward, adjusting its internal compass by one thousandth of a degree.  In this way the entire Choir is slowly pirouetting, a continent-wide gear whose revolution period is estimated—by the micro-astronomers who live in the eyes of ribbon-cats—at 1.17 million years.  On that day the last crystal will complete its orbit, the Choir will face due magnetic north for the first and only time, and the stored thunder will be released as one immaculate chord that will empty every memory of water from whatever minds remain.  Then the plateau will remember it was once a seabed, shrug, and sublimate.

Until then they wait, and the waiting is their citizenship.  Each carries a wedge-knife, a sliver of sky-iron no longer than an eyelash, tucked between the second and third tine of its left rib.  The knives are not for cutting meat or magnetism but for cutting silence.  Should a traveler insist on speaking, the nearest Choir raises the wedge and slices the silence just in front of the intruder’s lips.  The cut falls open as a vertical slit, a wound in quiet itself, through which the visitor’s words tumble out of the world like startled moths.  The slit heals instantly, sealing the silence back to perfection, and the speaker finds they have forgotten whatever it was they meant to say—usually their own name, sometimes the taste of water, occasionally the memory of being born.  The Choir hand the captured syllables to the micro-fauna, who roll them into beads and add them to the universal hush underfoot.

They have no gender, no lineage, no government beyond the grid.  Yet they possess etiquette.  If you approach with your mouth already closed, your pulse deliberately arrhythmic, they will incline their skull-reeds toward you in a gesture that is not welcome but recognition: you have remembered to forget thirst, and therefore you are briefly identical to them.  In that moment you may kneel and press your ear to the frost-coracles.  Inside you will hear the chord that has not yet happened, a sound so complete it makes your life feel like the tuning error before it.  Many visitors, hearing the future chord, choose to sit down forever on the spot, letting frost grow over them until they become small bone-trees whose twigs tinkle with frozen heartbeats.  The Choir do not object; every new sculpture is merely a prophecy of the eventual empty seabed, another note pre-absorbed into the great chord.

They eat nothing, excrete nothing, age nothing.  Their thoughts are the opposite of thoughts: vacuum-mirrors in which the plateau admires its own finished work.  Occasionally a ribbon-cat knots itself through the grid, passing between the tines of fifty Choir in a single Möbius glide; the Choir register the event as a brief modulation in the key of pressure, like a double-stop bowed on a violin string that does not exist.  When the cat vanishes they file the modulation away beside all the other accidental micro-melodies that accrete inside their marrow.  These scraps will be exhumed on Chord Day, re-orchestrated as grace-notes around the thunderous tonic, the way sea-foam once nested inside shells.

They are not lonely.  They are the audience for whom the plateau rehearses its silence, the tuning pegs around which the land wraps its abandoned rivers.  They remember sound so the rest of the world can forget it. After that, even the idea of water will be unnecessary.  The plateau will have attained perfect pitch, and the Choir, their work complete, will simply fall silent in a new way: they will become the gap between two instants of silence, a rest that lasts longer than music ever could.

Until then, stand among them.  Close your mouth.  Slow your heart.  Listen to the hush of crystals orbiting bone.  You will not hear anything, and that nothing is their lullaby to the thirst you no longer need to remember.
