Whoever makes this rhythm their life does not call themselves a citizen.  They call themselves “the cadence-bound.”

They are the ones whose bones have learned to hum at sixty-three hertz, the exact pitch the sluice gates exhale.  Over decades that note migrates inward:	first a vibration in the ankles along the conveyor sleepers, then a soft syncopation in the hips every afternoon when the brass whistles warm, and finally a tick inside the malleus so fine that each cadence-bound can stop counting hours and simply listen to find out what part of the day they inhabit.

Some have pale ellipses along their necks where the inspector-automatons once riveted identification tags.  The holes never closed; instead the skin grew a rim of glassy callus, a surprising translucence in which tiny filaments of copper wire still glint—tools for completing unknown circuitry in case the Foundry ever needs to close certain loops.  Whenever the net of whistles drops a shard, these collars ring faintly for three seconds, like forks touched to crystal—that is how people know to steer away.

Children arrive with mouths already expert at holding water.  At four years they begin the Mouth-Walk: ten perfect paces, jaws so wide the mandible shows white scars at the joints for life.  Their elders mark growth spurts not by height but by how many heartbeats pass before the swallowed mouthful sluices out again.  Those who can still fold the water behind their uvulas after seventy pulses are set aside to become Registers, living clocks assigned to different conveyor modules so the city may stay evenly spaced in time.  A Register’s surname disappears; they are issued a single metallic whistle the size of a coat button which they wear embedded in the hollow of the throat.  When the city does its century reversal, each Register must reverse the whistle—turning the aperture inward, toward the larynx—so that in the inrush of swallowed air their pulse flips phase and keeps the reversed belts coherent.

Bodies carry other clandestine mechanisms.  In the elbows of coil-shifters you can feel small pads of cartilage grown to precise arcs, plainly engineered so they can swing overhead pulley levers with ergonomic graces neither forged nor taught.  The heels of Load-wives carry an inner shelf of bone that widens every year; when they plant their feet on the belt tread, the shelf forms an instantaneous socket that accepts the upward thrust of the next plank so nothing ever jars.  Under the tongues of Spray-trimmers lie micro-bladders filling with solvent whenever tears run downwind from the breached river; they taste the fluids for halide surplus and, if necessary, feed a coolant redirect through ducts in the gums.  Call this living chemistry.  Call it belonging by osmosis.

Sanity, here, is distributed.  There is no mayor, only the Frequency Choir, twelve people positioned at key vector angles—cocklofts, smokestacks, under the middle whistle net—that shape the soundscape so thought can happen without rupture.  Each carries a Ff-conch, a white-palladium spiral wide as a forearm.  They raise these shells like megaphones, letting the whistlenet resonance pour through their captured lung air and modulate into coherent chords.  When a quarrel starts on belt 47C the argument becomes an unresolved dissonance transmitting through the beams; the next Choir member on 49D widens the labial opening of her conch until the harmony is redirected—not solved, but married to an undertone that no longer threatens collapse.  That is how justice is breathed away.

The outermost ridge houses a single order: the Drifters.  They weave the same material as the whistles—phosphor bronze—but into long hide-like leaves.  One side is mirror-bright; the other dyed with a gunmetal foil that responds to ambient infrared.  In the ultraviolet day they walk the ridge wearing the mirrored faces outward, reflecting sun so violently the stone seems snow-bound.  At dusk they reverse the cloaks and become living negatives; hatched veins in the foil appear like black scars on chests, correlating with hidden fissures inside the granite.  By moonlight they stride silently overhead, cataloging stress fractures against the city’s slow descent.  The cloaks do not protect; they translate.  When the mountain underneath finally slips a fraction, the fracture patterns propagate upward through the bronze veins at the same speed sound travels in saltwater.  A Drifter may stand on the shore a mile above the surf and feel the plate grind underfoot before the cranes feel anything at all.

And the invisible other:  
Living under the granite, feeding off the steam that the Works never quite manages to vent, are creatures no human truly foresaw.  Formerly ore-chasing mites, they have multi-generationally repurposed themselves to the exact vapor constituents of cast-iron condensation.  In the darkness three strata below the lowest dormitory they coat the pipes like fur, building tessellated lattices that inch along seams night by night.  Their exoskeletons accrete excess copper stolen from every wire junction too small to be missed; over decades those flakes form filaments extending downward until the entire quarry root is webbed in trace-metal nerves.  One day, surely, the city will turn enough teeth south that the ripple equals a bolt as from rivet under Titanic plates.  The mites—now one organ, continent-sized—will tighten the latticework and the Works and the granite knuckles will groan into a new chord, deeper than any whistle yet imagined.  No one living will hear it except in the bones: a bony pulse-shifted lullaby resetting heartbeat time for whomever still exists two centuries hence.

Until then, generations reshuffle themselves across moving belts, counting, hoarding breath, tilting signs.  Their faces have the same gritty softness as worn basalt; their words arrive with the laconic inevitability of cooling glass.  If you meet one, they will not greet you.  They will simply raise their chain of tin squares until the one etched “visitor” slides over the “resident” plaque—an unblinking semaphore.  Only then may you follow, matching the slow five-kilometre pace, learning how to drape your mouth with water, how to damp your own song until the whistles spare your lungs.

Eventually you realize you are not being shown the city.  
You are being subtlely tuned to it.  
And when you finally hear the sixty-three-second hush between each sluice sigh—really hear it, inside the folds of your own syrinx—you discover that it is not the sound of machinery.  
It is a conversation still in progress between the millennia-old granite and whatever chooses, century after century, to live inside its knuckles.
