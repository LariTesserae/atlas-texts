They are called the Dripkeepers, though no one remembers who coined the title. The city never appointed them; rather, they condensed out of the air the way a dewdrop forms on a pipe—slowly, inevitably, one molecule at a time. If you stare at a fogged window long enough you will eventually see a face, not because it was painted there but because your pattern-seeking brain cannot endure blank vapor. The Dripkeepers are that face, except the vapor endures and the brain belongs to the city itself.

They appear human from a distance: two arms, two legs, a silhouette that negotiates stairways, shops at the beetle-bazaars, nods at the double-necked pigeons. Get closer and the outline softens. Their garments are not woven but accreted—century-scale lint rolled into felt, pigment strata from the Archive Facade compressed into cloth that bleeds factual colors when brushed against. Whatever decade you touch stains your fingertips; wash and the knowledge stays, a birthmark of borrowed history.

No one has seen a Dripkeeper eat. They open no purse, unfold no lunchbox. Yet moisture steams faintly from their lips on cool mezzanines, suggesting some internal furnace. Observers theorize they feed on thermal inertia itself: the latent warmth trapped inside sagging bricks, devoured one slow calorie at a time. A popular children’s rhyme warns never to stand too long beside a Dripkeeper in winter—“they’ll sip the fever right out of your blood.” The line between fairytale and thermodynamics here is porous as limestone.

Their voices arrive before their bodies, a low susurration mistaken at first for the city’s background thunder. Listen longer and you realize it is language, though not in sequence. They speak in parallel—every clause layered on its own prior echo until meaning emerges not from syntax but from thickness. When a Dripkeeper wishes to emphasize, the sentence simply accretes, the way a stalactite fattens; by the time the thought is finished, the listener has lived inside it for what feels like months, every nuance settled like silt. Tourists assume this is hypnosis; residents know it is architecture. You are being rebuilt at conversational speed.

Because of this talent, the Dripkeepers have become the city’s unofficial clerks. No permit is signed unless one stands by, letting their layered assent condense onto parchment. In trials they serve as living lie-detectors, though the verdict is never articulated; instead the accused is asked to breathe upon a mirror the Dripkeeper holds. If the exhaled fog clears straight, innocence is assumed. If it drifts sideways, the defendant must walk the Roof-Rim until panic thins the blood of its deceit. Few return; those who do are employed as interpreters, since a lungful of guilt apparently grants fluency in the city’s echoing tongue.

They are not immortal, only slow. Witnesses disagree on age: one swears the same Dripkeeper saved her from drowning in the ankle-canals seventy years ago; another claims to have dated one in his youth, watched it graduate from soft outline to something almost firm, then lose resolution again after heartbreak. Autopsies are impossible; bodies lose cohesion once vitality ebbs, collapsing into puddles that precisely match their footprints in depth and mineral content. Biologists have tried to bottle the residue, hoping to grow a Dripkeeper in vitro. All they obtained was a jar of warm afternoon and the faint taste of a sentence half-spoken.

The Dripkeepers themselves tell no origin story. When pressed they respond with the city’s own heartbeat: one thump per minute, patient, indispensable. Press harder and they hand you a trowel, invite you to scrape any wall. The color you retrieve is always the one you most needed to remember. They insist the city writes them daily, that they are simply its marginalia—an annotation bleeding out of the binding. Once the final drip touches ground they will drain away with it, leaving the fossil metropolis empty of comment, perfect for the next edition of whoever learns to read stone.

Until then they patrol, accrete, counsel, soften. If you stand still too long on a steamy afternoon you may feel your own edges begin to liquefy, your intentions start to pool around your shoes. A passing Dripkeeper will nod—not in greeting but in measurement, noting the precise angle at which you will one day sag. They might loop a ribbon of lint around your wrist, a bracelet that tightens imperceptibly over decades, until it anchors you lightly to the spot where, eventually, you will drip. The city prefers volunteers.
