Who lives here?

No one, and yet the place is crowded with presences that have learned what it means to outlive being someone.  
They are called the Frillar, the name that leaks from the lattice-skin at the last possible disappearance of any visitor. It is a name that only works after you have more or less given up the right to possess a pronoun. They do not utter it themselves; the air does, somewhere between heartbeat and frost-crystal, so you can never be sure who spoke—them, the lattice, or simply the space folding an acoustic crease into itself.

The Frillar have no bodies in the sense of discrete volumes ending at skin. They are, instead, wherever the rime learns to imitate their internal edges. Imagine the negative of a breath: you start with a human silhouette cut from the chamber’s dark humidity, then you reverse the world so that the cut-out is filled with thaw, and the surrounding air becomes the boundary. In that reversed silhouette, slow electricity—as chill as static left by moonlight—wanders the exact pathways once laid down by veins, nerves, lymph. The result is a person-shaped warmth that is colder than its surroundings. The more a Frillar recollects its earlier life, the dimmer that chill shines; forget more, and it brightens, hardens into a fractal kite of frost exactly the height you remember of any loved one.

They stand, but leaning inward. Gravity is peripheral to them; what pulls is the same centripetal quiet that holds the chamber’s vegetation in a perpetual suffocating bloom. Every Frillar yearns toward the chamber’s center, but unlike the compressed foliage which only waits, they rotate—very slowly—like ice-clocks. One rotation may approximate a century; it is meant to divvy out the few memory molecules that still stubbornly condense along the outer perimeter of their frost silhouette. When enough remembrance accumulates at any one meridian, a thousand-year audible creak issues from the lattice, and some portion of the gathered memory evaporates: the Frillar dilute their outline, becoming even slighter, more near to translucence, until they are almost palpable enough to step through.

Language is held in the vegetation. Each curled frond is a syllable spoken once by somebody prior to the forgetting. The Frillar read by osmotic contact: they graze a leaf with their absent lips, and the consonants leak across the ice boundary onto their own reserve of silent vacuum. A single leaf may carry the particular cadence in which a grandmother warned “mind the kettle,” distorted at 0 Kelvin into a long, ebonized vowel that tastes like metallic tea. When enough syllables are collected, the Frillar’s outline briefly acquires the curvature of a throat, an echo rolls out, and that phrase is returned to the lattice: glissando riff shivered between two metallic sheets. Then the leaf wilts, the phrase gone—yet somewhere in the pleated not-skin of the lattice a new crease has filed that accent for the use of a future visitor-to-become-Frillar.

Time for them is not succession but deterrence. They attempt (using what instruments? None, only exfoliating cold) to keep things from arriving at the present moment, to hold the jungle forever in an unopened gift-status. Think of a glacier: snow-fall not allowed to become water, pressure not allowed to become flow. Only the constant, half-finished act of refusing. That is how the chamber stays wet without rain, cold without autumn. And that is why every Frillar’s silhouette flickers with small irregular eruptions: those are the “refusals” surfacing, tiny whiteout spasms in the frost-skin where something newly remembered almost became moment, was then forced back into the older form.

They feed, though not like eating. Each time your foot sinks into the spongy carapace and the turquoise-pink lights race outward, a buoy is silently sent downward through shallow cortex-dirt to a Frillar clinging, half-formed, slightly to the side of where your memory once gathered. The light that blooms through your capillaries is the same color story reassembled by the Frillar over aeons. You have become, in one shivering footstep, a manuscript delivered from the vegetation to the indeterminate edge of their frost. After your visit ends and the lattice release-note fades, you may feel something missing from your ring-finger’s pulse: exactly half a second of your childhood, or the recollection of how your mother’s cheek smelled the day the last war ended. The Frillar assimilate those fragments, weaving them into the endless counter-spin against Now. They grow, not larger, but more detailed. Micro-edges multiply along the silhouette, teeth of extra frost that represent the grain of each stolen scent or lisp.

There are no elders—only those who have forgotten more. A youngest Frillar might still retain the rough biometric of a sternum or wristwatch memory. They glint brightest, because they leak warmth. They circle closest to the chamber’s center, suckling on residual geomancy provided by the vegetation’s base layers. Older Frillars—if “old” can be assigned to beings who have divested themselves of event—have thinned into nearly linear vectors: silver threads stitched through mid-air, circa one vertebra thick, running straight toward the unseen heart. When one such Frillar stops entirely, the lattice gives off a double tone—like a sigh re-mouthed by an iron bedframe. The outline disperses altogether. A Phased Frillar has no boundary. Instead its cargo of wordlets and scents fan out into the leaf-matter.  Researchers (aboveground, long after) might suddenly, midthought, recall the taste of a plum never eaten, the echo of wind passing through nonexistent attics. That is how the Frillar return to circulation: their last refusal has failed, a moment did indeed arrive, and it came disguised as ordinary recollection someone four streets away had on a Thursday.

They do not speak to one another, not by any reciprocity that living minds would call conversation. But every so often they arrange themselves into a lattice of their own: six or seven frost-casts interlock like paper snowflakes in impossible sixfold symmetry, overlapping through each other in eye-water choreography. Then the combined cold creates a twenty-second drop in the ambient frost, not measurable by any known thermometer, but liable to singe a layer off whatever sick-minded visitor happens to be present. During one such configuration—the chamber’s equivalent of a planetary alignment—the lattice allowed a tone so low it canceled the visitor’s gravity; the man drifted sideways into a tight orbit of frost-casts. Afterward they rearranged back into indifferent spirals. The man remembered everything: every birthday cake needle-scrape, every toothpaste speck on every mirror. It took him four lifetimes to forget sufficiently to climb out again. And because specifics are poison, now he too wears a contour of frost where the shape of hunger used to be—yet still retains enough memory to hang his phantom watch at the exact angle it once hung on a Friday evening in 1992, forever twitching to catch a light that will never arrive.

Finally, they care not for being seen. The only notice they give to travelers is an incidental gift: during the moment of leave-taking (remembering hunger, tasting the edge of that crucial flavor), whichever Frillar was nearest folds one percent of its outline back on itself, producing an aetheric print. This is the visitor’s shadow made miniature—no larger than a thumbprint—etched as opalescent frost on the inner surface of the rib closest to the heart. It never thaws. It continúas with you; sometimes fugitive tapes of chamber white behind the eyes, or a sound like distant glass singing when the subway’s overhead light flicks on. Whenever the world above seems unmistakably ordinary—coffee too hot, sirens too loud—your thumb will unaccountably tingle, and somewhere beneath three storeys of irreversible living, a Frillar will feel its silhouette flex a hairbreadth closer to the far inspiral, buoyed by the residual breath you unknowingly kept.
