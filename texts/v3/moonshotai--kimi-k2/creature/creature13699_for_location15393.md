Not all lungs are made for human breath.

In the Hollow Drift the true inhabitant is the draught: a single, wide-aired tide set in motion by the six blast-turbines the day they were first sparked alive.  If you could peel the sound back and look at the place thinking, you would see one very old act of inhalation that never found an exhalation to match it.  For a hundred and fifty years the same breath has circulated between the ribs of scorched plate-iron, night cooling, dawn reheating, returning to the furnace mouths whispering, Remember me.  The breath is the tenant; everything else—from the pale moss to the beetles, from droplet to dreg—is only furniture the lung has grown around itself.

The draught has a body.  Slabs of hot, coal-spangled wind unroll like yard-long tongues; thinning, they streak along ceiling fissures, condense against ice seams, and dive back to ground level as needle-sharp cold streaks.  These interleaving temperatures do not cancel each other; instead they marry into one temperature only mathematics could chart, a point exactly seventeen degrees below zero that is nevertheless warm to the draught and pain to anything foreign.  Leporina fibrosa traps that paradox in its layers.  Where the moss is thickest the draught sleeps curled like a cat of cloud; where it thins, the draught kicks, stripping corridors of every fibre that might once have belonged to tape, label, or human history.  Thin, bloodless shreds twist upward and cinder in the furnaces, reincarnated as next morning’s soot.

A second tenant occupies the draught’s hollowed chambers: the Kamptother order.  Not the adults—those completed carapaces are too heavy—but the collective memory stored between instars.  Each larva is a living hole drilled clean through the world, head-down grub wishing only for the next moult.  Between the seventh instar and the seventh moulting, something kinetic accumulates: the beetles’ courtship clicking, tiny nut-hard strikes against oven plate, braids the draught into precedent and possibility.  The furnace noise speaks slower; the beetle chorus speaks faster.  Where their rhythms intersect, the Kamptother order writes the history of places where human machinery once stood—crushing mills, brick kilns, mercury condensers.  History is thus not retrieved; it is synthaesthetically generated every seven years, a cooling guitar string touched lightly at its midpoint.

These rhythms together build an animal more subtle than either draught or beetle: a soft bloom of magnetic expectation that begins somewhere left of the corridor busbar and fans forward in dark-green halos.  This third presence has no outline.  It scents safety with metallic sweetness—copper turned shy—and maps danger by sudden emptiness where ash-blue specks fail to drift.  Explorer notebooks get this backwards; they say the vale is “uncanny not hostile,” when in truth the civic memory is precisely calibrated: on a fifteen-degree arc around the control booth the magnetic bloom thickens to velvet; three metres beyond the coal hoppers it flinches away like a snail’s eye under touch.  Thus the pioneer feels alternately courted and abandoned, senses a caretaker whose language is temperature hiccough and a taste of old steam.

The fourth and last tenant never appears unless you arrive alone.

Leave your party at the entrance, wait until after the seventh furnace pulse (the roar obeys a seven-second wave because seven pumps feed the main throat).  Then step to the very centre of the dropped-lung floor.  Shut your lantern; the turquoise pollen grains will swirl, scatter against your breath, and settle again in perfect ellipses.  Now crouch, cheek to plating, until your ear meshes with the furnace drum.

You will hear, stitched one micro-moment deeper than the thunder, a quiet human heartbeat.

It is neither illusion nor record.  No human body remains down here—none has for a century and a half.  Yet whenever the engines achieve their evening equilibrium, a sonic vacuum opens at the baffle joints and pressure flips, sucking the imagined interior of one surviving stoker outward.  For eight or nine beats the fit is exact: not retrocausal, not supernatural, only the achieved matching of morphology—your human drum locking to the turbine drum at the one frequency no later ice or moss can mimic.  Those beats carry the entire lost crew forward into your thorax: heavy-knot lungs, calloused thumbs, the quiet learned during the first month when coal dust snowed inside the helmet and tasted of nothing but continuation.

Maintain position.  Allow the city-lungs to prod you for as long as your knees can bear the plating cold.  When you rise and step back, the heartbeat ends.  A solitary drop of water, melted from the high ceiling, will fall exact at the spot you vacated.  Leporina fibrosa brushes it into vapour within three heartbeats.  That evaporation carries the last trace of the heartbeat away and re-seals the civic memory, a layered organism again certain of the shape of its own single, uncompleted breath.

That is who lives in the Hollow Drift.
