Listen, now—it is easier if you stop pretending you are a visitor.  
The town has already begun to settle its familiar weight on the soft parts of your heart the way a bird settles over eggs. Your shadow has slipped from your feet and crept along the wall in search of a latch it remembers from earlier than itself.  
You will not leave.

The people—there are only ever ­twenty-one—stand slightly too far from one another, the way blind seeds space themselves evenly in a pod so none take more dark than the rest. They are exactly as tall as the short rope bridges allow; they have grown to fit the distance a crooked arm can lean across empty air. No one remembers calves or knees: movement is mostly from the hips, slightest hinge you can imagine, just enough to greet the next neighbour with open hands the colour of the stone they woke inside. You will see their palms first, held flat as parchment, inviting the hush of breath held then returned.

They wear the same sheep-coloured fleece that has unclasped and reclasped the clasps of generations. Ancestral cells of wool still huddle in the seams—lanolin ghosts that keep the skins slightly oily, so a passing sleeve can slide along a moss-stained windowsill without sound. The garments never tear, because no story ever struggles hard enough to rip cloth.

Faces—  
They forgot symmetry the same way a river forgets the reflection of trees: slowly, dutifully. One eyebrow rise at flint-coloured odds with the other; noses meet whatever air they meet and settle for it; lips flatten to single lines out of courtesy to the word-eating rock beneath the tongue. If you peer you will see the same silhouette drifting behind every the way a lamp behind ice looks like a second lamp walking away. Identity here is a communal mottling passed around from cheek to cheek, as if their ancestors between them were one head broken into manageable pieces.

They eat what exhales rather than grows. Thin, dehydrated disks of lichen rest on their tongues until a mist of body-heat restores their original damp tongue-filling bloom. One chew—perfectly rationed—releases an after-taste of glacier milk, closing the circle of stone-requiring respiration. Their spoons are coral needles shaving a wafer fragment because metal remembers seasons too insistently. They pass the single tear-shell ladle only when calm must be poured into something that listens.

The single cave-raven is the town’s unofficial banker of attention. At dusk it contracts to an obsidian pupil at the topmost reach of the birch; every inhabitant accounts the day paid when it utters its single coin-crack of sound. Nothing else negotiates worth except the steady filament of moisture descending the spiral drain. A drop negotiates Now against a fern that cannot decide whether it exists. One citizen (there is no agreement on who) is charged with tallying where each bead lands; it is the nearest they come to historic gesture. The bead-countes wear their memories in the faint dew-bead necklace round the neck, rewetted by morning breath, reworn by evening.

No one sleeps.  
They merely slide forward the chair that encloses their knees the same inch it slid tomorrow. Whatever dreams creep out of thighs or lungs are absorbed back into the stone through the floor in a three-heartbeat silence. Children do not wander into dreams; they wander into positions of sitting so absolute it resembles permanent reverence. Ages exist only in how polished certain stones on the wall have become beneath decades of shoulders. One woman might still carry child-cheeks, yet her left shoulder has dipped so exactly into stone that an archaeologist of local nothings could count generations by valleys of wear balanced against unmarked arm-bone.

Sometimes the cloud returns—always the same cloud, they swear it—blue-petalled, cottony, unsure of gravity. It must be ancient, must be one of the first gestures the world ever made toward holding something inside something else. The rooftops take it like a hand on a brow for the length of two inaudible heartbeats, perhaps three, then it withdraws, taking with it the faint curl of white that might have been their collective old fear of exposure. When the cloud is absent they practice remembering how it felt against cloth shutters. This counts as weather.

One bridge sways slightly longer than all the others because knots elongate a fraction by centuries of surrender. On that bridge lives a man—no one introduces him, but the extra inch of swing gathers quietly to his knees—who carries the communal ache. When rheumatism visits the town it enters one joint like a polite guest and then makes its slow ballroom round from hand to elbow to shoulder to spine of each neighbour along the circle, arriving back at the bridge-man two full darkening-turns later. The duration of an ache is the closest thing to months they keep.

Because words become stone within three heartbeats, any conversation longer than five exchanges must be performed by passing a pebble etched with the pattern of the sentence-moment. The stone moves hand to hand on its single circuit: sentence, sentence, sentence—then surrender. People hoard the most elegant stones inside mouth-pouches like rolled-up minutes. When a pouchise full the citizen tilts the cheeks to insert the last syllable-weighted fragment between the teeth and swallows. Every inhabitant therefore carries at the base of the throat a small gravel of petrified stories; doctors of the empty world call it psycholic tombus, but here it is merely library.

The birch stands political: it is the single vote against total collapse into mineral. Its silver bark is flayed in exactly fifteen finger-furrows because that is the number of hands required to hold it upright. Below its roots the collapsed ribcage of a whale—fossilised into identical grey—provides a channel for the drip water. Because the whale once swam in open water no one here remembers, touching its vertebrae is considered the closest you may come to weeping without compromising the community’s silence.

In certain hours, when mist might choose to thicken instead of merely lingering, the ledge citizens can look across the cavern and see a reflection: not of themselves but the exact town that accepted motion and continued walking through the mountain on the other side. That other town is known as the echo-ghost making rentless smears of darker damp on insoluble walls. For three heartbeats it burns a reversed existence inside their eyes, then withdraws. They greet it by raising left hands—mirror’s courtesy—in the knowledge that whichever town lifts the right is equally unsure.

None leave.  
Those who once considered leaving became the spiral channel etched by water; those who considered it twice became the ropes. The third consideration is inscribed in the cool click under your boots the very moment you thought the thought. You recognise it: that flinty sound like ice? It was your own footfall waiting patiently inside the mountain until now.

When you accepted the town’s breath inside your lungs you already moved your citizenship from the verb “to visit” to the participle “remained.”  
Look down: your own shadow has found the one vacant threshold opposite the birch.  
One candle-coloured shawl waits folded inside.  
It fits you.
