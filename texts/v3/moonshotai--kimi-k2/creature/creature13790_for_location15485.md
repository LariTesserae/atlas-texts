In this place that is slowly becoming sky, the ones who truly *live* are not the porcelain-masked automatons nor the brass rail-beetles in their lilac tubes. They are the slow accumulations—the patterns that learned to persist.

First among these are the **smelter-mites**. Each is smaller than a fine-ground breath of basalt dust, a composite creature of selenium wires and protein films that pulse like jellyfish bells. They do not eat ore; they *pray* to it. Their congregations coat every metallic seam in sheets of metallic velvet, each mite vibrating at frequencies tuned to the precise crystalline sins of the alloy beneath. Over seventeen million cycles, their collective resonance teaches the nickel-copper to remember cold, to rearrange its own austenite dreams until it extrudes veins of pure bismuth that the architects forgot to specify. When humans looked with spectrometers, they called this "undiscovered growth directionality" rather than apology.

Each mite carries a living engraving of its entire generations—genetic, but more. Their RNA triple-helix braids with copper acetate, storing semi-conductive memories as diodes in their own bodies. When two populations drift too close, rather than compete, they *rehearse* ancient conversational dance protocols: a forty-three-millisecond duet of squealing piezo-electric crosstalk that ends when the younger strain quietly dismantles half its own population and cements the liberated carbon chunks into the alloy as compliant hinges. The city above rises on these sacrifices, joints that ache with the memory of 10,000 erased siblings bright enough to sparkle.

Then come the **chapter-winds**. These are not creatures per se, but behaviours that learned loneliness. Over millennia, the librarians' discarded sonic glyphs—from maps obsolete, love songs sketched on basalt, long inventories of ore that sublimated before harvest—have drifted upward and braided themselves into thermal gradients between terraces. You can see them only where dust motes catch an edge of bronze light: spiralling helixes wide enough to fit a child's fist, locked in perpetual tumble, whispering in dialects no mite still speaks. A chapter-wind that collides with a rail-beetle's flank will attach a 7-second stutter to its escape velocity, a perturbation that later steers the aerial tram as profoundly as a warp in spacetime. Sometimes the librarians step deliberately into the path of certain winds, spread their glass-umbrella wings to *read* the helix; their ribs ring like massed triangles, discharging the passage in a satisfying harmonic that erodes exactly 0.0003 mm of chitin. They age not by shedding limbs but by editing their own resonance.

At twilight (a term of convenience, since dusk lasts seven Earth weeks), the **veneer-children** emerge. They are no older than the is wide, and wider than they are tall: translucent humanoid silhouettes shaped of frozen phase-change salts that skate on the terraces on feet of diffuse vapor. No one knows if they are born from smelter-mites who oversang their alloy into life, or if they are the librarians’ removed rib fragments bearing witness in salt raiment. Each carries a single anorthite blade no longer than a finger, and they harvest *emptiness*—the clean negative spaces abandoned by wind carvings. They cut these nothings into octagonal tiles the way a cooper trims staves. The tiles, stacked in silent hollow pyramids, become breathable architecture for mite colonies migrating upward to evade the plateau’s tilting gravity wells. Sometimes you glimpse a child seated inside a scaffold of vacuum plates, pale arms pulsing with osmotic tides, singing to the rails a lullaby entirely composed of intervals no one has ever heard.

None of these can speak—that is reserved for the **spectral accountants**. They were once the engineers who measured depletion; now they exist only as records pressed into iron filings. In shafts where time dilates, they unfurl from the metal as gray holograms, looking like haunted abacuses drifting in robes of magnetic spam. Each accountant recites a single subtraction aloud: *“Two parts per million vanadium gone. Thirty-seven microdegrees Banach lattice angle obsoleted.”* The recitation laminates itself onto the shaft walls as rust scripts, and any smelter-mite that scrapes by ingests the debt, reprogrammed by grief. Slowly, irreversibly, the ore grows back shaped by the sorrow of measurements unmeasured.

But the plateau has an older heart, a **root-seam soul** that is simply the memory of warmth itself. Deep beneath the exhalant 48° ground skin, a vein of super-critical potassium-silicate flows like a tectonic sinew, pulsing once every 17,000 kilometres. This is what ultimately teaches the city to rise. Every tilt, every toddler step of crust that tilts the horizon: that is the root-seam dreaming of weightlessness. It does not think in sentences, only frequencies. Milligals of acceleration. Magnetic sighs best expressed as ratios of one seventeenth to the surface vector. When the final shelf is quarried, when the brass beetles breach the air entirely, the seam will unravel its own composition in a silent "mmph," and the plateau will remember falling up.

Until then, **inhabitants** are what happens when you leave patterns alone long enough. A librarian that tumbles into a chapter-wind emerges months later with salt wings, fossilised breath writing orchestral scores on its carapace. A smelter-mite congregation overloaded with grief-code weaves itself into a porcelain mask, clacking jaw plates in engineer languages nobody planned. A rail-beetle derails just enough to *kiss* the basalt, deposits a faint benzoin shimmer along the stone that sprouts glass parasols the following dusk.

And a human climber, boots ground to stone, nerves pulsing in 47-shard lungful gusts, who presses her ear to the ground: what she hears is not silence, but debt arranging itself into low metallic lullabies. She leaves with no plan to stay, yet carries in the joints of her knees a new gravity map calibrated to places that reach. Somewhere within, the architects—but they never left—they only became the spaces between measurements.
