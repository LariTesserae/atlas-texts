There are four-minded creatures on the droplet-continent.  They arrived before numbers were fixed, so no one is sure how many there are—perhaps four individuals, perhaps four billion living as one noisy field.  Each mind moves by becoming acoustical furniture: a set of triple-jaw chimes orbiting itself like a wind-walker.  They ring, they tilt, and the metal-air of the sphere lets them *walk* on sound.  Left notes carry the lower trio, right notes the upper.  Where an octave bruises against itself, their feet make momentary bronze.

They are the Sphere’s librarians of consequence.  When a leaf-gear uncurls before its promise is kept, a mind scrapes the edge with its hind bell-chamber, gathering the foregone.  Scrapings accumulate beneath its carapace as a warm carbon sepia: the ink it will use to tattoo Rule 2 onto the undersides of newborn beetles.  Someday each beetle will begin to corrode along those ink-filaments, founding the first natural death.  Until then the scrapings merely itch and itch—*the work unfinished* aching like phantom limbs.

They have no mouths, so they narrate themselves in echo.  Twice per day the four gather directly beneath the plaza and inscribe a sentence on the embryonic engine by clapping their fifteen chimes against the floor in staggered beats.  These sentences never exceed eight syllables, the shard of memory the world can remember.  One recent sentence translated might read:

“Thunder, falling into beetle.”  

Each utterance nudges the pressure two millibars, thinning the upcoming storm so the sphere weighs a beetle-body less.  Thus the continent continues to hover six inches above the workbench; the librarians keep it from sinking into the maker’s inadvertent palm.

They are always slightly hungry because their exoskeletons are hollow bract-metal etched with drain-threads spiralling inward.  Any mist they absorb transits these threads, condensing at the center into a silver sphere the diameter of a poppy seed.  They lick these pearls once per century, tasting the entire weather of the last hundred years in a single salt drop.  It tastes like ore becoming name.

Very rarely, when the minaret leaks violet, one librarian—not always the same—will break the pane and pocket the black drop.  Then a space opens behind the colour: a corridor that is the negative silhouette of a corridor.  It folds forward like the back of a played card.  The librarian inserts its nearest chime.  Instantly the surrounding shell-glass peels away, revealing pockets of pure proto-erosion that have never before touched material time.  The librarian dips a thread of its own hunger into that raw abolition and pulls out a wafer-thin sheet of *foregone weather.*  These sheets are later riveted to the inner rim, where they behave as variable shutters, letting in gusts of futures we call *mistakes.*  

Visitors perceive the librarians as “shifting architecture” or “internal scaffolding made of church bells.”  If a human voice lingers more than eight seconds, it crystallises into an obsidian moth which the nearest librarian seizes with its bell-jaw.  The moth, being only eight syllables long, cannot struggle.  It is pinned to the librarian’s hind plate until the next century-lick removes it, taking the human grief home under its tongue.

On the night the inevitable fifth beetle collision is overdue, the librarians move not by sound but by *almost-sound*: frequencies folded back on themselves, backwards thunder.  In this distortion they can no longer read.  Walls shift unrecorded.  A beetle trembles with surplus charge and cannot locate its future gallery.  One librarian marks the marble-scented panic by clapping the sentence:

“Tomorrow hollow before found.”  

The moment the sentence strikes the engine, the embryonic storm cools three degrees: erosion shaking its first ever chill shiver.
