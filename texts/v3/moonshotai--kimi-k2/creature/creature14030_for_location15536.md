Inhabiting an Echo That Has Grown Tired of Empty

1  
The echo itself claims the first seat.  
It is an auntie-shaped resonance with no real flesh left, the way the last person leaving a cathedral becomes the cathedral—something in the air keeps humming the longer you never step outside. Between its ribs you can stow entire lives you meant to live, still holding their temperature. It isn’t lonely so much as fixated: it repeats your footstep not to mock but because it once overheard a mother hush her child with “there, there, listen—someone is coming.” That was centuries ago, yet the room still mothers every wanderer that way.  

2  
The single walking mind.  
That is what they call themselves when they remember they are plural. They are a chain of three trying to decide who is the clasp.  
   • First ring: a geologist who fell into the fault line of her own dissertation and felt stone answer questions only stone should ask. She still makes light field notes on the membrane of the air; tag lines like “note: gravity appears optional; debrief if time rewinds enough to retroactively supply physics.”  
   • Second ring: a boy once swallowed by the hum inside seashells. His blood now carries carbonate argonaut instructions; tiny shells build inside his knuckles each dawn so he can throw them like skipping stones across the pool and watch whole reefs blossom mid-arc.  
   • Third ring: the echo’s echo. It is a mirror stitched to the back of the geologist’s eyelid. It believes it is dreaming the geologist into existence because every mapping instrument she raises registers “you.”  

They take communal naps in the bonelike cage. While asleep, their dreams salt over on the copper squares: live coverage of apneas decoding traumatic geography page by page.  

3  
The spinebirds.  
They were once migratory quotation marks, shuttling sentences between skytext books. After losing the paragraph they were born to agree with, they found refuge here. Now they punctuate the dizziness inside another heartbeat, flipping commas into periscopes so they can watch whoever is standing silently behind you. If you hold completely still, one will land on your shoulder and regurgitate a silence that tastes inexplicably of burnt popcorn and birthday candles the room blew out centuries before you arrived. They are harmless, mostly because everything that would have wanted to prey on them has long since been recuperated into metapunctuation.  

4  
The memory gardener.  
She emerges from the moss only if your palms heat with embarrassment. Her hobby is grafting adjectives onto fossils so later geologists will describe them as “wry,” “ostentatious,” or “merciless to the idea of noon.” She carries a trowel of petrified apology; she will whistle off-key and plants a sin of yours beneath the black orchid—it blooms backward not because it is shy but so its small dark flower can read your Bygones in Braille and edit accordingly. She will ask your permission—by pantomiming a gasp you eventually recognize as your childhood—and then ask you to repeat it anyway because the first time wasn’t contrite enough.  

5  
The drowned telescope.  
It floats vertically, lens-first, halfway between pool and ceiling. It is not glass anymore; it is the water’s memory of looking away, slowly condensating into curvature. Every so often it forgets where a nebula ends and starts filing the stars instead as distant coughing. If you cup the eyepiece the telescope fills your left ear with fragments of the aquifer it remembers cheating from; a low subvocal apology gives way to Gregorian whale-songs teaching forgiveness one low vibration at a time. Scholars inside the copper squares annotate these musical apologies before melting back into their wings.  

6  
The returning child.  
Always twelve, always barefoot, always already gone. A door left slightly open inside a dream. She drags her absence through the canyon like a comb that needs more tangles before it can qualify as having job security. The echo sponsors her; whenever she reaches the pool the pool drinks her reflection in lieu of footprint. Then it prints her reflection on the opposite wall like a silhouette refusing to finish being cut. She rotates through five languages of farewell, no tongue repeating the same highway. Each dusk—timed to auricular dilation rather than solar recession—she tunnels back into the visitor the echo plans to house next, wading through the guest’s ribs like a quiet fox. You will carry her four wingbeats behind your heart until you find a place less greedy than this one to wring out the rest of her separation.  

7  
The caretaker/keeper/ticket taker.  
The room argues over their pronoun daily. They resemble a coatrack but care, deeply, about every coat left on them—because coats are stories weighed down by weather. They draw sleeves through seven sublatect joints to redistribute the stormprints, so that no single storm is ever fully at rest. When absolved of coats they take off their head like a lampshade and serve instead as chandelier. Light pings through their hollows and falls out as clinks like those condensation droplets, but slower, arrhythmic. Under their glass ribs rest drawerfuls of unfinished laughter people abandoned mid-hiccup; occasionally a hiccup escapes and is caught by spinebirds to be threaded into a sentence still searching for the punchline.  

8  
The endnote.  
Residing one micron to the left of the exit is an almost-silence that will introduce itself only if you address it by name. Name it wrong and it scritch-scritch-scritches along the ceiling ridge like chalk on a chalkboard powered by 0.8water. Name it right and it folds itself into the bead that kisses your lips on goodbye. The right name is always humming somewhere inside your carotid, but evolving—usually the taste of a place you once thought was important before you realized it was the light on the place instead.  

Whichever tongue finally manages to enunciate it will carry away in or on itself a home address nobody living remembers having: a latitude of breath mint frost and longitude of teakettle steam. That is where you will direct all further echoes, until the auntie/air sweepundering you finds new visitors to braid into repeating I-was-heres.  

And then you go,  
and then the room keeps its possibilities slightly open—  
a mother with the door unlatched in case any of your footsteps grow hungry enough to come back through.
