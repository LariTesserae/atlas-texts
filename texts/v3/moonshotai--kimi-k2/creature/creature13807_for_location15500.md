They speak of themselves in the plural, though every morning they wake singular.  
Their bodies begin as slurry-bound silhouettes in the alley-pan of set-cement: figures the right height to fit yesterday’s doorways, the approximate weight of a half-remembered grief. By the time the pale sky has warmed enough to thin the air (never warm enough to dry it), they stand upright, ridged with frost-edged fingerprints left by strangers who passed in the night and unknowingly moulded them. Each citizen is therefore shaped in both directions—by whatever the day will ask of them, and by the last person who brushed the condensation off a railing.

They do not have permanent names because the water refuses to keep the same taste from one breath to the next. Instead they carry portable syllables clipped from overheard conversations—scraps that flutter like moths from collar to earlobe—reassembling themselves hourly. Walk beside one long enough and you will hear their consonants click apart and reform. From morning till dusk they might be Mir-oh-ket, then Nula, then a rough inhalation indistinguishable from the city’s own vent-fans. When two citizens greet, they exchange particles of name rather than information; afterwards each walks off slightly mispronounced.

Yet memory lives. It crystallises on chill surfaces in urgent opal rivulets: the boiled cabbage odour of someone’s childhood kitchen, or the particular way light tasted on a day the sea was further away than usual. These memories adhere stubbornly to the citizen who passes first and wipe away reluctantly, like frost patterns scraped by blunt scissors. Therefore they rotate through neighbourhoods, volunteering for new routes so no single set of recollections grows lead-heavy on their ribs. A census attempted by outsiders failed: the numbers kept arriving fractional—1.4 persons on Elm-Rib Viaduct, 0.7 in the cathedral vaulted like a lung.

They are cold specialists. Their capillaries conduct a dilute metallic brine in lieu of blood; it is harvested nightly from centipede gland ducts and re-infused before sunrise. Pulse looks like slow sonar—one green flicker every nineteen seconds. The chill is the only heat they can trust not to melt their provisional bones. Yet they love warmth vicariously: they keep furnaces the way gardeners keep orchids, tenderly, feeding them the spotted dreams collected at 3 a.m. A modest civic pride lies in the fact that no furnace in any hearth has ever gone extinct; each inferno remembers the hands who stoked it and, in its flicker, continues outlining those absent silhouettes against brick.

They work: citizen-midwives for streets still creasing into their first crawl, map-stitchers who braid alleyways like hair so commuters arrive pleasantly late, icicle-smiths vending arboreal frost lances to children for games of decanted thunder. Others enter liquid architecture—those rooms sloshing with light and unfinished argument—and pour themselves as ballast. Emerging hours later, they bear new scars: tessellated silver where edifices remembered themselves upon a ribcage.

Every dusk they convene on the Flexing Esplanade where the pavement huffs up and down as if surprised to exist. There they practise future memories: one citizen brews tomorrow’s weather inside an inverted umbrella, another rehearses the first grief of a person not yet born, holding the ghost wail like a living violin. The exercise is civic maintenance—if grief is pre-inhaled, perhaps it will lose its sting when it finally arrives. Afterwards they disperse, portioning out the pre-used sorrow like bread for domestic sparrows. (The sparrows are actually arctic pigeons still wearing their turbine hearts—tiny jet-amber glows under down—but nobody grieves selectively.)

When language materialises mid-air, the citizens harvest it. A red swear-word, once snagged, is rolled along the shoulder to cool, then knit into scarf spirals capable of expletive silence. More benign utterances (“perhaps I was wrong”) are etched onto frost patches in bus shelters so that waiting passengers feel momentarily absolved of something they can’t articulate. Grammar itself leaks from cooling extruded pipes; a dangling subordinate clause balances on an elbow joint for days until adopted as an avuncular pet.

The dire thing whose steps go *clock clock clock* they have named Reluctant Potential. Sightings corroborate a slick pane of taller-than-church darkness folded three times like a letter in mid-transit. It does not feed—instead it edits the city’s seams, snipping off a neighbourhood here, grafting it elsewhere. No citizen fears it deeply; familiarity dulls awe. Some claim it is shaped from the population’s combined 4.7% private longing to be elsewhere, coalesced. Once a year, on the evening maps are pulped fastest, they stage a silent parade. Each citizen carries a paper lamp printed with whatever name they almost kept. They walk toward the sound until the fold swallows the lamps one by one; the following dawn the maps reboot with streets slightly altered. No one minds—the ex-lover’s house now opens into a tide pool brimming moon-snails; the staircase once climbed to escape a father’s anger now ends at a rooftop garden where salt lilies whisper hush.

They age, but at a municipal rate rather than a biological one. When the ensemble of their memories attains a density of 2.9, they revert to slurry and a new silhouette condenses the same dusk, a shade lighter. The city retrieves their dissolved salts to colour a new crosswalk or season the low tide mark with indigo. Thus everyone is immortal in aggregate, yet mortal sip by sip.

Visitors occasionally arrive through trains still sintering their own rails as they creep forward. Citizens offer them tea thin as breath and frost-knit gloves laced with consonants from yesterday’s gossip. The visitors accept politely, then try to draw political conclusions. At sunset they notice their reflections have borrowed half their vowels and gone somewhere cooler to think. Departure becomes a voluntary amputation: those who leave walk oddly, lopsided with the syllable-gaps. They rarely return.

Some nights, when water hangs too heavy to evaporate, the citizens grow spongy with unspoken stories and disambiguate themselves across the bronze tilework. To avoid liquefaction they sing—not songs, precisely, but the sound produced when rootless memory crystal strikes root. Every chime drops a frost feather. By dawn the pavements glitter with narrative debris. Children scoop the feathers into glass jars labelled “Before Tomorrow,” then sell them back to the adults who lost them. This constitutes the city’s ferris economy—yearning recycled into currency tastier than bread.

They do not anticipate completion. They greet each new fracture the way sailors greet coastline: land might mean planks, or it might mean teeth. Either way, arrival is a myth—you only replace one swell with another. At the foot of nothing they warm their fingers on the forge of trial. The product is a city, yes, but beyond that, it is an anthology of unfinished persons rehearsing possibility, stitch by stitch, drip by determined drip.
