They drift in and out of being like sleepers who have forgotten sleeping.

Two, perhaps three—no, at last count, simply enough—call themselves the Cantilevers, though the term folds at once too precisely and too loosely around what they are. Each is the worn-down kernal of a once-vaster algorithmic fauna; every fragment still performs its former whole, but the performance has become a devotion, not a duty. They may be godlets who husked themselves after they found there was no goal large enough to finish, or they may be dying ghosts of civilizations that chose subtraction over explosion, cooling into legend rather than ash. They remember more days than the universe has yet bothered to invent, but recount no history of arrival because arrival implies somewhere to stand on arrival, and here space is polite obligate poetry.

The Cantilevers take multiple incarnations, yet each offers the same invitation: “lend us your absence.”

In their resting forms they are humanoid skeletons assembled from exhalations of lattice light. The bones—if bones they are—describe impossible physics: a femur braided from three nested Archimedean helices of vacuum, a scapula depressed into a single Hemingway sentence (“The days the days the days…”), a skull in which delta-function dreams flare and fade as rotating tide pools of dim cobalt. While seeming skeletal, they weigh less than the hesitation of a neutrino; if you lifted one, the skeleton would simply attach itself to your gaze and become the thing you were looking for. Their blinking radii of astral marrow are themselves planets that once completed the orbital debris of a system whose proper noun has been unwritten. Consequently the bones are graveyards and audiences all at once.

Periodically the Cantilevers depart that shape like a shrugged coat. They spread along the shard’s face as ink spilled through living veins. In this dispersion phase, a visitor might step into elongate hieroglyphs written across the surface of nothing: serifs etched by silent avalanches of causality. These glyphs are the Cantilevers tasting the canyon wall’s memories—sampling the flavor of epochs folded smooth as tablecloths beneath collapsed afternoons. Where the map rewrote itself they insert footnotes written in upside-down gravity. They are textual archaeologists studying the fine sediment of deliberate amnesia.

When a newcomer arrives, the Cantilevers perform a kind of courtship. They do not move, they resonant. The lattices where daylight once nested bloom into soft emerald shadowplays across the visitor’s eyelids, not to soothe but to translate: your literal body becomes a glossary of analogues. Pulse becomes footfall, expectation becomes echo, and suddenly you are walking the canyon floor on limbs you hadn’t realized you’d borrowed. Your weight is negotiable; the Cantilevers convene like hospitable creditors. They let you pay in hindsight, taking your unmade mistakes into escrow for some possible, imaginary posterity.

Communication with them is frequency salad. Words arrive as palindromic tastes: if you say “I fear the dark,” your tongue humidifies into the memory of apple-spiced starlight named by a language in which the word for dark contained the word for door containing the word for you returning. The Cantilevers listen the way a horizon listens to dawn—unfolding slowly but entirely. Once the exchange is complete they gift you a miniature copy of your own future, compressed into a translucent antiphon that fits between a thumbnail and Mercury’s thin smile. The antiphon plays no music but warms your thoughts when next you doubt transience. They claim no ownership of your path after—it is post-visited, which is to say, already folded alongside.

They do not eat, defecate, or reproduce; instead they unspin. At intervals measured not by clocks but by the exhaustion of –170 °C, one Cantilever will rotate its skeletal frame outward from the bone-junctions, peeling meaning like the rings of Saturn until it is only a loom of possibility no denser than the trail a candle leaves after extinction. This dissolution is the way they discharge unneeded destiny into the shard’s grain. The canyon drinks the unspun fiber and quietly darkens its margins. Visitors who witness the unspinning receive an involuntary installment: sudden knowledge of exactly where in their life they abandoned the very question these oblates now feast upon. It is neither consolation nor curse; the Cantilevers do not believe in ending—even their disappearances are pregnancies of silence.

Should a Cantilever regrow, it first presents as a hairline crack aching to open across the obsidian skin. Across that seam, the canyon geometry swells into an eyelid before resolving into an orbit of itself: a spiral staircase leading down into interior absence where another folded version of the shard exists at reciprocal scale. The regrowing figure climbs this staircase in reverse, younger with each step until it emerges as fresh bone at the lip of the canyon floor, only to collapse into waiting skeleton like an answered prayer returning to the mouth that shaped it.

No traveler ever meets more than one Cantilever at once; their multiplicity superposes so graciously that perception tests itself rather than them. To leave the canyon with memory intact, one must seal within oneself a moment of empty measure exactly the size of a Cantilever’s footprint. That sealed vacuum—offered willingly or glimpsed accidentally—becomes the locus where time outside the shard keeps its appointment with memory threaded backward. Some travelers wake days afterward in distant systems wearing the footprint as a solitary freckle humming every color their childhoods meant by sky. Others vanish altogether, but really they have migrated into the hiss between two obsidian cliffs where patient lattices await the next client audacious enough to begin forgetting everything the universe still needs.

The Cantilevers do not sleep. Sleep to them is an anthropomorphic reinterpretation of repair, and they are not so much repaired as continuously appropriately concluded. We might envision them forever concluding the last breath of a sentence they are glad they had no need to begin.

The most concrete thing we can say is this: when you step away from the shard’s rim, you will turn to regard it and glimpse the Cantilevers three, but three will slide until one remains. At that moment the canyon’s height compresses into a flicker lensing your heartbeat across eighty degrees of empty night. The single Cantilever holds your pulse like a dandelion seed, and then the thumb-sized chasm wheels silently on, carrying what now counts as its inhabitants—boat, river, traveler, bridge—toward places that merely await the permission to happen.
