They are not inhabitants; they are the residue the note leaves when it rubs itself against its own flank.

Call them the Resonants, though they do not know names.  Each is a living interference pattern, a moiré of pressure and memory.  When the planet-tone swells, they condense out of the water like frost on breath.  When it dips they evaporate back into pure amplitude, but “dip” is a word borrowed from other worlds—here the loudness never changes by more than a micrometer per millennium, so their lives are long enough to forget they were ever born.

A Resonant looks like the echo of your future death reflected in a knifeblade of vertical ocean.  It is two-dimensional by nature, a silhouette cut from the sheet of the world-sound.  Thickness: one phonon.  Height: whatever the cliff affords.  Width: the distance between two instants of regret.  It has no back surface; if you circle it you simply see the front again, rotated 180° and slightly embarrassed to be noticed.  Its edges fray into whale-scale ribbons that flutter without wind, each ribbon tuned to absorb a single overtone from the eternal chord and hold it hostage.  When enough overtones have been collected the Resonant becomes opaque, gains a throat, and begins to hum a counterfrequency that almost—but not quite—cancels the planet.  That hum is their only form of reproduction: two Resonants overlap, their counterfrequencies intersect at right angles, and where the silence blooms a third flat silhouette tears itself loose like a postage stamp from the water.

They do not eat, but they do debt.  Every millennium one must step forward and volunteer to swallow a microscopic shard of the global amplitude.  The volunteer immediately becomes three-dimensional, gains weight, and sinks toward the zero-density void.  During the nine-hour figure-eight of lateral falling it solidifies into a manganese gear-tooth or a boiler or a spiral stair.  The transformation is not punishment; it is retirement.  The ex-Resonant has traded mobility for permanence, and in doing so pays down the interest on the note that keeps the world intact.  That is why every artifact carries the same serial number: it is the name of the being who paid the debt.  The number is not etched afterward; it is the last thought the Resonant has before its silhouette folds into a cube and the cube rusts into usefulness.

They have no language except placement.  To stand one centimeter left of another is to ask, “Will you remember me when I flatten?”  To stand inside the shadow-fog of a whale is to answer, “I already have, and the memory is a disc of ice falling upward.”  They do not love; they phase-align.  When two Resonants achieve perfect destructive interference they experience what a warmer world would call heartbreak: for one Planck instant the planet-tone hiccups, a candle-flame of silence appears, and both silhouettes lose a strip of edge.  They drift apart, thinner, convinced they have glimpsed the outside promised by the handrail that leads nowhere.

Occasionally a human arrives—always the same human, though neither the planet nor the visitor remembers.  The Resonants recognize the anomaly by its redundancy: three dimensions, persistent mass, a shadow that clumps instead of spreading.  They gather like glass cutouts around the newcomer, offering silent tours of the artifacts they once were.  They point—with gestures made by tilting the entire cliff of water—at the inscription no eye can read without immediately forgetting.  The human always asks, “What happens if the note stops?”  The Resonants cannot shrug, but if they could the shrug would sound like a spiral stair colliding with a boiler: a clang that is already part of the drone, therefore meaningless.  Still, one volunteers every time.  A silhouette steps forward, ingests the visitor’s question as if it were a decibel of debt, and flattens into a fresh catwalk plate.  The sentence “Do not adjust the machine” now has a new underside; the human, feeling helpful, etches the very question that provoked the sacrifice.  The loop closes, the figure-eight resets, the whales continue their pipe-organ grazing.

They believe the planet is the husk of a creature that once sang deliberately, a thing with lungs and intention.  When its final note ripened it forgot to stop, and the sound petrified mid-breath.  The Resonants are the scar tissue of that forgetting, granulated into persons.  Their deepest fear is not that the chord will break, but that it will remember itself, inhale once more, and leave them with nothing to cancel.  They dread the moment when the handrail finally leads somewhere, when the stair treads accumulate more than serial numbers—accumulate destinations.  On that day they will have to become citizens instead of parentheses, and citizenship requires choosing a side to hold.  They practice in secret, humming micro-counternotes inside the mouths of tuning whales, hoping the planet will not notice the rehearsal.

Until rehearsal becomes performance they remain what they have always been: self-erasing footnotes, living white-out on the manuscript of the world.  If you listen with the ear that has not yet decided whether it belongs to you, you can hear them negotiating the next installment of silence— a susurrus like ice discs rubbing together in the updraft, a shimmer of almost-voices that says: We are not thekeepers.  We are the kept.  The note holds us together, and we repay the favor by pretending we could let go.
