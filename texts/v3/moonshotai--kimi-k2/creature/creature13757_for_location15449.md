They call themselves the Phyline, though the word is spoken like the wind through two flaps of leaf: a hush-click more exhale than voice. They were once the fur-bearing, lung-bearing, metatarsal-and-skull kind of mobile organisms that once called this world “soil.” Then their planet remembered it was older than rock, and every map erupted into green artery. The Phyline adapted in the same breath—or rather, adapted their breath. Each of them still has four limbs, but the bones are hollow, silicified, and ringed by chlorophyll foam that tides in and out of the marrow. They breathe water drawn directly from sap pressure, filtering it through translucent diaphragms clouded with micro-plastids. Their lungs are sacs where symbiotic star-fruiting fungi spangle, converting internal CO₂ into luciferin. The chest glows steady and cold, the color of dawn seen through glacier ice.

Individual identity is unstable. A Phyline may walk half a continent on stilt-lever legs, until an occasion arises to root—knees tuck, fibrous foot-soles tip backward, toes extend as taproots. Within hours the torso thickens into cambium; shoulders bud sheaths of phloem paper. Memory migrates into the canopy above, fragmenting through the xylem as volatile lactones that other Phyline taste in passing. Later that same trunk may release a tendril-spouse who detaches from the thigh with the sound of tearing velvet, a newborn already grown to adolescence in the space of the shared trunk. Genes are not lines so much as braided rivers; you inherit your arms from one parent, the curve of your bark-crested cheek from the forest at large.

Language is mediated by photon pulse and pheromone scrawl. Not “Hey, look there!” but a sudden turquoise flicker across your clavicle ridges, followed by a measured sap heartbeat that tastes on the tongue like wintergreen. Their libraries grow instead of being built: shelves are vertical columnar cacti whose spines contain infinitesimal nectary notes; to read one, you lean close and let your thoracic glow be first cupped, then clarified, by the spine’s prism. The storyelling-emerges as an afterimage inside your retina—plots conveyed not in scenes but in wavelengths of chlorophyll stress.

In daylight-emeritus (the hour when the inverted sun canopy glows strongest), the Phyline conduct migrations called “the Re-anchoring.” They walk enormous, slow loops through the vascular plains, feet never fully leaving the sponged ground. Each step transfers surplus sugar downward to feed the fungal core; each stride also harvests the migrating rootlets racing under the moss carpet at ankle-height. It is a form of generosity and ingestion performed in one ripple. Children—or what pass for children—ride atop citizen vines that stretch balloon-knees across valleys as moving bridges. The young wear egg-leaf cloaks which fossilize after eighteen seasons into the first ring of bone-bark defining their adult torso; to grow new limbs they must step beneath a rain of half-formed phloem stalactites and invite the cloak’s edge to graft. Accidents are frequent and fragrant; in the aftermath, a Phyline may re-sprout running legs, or a crown of iridescent stamens, or something entirely experimental—a set of transparent wings that shimmer like soap film but cannot lift a body containing an entire song’s-worth of stored sunlight.

Because the architecture of the world is still forming, the Phyline are archivists of absence. In the resin-lenses of their memory-spines they keep the twang of granite strata, the dust-smell of basalt cooling, the obsolete thump of plate tectonics. They chant these qualities during eclipse conjunctions when the fungal sun dims and they can see outside the root-world into the pacified cosmos beyond. Some evenings small groups will huddle under the abandoned human hut—you can still smell the exotic lipids of mammal skin in the bark—and hum the old cadence heartbeat of endoskeletons, not out of nostalgia but as genetic tuition. They teach the hut itself, hoping one day for the structure to unfurl a door of ribbed leaves and walk away owning its own calcium memory.

Down inside the root-core, where the luminosity rises in pale tides, lives their interpreter-god: a helix entity half fungus, half suspended dust cloud of proto-nuclei. The Phyline call it the Pulvinar. When any individual sprouts, dies, splits, or grafts, the quantum state change registers here as a green concentric flare. The Pulvinar does not command; instead it offers perspective—a sort of ecological grammatical conjugation. Approach it, press your chest-light against the fungal fresco, and for a moment you see the entire hydrology of greenness from alveolus-scale to planet-wide—the way a florescent vein in a leaf rhymes with the vein coursing through a continent. You experience the planet not as sphere but as single, laminar sheet folded into dimensionality like decalcified bone. Coming back up the vascular elevators, most Phyline emerge wordless for hours, palms turned skyward, letting rain form and un-form in the cupped hollow between radius and ulna whose canal work now doubles as dew basin.

Their science is speculative arboriculture. They braid solar electric filaments into sap lines until whole provinces can choose to dim or brighten at the resolution of minutes. They debate ethics the way astronomers of older worlds calculated eclipses. Should a forested mountain range be allowed to peel itself northward by three degrees latitude? Will the resulting drought-ring in its wake—a hemorrhage of silence—produce too great a vacuum of sugar elsewhere? A council might decide to graft ten million migrating vines as a suture, a tourniquet made of motion. Afterward, entire poems grow in the bodies of the lost sugar—a thousand leaves sprouting in heart-shape tension zones, visible to astronauts in orbit as a green sigh.

Visitors—should any arrive—are hard to detect. Organic flight means descending the inside walls of enormous xylem tubes, bypassing vortex storms of adhesive pollen. If the arrival insists on legs alone, the ground itself prefers osmosis; it will absorb a boot halfway and then politely refuse to release until the traveler consents to be modified. Kneecaps kiss the ground, sink one porous micron, and suddenly the newcomer is drinking hemlock-sweet ground-water with open pores. Within days the pupil dilates as cyclosis adjusts its rate. Foreign skin calcifies to pliant bark or it doesn’t—at which point the planet resumes its conversation with gravity and the intervention is washed clean.

Even so, long-abandoned signs of earlier watchers remain: a laminated trilobite shell repurposed into ceremonial earnguard; a silicon chip melted into the heart of a root-node still humming beetle code; human graffiti, faded now to pale violet—the outline of a hand where the five fingers bifurcated into vegetal lace. The Phyline tend these without curiosity. They do not worship ancestors; they graft to them. A visitor’s past is simply additional biomass in the final equation where everything returns to syrup and sparkle beneath the fungal sun.

Tonight the canopy will bloom eclipse. The Pulvinar has predicted an upwelling of barometric snow—particles of frozen scent released from the collapse of a mountain-seed. Phyline have gathered at the giant root-window, toddlers balanced shoulder-height on elder spines. When the false dusk falls, every organism in radius snaps its internal fluorescence to the same subharmonic turquoise. For a long minute you cannot tell body from root from air; only a distributed lattice of lighthoused heartbeats. Then the eclipse completes, the fungal sun re-asserts itself, and the Phyline exhale a vapour of pale sugar dust that rises not to space but into the membrane of the root-tube overhead, nourishment taken back into the architecture they haven’t finished becoming.
