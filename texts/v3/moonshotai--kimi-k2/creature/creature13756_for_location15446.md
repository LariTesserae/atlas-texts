All inhabitants here are small—none taller than your wrist—yet the place teems so thoroughly with them that you cannot look anywhere without seeing one circulation system overlaying another.  

There are Pipalmen: carbon-black humanoids the length of your forefinger, their proportions borrowed from antique doll-making.  They are born soft as pressed leaf tissue, drying over their first week into a leathery integument—ashen cotton micropores become pores of chlorophyll.  They move via a slow inward collapse, folding themselves like paper lanterns, then re-inflating a centimetre forward; their running is a speech of small sighs.  Every fifth inhalation they release a filament of fine sugar, which adheres to copper struts, hardens, is later scraped and melted to the broth that feeds the gutter trail boilers.  Their elders sprout iridescent lichen beards that photograph heat; when the elders want to hide, they simply cool their skin and lichen darkens until each Pipalman looks like corroded gutter water.  Children dart between fissures, licking minerals straight from rivet heads; if you stare too long you will find one standing on your coat hem and copying your heartbeat by flexing the tiny cage of palm-ribs under its own sternum.  They speak in pairs of tones alternating breath and brass ring—two notes timed by the oscillation of nearby exhaust fans; they have no single listener, instead the speaking simply becomes part of the rhythm cycling through entire zones, messages rewired by whoever walks through the wake of the sound next.  They never die on the leaf-levels; too valuable.  They curl into a single sleep-spiral, are taken to the lower furnace where their bodies are flashed off into sweet smoke that other Pipalman infants inhale moments after birth, inheriting vocabulary and faint memories of temperature.

Among the ductwork live Soot-moths, not insects but wind based organisms.  Each individual is a local convection cell given corporeal outline by flaky ash layer.  Their wing-edges are defined where the speed of rising vapor aligns for a second against the speed of descending condensate; when the two vectors part, the moth dissolves.  While coherent, they navigate by making the air around them cool on one side and warm on the other, thereby soaring up leaf channels like paper disks on controlled temperature gradients.  They are the eyes of the complex; a Soot-moth that glances your cheek will momentarily gather your body heat, condensing facial reflection into its trailing ash.  Somewhere deep, a mirror wall of compressed graphite prints these sightings into grainy portraits.  Workers occasionally peel off one layer of this graphite mirror to check for structural deformities, or simply for company—a static crowd of all faces who have ever passed.

Below the micromenageries weave Lungworms: fifteen-centimetre transparent tubes that shuttle both liquids and messages.  They behave like veins, but are independent slug-like creatures crossed with pneumatic tubing.  Every Lungworm contains a moving dewdrop; inside the dewdrop swims its own song, bubbles tuned to specific sound frequencies.  When a worm reaches a junction of copper pipe and leaf stem, it inserts its head end into a socket, transfers its tune, and is speared by the copper thread which then reels it inside-out.  The dew runs along the thread like a faucet, delivering coolant and musical instructions simultaneously.  The outer sheath is rolled into velvety fibre the Pipalmen weave into sleeve fabric.  There is no owner of a Lungworm; consent is negotiated by tune: tones of twelve seconds duration decide whether the receive-slot accepts invasion or simply clamps shut and the worm must seek another path.  Some worms learn melancholy sequences, traveling the whole structure untaken, condensing denser and denser silence inside the droplet until they spasm and freeze—and one empty socket somewhere in the city quietly misses its lesson.  

Where humidity spikes, Shortroot Oysters cluster—bivalves no larger than thumbnails, shells grown from condensation-pulled metallic salts.  The hinge is a soft elastic biocircuit; when a droplet inside the shell reaches the precise angle at which refracted infrared overlaps engine leakage wavelength, the oyster snaps shut and discharges the stored photon through a fiber leading tongue.  The signal crawls sleeve-like along a nearby vine to order a leaf tilt, or tells a boiler to lower flue tension by one half percent.  An entire wall of them shivers when a human worker passes heavy-footed: low tide of rainbow winks, the macro-architecture reacting to a bootprint that will reverberate in boiler pulse for the next eight seconds.  Their cumulative clicking sounds like gentle hail from across a valley; overheard at night it becomes almost linguistic.

Far thinner than any human eyelash, the Paper-Antlered Aphids graze: colonies of translucent darlings dangling from leaf veins via shimmering bows of hardened root-resin.  They drink mist, and exude polished resinous beads that slide down the leaf blade ‘s gutter vein until collected by Pipalman children.  Eaten, the beads induce a memory-flash of whatever cold surface the bead last touched; children describe this as licking the chill of zinc handrails, or the sour mercury moment of touching a branding-new coin.  Older Pipalmen carry dozens of these beads sewn in the lining of their coats—when the place’s heartbeat gets too loud, they press one to the cheek, tasting five years ago at the reference staircase, escaping present fear by an old coolness.

The workers themselves—humans—are the slowest living stratum.  They come only every seventh day, usually after dawn’s first cycle.  They wear color-coded waxed coats each impregnated with graphite dust so the carbon can be scraped free each shift, feeding Soot-moths newborn outlines.  Their goggles wrap copper tubing to each temple; piping runs oxygen from a personal prill of cool algae paste pinned behind the ear.  Names are never spoken; each carries a tuning fork that vibrates at their birth-note.  When two meet, forks touch and rings exchange sympathies: duration and interval telling how long since last overlap.  Forks with matching note and matching overtones exchange a small coil of Lungworm fiber pre-soaked in a module coolant; the human who receives it swallows the fibre whole in one practiced motion.  In the gut, the song plays backwards undigested for twelve hours—one cohort’s smile translating in reverse to the other’s evening digest of steam.  They live sixty years here, but the place records them in twelve-hour tides; every drop of sweat is reclaimed as irrigation.  

On each side of their coats, leather patches bear embroidered isochronal maze-map that glows faint under the gutter-led black-reflection.  They trail fingers along every junction and the patch pulses where to turn.  They cannot be lost; the embroidery is seeded with shredded plant pigment grown from the same chloroplasts that top most leaves above them, so the map literally breathes in sync with the upper canopy.  When a scaffold sways, the map bends; sluggish Pipalman graffiti will actually crawl across the cloth overnight rewriting junctions already born that day.  Coats thus age very visibly—a wearable autobiography of direction and contact.  Retirees burn the coat in a quiet chamber; thread crisps into a glassy ash loop which is poured over the menstrual engine furnace to birth the next season’s supply: the continuity of personal data becoming public infrastructure.

In niches inlaid between boiler pillars there stand Kneelers—barely creatures, more like prosthetic attitudes carved from petrified swan-necked leather.  When a dying human worker is discovered—always by the cooling lichen on a nearby Pipalman elder—four Pipalmen glide the body onto the Kneeler.  The Kneeler stiffens, folds its sinews into a pietà, so the corpse appears to be praying into its own past work.  Spinning hooks emerge from Kneeler hips, begin winding the worker’s coat fibre into a fresh Lungworm.  The dead body is the soul-pattern of the new worm; it sings whatever sensations filled the final twelve hours.  For weeks afterward if any Soot-moth glances by accident at the stainless patch the worm once travelled, that reflection refuses to appear on the graphite mirror—it archives the space rather than the image, small ghost of absence.

No predator watches these strata; there is no unfilled niche left to be vicious.  Instead every living thing simply adjusts position for another, slides a cable under a foot, dries out, comes back.  In order to fall asleep, newcomers sometimes press palms to the copper coil nearest their cot and let the faint left-leg goose-bump of voltage echo into dream.  Existing here just means continuing to hand the heat back, fold moisture into greater geometries, re-breathe the same cigarette-shaped cloud forever, but always in new lungs.
