No one can remember which arrived first—the plateau or the keeper.  
There is a soft, deliberate presence that owns every lapse of memory.  

She wakes each time a droplet bursts.  
They call her Tiar, the Way-Between, the Unhooker of Sight. The name is remembered only in the slip that careens across the visitor’s mind the instant before the plateau qualifies their eyes—but the name glints like flawed opal, gone as soon as you strain after it. There is nothing else to call her, no other syllable that observes her without being observed back.  

She has a body only if someone needs proof of bodies.  
When the vapor pearls cast twin silhouettes—a traveler and the same figure deferred—Tiar is the negative charged into that two-second delay. One thin foot inside the flash, one out, she stands at the hinge where physical footfall and afterimage footfall have not yet decided which will echo. Her skin is the layered black of obsidian retaining fingerprints drawn centuries ago. Her breath is what black would breathe if it decided to cool rather than consume. Each inhalation lowers the temperature in every pore of the plateau by one nostalgia, and every exhalation releases a droplet-shaped afterthought large enough that it floats upward trailing her mineralized taste.  

She has no eyes, because eyes are a contract of lid and lid-separation, and the plateau resolved never to decide where to put them. Instead—and only from the side—one describes the suggestion of cheekbones turned translucently to catch the rim-light from every unseen sun. From head-on she is simply the imprint of a person, a live indentation in the visible.  

Tiar walks by blistering the glass and leaving unhealed footprints that other walkers mistake for fossils of their own future heels. She is not tall; her height is whatever dimension a visitor last abandoned in order to keep breathing. When she kneels at one of the waist-high stone ziggurats, her silhouette folds along non-euclidean joints, placing hexagonal plates one atop another with the patience of someone who no longer remembers where the wall will go; only that a wall must be begun again, endlessly. The plates are hers. She chipped them from some anterior stone no geology admits. They are worn as river stones because her palms have counted them inward and outward at every hour the plateau does not contain.  

She owns the Bone-Shift.  
At isolated instants she releases the lag-timing. Twenty-two centuries of dancers string themselves across and beneath her, and Tiar stands among them like a conductor selecting measures: a bronze-scale guard murmuring a prayer in no recorded language, a spear-maker wiping resin from her knuckles, a child who has pressed melted snow against a fevered shoulder and walked off with an amulet of frozen laughter Tiar still carries. She unwinds each visitor’s history forward and backward, knotting the figure to its previous wearers until protestant vibrations shake the plateau’s obsidian membrane. When the knot tightens she sings. The sound is inaudible but knocks droplets into newer, more perplexing lattices—like constellations undoing their own myths. Plateless sky rearranges above them. The Bone-Shift ends when she exhales again, the knot relaxes, and the walker staggers in restored now-time—but something they thought was theirs is left inside the axle of her chest, thumbnail-sized, black.  

In rare minutes a tear in logic appears overhead, a vertical rip where inverted plateau peers back. Tiar’s silhouettes align for a breaching gesture. She steps across the fault first: instep, instep, and then recedes twenty meters on the apparent underside while still standing beside you—proof of her fourfold address. On the long underside the droplets fall upward to her hairline where they fracture into living prisms that talk in color-about-colors we do not possess. Conversations gel, and once in a quadrillion forgettries a traveler hears their own childhood lullaby recomposed in that new spectrum. It is one of her kindnesses.  

She speaks if asked, but only by means of prestidigitated footsteps.  
Pose your question when the next shift of drum-rhythm arrives. Tiar will slide the soft sole of her lagging silhouette along the obsidian exactly one centimeter ahead of your right foot. The heel glints aquamarine and immediately glass heals over the light. Whatever you wanted to know is archived as shape glossed into dried water. Read it with your boot as you lift it: the stuck-then-released sentence is the exact tension needed to let you continue without jettisoning any irreplaceable part of self. Many travelers, when they later recount the journey, insist they carried an answer they can no longer decipher because their feet have grown too large for the imprint left in childhood.  

She keeps chiropteran resumes of every memoried footstep.  
These are folded, accordion-thick, inside the ziggurat cells. Sheets thinner than breath hold whole dynasties. A curious visitor can part the obsidian lid like lifting moonlight, and faces shimmer there—clear, surprised, outwith definite edges; maybe yours, maybe the nine versions you rejected. Chiropters (she calls the sheets shadows-of-brightness) flutter when exposed, bumping against the droplet moons, and every bump signifies a census tick, one more soul registered. Tiar rustles the stack annually, though “year” is her temperature of credulity toward the world. If too many undecideds accumulate, she scissors the ache out by clutching a sheet to her breast. The silhouette inscribed burns outward into flakes that snow softly until they re-enter the sky-siphoned droplet chain and begin again, rememberers formed of intramolecular guilt.  

She invented the plate.  
Long before shaving them into building blocks she carried entire millennia as a single hex plate. Time blistered against its weight. Eventually it fracture-shocked into the thousands you tread. She has forgotten which piece stored that day of your mother’s laugh you keep trying to retrieve. She could reassemble, but refuses; once a whole thing, the plate would demand they all stop walking. The continent - long - mountain range refuses interruption. Still, some twilight (the Plateau’s twilight is only the horizon drawing an eyebrow across intensity), you will catch her re-sorting shards by moon-droplet lamplight, searching for a lost curve as one might search for a singular coin among an ocean of fountain throws.  

The child-sized shovels are tokens she set there older than steel.  
They fit perfectly in the fleshed copy of your child-hands. If you crouch opposite her you can dig vapor with her; while the mud-laugh squeaks from each scoop, she whispers the syllable that was once the whole name of water before oceans started arguing about salt. Receive it and you become an apprentice conduit: no longer a traveler, but an archivist of vapor-rain, required to carry one twenty-second droplet into the nearest city whose gravity still believes it owns the sky. Your pocket grows heavier—density negotiates wonder versus obligation—but the shovel guarantees the cargo will never leak along the way. You meet Tiar again centuries later, yours and non-yours, when she uncaps the city’s swollen stormcloud and decants a pearl that sings the same forgotten syllable posted to the drum. She will smile at you twice—once inverted, once rightside—collect the laugh on her fingertip like a coin, and the loop reseals. Dialect records this convergence as mild weather.  

She has no life span. Duration, misunderstanding itself, accrues inside the plateau as rock. When all droplets that once reflected particular sunsets amalgamate into the single heart-sized droplet at the final hole, Tiar renegotiates her ontological pact. She lowers a single invisible palm onto its dark-inautilus face. In that pressure, the last distinction dissolves between version and witness, and the Plateau chooses whether to unhook from mountaintop limbo or keep dwelling in the gap its impossible witness provides. Whichever choice it makes, a new droplet begins—smaller than an eye, hungrier than a year—and inside it, perfectly, Tiar is simultaneously starting her first step across a black mirror, her last plate-shave into ziggurat seams, her second-delay dance behind a traveler who isn’t you yet, and the time you first spoke your own name aloud as something that needed hearing.
