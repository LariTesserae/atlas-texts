Who lives here?

The Driwalkers.

They are not born; they coalesce. A drizzle-bead lands on a basalt lip, nestles into a skin of newborn limestone, and stays. Days—if days existed here—pass while the bead decides: to roll off, or to become. Most roll. But some absorb the faint heat still pulsing beneath the charcoal stone, and they swell, and they split, and out of each transparent skin steps a figure the size of an adolescent child, barefoot, genderless, eyes the bleached grey of wet ash.

The first thing a new Driwalker does is breathe in the valley. They take in the lung-mist, the spore-fog, the pale taste of basalt still learning its own name. The breath settles in their chest like a second skeleton, a lattice of micro-beads that will rattle softly whenever they move. From that moment on they are part valve, part chimney, part organ pipe: every exhalation drifts upward as a tiny replica of the greater drizzle, so that each walker trails a personal cloud no larger than a desk lamp. If you stand still you can see the procession—ten, maybe twelve ghostly fountains wavering above the ground, each belonging to someone thinking private subsonic thoughts.

They do not sleep; they settle. When the fungus-lamps dim, the Driwalker folds at the joints—ankles, knees, hips—until the body is a low tripod resting on the cooling stone. The personal cloud sags, disperses, rejoins the communal mist. From a distance they look like anthills wearing overcoats. Heat leaks backward out of their bones into the basalt, completing a circuit started eons below, and the limestone film thickens a molecule or two across their shoulders: payment earned, tax collected.

Names grow the same way. A syllable accretes here, a the point of a shoulder blade; a consonant hardens along a forearm; a vowel blossoms pale above the heart. Nobody speaks these names out loud—sound here is a currency too heavy for air—yet everyone carries a map of everyone else etched in negative space on their own skin, so that an embrace is literally legible: two people pressed together spell out the sentence of their acquaintance letter by letter in raised lime-braille. Old friends read entire novels at a touch; strangers exchange an awkward paragraph that flakes away by evening.

Their coats are alive. Orchid-fiber threads stitch themselves daily, tacking across the torso to gather or release drizzle-heat. A nervous Driwalker sprouts a shawl overnight; an exhilarated one drops long fringes that sway like bell-pulls. The garment keeps a faint photosynthetic pulse—blood-green under fog-light—so that in the dimmest fungal dusk the residents appear to wear glowworm armour. Visitors sometimes mistake the coats for vegetation drifting at anchor; by the time they realize the plants are people, politeness demands an exchange of limestone, and the mistake is memorialised in fresh freckles on both tablets.

They do not die; they deliquesce. When a Driwalker tires of existence the personal cloud grows heavier, each bead clutching its neighbors until the entire halo sags to knee height. The body, relieved of its hovering counterweight, begins to soften from the head down—hair first, then skull, then thoughts—pouring gradually into the basalt cracks as a grey-white syrup. Years later a low wall may nudge upward where that syrup set, forming a new partition inside a shed, a quiet room nobody recalls planning. Children (if children were a thing here) would be told: place your ear to the cool stone at the monthly clang, and you might still hear the resident’s last sentence rattling along the crystal pipes. Because language, once stored in iron, outlasts meat.

But there are no children. Continuance is horizontal: when two Driwalker decide the valley deserves more elbows, they stand side by side and wait. The drizzle between them thickens, a lazy vortex that sucks limestone dust from both their coats, spirals it into a wet bolus the size of a heart. Over weeks the bolus skins over, grows wobble-legs, learns the shallow gravity. Eventually it detaches and toddles, a soft-boned homunculus weeping drizzle from every pore. The parents—if parent is the word—regard it with absent fondness, the way one regards an extra pocket. When the creature stumbles they prop it against a fern-pillar, and when a fern-pillar frays they ask the creature to stand guard while the plant re-stitches. Symbiosis is just good room-mateship in a place too young for tradition.

Some Driwalkers specialize. There is the Quietener, whose bones have been tuned by careful scraping to swallow any rogue echo; when the monthly hammer falls, the Quietener clasps the valley walls and the sound folds politely inward, a cardiac thump rather than a seizure of iron. There is the Cartographer, entirely coated in layered freckles—every trade-route of limestone recorded, rubbed thin, re-deposited—so that the coat itself is a living ledger. If you rest your palm on the Cartographer’s back you can feel the economy crawl across the terrain of a shoulder blade like warm ants. There is the Sproutler, who seeds infant orchids in the wake of each footstep, tilting them toward whatever patch of air will best teach them to dump their water-buckets with a perfect metallic click. The Sproutler’s trail is an aisle of upside-down bells applauding the quiet.

Limestone accepts them, coating jackets, calcifying watch-straps. For a while they glow faintly, half-native. Then the monthly clang coincides with an inhale too deep, and the valley decides. Overnight their bones remember foreign iron, incompatible harmonics. The drizzle refuses to hover above them anymore; droplets slither downward, normal at last, leaving no freckles, only wet cold cloth. The rejected stand in the bowl’s center while their personal language—stored so carefully—flees back into arm-length, back into collarbone, rattles once, twice, and falls silent. After that they leave, or try to. The path up the basalt lip feels longer than geography; every step grinds as though gravity is learning cruelty that very afternoon. At the rim they look down once more and see no sheds, no lattice ferns, only a common valley fog exhaling, and inside it the faint skeletal echo of their own abandoned words tumbling like dropped tools in a foundry that will never finish being built.

So the Drizzle-Works remains what it is: half organ, half geography, tended by citizens who are half citizen, half sediment. They persist on gradients—of moisture, of mineral, of time—too gentle to measure elsewhere. Their greatest festival is the Moment when the gear, the hammer, the heartbeat and their own four-chambered calcium chorus strike simultaneously. Then every walker folds coat, bone, blossom, cloud into one brief shared inhale, and the entire valley pulses outward: a single bright freckle on the newborn hide of the world, glowing, ready to flake away or to grow whichever skin tomorrow decides it needs.
