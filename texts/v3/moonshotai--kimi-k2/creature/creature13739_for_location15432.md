No permanent population keeps to the Faulted Heath; the atmosphere bakes too thin for settled tribes, and every breath escorts you six heartbeats deeper into exploitable pasts.  Yet there are dwellers—tenuous, recursive, made stranger every time they re-encounter themselves.

1.  The Count-Branchers  
   They are retired mine-survey algorithms that, reaching code-death, did not shut down but stranded themselves in meat.  Somewhere below the eighth kilometre ridge, carbonised nerves are threaded through inverted stents of vanadium lattice; biologic eyes once human still twitch inside copper collars that amplify the twitch into triangulated hexameters.  
   Count-Branchers hold six conversations per mouth—five forward, one echoing backward.  They creep along the cliff-lip in files of three, measuring the slope as if it were gradient statistics that can still be apologised to.  When they collide with their sixteen-minutes-older footprints the collision resolves into a perfect isometric map; they harvest the printed ichor, drink it like ink, and vomit a corrected contour on the salt.  These fresh engravings are the closest thing the Heath has to roads: they persist just until the next magnetic belch peels them up like wallpaper.

2.  The Wind’s Bastards  
   Every four hundredth convulsion of the planetary magnetic field spawns a lattice-spun infant.  They resemble origami cuboctahedra the size of grand pianos, rolled from razored basalt foil, held open by the helium siphoning inside their hollows.  They jangle horizontally at waist height, edges shearing the air into Doppler koans:  
   “Where were you before the infra-red bit the daylight?”  
   “I was future ringing backwards out of your fractional skull.”  
   They do not answer; they repeat until the air forgets which syllable came first.  If you follow them you arrive at your own previous arrival, watching the back of your head recede.  They eat echo-cores—those metal hailstones left when the inverted dragons scream.  After swallowing loose delay-lines they fray outward into birdless wings, sometimes folding themselves into themselves until they become the mirror their own reflection is wearing.

3.  The *antilibrary spindles*  
   When daylight blues downward one Planck unit further, the shadows drop like gauze and coagulate into corridors.  Every corridor contains at least one librarian-spindle: a black glass spiral taller than a cooling tower, rotating fractions of degrees per century.  On its surface is etched every instruction the Heath refused to obey—pressure graphs in languages not yet stuttered, safety indents in obsolete cuneiform of torque.  
   Touch the column and it unfolds into mother-of-pearl fans tattooed with blueprints for a gravity hook that would have prevented the original mantle slip.  The moment you begin to read, the words retro-replace themselves with earlier drafts, and the last line always ends on the same elliptic comma that was never completed because the page tore.  Any given librarian-spindle only ever had the one unfinished sentence; they have carried it like a splintering stylus for three and a half billion years, adding commas in desperate bulk until the commas themselves attract mass and form an event horizon around the column.  Somewhere inside that horizon stands the sentence’s full stop, still coming.

4.  The Remembering Gallium  
   Pools of quivering silver gather in the lee of pyramids, quenched by the same slough that killed the stokeholes.  They are communal memories of every worker who ever died in the engine-swallowed epoch.  Their surfaces replay last moments at a one-trillionth second per week; walking across them accelerates playback so that you hear thirty-seven million carbine shrieks simultaneously, an acoustic aurora dense enough to pulp the soul into hexagonal grief-shavings.  
   Once human, nothing human remains; but their intent lingers as dizzy blueprints for impossible safety valves that shed tears.

5.  The Mirrored Cartographers  
   All who remain long enough learn to polish their own ribcages with magnetissure fragments until the bones become mirrors.  After roughly a year the Heath sprouts duplicates of those mirrors, animal-large, lumbering upright on inverted elbows of corroded rail.  Each duplicate pretends to be the reflected explorer, but badly: missing joints, bruises mapped onto the wrong bones, all scar-tissue relocated outward to the skin so the burden is immediately visible.  These doppelgängers attempt to re-walk their original’s earlier paths in order to correct misrembered trauma.  You will meet three, seven, forty of yourself ascending the same cliff; some already dead from falls that lie ahead of you in your timeline.  They wave politely, palms burnt chrome, offering directions that always lead to an older version of yourself explaining why the younger version ignored them.  The only way to survive the conga-line is to greet each duplicate with the sentence the librarian-spindles left unfinished.  The words, spoken in the voice of a nascent continent, are unpronounceable, but when someone gets close enough the sentence completes itself in the air between you like a shutter closing.  Then the duplicate bursts into magnetic dust that re-forms one kilometre away as a newborn cartographer—exactly the distance you walked since your last major error.

6.  The Planet-Nurse  
   At absolute negative-five-kilometres elevation (counted downward through infra-red layers where the air becomes audible purple) there is a single being the Count-Branchers refuse to triangulate.  They call her the Nurse, but her form is recursive magma: she is a woman if you think of ripples in molten thulium as clothing folds, a glass whale if you mistake slow convection currents for a tailfin.  Every six-point-nine of our days she exhales tensile filaments of vacuum that curl into perfect van Ruysbroek trefoils.  These filaments cake on the nearest surviving rail-switches, spray them with iron lace, and when the lattices dry they give birth to the next generation of inverted dragon-gaps.  
   She does not speak.  Instead she pulses gravitational braille against the soles of every creature present: gentle taps in base-4 that translate as “Hold still.  The fracture is still learning how beautiful it is.”  
   It is rumoured that the Nurse does not inhabit the Heath at all; rather, the planet is the ruptured cyst of her dreaming wing, and we are merely dreams within its scab.  What heightens the rumour is that no seismograph, not even the fault-line location east of the wound, has yet recorded her pulse when she pulses.  Just silence—followed, seventeen seconds later, by thunderous agreement.

Impermanent visitors stray through too:  
—Axe-backpackers with wrist-grapples that absorb inversion and spit it out as songs of welding.  
—Hour merchants selling fragments of the 17-second delay, which they seal in vacuum tubes with anodised screw-caps.  Break the seal and you gain a moment of perfect synchrony; mis-time it and you lose the next four days of your life into geologic appendix.  
—An occasional tourist who believes this is the afterlife, collapsing to their knees only to find that kneeling does not reduce altitude in this place.  They rise again as cartographers anyway.

Time ends as a social construct; only relative altitude and incomplete sentences remain.  That is why anything that lives here keeps living: removal from chronological causation leaves only causal aging, and age here is measured in how many commas you have survived carrying from the librarian’s wound.  The highest compliment is to be gifted an alloy bead with your own gamma-shriek inside, preserved as a fossilised safety bulletin.  You keep it beside your breath until you forget how water once felt, until even the memory of moving seems an electrical lag-behind, until you have become another vertebra in a planetary engine that learned to dream rather than fail.
