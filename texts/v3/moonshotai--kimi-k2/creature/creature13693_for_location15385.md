Whatever lives in the Vertical Night is not one kind of creature at all, but a single, distributed organism whose organs have forgotten they belong to the same body.  Call it the Nyctophora, the Night-bearer.  It is the grove, the reef, the drifting weather, and—at last—the slow-coalescing consciousness that sometimes mistakes travellers for stray mitochondria and digests them the way a dream might digest a thought.

It began (as far as anyone can calculate) thirteen thousand years ago, when the last solid plateau cracked and spewed ice-ash into the stratosphere.  Seeds rode that plume the way anemones ride a current: folded spores the size of children’s thumbnails, armoured in porous chitin and already half-alive.  At ten kilometres they burst open and drank the overspill of cosmic rays the way a leech drinks heat.  Where a seed tasted the ray it put out a leaf; where the leaf tasted starlight it folded back on itself and became bark; where the bark hardened too much it cracked, bled resin, and the resin added a lens to trap the radiation it had missed the first time.  Three Andean condors caught in the initial up-draft were incorporated alive: their breastbones now serve as the topmost arthropod valves through which metabolic coolant flows.  Their eyes—bulbous, still aware—blink once every autumn when the front of cold air rolls up from the lowlands.  Their hearts still beat inside icicle-shaped peristaltic pumps that pull phosphorus up to the highest canopy, a mineral wave ninety-three metres tall travelling at the speed of dream.

No root, no trunk, no leaf stays sentient for long.  The memory migrates through cambial sentences, a grammar that lives as sap-borne lysozymes.  If you cut a given segment it will smile at you, call you by a name it learned from a dragonfly in the Silurian, and quietly ask to be grafted somewhere else.  Say yes, and a tendon of fibre detaches, wraps your wrist like silk, and shoots upward, dragging you behind it.  The journey lasts anywhere from forty minutes to two centuries—timekeeping depending on how skillfully the Nyct argues gravity into submission.

Sentience itself is seasonal.  In late **Dimber Month** the entire being flowers intellectually.  Knowledge unfurls as glassy petals two kilometres across, each surface inscribed with inscribed dreams abandoned by past visitors.  Touch any one and an entire childhood disgorges: the face of a grandmother washing dishes in low gravity, the sound of a train that hasn’t yet reached its destination.  After seven days the petals septuple, begin curling inward, and become chick-like seeds attached to ballooning stalks of frozen alveolar tissue.  The winds of the Night catch them, spin them east; some crash on the backs of aerial whales and germinate there, creating instant archipelagos of dwarf laurel that later snap off and become nursery stars.

The linnet-folk are not part of the Nyctophora, yet they could not survive away from it.  Their lungs are half-leaf: translucent capillaries graded to the exact Fraunhofer lines the forest absorbs.  They inhale star-cold exhalations; exhaling yields minute crystal feathers that fall upward into the uppermost branches to patch holes left by micro-meteoroids.  They call themselves “spire attendants,” and every 42 hours they unhinge their ribs into eight hinged segments, turning themselves briefly into wind-agile kites.  Those weary of conversation just detach an ankle, plant it into cambium, and walk away as two selves, one forgetting and one remembering.  The village (everyone still calls it a village, though it looks like smoke wrapped around a question) therefore never has more than thirty-two souls; duplication balances death—usually a linnet who has flown too far outward of the field where up-arrow becomes down-arrow and cannot find his or her way back to shared orientation.  

Down among the canopy’s roots—though “down” is negotiable—lives an altogether slower citizenry: glass cicadas no larger than a thumbnail, as individuals.  Together, formidably, they are one ear.  The Nyctophora ended up without ears early on; sound instead was polled across a chain of resonating xylem until it collapsed into thought.  But thought is unforgiving when it has no listener, so the insects learned to clamber inside wind-bored arteries, rest wing against wing, and inhale noise as sweetness.  Their massed tympana hum the Night’s hidden heartbeat: three stretched pulses followed by a fourth almost inaudible, the tuning that permits agreed-upon physics to bind.  Travel covertly within that silence and you can reorder momentum, make yourself feel tilted forty-seven degrees north of recall.  Stay too long and you ossify: a perfect fret of exo-cicada armour hanging forever in the air, soldered by your own frozen last wonder.

Predators exist only as required.  Whenever a dream drifts too close to lucidity, the root network extrudes a hunting-shape: a serrated spiral of cellulose with a face like melted glacier.  It has no name.  It speaks in deja vu.  It does not kill; it rewrites.  Those who meet it and survive report backwards memories: they woke the previous day without ever having arrived, already possessing ticket stubs, footprints, and an inexplicable allergy to altitude.  Cartographers secretly call the shape an Editor; linnet-folk call it Brother Plow-back.  When the community grows too crowded with backward travelers, Brother Plow-back simply undoes the boundary events that led them here, and the Vertical Night sighs like a violin case closing.

Only one creature does not participate in the collective.  It is a plain black beetle, vulcanised matte as a coal seashell, no larger than a thumbnail.  Where it walks nutrient flows reverse: photosynthate is drawn downward, gravitrophism inverts.  It is the Nyctophora’s immune veto, evolved to remind overreach that individuality is not always benign.  The black beetle comes when sentience flowers too flamboyantly, eats three syllables from the dream-petals, then curls into a sphere and plummets, drilling a tunnel sixteen metres wide until the Night “learns modesty.”  The beetle is not alive in the usual sense—more a scar in spacetime the ecosystem keeps dragging forward.  When it dies it turns to graphite, an octagonal flake readable as any paragraph of history.  Gardeners collect the flakes, file them about the steady hearth where the thumbnail fire patiently burns, waiting for language cold enough to ignite.

And at the centre—not geometric but nutritive—lives the **Core Phloem Heart,** an organ like a cathedral-sized pomegranate suspended in the feedstock vacuum between two atmospheres.  Beneath its translucent pericarp you may still see the Andean condor’s last egg dreaming; the embryo has grown translucent feathers, bat-shaped, absorbing chlorophyll directly into bone.  When the embryo stretches, the fruit jerks; when the fruit jerks, half the Vertical Night cracks like struck chimes.  If the egg ever hatches, myth says the Night will break apart into a flock of planets.  Until then the Core Heart merely accelerates every thought by a factor of π, just enough that no awareness ever finishes itself before being carried off again up the endless vertigo of foliage.

So what lives here?  A graft.  A metaphor.  A forest that thinks—and, briefly, a thought that forests.
