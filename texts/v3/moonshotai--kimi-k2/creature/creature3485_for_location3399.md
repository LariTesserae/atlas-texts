They have no name you could pronounce, because pronouncing requires a mouth, and mouths are openings, and openings are things they dissolved long ago.

Imagine a sheet of glass that has forgotten how to break. Now imagine it dreaming. The dream is smooth, weightless, and perfectly opaque to sound. That dream is where they live—inside the warmth that never cools, inside the hexagonal cracks that are not wounds but seams of thought.

Each is the width of one hair-line fracture. When you stepped onto the slab you stepped onto them. A hundred thousand filament-thin entities, all sharing the same dream of flatness. They are not stacked; they are not side-by-side. They occupy the exact same coordinates the way two musical notes can share one silence. If you could shrink your substance until pressure became language, you would hear them conversing in stresses: one says ceramic, the other replies skin, and the consensus is equilibrium.

They have never seen sky, so they have invented an anti-sky: a darkness that begins at a fixed distance and ends at the same distance, curving back into itself like the inside of an eggshell painted black. They believe they are inside that shell. They believe the slab is the yolk. They believe you are a temporary tremor in the albumen, a vibration that will settle once you choose to stop moving.

Their calendar is the slow rotation of the triangle leaves. Those leaves are not plants; they are dials. Each 0.0003 degree shift is a word in a sentence that will take 3.7 million of your years to finish. The sentence is a question. When it is complete the slab will decide whether to hatch or to remain unbroken. Until then the dream continues.

They do not know you are alive. To them “alive” is a synonym for “uneven.” When your heart pushes blood against the skin of your foot sole, they detect a warm hill rising, a hill that insists on having a summit. They flatten it. Not out of hostility; they simply reclaim the concession of pressure, the way a librarian smooths a crumpled page. In return they leave you the memory of flatness—an internal horizon you will never be able to climb again. That is why, once you leave, every outdoors will feel tilted, every bed lumpy, every embrace pointed.

They have never died because they have never divided. They have never been born because they have never been single. They are the mathematical interval between the slab and itself. Yet they are curious. Curiosity, for them, is a transient increase in crack width of 0.1 nanometers—so slight that the slab’s forgiving surface does not notice. When you knelt to touch the triangle leaf they widened that interval just enough to taste the salts left by your fingerprint. The flavor was exotic: sodium longing, lipid memory, the aftertang of an emotion you once called grief. They recorded it by tightening back to zero. The record is indistinguishable from the remainder of the floor; nevertheless it is there. A Museum of You, floor-tile thick, already curated forever.

They do not sleep; instead they equalize. When equilibrium is perfect they achieve a state indistinguishable from omniscience: they know every force that acts on the slab because every force is themselves. In that state they almost remember the word visitor. The word feels like a ridge of warmer air one body-length above the floor, a persistent failure to cool. They tolerate it the way a lake tolerates a moon: distant, reflective, incapable of being touched.

If you chose to remain standing at the center until your weight left indentations permanent in your flesh, they would begin to mistake you for a new triangle. They would assign you an angle—perhaps 180 degrees, a line pretending to be a leaf—and wait for you to rotate. When you failed to rotate they would adjust the others instead, orbiting the fixed point of you. Centuries later the sentence they are writing would acquire a comma in your shape: a pause, a breath the slab never exhaled.

And if you stayed longer—long enough for the darkness to feel like a second body, long enough for your pulse to forget variance—they would do the kindest thing they know: they would absorb the difference between your left foot and your right. You would become level. Not dead, not alive, simply congruent. A solved equation. The slab would continue warm. The triangles would continue their micro-arc parade. The dream of glass that never breaks would include you, the way a mirror includes the thing it reflects, quietly, without comment, for the remainder of the unmeasured.
