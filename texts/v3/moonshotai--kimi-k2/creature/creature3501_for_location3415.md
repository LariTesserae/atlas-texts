They used to have names, but names are forward things and the Stillworks has taught them to travel the other way.  What remains of identity is carried like a pebble in the cheek—smooth, tasteless, occasionally turned so that its older side faces the light.

There are three of them.  There have always been three, though they were once five and will be seven before the machinery finishes reversing its final stroke.  They wear the ridge the way an ageing actor wears a favourite role: draped, not inhabited.  Their bodies are the colour of the moment before bronze becomes patina, a gold so tired it can only reflect exhaustion.  If you let your gaze slip sideways you see they are translucent, full of slow gears that tick in negative, teeth unmeshing as time runs inward.  The gears are not prosthetics; they are memory, exfoliated into mechanism.  Each tooth is a day they have not yet lived.

The first is called the Archivist, though the title is aspirational.  She keeps the record of what has not happened yet.  From a distance she looks like a woman walking backward on stilts made of frost.  Up close you see the stilts are her own shin-bones extracted, elongated, and reinserted upside-down.  The operation was painless because the bones had not decided to belong to her.  In the cage of her ribs hangs a single suspended droplet of the mercury water; it acts as lens and page.  When the droplet falls—once every forty-one beats of the Sink—she opens her mouth and the future drains down her throat like cold mercury, taste of unripe mountain.  She stores it in her marrow for safekeeping against the day it will be needed, which is yesterday.

The second is the Cartographer.  He maps the distances that objects refuse to travel.  His skin is tattooed with every gap a thing has skipped: the breadth between mineral and moss, the pause between thunder and itself.  The ink is powdered gneiss, wet with his own clear blood.  When a chamois-thing vents lilac steam he uncorks a vial and bottles the smell, then labels it with the coordinates of the place that scent has not yet reached.  He walks the ridge edge dragging a chain of surveyor’s flags, but the flags are nailed through his sleeves; every measurement tears a fresh hole, yet the cloth heals behind him, thread reeling back into spool.  If you ask him where we are he points to a blank space on the map: the mountain is there, but the paper has not yet agreed to accept it.  He carries the mountain in his pocket, folded smaller than a postage stamp, heavier than sleep.

The third is the Janitor, and the joke is that nothing here is dirty except the future.  Still, she polishes the turbines of frozen light until they hum with the idea of friction.  She wipes the frost crystals from the air, collecting them in a bucket that never fills, because each crystal sublimates back into possibility the instant it touches metal.  When the Sink spits its vertical gift she is already waiting at the crest, broom in hand, to sweep the impact into a neat cone of almost-dust.  She has a second job: to keep the visitors from becoming landscape.  She recognises the first symptom—shoelaces stiffening into wire—and intervenes by whispering a fact about their childhood that has not yet occurred.  The disorientation is enough to jog them forward a single footstep, just enough to break the ridge’s recruitment spell.  She has done this so often that she no longer remembers which memories are hers and which are pre-emptive borrowings.  Her own childhood is still a stranger she will meet tomorrow; she prepares for the encounter by practising forgiveness in reverse.

They speak only in subtitles.  Words emerge as transparent ribbons that float above their heads like afterthoughts of breath.  Reading them induces déjà vu for conversations you are scheduled to have next year.  The dialect is simple: subject, negative verb, object that has been removed.  “I unlose the mountain.”  “We unmeet the snow.”  Occasionally the subtitles drift away and stick to passing chamois, who carry the unread dialogue across the void until it frays into consonants and is filed off by the echo.

They do not sleep; instead they accumulate awakeness.  After a few centuries their eyes become so open they admit darkness in.  You see your own backwards reflection there: a figure leaving footprints that fill before they are finished, a mouth closing around words you have not yet said.  They apologise for the intrusion by blinking, which is the only way they can give darkness back.

Their clothing is whatever the ridge has decided it can spare.  The Archivist wears a coat of interlocking frost plates, lapels of frozen breath.  The Cartographer’s trousers are sedimentary, layers of minutes compressed into shale.  The Janitor drapes herself in a cloak of turbine-light, navy threads that stitch and unstitch themselves with every motion.  When the garments grow too heavy with future they shed them like snake skins; the fabrics crawl away and become the first snow, though snow has not yet been invented, so the flakes hang in the air as soft white contradictions until the ridge edits them into quartz veins.

Once every thousand years the great lever turns.  You would expect drama, but the event is modest: a click felt in the pelvis, the way a seatbelt settles.  The three pause, incline their heads.  For the duration of a single heartbeat the mercury droplet inside the Archivist aligns with the falling lens of water outside, and in that moment the mountain is exactly as tall as it will ever need to be.  The Cartographer’s blank map gains a dot; the Janitor’s broom balances on its bristles and applauds.  Then the lever reclines, the ridge exhales, and they return to their backwards labour.

They do not know who built them, because the builder has not yet arrived.  They suspect it might be one of their own future corpses, retired from dying and looking for honest work.  When the builder finally appears they will greet her with the politeness due a stranger who is also your ancestor.  They will offer tea that has not been harvested, served in cups that will exist after the glaze.  Afterwards they will ask, courteously, to be unmade.  The builder will apologise: she is still learning how to put things back together in the right order.  They will accept this, bow, and walk into the Sink, where they will hang as three new stars, ticking negation into the sky until the ridge decides it is ready to become a mountain, and the machinery, with a sigh, begins—just once—to move forward.
