What lives here is a collection of unbeings, each halfway between noun and weather.  

First comes the hush-speaker.  Imagine a neckless body the height of a kneeling child, its skin loose as wet silk, colored where lavender corrodes into ash.  It has no face: only a fault-line running vertically from crown to navel, constantly agape a slit no thicker than a hair.  Through this aperture the creature exudes the insect hum—not by lungs or wings, but by osmosis that leeches sound from the general density and concentrates it into a single, breath-thin note.  The hush-speaker can never choose the pitch; the swamp gives what it will, and the creature merely regifts it in atomic fidelity.  Because of this servitude it is listened to by everything else as if it were oracle and thief at once.  A beetle tower will sway towards the slit, aligning every frozen limb so the tone threads through the crevices of their shells and delicately retunes their architecture.  When the corridor of beetles is quiver-perfect, the hush-speaker inhales, re-swallowing its own exhalation.  It does not feed, exactly; it sustains itself on the echo-lag of a sound it never authored.

Beneath the corkscrew vines hang the unripe.  These resemble infant jaws, each the size of a curled thumb, skin glazed opalescent pink.  They bloom—though blooming is hardly the verb—upside-down, gingival buds drooling a sap so sweet it will crystallize into salt before it touches the ground, a mineral marrow safely returned to the sky.  Inside each translucent jaw rests a single milk-white seed-tooth.  Once in every three-syllable aeon the seed-tooth will part its own gum and exhale another hush-speaker, fully aware and already bored.  Thus the unripe are cradle, placenta, and pensioner of the species.  When catastrophe arrives (though none ever has) they can be prised open and reassigned: the teeth melt down into bead-spools of mercury, to be traded for moisture or silence across blurs that have momentarily split into economies.  But such transactions only add new loops to the spell.  

Wading the breath-fog is the torque-heron.  Tall as a man’s forearm is long, it possesses nine legs arranged like compass points.  At any instant seven legs are bone, two are liquid.  The bone legs plant themselves, the liquid ones fold and pour, and every seventh heartbeat the functions switch, so the bird’s center of mass is perpetually off-center.  Progress proceeds by a rolling stutter; without momentum the heron glitches forward one wavelength at a time.  Each step does not disturb the moisture-humus because the bone point is never heavy the moment it is solid; the poured leg spreads so evenly it becomes indistinguishable from the swamp’s own ground-skin.  The bird’s bill is a pollen-funnel made of pewter, long and so thin it appears to vanish at most angles.  It samples not for food but for color.  Where a beetle spine glows verdigris from trapped light, the heron extracts that hue and drinks it, refining the pigment through interphase veins until it issues from dozens of horizontal eyelids on each side of its neck as bands of sunset that smear across whatever surface they find.  There is no purpose in this repainting, only the slow diffusion of a quality somewhere between beauty and airborne staircase.

Deeper than root-depth lie the marrow-coils.  Picture a continuous digestive tract that has never needed a stomach; instead the tract has wrapped itself around a core of luminous fungus like a clock spring made of intestine.  The fungus is warm; the intestine glows transparent lilac in response.  Both grow, cell for cell, in mutual hesitation—neither advancing faster than the other—until the entire apparatus achieves a circumference of miles.  At three places on the coil’s outer skin, the tissue flares into sphincter mouths ringed with translucent teeth that never meet because the substance they might have bit has already been pre-dissolved along the gradient.  Through these mouths rise ubertendrils that braid into every root, beetle, hush-speaker, torque-heron, and dew-dream still floating understory.  Nothing drinks directly from the mouths; rather, one’s own matter momentarily twirls around the tendrils in a centrifuge of identity.  Travelers who trespass into the swamp long enough begin to feel their memories leaching along the marrow-coils like ribbons of separateness excavated and returned to the overall sip.  Some leave with the smell of orchids in their ear-drums; others arrive home to discover their childhood pets now extinct but present as thumbnail fossils hidden under every ridge of their fingerprints.

Finer than any of these are the stitchlings—clusters of convex membranes no thicker than soap bubbles, each carrying a fleck of night at the center.  They rise from the beetle towers when the hush-speaker releases a sudden harmonic hairline above its usual drone.  At that pitch, the backs of the beetles sweat viscosity; the sweat curls into shells too weightless to persist.  Each shell inflates with a single thread of dark that cements itself into a miniature pupil.  The stitchlings waft upward until caught by the inward-facing leaves overhead.  There, they act as single-shot needles, suturing the skyless space to the sogged earth until every mist veining has been anchored to embodied root.  The result is a net that keeps the world from slipping outward or skyward—except on occasions when a stitchling bursts, releasing its dark fleck.  The fleck becomes a point of absolute night, a pinhole through which an entire other ecology momentarily peers and, unable to corroborate what it sees, retreats in jittery dismissal.  Night, once freed, never recolors the landscape it merely absents a cylindrical shaft of visibility edged by translucent wound that heals by forgetting.

All these beings—together, apart—exist in perpetual negotiation over a single verb tense whose native language died before it could conjugate.  They collectively remember what a future should taste like without ever forming the intention of tasting it.  Individuality is neither abolished nor achieved; it is suspended like the wings of the moth- butterflies in equalized pressure.  Each unbeing, therefore, is a sentence spoken one phoneme per millennium by the slowest possible mouth, and the ear we bring to the listening is likewise slowed until the difference between saying and hearing evaporates into the same lukewarm inhalation that never condenses into rain.
