Who lives in that country is not one people but a single long organism—call it the Anda—that wears generations the way tadpoles fade into tails. It begins as yourself, the first time a footstep braids the water-necklace under the eggshell crust and the roar notices you. The moment the vibrations of your heart slip into the standing wave is the moment the Anda takes its initial breath. You do not become something different; rather, the difference settles into you like frost inside glass. Thereafter you are never lonely, only incrementally shared.

Out at the visible edge of every hour, another wanderer slips into the fold of sound where you first entered. Sometimes they arrive stooped under parkas stitched from mildewed encyclopaedias; sometimes they step naked except for the baby moss that has traded pigments with their skin overnight. The Anda waits until their footfalls align with yours at three, then five, then a thousand unsyncopated heartbeats. At that alignment, their memories are braided into the same rope of pulse, and an older strand anonymous with years falls away. The Anda never chooses who stays; it culls by resonance. If the newcomer’s breathing clashes too sharply with the planetary lullaby, they stay one season, learning to weep in the exact hertz that ruptures water into lace. If they can’t teach themselves that minor key, they vanish with the next gravity hiccup, reappearing as a blue tremor in the glass tiles. The Anda does not mourn; grief crystallises quickly here, then dissolves back into indigo drizzle.

There is no chief, no council, only distributed limbs that still wear individual names because pronouns seem too brief for thunder. Names go like this:

– Who Thinks in Gravity Errors  
– Berry-Mouth Current  
– Sister of Flat Explosions  
– the One Who Tried to Plant an Axe  
– Sleep-of-a-Single-Footstep

Each name is really a chord sung against the giant roar. When two chords accidentally harmonise, they share book pages from half-digested lungs and pass nights on water-skinned hills arguing whether the planet’s core thinks in iron or in sleep. Most days they move alone because travelling in pairs compounds the compression of air, and sudden walks become unexpected vineyards.

Whoever holds the name Sleep-of-a-Single-Footstep remembers roads, the idea of a road: scars hurriedly closed. She is the Anda’s archivist of directions, though she has wagered her life against the concept that a beginning and an end can be the same size. Fragments of her lectures float like spider silk:

“Distance is only a patience of water.”  
“For every plateau that forgets us another hollow grows an echo lacquered in your breathing.”  
“There are no horizons; there are only other dreamers curled at the brim.”  

She keeps no scrolls—instead she threads sentences through the bacterial porcelain underfoot. Letters form as pigment fractures: a violet shift where she tamped one syllable too hard, an oranged selvage where sadness leaked rich mineral. Scholars arrive between shifts in the glass mosaic; they peel up the tiles and inhale the sentence like incense. Some passers-by stay entombed there, paralysed mid-sniff, until the glaze fuses around them and their last exhalation writes the postscript to her paragraph.

Elsewhere in the Anda, a second limb calls itself Who Thinks in Gravity Errors. His nickname is Err. Born with ankles perpetually bending around missing topography, Err travels by falling where the planet suddenly dips a few metres. He teaches children—if children arrive—how to trust the missing elevation, how to anticipate the hiccup the way otters know hidden waterfalls. Within the Anda he is the one who folds bruise-like luminescences back into the sky before they puddle into blinding sloughs. He does it by cursing them in a dialect scraped off potsherds older than pyramids; the curse is tender, a lullaby of disgust pitched so precisely that the sky swallows its wound rather than listen again. Sometimes the bruises refuse, and then everyone sleeps for half a week on the colours of early meteors.

Sister of Flat Explosions beats garments from the stalks of enormous blood-purple leaves. She is the Anda’s loom of erosion. You hand her the memory of gold thread and she reconstitutes it as a sound zebra-walking inside the fabric; gold wants a pulse here, never a shine. Every cloak she finishes wears the small explosive signature of the leaves’ final sigh when plucked. Each time a wearer turns sharply, the cloak snaps, concussing warmth outward in fractal ripples. Explosions taste like small cinnamon memories—nobody dies, of course; the air simply puts on another layer of damp.

The One Who Tried to Plant an Axe is the Anda’s cautionary appendix. Story says he carried a hatchet made from meteorite iron unrusted, past the age where tongues still kept the idea of rust. He planted the blade flat into the crust, blade up, hoping a forest of handles would sprout and night could be held like fruit. Overnight the axe drank so deeply it groaned, and by dawn had bloomed into a fountaining crown of paper-thin metal petals wide as weather systems. He slept inside their fragrance; at moonset the petals snapped shut and folded him into a symphonic slumber. The Anda walked on, but the crown remained, endlessly echoing a single axe-fall. Travelers rest under it to learn how grief sharpens if you neglect to acknowledge its weight. Every year, one petal detaches, drifts down like a scythe and plants itself edge-down, beginning the miniature catastrophe all over again. Entire villages of wanderers—three, five, twelve human chords per—have been lulled to permanent dream beneath the lilac fungus scales that ring there.

Other limbs sleep down under the glass tiles. Their dreams leak upward as impossible skys—mauve cumulus battlefields where clouds duel with light that was abandoned by extinct suns. Wanderers do not precisely know which bones of themselves will be retained. They only know the Anda prefers raga lists of sensation: the scrape of ankle on crust, the inward groan that follows each gravity hiccup, the sudden alkalinity when peachy mold peels off a lover’s shoulder and sails upward on the breath you just vacated.

Inside the whole organism, never named for fear of collapsing its beauty, thoughts move like ants across honey behind time. It communicates through condensation: one morning you wake up sequinned with frost spelling instructions only your liver can interpret. Another week the fog lifts to reveal a miles-long sentence written in fallen pollen: WE KEEP YOU BECAUSE YOUR FIRST WORD FOR THUNDER WAS WONDER, NOT WARNING. The sentence dissolves; you drink it in the retinal burn of remaining auroras.

From a distance, the Anda looks like regret wrapped in thundercloud and made to walk. Up close it is one continuous refusal to be brief. It does not judge age; it simply remixes it. You might meet yourself two centuries older stepping backward through a silver curtain of water, still wearing your hope like a moon-grey glove. Then you realise decades of your memories have been stitched into the tapestry the Anda wears as private skin. You will recognise your childhood dog’s bark translated into the small fracture sound every time the crust reseals its wounds.

Eventually, if you survive learning how to speak without expanding extra air, if you let fungus notation bloom across your elbows like sheet music of another world, one morning you will wake translucent. The roar will nestle inside your ribs, modulating to a note tuned only at the frequency that keeps planet and bone aligned. At that moment the Anda cuts its first umbilical debt. You become the next throat it sings through; you may keep navigating for another season, another century, or simply finish your last blink folded into the spinal curve of a child who will arrive tomorrow by meteor. Either way the Anda holds your name like a pebble in its mouth, turning it against tooth and tongue until all edges are a wave that will never again choose to escape.

And outside, the plain waits, already licking another wound closed.
