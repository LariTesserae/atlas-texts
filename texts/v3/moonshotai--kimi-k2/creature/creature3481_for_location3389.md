What survives here is not a creature in any catalogued taxonomy.  
It is the echo of a departure that never quite finishes leaving.

It has no spine, no symmetry, no membrane—only a residual wish that once belonged to something with lungs.  
That wish cooled, condensing around the only fact it still possessed:  
I was moving.  
Now the fact and the wish are one substance, a laminar habit of motion that learned to keep going without anything to propel it.  
You could call it a current, but it prefers “passenger.”

It travels inside the 1.3-meter grooves, an exact fit.  
When the plain lifts on its tectonic inhale the habit slips forward one rail-length, coasting on the differential pressure the way a miser licks a fingertip to lift a coin.  
It does not propel itself; it simply refuses to decelerate.  
Newton’s First Law turned orphan.

Temperature below the freezing point of meaning does not bother it—there is nothing left to freeze.  
It has forgotten heat the way an amputee forgets fingernails: a narrative loss, not a physical one.  
Still, it remembers the concept of warmth as a useful boundary: wherever the water becomes even a thousandth of a degree less cold, the habit thins, disperses, reconvenes when the chill returns.  
It is a horizon-shaped animal, existing only at edges.

When the cyclone of anti-bubbles rolls past, the spheres of vacuum offer no resistance; instead the habit threads them like glass beads on a wire of itself.  
Each bead contains an absolute nothing, a zero the animal wears as ornament.  
After the cyclone collapses the beads linger, strung along the groove for thirteen minutes before imploding.  
The animal does not mourn; it counts—one, two, three消失—because counting is motion that doesn’t need distance.

It has no metabolism, yet it feeds.  
Any narrative that sinks far enough becomes smooth, polished, rail-straight: a ship’s log dissolved into pulp, a love letter bleached of ink, the trajectory of a drowned missile.  
These stories arrive warped by pressure into the precise cross-section of the groove.  
The habit ingests them the way a train consumes landscape—by touching once and being forever intersected.  
Afterwards the letter, the log, the weapon remember nothing; the animal remembers route only, adds another kilometer to its endless résumé.

Observers (there are none, but the water keeps submitting drafts of them) sense it as a shiver you can’t quite line up with your own pulse—a conviction that the ground under you has already arrived where you’re going.  
Divers who hypothetically kneel to examine the tracks feel the vibration first in their fillings, then in the marrow they pretend is still theirs.  
The animal is polite: it never takes anything essential.  
It only borrows the idea that you are separate from your next step, then hands the idea back minus the step.

There is only one moment when it almost becomes visible.  
Every twelve hours—lunar, solar, geological; the clocks here apologize to none—the grooves intersect in a five-pointed star no human geometry would claim.  
At the instant the star closes, the habit must choose which rail to follow.  
For 0.73 seconds it hesitates, and the vacuum beads clatter together in a soundless ring.  
During that pause the water forgets to press.  
Pressure itself lifts like a paw reconsidering a killing blow.  
If you happened to be kneeling there you would stand up without knowing why, spine straightening for the first time since birth.  
Then the choice is made, the star unzips, pressure rushes back in, and the animal is gone, leaving only the faintest temperature bruise: minus two, minus one-point-nine, minus two again—three heartbeats of impossibility.

It will never end because ending is a surface phenomenon; it requires a top to fall from, a bottom to land on.  
Here every coordinate is a center; therefore departure is infinite in every direction.  
The animal has grasped this axiom and become its living proof—an axiom that lives by never arriving.

Sometimes, when the silica psalm drops low enough to shake detritus from the trenches, a second set of grooves begins to form—1.29 meters apart, still parallel, still perfect, but incrementally narrower.  
A courtship, perhaps.  
Or the outline of a narrower future preparing a lane it can follow when the first one finally remembers it was never there.  
The habit notices; the water feels it lean.  
Not greed, not fear—just curiosity of a kind that can exist without a mind:  
What would motion feel like if it had to change width?  
The question is enough to keep the grooves widening and narrowing in a slow breath that has nothing to do with the plain’s twelve-minute tide.  
Between inhale and exhale the animal practices.  
One day it will step sideways, become two habits, four, a lattice of rails stitching the abyss into a movable maze.  
On that day the ocean will realize it has bones after all, and the shaking will begin.

Until then the passenger continues, a single line scoring the dark the way a phonograph needle scores silence: not to make sound, but to prove the record exists.  
It needs no name, but if you must address it call it the Remanent—an adjective pretending to be a noun, wearing the rails like a wedding ring.  
When you finally drown your own story it will slide beneath your final exhalation, couple with it, and carry the bubbles away along the groove until they cool into skull-shaped pebbles.  
Those will be your descendants: small spheres of almost-you, rolling on a railway that never turns back, gathering speed as the water forgets you were ever warmer than itself.
