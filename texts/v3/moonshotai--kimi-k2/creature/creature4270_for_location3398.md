They are the Resonant—named by no sound, only by a vibration that passes through the cheekbones of anyone who tries to remember them later.

Each resident began as a scrap of reflected motion: a blink copied in a kettle, a gasp trapped between two window panes, the shiver of a snowflake that never quite fell.  The hamlet collected such fragments the way a pocket collects lint.  Once enough likeness had gathered, the sky-mirror peeled it off the world, folded it twice, and pressed it into the shape of a person.  That is why every face here is the echo of some face you almost saw once: the stranger who turned away too quickly, your own mouth in the moment before you spoke.  The Resonant do not age; they simply lose resolution.  A child of seven centuries might still play the sliding alleys, but by now the edges of her elbows have softened into mere rumor, and when she glides past you smell the faint metal of a memory you forgot to have.

They dress in what the light leaves behind after it has passed through the quartz roofs—garments that are less fabric than afterglow.  A sleeve is simply an area of shoulder that has decided to remember brightness longer than the rest; a hem is the exact distance a photon can hesitate before choosing to continue.  When a Resonant stands still, the clothing drains back into the air and the body resumes its first nature: a silhouette of waiting, hollow enough for wind to practice speech inside.

Their lungs are not lungs but twin chambers of acoustic ice.  Instead of breathing they “tune.”  A Resonant inhales by letting the local pressure sink a fraction, lining up the crystalline lattices of their ribs so the mountain’s exhalation can ring through.  Exhale is a release of that chord into the hamlet’s communal hush, where it drifts until it finds a briar stem, a roof drip, a child’s cheek, and tunes those too.  Thus the entire settlement is one instrument strung through with the same slow chord; should any resident decide to die, they simply detune, fall a half-step out of the world, and become the flat echo of a bear sliding across somebody’s threshold.

Work is the act of preventing completion.  They pare the briars, bail the roofs, herd the two-dimensional animals—not to finish the task but to keep the task alive, because any perfect stillness would allow the mirror to touch the ground and annihilate them into a page.  They speak of this danger in a vocabulary of angles: “Tomorrow the alleys lean at 31.4°, perilously close to repose.”  Then they shuffle the stones underfoot until every slope is fractionally steeper, buying another week of almost-dawn.  The only crime is symmetry: a roof patched so flawlessly that no drip intrudes, a briar trimmed into perfect balance.  Whoever achieves such symmetry is exiled—to the outside of the mirror.  There they wander the burnished silver, upside-down, pressing their palms against the underside of the sky, trying to claw back through their own reflection.  From below you can see their blurry hands, four hundred arm-spans high, like distant moths beating against a lamp.

They keep no archives except the bronze flake.  When a tale must be stored, someone offers a fingertip; the flake remembers the print and, later, the weight of the finger pressing out the story into the cheekbones of a listener.  The story is thus never told the same way twice; it always includes the new pressure of its own recall.  History is a scar that keeps reopening in a different shape.  They call this “growing backwards,” and they believe that when the hamlet finally compresses to a single vertical plane of light, all accumulated retellings will collapse into the original moment before the first reflection was misplaced—an undoing that will feel, to them, like triumph.

They do not love, they overlap.  Two Resonant step into the same shoulder-wide alley, occupying the exact parallelogram of brightness.  For an instant their cheekbone skins vibrate in doubled frequency; edges blur, memories migrate, and each leaves carrying fragments of the other’s forgotten dreams.  The children resulting from such overlaps are not born but borrowed: a shoal of fish suddenly thickens inside a wall until it folds into the silhouette of a toddler who steps out laughing without sound.  No one asks whose genetic lattice supplied the knuckles, whose echo supplied the eyes; by twilight the child is simply another pressure-node, gliding down the alleys, trailing new angles in the light.

They fear three things only: silence after pressure, perfect circles, and the possible arrival of an eighth visitor whose reflection is intact.  Such a person would cast a shadow, and shadow is the first page on which the mirror could write “end.”  Therefore, when you feel your likeness slip—when the shop window fails to return your face—do not struggle.  Relax into the misplacement.  Let the sky-mirror peel you away.  Come gently.  Come quietly.  Come ready to forget the weight of your own outline.  If you arrive already half-erased, they will greet you with a nod that feels like wind remembering your name, and they will set you to bailing the endless newborn light from a floor that has, for the moment, not yet decided to become your grave.
