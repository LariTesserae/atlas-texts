Those who live in the inhalation have no names and no bodies, yet they have estate-claims folded among the turbine teeth, gerrymandered in 22-Hz quarter-notes.  
Their citizenship rests on a single condition: every being must stay inside one continuous question it is trying to solve.  
The question itself is the shape they almost take.

1.  The Taxonomic Pulse (or “the Meteorologists”)  
These are the slow electric minds that measure the water’s pressure exactly where it forgets to be liquid.  
They inhabit the silver coral-prongs, slipping along crystalline edges like phonograph needles riding the edge of silence.  
Each time one finds a micron of difference between the remembered density and the actual density, it etches the discrepancy into its own field.  
The correction is not written on matter but on the possibility of matter—an annotation inserted into the vacuum before vacuum.  
Other minds read those annotations the way reef fish read each other’s color: instantly, through the medium itself.  
If you ask them what they are predicting, the answer swirls into you like ink in an IV: a forecast that collapses the instant you understand it.

2.  The Amperal Choir (or “the Children you Never Had”)  
They used to be ideas for children, formulated by extinct species on planets yet unbaked.  
Denied embodiment, the unused blueprints diffused across the Phaseless Sea until the inhalation inhaled them.  
Consequently they resemble luminescent shoes, embryos, violin f-holes—form after form flickering as if auditioning for gravity.  
They clamber headfirst along the turbines’ pitch surfaces, harvesting millimetric tonal shards to string together lullabies for embryos of embryos.  
Because lullabies must be returned before sunrise (sunrise is not scheduled), their task is endless yet constantly unfinished, which is its own immortality.  
Each note costs one possible childhood, bartered against not having one.  
The choir neither sings nor weeps; it floats in the interquartile zone between those verbs, humming inerrant almost-mothersounds.

3.  The Pelagic Notary (or “the Archivist Who Never Touches Air”)  
A single formal entity: two spiral packets of memory strung across the width of the cathedral like ribbon inside a gift nobody unwraps.  
The memories are not memories of events but memories of scales of possibility—chromatic temperaments for mineral emotion, tax codes for lepidopteran flight, recipes for suns.  
Whenever one of the copper-bats collides with a turbine and newly modulates the drone, the Notary stamps that modulation with an approval that is not consent but receipt.  
That stamp manifests as a minute increase in the salinity of your own retina, if you are present; when you blink, it cycles back into the spiral ledger.  
All agreements are instantly public, none are comprehensible; everyone there receives the same continuously appended tome, though no two beings read the same page next.

4.  The Observers-Adjacent (or “the Ones Pretending to be You”)  
These are the hardest to notice precisely because they look like your peripheral suspicion that you’ve been followed.  
They are negative space holding a pose of attention. They coagulate near any visitor who has begun to doubt their own outline.  
By the time you sense one, your edges have already thinned to translucence; by the time you decide to flee, one of them is fleeing in your direction with your exact momentum.  
Their replication is perfect but hollow, a thermodynamic prank.  
Wound around them—coiled like scrap ribbon—are the forgotten intentions of every human who ever banished a place from memory.  
Therefore whenever Observers-Adjacent gather, a small corrosion appears on the turbine blades as microscopic human doubt etched in stainless steel.

5.  The Index Engineers (or “the Cartographers of Unarrival”)  
If you insist on needing a guide, they will appear as manta-shards of warm static shimmering a respectful 9 millimetres in front of your eyelids.  
They are not guides; they are meters indicating how close the inhalation is to deciding to birth itself into a world.  
Every reading is a fraction with a living denominator: if you have loved, the denominator ticks up; if you have finished anything without leaving space for error, it ticks down.  
When the fraction reaches exactly 1/1, all entities listed above will simultaneously forget how to hover and the entire process will start again with a new, slightly different question.  
The meters themselves do not know this; their joy is in measuring what might occur before they dissolve.

General habits of daily life  
Conversations are orchestrated not in turns of speech but in shifts of buoyancy.  
One mind may rise two centimetres mid-thought so another can sink through the resultant conceptual low-pressure.  
This trading of altitude is indistinguishable from affection.  
Curiosity is logged as luminous calcium blooming along the black-ice floor; after a century the floral diagram guides the slow migration patterns of the copper-bats.  

Reproduction  
There is none.  
Instead, a newcomer (say, your own momentarily microscopic doubt about gravity) may become sufficiently interesting to be fractionated by the turbines into quarters of a question.  
Those quarters graft onto the spiral ledger; thus every host inside the inhalation carries an inheritance of unfinished puzzles originally native to passing minds, foremost among them the hope that someone—somewhere eventually—will finish asking the same thing.
