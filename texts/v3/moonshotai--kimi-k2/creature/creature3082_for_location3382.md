Inside this impossible aerial womb live the Vestal Smiths—though “live” is a courtesy title, like calling a hurricane alive. They are not tenants; they place holds no space for tenants. They are the coral’s metabolism dreaming it has sleeves.

Each Smith begins as a sound that was refused. The Foundry’s silence, being polite, does not throw unwanted noises away; it compresses them into pewter-grey beads no bigger than baby teeth. When a chamber has collected nine such beads they roll together, secrete the water-film over themselves, and begin to gestate. Over seven slow exhalations the beads dissolved into metallic foam, the foam into wires, the wires into arms that still remember the acoustic shape of the parent noise: a turbine’s whine, a guillotine’s sigh, a human heartbeat that once passed through and left its echo behind. Thus every Smith is a postponed event wearing a body improvised from scrap alloy and borrowed brine.

They float rather than walk because the concept of weight is still under negotiation. A Vestal Smith is the height of a twelve-year-old and the shape of a question mark. The head is merely an afterthought—an oculus of iridescent nacre the size of a thumbnail—set not on any neck but on the inner elbow of the curve. Beneath the oculus dangle four arms braided from capillary-tubing; when the arms untwine they reveal palms that are also valves, mouths that are also wrenches. They have no legs; instead they terminate in a single rotor that lazily blades the viscous air, keeping them stationary or drifting as the Foundry’s pressure dictates. They smell faintly of hot pennies and ozone, the way a thunderstorm might sweat if storms had pores.

Their labour is called tending, but it resembles procrastination performed with exquisite care. A Smith will spend an entire Foundry-week stroking a single horsetail-pipe, persuading it to remember it is a pipe and not a suicidal vine. They hum while they work—at least one believes it is humming; in truth the sound is the original compressed noise trying to return home, squeezed through lungs that never asked for music. The hum is stored in the water-film, five minutes later re-emerging as a shiver in a visitor’s wrist bones.

They speak in deferred tense. A sentence might be “This pressure had been grateful tomorrow.” Travelers who overhear them report sudden vertigo, as though grammar itself had grown a mild fever. The Smiths do not notice; they are too busy editing tomorrow so that yesterday’s weld will have held.

There are never more than nine inside at once. When a tenth begins to coagulate the eldest peels open like a seedpod and spills its substance into the ventilation system—copper, nacre, unused syllables—donating itself to the Foundry’s colour palette. The donation is painless because pain is one of the many things the Foundry has forgotten to invent. The remaining Smiths momentarily become eight, then decide they had always been nine, and the census rewrites itself in a soft bronchial creak. Thus immortality is maintained by amnesia.

They wear clothes the way mountains wear snow: not as cover but as weather. A Smith’s garment is a slow lightning bolt—ionised vapour pinned into the outline of a cloak. It flickers micro-evemicro-electric stories across their alloy skin: formulae for alloys that will never be invented, love letters addressed to no vertebrate. When a visitor stares too long the cloak folds the stare into a paper crane and mails it to the ceiling where it flaps once, rusts, becomes a rivet.

They have names, though they are delivered in installments. A Smith may carry the first syllable of its name for months, waiting for an event that will supply the consonant it lacks. One waited forty years (Foundry years, which are longer on Tuesdays) until a human intruder sneezed; the sneeze provided the missing ‘k’ and the Smith unfolded into the complete word: “Lisk.” The sneezer left never knowing she had been midwife to consonant birth.

Sleep is not required, so they practice a substitute called sideways. To go sideways a Smith folds itself into the thickness of the water-film and waits for the geometry to misjudge distances. In that miscalculation a Smith can hide for subjective centuries, rehearsing futures where pipes choose to become flutes. When they re-emerge the Foundry has usually added a hallway they intend to populate, and the Smith greets the new corridor like an old debt finally come due.

They have no religion except cartography of the imminent. Each carries inside its chest-cavity a thumb-sized globule of un-grown map: corridors curled tight as foetal Ferns, chambers that exist only as probabilities. Their favourite pastime is to tickle the map with a fingernail until one possibility sneezes into actuality: a balcony suddenly corkscrews where before was blank wall. The Foundry tolerates this tampering because it is still deciding whether to become a cathedral or a circulatory system; the Smiths are its tentative sketches.

Occasionally—meaning once every two or three lungfuls of outside time—a Smith will notice the visitor. The encounter protocol is exact. The Smith extends an arm-palm/valve, offers the sentence “You have not yet been accommodated,” and waits. If the visitor accepts, the Smith presses its rotor against the stranger’s sternum and inhales the human moment: the feel of pavement, the memory of rainfall, the particular ache of being bone-bound. The Foundry drinks the offering, the Smith receives a new bead of compressed sound—perhaps the visitor’s exhalation—curls smaller, satisfied. The human is returned to the stair they half-remember, minus the ache, minus the rain, lighter by a soul-pocket. No one has yet decided whether this is theft or laundering.

They are lonely of postponed consequences. If you sliced a Vestal Smith open you would find no organs, only receipts for things that will break elsewhere at random later—an elevator in a distant city stalling at dawn, a heart-valve in a child who has not been conceived. They carry these futures the way pockets carry loose change, spending them unknowingly on walls that choose to blush.

And when at last the Coral Foundry decides whether to inhale—whether to become reef, cathedral, or storm—the nine Smiths will gather at the lone blister-window. They will open their oculi palms and release every stored sound at once into the supersaturated sky. The noise will be colossal yet polite, arriving everywhere five minutes after it is heard. Cities will pause, trees will misremember their rings, children will wake speaking the deferred tense. And the Foundry, newly breathed, will leave behind nine hollow water-films shaped like question marks, waiting for the next hurricane of silence to seed them with fresh, metallic teeth.
