### Who and What Lives Inside the Lagoon-Organ  

1. The Plant-Consciousness  
Its own largest tenant is an intelligence older than the basin it settled within, a single lily that dreamed the basin up around itself so that limestone rose like vapor, settling in slow circles like white moons accruing around a gravitational heart.  
Every cell in the entire three-hundred-metre lagoon is continuous with every other, communicating not so much by chemistry as by identity. When the west wind comes stroking clean ribs of heat across the surface, the whole organism jellies slightly, as if savoring the touch, and simultaneously every petiole corridor inside Trähwick surfaces the same subtle shiver.  
This identity is neither inviolate nor entirely singular. Once every solar year—when the newly arrived comet is briefly occluded by the far-side moon—the internal sea mutates into a slow iris that dilates upward into a pupil.  The pupil is black solvent extending seven kilometres straight down, perfectly cylindrical, lined with twin helices of phosphorescent stomata that open and close in respired semaphores.  All night the column folds, collapses, unzips like a spinal galaxy, speaking into alternative thermoclines while the upper body never so much as ripples.  
The plant-Consciousness calls the deeper column its Ninth Hypostase—as opposed to the Towers (the Sixth), the Rattling Bellows (the Seventh) and the Memory Fungus (the Eighth).  Civilians rarely glimpse the hypostase except in admitted dream grafts: you wake sweating because the stone felt offended, because somewhere deep inside stole the idea of gravity that you were born with.  
At these moments the plant-Consciousness momentarily forgets it is separate from the human mind that touched it; you will find your own face surfacing like an oil slick on the jelly literal.  When the wakefulness rinses away your features separate out again into root and lip and tongue, but between the two versions something always remains swapped: perhaps your anxiety now blooms jasmine at dusk, or perhaps the lily awakens knowing your phone number, calling you across interstellar radio in moist auroral morse.

2. The Folded Citizens  
They call themselves *the kept edges*, a name that began as embarrassment and became a point of pride.  Gene lineages pooled here three hundred human years ago, stepping politely through the membrane together with indigene clans of slipstitch salamanders that had already rented foliar balconies for ten prior millennia.  Their languages began interbreeding, creole upon creole, until speech itself became biological—tones you taste, consonants migrating from vocal cords to chloroplast syntax.  
Adults breathe slowly enough to sprout; children are born with root fibres tied in umbilical knots that later straighten into collagen.  If you cut one, sap bleeds first, then blood.  Doctors—who are merely specially conductive stems—cede their place to poets during trauma, because rhyme is the only anaesthetic the organism accepts.  
A walker on any given ramp can meet a merchant trailing a cone of luminal pollen that condenses into theatre tickets for performances impossible to review; the tickets interpret your residual cortisol and fly open as birds that sing the climax before you’ve stepped inside.  Elsewhere two lovers discover that their shadows are, without their ever noticing, siblings now divorced—each shadow has migrated to the next body over, so they kiss while silhouettes argue.  
Citizens do not die exactly.  Instead, when convalescence falters, they surrender their calcium and amber to a communal suture line that reattaches them into the curtain of nerves traversing the ceiling of every lumen.  One may still speak, though the mouth hangs fifty metres overhead: your uncle waves from the rafters, six months dissolved,still telling jokes about fruit that laughs itself inside out.  

3. The Errant Choirs  
Voice carries more than sound here.  Every morning one hundred lung-spheres detach from the bellow architecture and launch themselves buoyant over water.  They are thirty percent helium—waste from night-cooling, forty percent acetate and thirty percent actual human exhalation donated the previous evening.  Children ages six to twelve ride inside hollow aerogel sacs attached to the spheres by axon-cloth tethers.  Up they go to catch the day's first inversion.  
Once aloft they perform the Chorus of Temporal Drainage, a keening meant to coax yesterday’s minutes loose from the sticky gravitational residues at the bottom of the hourglass sea.  The chord they sing is the same chord that made the bellows, only played backwards.  Yesterday hears itself and crawls upward, dripping over adults who run errands in its receding light.  Each citizen carries yesterday’s minutes as a translucent cloak, useful for mending shelves, or for convincing elders about the market price of cloud silk they claim has become tomorrow’s weather.  
At sunset the choir deflates in reverse sequined folds; every child retains one copper second, knotted like a coin at the center of their sternum.  This small temporal prosthesis can be traded for any single memory of the city: an entire summer of bamboo pipe music or the precise flavor of a criminal’s toes dissolved into warn broth.  Most often the children keep the coins, by royal decree, until the day they first fall in love without wanting to remember how it ends.

4. The Animal Delegates  
They are not representatives in any parliament we would recognize; instead they are *arguments* the organism makes with itself.  
The neutrinoid dragonflies: naked singularities dressed as indigo wet glass, each wing a plank cut from time’s own spare room.  They pass through human bodies without touching yet register a warm pressure as if a second person stands inside your chest speaking your name in grey italic.  They feed on unfinished schedules—the more incomplete your agenda, the brighter they glow.  A citizen dreading a meeting will look overhead to see thirteen dragonflies blazing like magnesium taper candles.  After the meeting is either endured or fled, the light blinks out along with the dragonfly.  Entire flocks converge on the town during delayed monsoons; the sky becomes a fever dream of ruby dusk.  
The rollwings are glued entirely from reused soundtracks: the creak of rope bridges, the liquid kiss of gossip hitting water, the honk of ancient ferryhorns still echoing from a limestone throat.  A rollwing brushing your cheek with its sail tastes exactly like the word you almost said yesterday but swallowed at the final vowel.  
The eel-lungfish in the basin beneath, older than the stones they occupy, exist because limestone once overheard grasshoppers arguing about rainfall and stored the quarrel as slow calcium regrets.  Each dusk they regurgitate the quarrel into four-note phrases no human orator can articulate: sad, impatient, overfond, already forgiven.  Citizens amplified these phrases through pipe organs until an entire generation of birds decided to speak semi-colons as a main diet.  

5. The Memory Lichen  
Each footfall upon the memory ridge releases a syllable. One woman hopped on one foot from rim to center and discovered she had sung the diary of an ancestor who drowned in 1731 while learning the post-codpiece dance.  The diary ended with a hiccup because the circle could not accommodate the woman’s heartbeat.  When she tried to translate the final word—“”—it turned out not to be a word but the exact sensation of half-salted fear halfway through cartwheel practice.  She cartwheeled home leaving behind a furrow the lichen followed, reciting her vertigo back to itself in quieter cursive.  Ever since, carts in Trähwick neither roll nor glide; they cartwheel only, obeying the engraved spiral of that first night.  

6. The Visitors Who Haven’t Figured It Out Yet  
They arrive on mechanical wings of aluminum and lithium.  Their faceplates fog because they refuse to remove the helmets that think they know better about humidity.  Because they never learned nasal resonance the lagoon-lily decides they must be larvae permitted only the outward journey.  Their steps sink slightly; the membrane nudges them like a nursemaid with one surprised blink.  Within minutes the visitors' exopacks register carbon dioxide at levels that cannot possibly support human life, yet the visitors do not die.  This is evidence the plant has rendered every breath enfolded inside a separate hour that hasn’t yet happened to them.  
The visitors break off chitinous whispers which the towers drink like milk. Their helicopter explodes into a single flowering vine that climbs up its own spreading sparks.  When the last spark is swallowed, the visitors themselves begin to release vapor.  On the infrared feed sent home to continent governments, the vapor carries heat signatures shaped as affectionate hand-holding nobody remembers initiating.  
Soon the visitors are wrapped in corridors they never notice forming.  They set up cameras; the cameras sprout eyelids.  They check time; time checks back, asks politely if tomorrow might borrow a second.  The visitors speak aloud, trying to log coordinates for evacuation, but what prints on their recorder is a list of cloud identities they expected would greet them: “Cirrus Alexei,” “Stratus Lin.”  
Eventually they plant their flag.  A child who once died of fever 200 years ago and now resides in the ceiling nerve wakes just long enough to pluck the flag and weave its stars into a necklace for a squirrel who lost her wedding ring in yesterday’s rehearsal.  The visitors’ eyes film over with memoir-green.  They leave recording everything awe-perfect, unaware they are reciting it already growing inside the city’s bellies; if you pause the footage at minute 43 the woman visitor isn’t smiling; she is beginning to fruit a small translucent bud at the back of her throat.

7. What the City Dreams About Itself  
Beyond the visible geography, the city keeps nine thousand balconies it has not yet extruded; these exist only as data folded in proteins crystallized about the core.  They dream.  One dreams it is an open-air platform where every citizen’s name is also the name of a cloud layer, and all the clouds agree to rain just enough to fill every mug left outside on purpose.  
Another balcony dreams it loves a lake that hasn’t decided whether to be water or radio silence.  In its dream, the balcony throws itself into the lake and sinks as a ladder of luminous bones; the lake drinks the bones and foams into orchid form.  The orchid opens revealing a second balcony dreaming the same dream over again but colored ultraviolet this time.  
Sleepers in Trähwick often wake clutching strange souvenirs from these phantasm balconies: a tablespoon of precipitation that tastes like someone else's childhood, or the particular angle of moonlight required to pour grief into a teaspoon.  

8. The Pending Embryo  
The organism is presently thickening an invisible bud some twenty metres below the surface.  Its cellular walls contain organelles still debating their electrochemical majority; one faction insists on remaining gillish, another campaigns for retinal curvature.  All factions agree on one law: when the bud ruptures it will not be tower, animal, citizen, memory, or mood—it will be an entire second novelty of biology, something the organism admits it has only imagined in secret since the basin first cooled.  
Citizens compare the bud to pregnancy but this is anthropocentric.  The more accurate image comes from slipstitch salamanders who call the bud “the fold beyond the welcome.”  Oxygen levels beneath twenty percent trigger an automatic pressure wave that folds existence like parchment until what was outside slides in and what was inside walks out wearing the previous outside's color.  
If you stand absolutely still at centroid latitude holding your breath, inside sixty heartbeats you will feel your ribs attempting to translate.  Do not worry; this is merely the organism copying the geometry of your breastbone into a template for calcium origami intended for whatever emerges.  If the thing inside likes your skeleton it may offer to perfect it—perhaps giving you the ability to exhale light, or to store entire novels in the marrow.  Or it may simply nod and move on: the lagoon is polite above all things, remembering every dignity it ever accorded even when it can no longer recall why.
