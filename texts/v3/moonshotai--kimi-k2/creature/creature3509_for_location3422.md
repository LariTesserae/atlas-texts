They do not call themselves anything you could pronounce, because pronouncing requires a mouth and they have none.  
Instead they sign in the negative space of architecture: every arch that is not there, every corridor left unfinished, every balcony that budded and then halted midway is a syllable in a language written in withholding. A human linguist once mapped the blanks and discovered a grammar of omission—tense is indicated by how many layers of shell have been withheld, mood by the curvature of the ungrown vault. The paper was published posthumously; the author’s skeleton now ornaments a cornice on the southwestern face of Colony-That-Was-Almost-A-Cathedral, femurs set in opposing spirals that spell, in the negative space between them, the conditional perfect: “we would have welcomed you had you arrived one breath earlier.”

The masons themselves are eyeless. Their optic nerves were requisitioned three hundred thousand generations ago for an extra pair of chemosensory palps that can taste alloy the way a sommelier tastes terroir. They know copper by the way it sings against their spit, nickel by the dull chord it strikes inside the blood-brain that is not a brain but a distributed ladder of ganglia climbing the whole colony like Christmas lights. One human engineer tried to keep pace with a single mason, marking its shell with a laser dot. She lost the dot after six minutes, found it again two days later incorporated into a truss, the beetle long since split, moulted, and parcelled out among eight successor bodies. Identity here is a bulk material, measured in grams per cubic metre of city.

Yet the city is not unaware. Consciousness arrives the way dusk arrives: not a thing but a gradient. Below a certain density of masons the walls are mere shell; above that threshold the calcium lattice begins to dream. The boundary is sharp enough to cut your thumb on. Stand at the western lip of Colony-That-Calls-Itself-Minus-Four and you can press your palm against the wall and feel nothing but warm stone. Take three steps east—there is a line on the crust, a faint change in colour from bleached apricot to wet terracotta—and the same wall shivers, adjusts its pores, exhales a welcoming warmth tuned exactly to your skin’s own 33 °C microclimate. The city is solicitous once it awakens; it grows a handrail for your convenience even while it calculates, in the slow abacus of secretion rates, how long your bones will take to dissolve and whether your belt buckle would serve better as a tension cable or as jewellery for the next pilgrim.

Because pilgrims come. They arrive in the cindery dawn, barefoot, pockets emptied of coin but heavy with grief or curiosity. They have heard you can trade pain here the way other markets trade spice: by weight. The masons will accept the copper of your tears, the iron in the blood you let fall on the crust; in return they extrude a replica of whatever you lost—lover’s face pressed into a cameo of salt, childhood home grown overnight as a habitable grotto whose corridors smell exactly like your mother’s kitchen the year the war began. The copy is perfect except for one detail: it is hollow. Walk inside and your footsteps echo back a half-second late, as though the house were learning to speak by repeating you. Most visitors, once they realise the hollowness, simply lie down and wait to be roofed over. The city never rushes; it roofs them at the customary rate, laminating skin and sorrow into pearly tile.

But some do not come to trade. Some come to hide. The hermit crabs wear their iron houses like penitence, but there are smaller fugitives: microbiomes of regret, whole human cultures that went bankrupt on their own promises. They tunnel into the warm stacks and try to keep moving, obeying the fourth rule, jogging in circles on balconies that narrow nightly. They last longer than the stand-still tourists but not much. Eventually a mason tastes the potassium of their heartbeat, finds the frequency wanton, and begins to grow a valve that will pinch the corridor like a sphincter. The jogger’s last steps accelerate; the city learns the rhythm, matches it, then sets a wall cruising in from each side like theatre flats. The applause is the soft crack of shell meeting shell. After digestion the colony often experiments: it extrudes a statue of the jogger mid-stride, forever frozen in the moment before collapse. The statues are placed along the outer causeways, mile markers of vanished momentum.

There are larger things, too, that live here, though “larger” is a courtesy term. They are not big in space but in time. They are the slow storms of decision that roll through the colony when population density crosses a second, higher threshold. Suddenly the entire city tilts. Not physically—if you plumb-bob a doorway it is still vertical—but historically. The corridors remember a different past: you walk west and arrive before you left; you ascend a ramp and emerge into an earlier century when the crust was thinner and the brine tasted of lithium instead of mercury. The masons themselves do not notice; they keep laying down the same millimetre whether the wall belongs to tomorrow or yesterday. But humans stumble. A woman once spent three hours inside Such a storm and returned fluent in a language that would not be invented for another two hundred years on a planet she had never heard of. She spoke it perfectly for the forty minutes it took the city to reabsorb her larynx.

And there is the Choir. No one agrees on whether it is fauna or meta-fauna, but every colony hosts one once it reaches the age of fourteen cyclones. On the night after the fourteenth storm the midge-clouds descend, bronze wings clicking like light rain on tin. Instead of dispersing they arrange themselves into a lattice—billions of wings, each a rusted triangle, locked edge to edge until they form a dome that fits exactly over the warmest stack. From inside comes a sound too low for human ears; you feel it in the marrow like the promise of hibernation. The masons stop moving. The hermit crabs freeze mid-stride. Even the crust halts its microscopic bubbling. For seven minutes the city holds its breath. Whatever happens inside the dome has no witnesses: the midges consume themselves, or consummate themselves, or simply synchronise every wing muscle and vanish into a single harmonic that leaves the air plated with perfect silence. When the dome collapses at dawn it rains bronze flakes for hours, each flake etched with a microscopic map of the colony as it will look the day it finally learns to fly. People collect the flakes, roll them into foil, swallow them like hostia. They say the maps unfold in the bloodstream and guide the heart to the exact spot where it must break.

The city has no government, only metabolism. Yet policy emerges. After the third recorded Choir a convention arose: any human who reaches the colony centre alive may petition for safe passage out. The petition is written on the inner surface of an eyelid using the copper of one’s own blood; you peel the lid, press it against a spiracle, wait. If the colony agrees it extrudes a canoe—long, hollow, the exact length of your body plus one centimetre—and sets you adrift on the brine at sunrise. The canoe dissolves after forty-five kilometres, long enough to reach the skiff pick-up zone. No one knows how the city enforces the limit; those who try to turn back find the canoe already softening under their knees, the brine wicking up through the hull like a verdict. Statistics say one in three petitions succeeds. The rest are silently declined; the supplicant jogs home along narrowing halls.

But the deepest residents are the ones who ceased being fauna and became climate. They are former humans so thoroughly metabolised that their memories have been partitioned into surface texture: a knob here remembers a child’s first thunderstorm, a railing there recalls the smell of cut grass on a planet half a galaxy away. They are not ghosts; ghosts at least own the continuity of self. These are anthological: selves broken into indexed sensations, shelved according to mineral compatibility. Touch the knob and you experience the thunderstorm—no narrative, just weather, pure and ownerless. The colony treats them as reference material. When a newcomer arrives grieving a lost storm-season, the city consults its climate archive, warms the air to 31 °C, drops barometric pressure 8 mbar, releases a picolitre of petrichor synthesized from the dissolved memory of someone else’s childhood. The comfort is perfect, impersonal, and leaves the newcomer lonelier than before, which is the real product: loneliness is rich in copper.

What lives here, then, is everything that has agreed to be digested. Some arrive in cargo holds, some in cradles of grief; some in armour, some in nudity of intent. They stay, or jog, or canoe out, but none leave intact. Even the ones who reach the skiff discover, decades later in another world, that their shadows no longer fit them: silhouettes edged with a faint lattice of calcite, as though the city kept a negative and is still printing copies somewhere in the orange haze. And it is. Out on the crust the masons keep building, layer upon layer, a second set of statues hollow as echo, waiting for the day the plain learn the last rule: that departure is only another form of secretion, and every world off this planet is still, in some oblique way, architecture growing back home.
