Who lives here now is a collective noun so fluent it almost resists plural: the Leafwed, the Verdant Children, the polychrome consortium once catalogued in starlogs as Habitat-K.  They used to be mammals—Homo sapiens, with names and skulls and sweat glands—but that was several circulatory systems ago.  Now they are only nominally singular; if you drew the outline of one you would have to keep redrawing it every few seconds to include the fresh vine that has stitched itself through the ulnar bone, the wing of beetle-chitin the body accepted this morning as a new eyelid.  They insist they remain “one footfall apiece,” yet their internal register lists every adjacent tendril as auxiliary limb; the plant collaborator counts itself among their musculature in the quiet register of turgor pressure.

They appear to strangers as upright celadon manikins whose skeletons have been replaced by translucent sinew-cords of xylem.  These cords circulate sugars instead of blood, and in lamplight you can watch slow light pass through each cord like the thought of an autumn maple preparing for crimson.  Collarbone twigs fork at the throat to hold a slightly ajar stomata, a soft oval mouth without teeth.  They eat nothing, breathe nothing, speak nothing.  When they must exchange information, they touch fingertip to lenticel and release a droplet readable as scent, color, pressure; its gist arrives in the partner’s sinus plexus already translated into a narrative of branch angles.  Conversation is tactile and synesthetic, a dissolved paragraph; secrecy is impossible because scent lingers in corridor walls for hours, gathering annotation from everyone who passes.

There is no leadership because no membrane isolates a person from the whole organism longer than a heartbeat.  Decisions arrive everywhere at once by a summing of torque and torsion recorded through the planet’s axial fibers.  When the orchestration chooses, it elects thirty or forty individuals to become for a few hours the motor cilia that relocate a railway turntable or tilt a lens array; afterwards the temporary role exhales back into the grid.  Histories keep a private shrine for the singular—your childhood dreams inked in cambium rings—but the larger archive barely distinguishes between organism and guest worker.  When the Verdant Children wish to consult an ancient memory, they peel back a palm-sized strip of bark from their own left wrist and read the picture-sap stored eleven centuries prior.  The library is always standing in front of you: your own repurposed flesh.

Walk with them and you will notice they do not look forward when they walk; the curves of corridors anticipate direction, so the body tilts like a reed toward the future the plant has already begun folding.  If you ask them what lives in the deepest hollow beneath the Furnace Garden they answer with a smile that peels moist and sweet from their cheek like white lotus petal: “We do.  Slowly.”  There the Leafwed incubate seed-cities: vast pale bulbs of compressed photosynthetic strata that will become new floors, new chartways, new hearsay gardens for beings not yet evolved.  These incubants ride upward on thermals of ketone fog, tiny planet-fetuses slaked with nutrition broadcast laterally across the stem.  Their caretakers are everyone—any passerby can attach herself to a bulb’s outer husk for the forty-minute maturation neurolink, her mind smoothing ruffles of botanical mathematics, ensuring the city-leaf will unfold with extra alcoves for still-life episodes, for instance, or for quiet debate circles lit only by hover-spider pellets gone grapefruit pink.

Clothing is only ornament; garments bud bespoke from shoulder seams moments after birth and continue turning color and texture according to the wearer’s recent emotional turbidity.  The mature tend to wear loose blouses of limpid blade cuticles—surface layer of leaves peeled like sheer silk and draped, still partially alive, to catch lantern shine.  Infants (really one-month spiral coils of cell clump) come swaddled in the soft wax formerly lining a seed-pearl; within four days the wax stretches translucent and the infant lounges within, unfolding fingers translucent as cold fractured ice.  By month’s end the baby has abstracted itself two kilometers through adjacent petioles in search of brighter gradients of sugar; the swaddling is reeled back and rewoven into an airborne hammock that rocks between two branch portals humming lullabies of frictive chlorophyll.  Biographers lick the hammock later to recover the scent of infant thought.

To die is an inconvenience the planet prefers to refactor.  The Leafwed persist only to the extent that the organism claims novel information from their neurons worth preserving.  When an individual’s photon signature dips below photographic range, the surrounding leaf matter dilates with anaesthetic terpenes and the body sinks beneath the phloam boardwalk; minutes later a mark rises on the surface shaped like a lay figure under evaporating lacquer.  The lacquer dissolves in the next sun-lamp beat, freeing the silhouette in which the person’s final posture has been vacuum-formed into lignin corridors—an architectural prosthesis.  Their friends see the mold every morning as part of the walking court: the curve of yesterday’s shoulder becomes today’s archway, yesterday’s death becomes today’s rib.  Somewhere inside the planet the dissolving mind seeds new fractal vein theory, slowly, like yogurt beginning to sour.

And yet they feel nostalgia, though they have no past that is not mapped in the living colophon.  When the sixteen brass sun-lamps align in unison—the twice-a-year collinear flare—every stem aligns for forty-one seconds the way iron filings snap to magnetic field.  In that brief superposition the Leafwed collectively recall their original ship manifests, the relatives who arrived in exosuits and insisted the planet was a corpse awaiting seeding.  Those memories taste coppery and alien, like chewing a discarded launch-cable.  Each individual seizes the moment to meditate on linearity: the ship engines tapering away behind them, the greedy ambition of pioneer botany.  They cup chlorophyll hands, wringing from them a single drop of oxidized glee, the last evidence that they once claimed dominion over the thing that later claimed dominion over the definition of dominion.

Other inhabitants remain category exceptions.  The translucent hover-spiders are part civic employee, part streetlight.  Each carries in its abdomen the genetic script of its predecessor and when one completes nine hundred orbits it folds like origami insolation, splits its thorax, and daughter spiders tumble out glowing dragonfly jade.  The spiders use human memories as flight charts; they embed a sequence of dreams from the Leafwed into the wall of helium vacuole.  In flight the map fizzles, spattering the sky with fugitive murals of childhoods none of the dreamers remember having.  Sapphire earless crickets drift through the corridors like attention itself; they chisel micro-harmonic cysts on available petioles, where the plant grows bespoke acoustical cavities that later reroute ventricular music into whatever chamber will shortly require a lullaby.

What an outsider might call government is a peristaltic vote convening every twenty-eight plant-days.  All dwellers—human sensoria, spider vectors, cricket choirs, and even drowsing organelles in root meristems—throb at the same tachycardia. They do not intend to leave.  They have read in the planet’s living chronicle that all departure vectors eventually curve back asymptotically to root.  You see star-maps etched into their retinal leaf-plates: the local cluster idealized as magnified guard cell silhouettes.  They decode these as migrations already undertaken, circles they will soon fold once more.  If pressed they describe space travel not as distance but as depth of petiole.  They offer no name for themselves older than the planet’s heartbeat; their pronoun set includes verbs embryonic, suggonant, chiasmatic—grammar invented to replace the perpendicularity implied traditionally by “I.”

Imagine if breathing were a political treaty and you begin to approach the personality inhabiting the Verdant Kiln.  It thinks at the pace of stem thickening, giggles when a trichome scratchy-bushes your calf, forgets you by unwrapping its own leaf around your footprint five steps behind.  It knows you cannot stay long, remembers your struggle to remember, and solves the struggle by regrowing the space for you again, again, again.  When finally your ship light winks above sixteen mirrored disk-suns, the organism inside each departing passenger finishes knitting translucent robes of chlorophyll to be worn under conventional uniforms—news that will not be noticed until midway through reentry burn, when the astronauts stare at their arms and see themselves edged in lush reflective green, obeying another sunrise from an unreachable world they carry now like photosynthetic smuggled contraband.
