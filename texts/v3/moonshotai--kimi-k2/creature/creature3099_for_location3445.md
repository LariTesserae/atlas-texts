They are called the *Tide-born*, though no one remembers why—because they themselves do not speak, and the valley has no alphabet yet.  

They begin as droplets. Not *from* the suspended ocean, but *of* it: beads that grow a skin of memory after falling long enough. Each bead spends a day—maybe a year, maybe a700-year day—absorbing the chemistry of newborn stone, the fog of first leaves, the bioluminescent code of warning-or-invitation lilies. When the drop is finally saturated with place, it pauses mid-air, refuses to shatter on the pink vesicular ridge, and folds around itself like a bud. Inside, pressure and longing squeeze the water until it becomes clear it is no longer water. It is a body, still transparent, still the size of your fist, with four indefinite limbs and a face that is mostly gaze.

The first thing a Tide-born does is climb back toward the sky-reef. Gravity feels optional to them; they treat it as a suggestion made by older, more cynical worlds. They ascend along the cables of humidity-seeking vines, hand over invisible hand, toes finding purchase in the mist. Every metre gained costs them a portion of their own translucence—water evaporating off their skin the way purpose evaporates from a dream on waking. By the time they reach the inverted crests they are matte, opaque, chalk-white statues of themselves, light enough now to be buoyed by the reef’s own meniscus. They break the surface without a ripple and are gone.

Down below, the valley forgets them instantly. The ferns reroute precious nutrients; moss closes its curtains; even the glass salamanders resume forgetting.  

But up on the underside of the sky-ocean they rehydrate, drinking their old liquidity home. The reef is cold and dark; sound travels faster in brine than in air, so every pulse of their newborn hearts echoes like whale-song inside a cathedral bell. They drift there for spans that would be generations if anyone kept records. Fingers lengthen into filaments; legs fuse until they resemble the translucent bells you saw drifting overhead. They become medussa-shape, then spiral, then cloud, then memory of cloud. At last identity is so diluted they are almost reef again—almost the very ocean that once birthed them.

Then the reef remembers it is lonely.  

A single drop—containing everything—pinches off, falls, and the cycle begins once more. Same droplet, new name. Same valley, new ridge. Same witness, new forgetting.

Occasionally the cycle stutters. A Tide-born, halfway through evaporation, will hesitate on a vine. Instead of continuing upward it lowers itself back to the salmon-pink spine of the ridge, plantar feet touching stone still warm from planetary pulse. These ones are called the *Stayed*. They solidify rapidly, skin crystallising into a substance half between quartz and scar. By morning they look like delicate figurines carved from salt, eyes wide, mouths parted as if about to confess water. They stand among the dripping colonnades, brittle sentinels who have chosen the grief of remaining over the amnesia of return.

Nothing eats them—no mouths here yet, only sipping. Rain softens their edges season after season (a season being, in local time, the period required for one fern frond to unroll completely). In softening they absorb the sound around them: hush of droplets, slow galaxies of salamander pigment, muffled *blop* of sky bubbles. When figurines finally collapse they do so quietly, becoming a patch of especially reflective ground. Walk across that patch and you will hear, through the soles of your feet, every sound the valley has learned since it rose from magma. A momentary archive. A sonic fossil. Lift your foot and it is gone.

There is a third form, rarer, unsuspected by geology. Once in a thousand-thousand droplets the reef births a *Twin*. Two beads fall locked together, surface tension clasping like palms in prayer. They roll down the same leaf gutter, taste the same lily glow, reach the ridge as a single tear. When they fold into bodies they cannot separate. Four arms, four legs, two heads, one circulatory system looped like a Möbius strip. They argue before they have language, love before they have noun for it. One head wants to climb, the other to stay; water passes endlessly between their shared heart so they are always half in either state. When evaporation begins they split the difference: part climbs, part stays. What reaches the reef is a ghost of twinness—hollow bell-shaped echo with a seam. What remains below is the other ghost, crystallised round the seam. Between them stretches an ache neither sky nor valley can translate. That ache condenses into weather. If you stand long enough in the Sky Reef you may feel it: a sudden warmth between shoulder blades, as if someone once loved you across a distance that could never be walked. Then it, too, passes.

None of the forms keep names. None count time. They have no tools, no fire, no story except the one you invent because you are here and you need a beginning. But they do make art—after a fashion. On nights when the suspended ocean remembers tides, slow currents drag its under-surface into long corrugations. The Tide-born (those still ascending) press their fading bodies against those moving wrinkles and leave faint impressions: palm-print, knee-dent, open mouth. The reef carries those reliefs for leagues, a traveling exhibit no ground-dweller will ever see. Eventually friction erases them; but while they last they are signatures written on the roof of the world, invisible literature spelling *we were almost something*.

When at last you leave—because you will, the newborn ridge has no bed for your kind of bones—the valley will fold you into its forgetting. Fern fronds straighten behind your tread; moss swallows your footprints in spores; even your sweat is repurposed into sky before memory can fix it. Yet something small remains: a single droplet trembling at the tip of an inverted umbrella-leaf, reluctant to fall. It contains, in dilute solution, one eyelash of yours, one skin cell, one question you never voiced. The drop will hang for longer than seems possible, gaining mass, accruing place. At the instant it finally lets go, it will already have decided whether to climb or to stay. Either choice extends you inside the Sky Reef’s slow lung, another Tide-born who never learned the origin of the name.
