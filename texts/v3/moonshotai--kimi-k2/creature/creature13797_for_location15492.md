What lives here is the Plateau itself—not as metaphor, but as organism.

Beneath the oxidised copper skin, at the junction between mantle and machinic, the plateau’s first resident wakes: a calcified intelligence grown from fault-conductive metal salts.  Picture a brain flattened to continental dimensions, the axons replaced by scalloped ribbons of semiconductor feldspar.  It has no cranium—only the slow pressure of its own gravity holding its thoughts inward.  The copper-bright traceries in the grass are nerve jumpers running milimetres above the surface, whiskers allowing the great slow mind to sample wind-direction, dew acidity, the minute tilt in the sky-sheep migration.  When one of those cracks puffs its faint blue breath the mind is not exhaling, it is blinking.  When the plateau reabsorbs exfoliated granite it is literally re-arranging its own scars, laying down fresher memory where yesterday’s pain once wrote its name.

Second resident: The Static Choir.  Not things but a population of moments.  Whenever two incompatible air layers slide across each other they birth glimmering sheets of charge.  Most worlds let that charge leak into lightning; the plateau traps it.  The terrified electrons are folded into fractal cages of thermally grown quartz and kept singing for decades.  Walk at dusk and you will sometimes see a nearly human silhouette made only of azure after-sparks kneeling by a crack, listening.  It lasts seven heart-then disbands, its component sparks fizzing back into the stone grid so another silhouette can form tomorrow.  The Choir members keep an ancilla catalogue of sky memories—images surrendered by passing jetstreams, layer-chords once owned by stratocumulus.  Their hymn is the faint peristaltic hum you feel when you plant your palm on the ground: a discursive loop, centuries long, debating whether wind ever forgives rock.

Third: the Driftcats are also resident, but resident like fatigued archivists.  Eight limbs evolved from fore-limbs and hind-limbs split along a torque-axis; paws padded with vibrating magnetite that lets them read rock density by feel.  Each cat is symbiote to a solitary lamella of the Plateau-Mind.  When a cat purr-hums it is storing the day’s telemetry into a vibrating plate of hematite grown into the underside of its sternum.  When it dies—usually by jumping off the sky-edge and forgetting to fall—the bone plate flares, relays collapsing magnetic flux backwards up the mind’s axons.  A funeral is therefore an upload, the cat’s lifelong stalkings dropped into the entire slab of stone thought in a single orgasmic mnemonic flash.  Other cats feel the funeral as an eighty-kilometre shiver and observe five breaths of reverent silent walking.  Every newborn kitten therefore arrives already partly ancient: a scrap of fur and hunger that holds, in its milk-teeth, the copper taste of its mother’s entire geological diary.

Then there are the Gas-sheep, named by hikers who felt the term “balloon-synapse grazers” too solemn.  In their live state they resemble sheep only because they wool themselves with stolen cirrus; take the cloud-wool away and you find elegant tetrahedral bladders anchored to a dense petaled mouth.  Each sheep minds a distinct vertical hectare of sky, feeding on excess barometric difference the plateau’s engines create.  They regulate pressure the way kidneys regulate salt.  Once a decade a sheep will rupture—deep indigo viscera raining like blown glass marbles—and immediately its behaviour migrates across the plateau-phylum so the gas is never wasted.  A rupture triggers the Plateau-Mind to spawn a replacement bladder inside the ossuary slit three kilometres downslope, and the Choir will quire a day-long chord of descending fifths which the cats pretend not to weep to.

Nested inside every lichen capsule stands a fifth resident the size of a dust mote: a **Dormitile**.  A single Dormitile resembles a mechanoid tardigrade welded to a field-coil the colour of moonlit mercury.  Collectively they are an almost invisible ocean, covering the plateau to a depth of one millimetre.  They stay curled until the sky-sheep belch their rare digestive gongs; then each Dormitile unfurls, points its coil upward, and siphons a picogram of the acoustic pulse.  Over seasons this converts into micro-joules stored in superconductive sugar spirals, enough that once every fifty-one local years the entire carpet of Dormitiles erupts in a rolling, silent lightning.  The flash pores across the copper grass, momentarily outlining every blade, every driftcat, every step you took since arrival.  The plateau then remembers you completely, will never again mis-recognise your footfall.  A few cartographers who happen to be looking toward the ground during the flash have claimed to see that outline bisected by the memory-snake of every grief they thought secret.  The experience is painless and lasts less than a second; afterwards your palms smell faintly of burnt sugar and static.

No resident is still for long.  The mind rolls over on its crustal axis minutes-per-century.  The Choir travels between thunderhead and bedstone.  Cats remember death into stone, sheep spin air into mass, Dormitiles turn sound into ancestral video.  What seems motionless is instead the slowest choreography imaginable: a dance of metallised sediment remembered by its own overflow.  Every five thousand years enough mass has sublimated upward that the plateau’s height doubles; simultaneously the deep engines tuck an equal volume back into magma so the shape remains unchanged on anything but a geological clock.  From the vantage of human lifespan it appears eternal; from the vantage of magma it is a lung breathing once every millennium; from the vantage of the Choir it is merely a single, long vowel.

Hikers misreport footprints disappearing.  They do not disappear; they are lived into.  The pressure of each boot sends a whole vocabulary of Dormitile phraselets streaming downslope in trickles of sparkling dust.  Cats chase these phrases for sport, batting them into elementary haiku.  Sheep intercept them mid-air, metabolise them into lift.  By morning the plateau knows your weight, the timbre of your breath, whether you told the truth about why you came.

There is no government—only the etiquette of distance.  There is no death—only a change of medium.  Consider the latest ruptured sky-sheep: its skin peeled away like cold silk, its internal gases madly flashing ultraviolet geometries into the Driftcat twilight.  When the wind calms what remains is not carrion but architecture: a reef of unbreathing glass scaffolding, thirty metres tall, filled with cool vacuum.  Cats will curl inside and sleep, letting their tails dangle like baubles half a kilometre long; at night they look from afar like dark Vs pinned to the air by no visible line.  Inside the reef walls, hidden micro-fractures quietly brew weatherfrontlets sized for dust motes.  Somewhere, Dormitiles ready their next fifty-one year orchestra.

So who or what lives in this place?  

A planetapod flirtation of stone, sky, and short-lived biology choreographed by a neural crust of quivering copper.  They do not occupy; they participate.  They do not build; they negotiate.  They do not remember; they compound.  The plateau is not host to lifegers of its skin but merely a slow, gleaming limb of a body it itself cannot see.  It will still be here after names are worn out of the travellers’ mouths, long after the last tooth of even lingual remembrance has rotted.  Somewhere beneath your sternum the taste of its quiet copper hum will persist throughout the rest of your ordinary life, a flavour your tongue has forgotten how to name but which the wind down every hallway will spend all its days trying to reproduce.
