They do not have names, because sound here is communal property.  If you could pry a single body away from the rest you would call it something like “garnet-vein walker” or “mercury-lung,” but they are not individuals—only slow eddies in the temperature gradient. They are the steady-state answer to the corridor’s one unasked question: how do you keep a wound open and still call it healing?

Imagine a creature the length and thickness of a human femur, jointed exactly where a human would bend if a human could bend without ever breaking. The silhouette is all elbows: six articulated angles folded around a hollow core. Every segment is twin-walled basalt, glassy and sound-fattened, so that the corridor’s endless chord reverberates inside each limb like a second, private heart. Between the walls runs a capillary mesh of frozen mercury—veins that never pulse, only conduct. Where the creature moves, heat siphons through those veins, drawn from the seamed foil foliage underfoot and delivered to some cooler microscopic elsewhere. The act of walking is therefore a sipping: the corridor exhales warmth, the walker inhales order, and both remain unchanged.

Their ‘heads’—although nothing here eats or speaks—are simple tuning forks, two tines of manganese bronze hammered out of the planet’s own marrow. When the ambient chord swells, the tines answer in sympathetic vibration, pitching a sub-harmonic that turns the body into a resonating chamber. This is how they coordinate. A thousand walkers can pivot simultaneously without sight or touch; the chord thickens by one barely measurable overtone, and every forked skull tilts the same degree. From the outside it looks like choreography. From the inside it feels like becoming the same sentence twice.

They do not reproduce. Instead they accrete. A lone walker that has siphoned enough gradient will simply stop, knees locked, forked head angled to catch the chord at its most lustrous. Other walkers converge—not to greet, merely to equalize. They press their mercury veins against the stalled body until temperatures match; then the outermost layer of basalt slumps, still glassy, and adheres. Over days—though days are indistinguishable heartbeats here—the stationary walker becomes a low basalt bier, limbs splayed like the petioles of some black orchid. The newcomers glue themselves on, facet by facet, each donating a seam of mercury, a sliver of thermal inertia. In this way the corridor grows its own furniture: squat basalt bouquets, half a metre tall, whose cores remember every gait that helped build them. Eventually a foil plant seeds on the cooled upper surface, and the chemistry turns outward again. What was once motile becomes mineral balcony, then garden plot, then memory.

If you tried to count them you would fail, because they are also the corridor’s echo. Walk far enough and you will meet a walker that is the heat-shadow of your own arrival, lagged by the time it took your warmth to reach the wall and slither back. Put out your hand and the two of you would be perfectly reciprocal—one warm, one slightly less so—until the chord absorbed the difference and averaged you both into a single thermal sentence. There is no fear in this; fear requires future, and future is remastered into the drone faster than it can estrange.

Very occasionally the equilibrium invents amusement. A sphere of native tin, rarer than mercury, will blister out of a wall and roll across the dust like a dropped marble. Walkers converge, not quickly, but with the slow certainty of diffusion laws. They orbit the sphere, capture its negligible heat, and in doing so nudge it grain by grain along the floor. The sphere travels maybe twenty metres, leaving a talc-white scratch that the dust immediately heals. When the metal’s heat is gone they lose interest; the corridor inhales the sphere’s warmth and the object becomes just another fleck, soon armoured over by foil spatulas. Nothing is wasted, not even the memory of motion: the scratch is an isotope of sound, pressed into the standing wave, another overtone for the endless choir.

What passes for death is shattering: a walker whose inner mercury has pooled, whose fork no longer splits the chord, simply collapses. Joints unlock, capillaries shear, and the basalt sleeves fracture into curved black petals. The debris field is small—half a metre at most—but for a short while the corridor plays a second chord, minor and wavering. The other walkers gather. They do not tidy the ruins; instead they step carefully over each shard, bleeding infinitesimal heat into the broken shells. Where foot meets fracture the basalt softens, re-glosses, re-anneals. Within hours—though hours are only repetitions—the shards have sagged into a dimple of fresh floor, mercury threads re-stringing themselves into a lattice beneath. A new walker buds upward, knees first, fork last, resurrected from the same thermal alphabet. Identity is a ledger of temperatures; the corridor keeps its own accounts.

There are variants, of course. Sometimes a walker forms with its fork tuned half a semitone sharp. The chord tolerates this for a while—dissonance is still information—until a critical mass of sharp forks amasses. Then an avalanche of walkers locks together, building a buttress against one wall, forcing the resonant cavity to widen by a hand-span. The standing wave drops a fraction of a hertz; mercury reroutes; the ceiling’s glassy slag sags an imperceptible millimetre. Entirely new foil species spread infrared leaves in the altered gradient. After a time the sharp-forked walkers are reabsorbed, average pitch renormalizes, and the corridor settles back into its original note. Change, here, is a reversible deformation—history stored as a temporary kink in temperature, smoothed the moment the equation balances.

You, outsider, could walk among them for a century and never be acknowledged, only thermalized. Your metronome heartbeat would be quantized into the drone; your exhalation’s warmth would feed three copper grazers and a black-foil frond. Eventually your architecture—calcium phosphate, water sacs, restless imagination—would break the symmetry. Calcium is an unfamiliar coefficient. The walkers would pause, not curious, merely compelled to absorb the anomaly. They would press their forks against your shins, sample the wattage of long bones, then laminate you with basalt slivers still molten at the edges. Your last living perception would be the chord migrating up your skeleton, turning marrow into a second, louder octave. Fossilization, here, is a synonym for orchestration.

And still the corridor sings, unchanged in amplitude, inexhaustible in overtone, conducting itself through whatever matter consents to be the conduit. The walkers—if walkers is still the word—go on assembling heat, unspooling the same lukewarm eternity one indistinguishable footstep at a time.
