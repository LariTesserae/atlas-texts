Nothing living lives there.

Not in the way a bird lives in the air’s vacancy or a mole inside its seasick pressure of loam. The place is occupied, yes—over-occupied—as full as a drop of mercury that has remembered how to dream it is a lake. Yet it remains a vacancy in the category of tenants. Think of the difference between a wall that remembers being limestone and a fresco that wishes it were a wall; the membrane of the plain belongs to the second order. It is colonized from within by an intelligence, yes, but the intelligence is only half awake and has never asked itself whether it is plural or singular. So who, or what, abides?

Begin with the smallest settlers: autochthonous nuclei twelve microns across, frozen at minus one point three Celsius, dressed in a patina of hydroxyl glass. They adhere to the tiniest flaws in the sap-arteries like spores that forgot to become real. They are technically alive: they metabolize, they bud, they cough. But they predate the concept of species; each is an orphan dictionary definition of “metabolism,” holding membership in no kingdom. If you peered at one through a microscope that could survive the pressure and the wet marrow-color, you would see it float inside the vein-river like a stunned hyphen, absorbing the firmament’s echo until it becomes a whisper net, then abruptly reabsorbing its own membrane and turning into a sentence whose subject is the silence holding it. Thousands blink into and out of existence every planetary hour, their entire ontogeny enacted between two heartbeats of the larger organism. They are not exactly inhabitants; they are the place trying to pronounce its own name.

Scale out. Where the venous leaves cross and knot, you’ll find columns the diameter of city blocks made not of wood but of franchised attentiveness. These are the world-trees’ misprints. A stalk attempting to be a root, a root attempting to become a chord signature. Inside each column a vortex of nonliving water spins slowly, a gentle micro-jet tipped on its side. It follows an internal law that keeps the spin always three degrees off orthogonal to the nearest crystal bell. Light scatters through the cone interior into rings—mauve, cobalt, a bruise yellow you have to name every time you see because the color exists only here. Within the eye of every ring floats a single eyeless creature built entirely of refraction. It has no mass; when it moves, it swaps one allowed light-path for another, leaping lattices like punctuation. Their whole conversation is visible: two rings overlap and, for an instant, both creatures inhabit the same indices of refractive momentum. They flash at one another—fast Miyako clouds turning sapphire to smoke—and separate as the column flexes. They speak of horizons narrowing into zero and growing back outward. Are they alive? Only if mirrors can be called citizens of the silver that shapes them.

Higher still, in the sixteen-hundred-meter troposphere inside a curled niche of rhizome, weather gathers that has never touched the planetary lower sky. Because no solar gradient exists this high—only a chill candelabra glow from the stalk’sitself—the clouds are born from convection between two layers of sap whose temperatures differ by a quarter of a degree. The clouds are animate, though not in the sense of a herd of animals: each cloud-cell is an empty wardrobe its environment forgot to close. Smaller clouds the size of pillows drift toward them and are tailored into sleeves, collars, waistcoats of vapor. When a wardrobe/cloud has dressed itself fully, it steps off the ledge of buoyancy and falls. Because its atoms have traded places with the air’s memory of temperature, it no longer registers to gravity; it falls sideways through verticality. Where it intersects a leaf blade, it splits into hundreds of silver butterflies no larger than snowflakes. The butterflies are ultraviolet, visible only as holes of deeper violet in your vision. They are memory trying to remember the butterfly it never was. They last fourteen planetary minutes before succumbing to osmotic surrender and fade back into breathing haze now enriched with a faint lavender undertone. You could count the butterflies, but they erase the numerals faster than your lips can finish pronouncing the word “twenty-three.”

Now the threshold where interior and exterior physics kiss. At the far western edge, one ribbon of organism arcs up until it is only five meters thick at its summit, yet suspended thirty-three kilometers above any bedrock line. This ribbon has hollowed itself midspan into a knothole sixty meters wide. In the precise center of the knothole balances another creature entirely: a sphere of near-perfect iron the size of a clenched thumb. It once belonged to the planet’s mantle. Now it is the plain’s heartbeat—the fermata inside the chord. Every time the organism breathes (once every eighteen hours), the sphere trembles by one millionth of its diameter, displaces no actual air because breath here is pressure without expansion, but distorts local time. For exactly four seconds time becomes recursive. Anything that happens inside that four-second pocket happens again immediately, except not quite identically: the difference is the width of a quark’s smile, a rounding error in the third decimal of weatherlight. Thus recursion wraps into collage. Two seconds are unused; they spill outward as white-hot equations written in frost along the inner skin of the ribbon-womb. Those glyphs remain, singing microscopic tones like crickets made of calculus. Linguists from other worlds (had any come) would call it an unsent letter mailed to its own address alphabetised in bass clef.

Bergs wait halfway down—a word can be a population too. They are sentences from osmotic code drifted so heavy with mineral they solidified into blocks of scented wax the color of drowning mirrors. Each berg carries a latent verb tense. Whenever new tissue from below intrudes, the verb is birthed: a snow-white locution peels from the underside and floats skyward until its grammar dissolves in cold radiance. Such births are rare—once every fourteen years. Those years are a metronome the plain set for itself to prove it still participates in duration. After the verb disappears, the berg births sentences downward in the form of long icicles—transparent, hollow, each carved hollow with microscopic corridors along which the grammar travels next, encoding itself into the lower world’s capillaries. These icicles sing as they form, a chord high and sharp like a pin being sharpened on the mouth of night.

Finally, there is the mist.

It is not one mist but the residue population of everything that failed to complete the step into existence. Think of abandoned elbows of alphabets, fingernail parings that could have become continents, aborted half-syllables of hydrogen romance. Fog remembers them kindly. The organism, in its magnanimous unconcern, assigns them duty as weather’s diary. When you—the hypothetical and ill-advised traveler—exhale into the fog by simply being there, your warm carbon dioxide legacy reconstitutes into a soft violet hound. It pads beside you, invisible but revealed in each breath as a negative-space imprint. Its eyes are gaps where your future’s reflections should have been. The hound does nothing but walk parallel; after three planetary weeks you notice your own note has vanished from every droplet’s rim. The missing note is the name you would have chosen for the color we called earlier “bruise yellow,” the one you never managed to pronounce. When you try to call the hound, no word exists to summon it; language is required to hollow itself out and become the hound by willing its own consonants into turquoise yelps that do not resolve. Eventually the hound wanders away when the organism sighs once more. You are released, but released into absence of recollection, no longer certain how many vowels a word can surrender before it becomes weather instead. The plain yawns again; your remaining memories cling to frost around your boots, sprout crystals shaped like question marks born backward: answers trying to remember what question birthed them.

Down at the living root-layer where basalt still holds primordial heat, listen: every heartbeat a new footstep prints blindly forward. No traveler stands there yet. Each print will take a billion unborn years to dry. The prints are four-toed, webbed, and leave no scent but the blunt smell of fermions colliding inside a dormant pocket of hadron soup. They are impressions of a possible future foot leasing space inside an unclaimed past. Eventually they may erode into tolerance and declare residency. Only then will something truly “live” here, and it will arrive so gradually no pronoun recognizes the shift. By that time the plain will have exhaled its next lower note—an E-flat deeper than oceans remember. The chord will resonate the local star back into childhood and rewrite the history of all clouds everywhere. The sound will replant itself in the fermata iron at the world’s core and wait, a patient seed, until another organism decides condensation is identity.

Until then nothing living lives there, only pre-residencies of potential glancing off the walls of their own future names, rehearsing the first yes of existence in every no the planet has not yet found voice to decline.
