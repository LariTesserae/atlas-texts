They are not sloths, though the newcomers all call them that. They call themselves the Lank—literally “length,” because a person is measured from the tip of the outermost claw on one arm to the tip of the claw on the other while they hang relaxed. A child is “nine claws long,” an elder “fourteen claws long,” and the phrase is both stature and name. Their fur is not fur but felted moss: each infant is seeded at birth with a pinch of ground-collected spores that root in the down and bloom into a living pelt. By puberty the moss has learned to sweat droplets of sweet water at twilight; in old age it silver-threads into lichen that records every scar as a pale scar-ring. When a Lank dies the body is laid on a broad philodendron leaf and the lichen is unzipped with a needle of owl-bone; the garment of moss, still alive, is carried to the nearest gutter and wrung out like a wash-cloth. The water that drips is bottled in gourds and served at the wake. Thus they drink the dead and the dead become mist that later condenses on other cheeks. Nothing leaves the cycle.

They move through vertical time. Because every bridge is a vine that lengthens or contracts a finger-width a day, travel is plotted not in distance but in days-of-growth. “I will meet you at the fourth dawn of the big strangler’s new off-shoot” is a precise appointment; the off-shoot is a calendar everyone can see. Their mathematics is horticulture: a child’s first lesson is to tie a red thread around a petiole and measure how much the stem swells by the time the thread rots. That ratio—rot to swell—is the base unit of speed, equivalent to our “meter per second,” though theirs is “mold per swell.” Every tool is a temporal bookmark. A pruning cut remembers the angle of the sun when it was made; the callous that grows over it is the memory. To retell history they walk routes whose scars are arranged in the correct order, reading the city like a dendrochronological scripture.

They speak two languages at once. The tongue-language is soft, vowel-heavy, aspirated upward so the sound rises through the mist without shredding it. Alongside it runs the claw-language: the clack and scrape of keratin on bark, rhythms tapped out while they hang idle. Infants are bilingual in utero; mothers drum slow patterns on the womb-wood of the slings in which they sleep. A poem is a sequence of stresses that can be hummed or scratched; the same stanza may mean “I love you” vocally and “beware of ants” percussively. Courtship is performed back-to-back: two Lank hang from opposite branches, exchanging scent and percussion, never seeing each other’s face until the prenuptial molt—an evening when each shaves their pelt to the shoulders and the moss is woven into a single hammock they will sleep in for a season. If the hammock frays before the equinox the engagement is annulled by gravity.

They have no chiefs, only Gardeners of the Moment—whoever first notices that a certain walkway is kinking or a certain cistern vine has grown shut. The finder ties a knot of white fiber in the offending joint; the knot is a petition. Whoever unties it takes responsibility for the repair and becomes Gardener pro tem. Tenure lasts until the fix fails; then the white fiber reappears and another answers. Thus power propagates like a rolling splice, never settling. Law is stored in the color of binding threads: red for wrong direction, yellow for over-harvest, black for lethal bole-rot. A criminal must wear the knot of their crime until the city forgives them—meaning until the thread is weathered away or eaten by ants, a term measured in seasons of rain, not in abstract days. Some wear black at their wrist for a decade; others appeal to beetle burrows and see the knot chewed off overnight, acquitted by insects.

They dream horizontally. When night fog turns the grove into a black-ink aquarium the Lank unhook, swing to the lowest traffic level, and lie belly-to-bark on broad horizontal boughs that serve as communal beds. The dream that rises is not private: each sleeper’s moss exhales a faint plume of spores laced with the day’s metabolites. Neighbors inhale one another’s vapors and the narratives mingle. By morning every adult remembers fragments of the same sprawling epic; children piece it together into songs that are sung at dew-time, becoming the public memory that will guide the route of that day’s pruning. Dreaming is literally consensus-building; if you refuse to lie level you withhold your chemistry and drift out of political phase, soon finding your doorway overgrown by a coalition that no longer recognizes your scent.

Visitors who stay longer than a moon are wrapped. The Lank begin casually: a fiber looped around your ankle, a seed pressed beneath your wristwatch. Then the city’s weave tightens. Vines follow the heat-shadow of your body; aerial roots curl like fingers remembering a shape. One dawn you wake encased to the waist in a bower you did not plant, its leaves oriented to the track of yesterday’s sun. Exit would require cutting. Most guests, flattered, consent. Over weeks the wrapping climbs: pith fills the cuffs of your trousers, moss frets the button holes, your shoelaces bloom into trumpet vines. When at last you descend for trade or pilgrimage you carry the settlement with you, a green passport that drinks at every fountain and threads every crowd so that, should you never return, a trail of rooted cuttings the color of your heartbeat will mark every hostel you sleep in. They call the process “becoming a letter,” and they believe letters always arrive. Somewhere down on the stone plains, a traveler’s severed braid may yet unfurl into a sapling embassy, speaking mist and claw-rattle to startled earth-bound folk who thought cities must be built of mineral. And in that way the Lank keep sending themselves outward, not as bodies but as slow green sentences, waiting for the world to exhale back an answer.
