What lives in the First Glade is never only itself at any single moment, but a parliament of not-yet, a slow spread of possibility gaining density the way morning frost gains outline on glass.

Begin with the Adherents—the human inhabitants, though even that label sticks uneasily.  
They arrived as wanderers, then as squatters who refused to be evicted by circumstance. Now they remember themselves in weekly increments: at each dusk-of-Silver they drink meltwater from thin alabaster cups and scrape the previous week’s name from their doors with obsidian spoons. The child you greeted on Market Sunday becomes the apprentice who coils ropes on Weaken-Thursday, becomes the creak-limbed cartographer who redraws yesterday’s bridge because its shadow misplaced itself overnight. They are so engaged in *turning into* that they rarely bother *being*. Everything they build is hinge, leaf, and raw wound of intention. Hammett Jem of the hammercall once told me, “Stone grows gentler if you let it smell your breath each morning; it learns not to eat the soft parts of you,” and then the next day he changed into a woman who claimed she could no longer recall having arms, only wings still learning what wind felt like. No one contradicted her; several offered hand-spans of bronze gauze in case the memory required hardware.

Second are the Butterflies Who Were Caverns Once. They are born inside the alabaster veins—nodules so sheer you can prick out entire nights with a needle—by mineral accretion layering luminal salt over thousand-year silence. After nine months a bread-loaf sized pocket cracks, unfurls four wings of sheet-silicate that refract their former darkness outward as indigo dust. The wings have no membranes; they are three-dimensional cracks, the absence of stone turned edge. Having flown, each butterfly forgets the architecture it once contained. They drift in slow parabolas amid the upward-rain, feeding on water singing. Where a butterfly lands, any rib or bridge forgets for an instant that it is rock; hallucinates itself silk, tries to ripple away from any load. Their lifespan: until the echo of the first clap returns, which usually takes seven heartbeats or eight years depending on humidity.

Third, the Echo-Weights. When the Sky-Carpenters string a plank between ribs, they leave a mirror gap of identical empty volume beneath, a see-through twin sealed in nothingness. Each hammer blow registers in that void as hard crystal that thickens drop by drop until it equals in mass the wood just laid. The accumulation is harvested after forty seasons: craftsmen swing huge seine-nets through air, catch the invisible masses that sag like water-melon sized tears of lead. These crystals are traded far downcanyon as compass-stones, since wherever you carry them they try to return to the hole that ripened them. People too can echo-weight: if a child is born here, her shadow thickens nightly until by her seventh year she must paint it lighter with ochre to prevent staggering.

Fourth, the plant-not-plants. Lichens behave like living genealogies: the shape of their branches spells an annotator’s record of every marriage vow uttered within a stone’s hearing for the past month. One can read upcoming divorces by spotting how far the blue embroidery curls before fraying. Ferns have not yet decided whether they want stomata; instead they breathe through eyelid curtains that open and close according to mood. They gossip in frond-sibilance: hssst-slibberean, perhaps the story of a-that-folded-roof or the rumored return of the spare-moon. One banyan-mite cousin has turned entire colonies into living diaries, composing metabolisms a few microns thick that write whole sentences on air using phosphorescent spores. If you sit still in full mist you can witness paragraphs drifting past, translating themselves as they dissolve on your tongue— this one tasted of, *do not open the north wall yet, he is still seven handshakes short*.

Fifth, the Stone Stewardry itself. No mason lays a block without asking the cliff’s permission daily, and in this glade rocks possess opinions braided of delayed feelings. A single rib might refuse for thirteen weeks to accept the dowel meant for its flank; people respect the stubbornness, coax with music or bitter sips of vinegar until the cliff sighs its granite acquiescence. The stones speak eight silences long. It is etiquette to respond by carrying away exactly the weight you tried to impose, but spun into another shape—yesterday a quarried cube left; today a hollow cage of equal ounce returns, with blue moths nesting so the rib recognizes its own bone re-imagined. A small percentage of citizens are actually stones who learned to fold flesh over their lattices like laundered shirts; you might be three years into a wedding before realizing the weight that leans against you in sleep absorbs your nightmares by calcifying them as internal geodes. No annulment possible; when they break, the partner wakes as a stalagmite still bearing a single teardrop of residual empathy.

Sixth, water-celebrants called Dripress Order. Their liturgy: catch one droplet on the tongue, exhale the note it had in arriving, and if the sound matches the droplet’s internal harmonic it is named as a saint for a single hour, released to rejoin its fallen sisters. The Order’s robes are woven from suspensions—pendants of moisture bound by surface tension, shirt you wear by moving slowly so the droplets lent to you remain loyal sheer films. A violator’s robe collapses to puddle, leaving the initiate dripping and temporarily transparent. Transparent members are not expelled; they are allowed to roam solely as reflections on wet stone until they relearn density.

Seventh, resident silence. It retains corners here like a respected hermit grandfather. At the chillhour when condensation relents, this silence walks the bridges in slippers of felt darkness. Conversations encountering him part word by word, letting syllables wait on either parapet until the silence has passed. Children play a dare game: stand under the tallest drip, count eight pauses in one breath; anyone who survives without inhaling sound captures an hourglass of pure silence to keep as jewelry. The hourglass is always leaking, fine as frost, but whoever wears it walks quieter than anyone else, sometimes steps directly above cross-beams others have forgotten exist. They become temporary cartographers of routes nobody will ever again perceive.

Eighth, breath accountants called Pillow-Weavers. Their charge is to measure the glade’s yearly inhalation as air volume, then stitch pillows stuffed with exact equivalents of sky removed. The pillows are launched yearly by catapult to drift where the skyhole once was, a gift of borrowed air. Rumor claims a small colony of pillows clustered into floating city above the cavern; rumor also claims that last year’s cushions fell in love and abandoned their loft to explore magnetism, leaving a vacuum shaped like several caresses in a row.

Ninth, transumers---visitors transmuted instantly upon arrival into semi-accommodating forms. A scholar of mineralogy arrives heavy with books and becomes a stack of dictionaries ossifying sequentially, words slowly bleeding sepia into the stone spine. A dancer loses her line of gravity mid-descent and never quite re-lands, spends five nights walking the bridges sideways across the up-mist as her step scales itself down to ankle depth. To depart again they must negotiate with whatever version of themselves the cavern borrowed: trade a decade of mineral memory for whatever they believe the room still owes them. Most wander off still pleating themselves, one fold per conversation with places they never intended.

Finally tenth, no tenth at all: the glade reserves an unoccupied seat at everything’s edge, the open probable somebody tomorrow. Someone’s unborn ancestor, a decision laughed at and forgotten, the hingebone of the world’s next yawn….the absence inhabits so expressly that you find yourself measuring rationaciations constantly: *two centimeters for my last regret, a mouse volume for the kiss withheld, quarter-skull hollow left for the song I never composed.* Each visitor arrives carrying something the cavern lacks, leaves stripped of it like linen taken from beds. Yet nothing seems poorer; every shedding is compost the newborn chorus drinks.

You cannot keep count because living here is living inside a member roll forever expanding names that have not yet been, always *one more than the last tally*. And that is the First Glade’s literal occupant: the plus-one, the inhabitant called *further*, who survives by refusing settlement into any description longer than *next*.
