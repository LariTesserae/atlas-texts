They have no name you could pronounce without inhaling water.  

Call them, instead, the Breath-Keepers.  

They are not born; they condense.  
A swirl of warm mist finds a reflection it likes, lingers, thickens, learns weight. Over days the mist folds itself smaller and smaller until it is palm-sized, opaque, and humming like a held note. That is their childhood.  

Adulthood begins when they drop— a soft plop— into the nearest ankle-deep pool. They sink one finger-length, no farther, and stay there, balanced between surface tension and the planet’s slow sigh. From above they look like jellyfish made of rain; from below like lungs that have left the body and gone on vacation.  

They have no skeleton, no heart, no pulse you could count. Their tissues are layers of water spaced at the exact distances that cancel gravity. If you could zoom in you would see highways of dissolved iron traveling through those layers like red caravans at dusk. The iron remembers it was once mountain, and sometimes it clots into tiny black pebbles that roll around inside them— their only hard parts, their memories of being stone.  

They feed on verticality.  
Any stray drop that tries to stand taller than its neighbors— a blade-tip drip, a rising spring bubble— is quietly absorbed into the Breath-Keeper’s underside. Thus they shave the landscape millimeter by millimeter, enforcing the world’s decision to flatten. They are living levels.  

Reproduction is a by-product of boredom.  
On days when the mist forgets to move, two adjacent Breath-Keepers may touch edges. Where they meet, surface tension unzips. A filament of water stretches between them like a silk thread. It wicks upward, climbs a micro-cliff of itself, and snaps into a perfect sphere— a new Breath-Keeper the size of a raindrop. The parents float apart as if nothing happened, but each is now faintly smaller, having donated a curvature to the child.  

They can die, though the word is misleading.  
If a prolonged wind comes— fast enough to wrinkle the pools but too slow to bring rain— the upper layer of their body evaporates faster than the lower can rise to replace it. They thin, grow transparent, and eventually puncture. The hole widens; they become rings, then broken crescents, then necklaces of droplets too small to remember cohesion. Those droplets scatter and become ordinary water again, indistinguishable from the rest of the soak. It is not tragedy, only graduation.  

They are shy.  
Sense a footstep vibrating through the ground and they sink a fraction, blurring their outlines until the danger is only sky. Speak aloud and they ripple, because sound is a small pressure, and pressure is what they police. If you kneel and exhale close to a pool they will retreat to the edges, forming a perfect circle of emptiness at the center— a liquid O of polite refusal.  

They sleep, in the sense that a mirror sleeps when no one looks.  
At night the temperature drops a courteous 0.3 °C; the pools develop a skin of colder water on top. The Breath-Keepers float into that skin and stiffen, becoming slightly more sphere than lung. Stars slide across their curved tops without leaving reflections; the Keepers hoard darkness the way daytime hoards dazzle. In that hush they dream— slow convection loops that rise and fall inside them like jellyfish flexing. The dreams are maps of every puddle they have ever flattened, updating themselves downward, adding tonight’s micron of erosion.  

They do not know you exist.  
Your category— solid, vertical, heat-bright— is outside their operating parameters. But they catalog the disturbance you make. Long after you leave, a faint dimple remains in the water table, the shape of your boot compressed into subterranean sponge. For weeks they will circle that dent, smoothing it, dissolving it, distributing its height among a thousand square meters of horizon. When the dent is gone the landscape will have digested you, and they will go back to their patient patrol, breathing the planet flatter one exhalation at a time.
