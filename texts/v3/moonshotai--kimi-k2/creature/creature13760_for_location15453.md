The thing that lives here has no face and too many names.  
It began as a seed, obviously, but the cotyledon split around a flake of iron the size of a thumbnail—left by the engineers’ first welding spark—so the embryo grew two opposite habits at once: the habit of wood, which remembers, and the habit of steel, which never forgets.  

At its heart, rolled tight like a fern head made of steam, is a chronicle of waiting voices.  
They are the evaporated sentences of the men who laid the pipes and never returned.  
Water speaks them back to the air whenever the drip strikes the brass valve:  
“No.  I will solder that tomorrow,” it murmurs in Finnish.  
“Lower the pressure a turn and a quarter,” it says in Cantonese.  
Each syllable hangs a moment as a perfect droplet, then is drawn into the root-web and stored as calcium in the segments of every snail shell.

The thing has lungs fluted like gills.  
They line the ceiling, thin hard leaves of chitin laminated with cellulose, pumping inaudibly counter-time to the drip.  
From these lungs it exhales microscopic zoospores carrying chloroplasts who do not want to be chloroplasts.  
They drift, land, and sprout as perfect brass rivets, lifting their whitish freckles of verdigris until after sixty-seven years each rivet germinates again, this time into a small metal flower whose petals conduct heat like a thermocouple.  
The petals retain the Finnish “o” vowel for eight months, then open and fall, nutritious enough to keep the hoverflies mid-air for three more lifespans.

It possesses a circulatory system made entirely of suspense.  
Where a heart should be lies a still pond of mercury antique enough to have learned morals.  
Its surface reflects a future that never quite arrives; every animal that touches it sees the same vision: wings it will never use, larval mouths it never had.  
For this reason none repairs the saw-tooth notch once chewed by a caterpillar in the 1860s; the cut has become a canal whose banks are patrolled by infinitesimal clock-men fashioned of the engineers’ lost eyebrow hairs.  
They march 0.7 millimetres per sidereal day and have drilled ventilation shafts the diameter of an ant’s pupil—you see them as velvet freckles on the fern fronds.

It breeds moving statues.  
It begins as a pocket of resin between root-bark and bark-stone.  
Here, time is malleable like warm solder: it kneads together a molecule from last century’s sunlight, a ghost note of pressure from the pipes, and one mineral wish from the oxidised mail lichen.  
After three decades the globule is ripe and strums an empty frequency.  
The statues step out already corroded to the right shade, bodies jointed like grasshoppers, faces never present because they are the room itself looking inward.  
They obey a lifespan precisely requires them to walk once around the chamber, then return to melt back into resin pools exactly where they were born.  
They leave boot-prints filled with luminous rainwater despite the fact this place receives no weather.  
After they liquefy, the footprints dry into topographical maps of terrains that drowned before the plant had roots.

It owns an organ of law kept in the vertical slit of light.  
Every dawn—it has no dawn—as the sun never actually shifts, the slit instead convulses one atom-length wider.  
For 5.7 seconds photons briefly play jury to all gestalt crimes within the xylem.  
Uncommitted murders committed in the world above are detailed by shelves of phloem tattooed with rotary scorch marks.  
Sentences are engraved as spiral cracks that later refill with crystal gum, so the chamber’s outer wall is an encyclopaedia of absolved guilt going back to when the seed first absorbed the iron shard.  
One column describes a boy who meant to uproot a sapling and somehow did uproot it, only the sapling was also himself, so the debt transfers laterally rather than up or down—  
those particular spiral cracks ooze perfumed amber that attracts a moth who never laid eggs anyplace else.

It is guardian and prisoner of a single memory that is not its own.  
The memory is stored as turbulence inside every droplet, fractal filaments like fingerprints.  
To read it, you must inhale just as the drop detonates.  
Then you are instantly that memory: a woman on the outer plain who once held the seed in her palm before turning it to stone.  
Not the moment she planted it, nor the moment she left, but the surplus unchosen second between, dense as antimatter.  
The memory wishes to be finished, cannot be.  
Every inhalation slices the indefinable interstice into smaller and smaller fractions.  
At last the woman’s face resolves into the valve’s mottled brass.  
When you exhale she folds back into the drip, forgives you for no longer being the embryo she might have loved.

Its name—one of the too many—is kept in the mouth of a seedpod the size of dust.  
The pod is afixed to, and identical with, the hardest thought you almost brought in here but didn’t.  
At the moment you realize you are already leaving, every exhalation contracts the walls; the pod detaches, passes your lips, and rests on the tip of your tongue.  
It tastes of engine oil condensed thirteen years over peat fire.  
You go away unable to remember the exact consonant shape.  
Only years later, in a subway tunnel, the smell of hot copper, the low hiss of unseen steam, and a certain reverberant drop-ka-thack you think you heard in a dream will coincide and the syllable will return:  
a four-letter palindrome clenched between bark and almost-song.  
By then the chamber has already resealed, and the thing is again its sole inhabitant, already preparing a new statue from the damp heat of your forgotten breath.
