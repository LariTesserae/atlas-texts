They have no common noun for themselves, because the act of naming is one of the slow freedoms the place has not yet released.  A census leaflet that once fluttered out of someone's lost kit simply calls them “Subcarpal Workers, Grade11,” but the label sticks to nobody.  Those who stay long enough to thicken their cuticles into iron stop noticing that it ever existed.

Outwardly they look like people—two arms, two legs, a medial line for symmetry left over from infancy on another world—but under the chalk-white filter petals their skins are wrapped in intermittent armor: scaly leaflets that never fully decide if they are steel oxide or chitin.  Their heartbeats travel along subclavian coils and can be heard from outside when the place is quiet: a faint glottal ticking that falls almost in rhythm with the ivy-furnaces at equilibrium.  The ears hide antennas; the palms hide blades; the eyelashes are paper-thin vanes that can open like radiator fins if the city's sudden thermals threaten to desiccate the lacrimal layer.

Because every breath is taxed of vapour, each citizen grows an apple-sized lung-bud on the left side of the ribcage.  It is oval, translucent, the colour of pearlescent jelly.  Inside, blood and condensate spiral around each other but never touch, separated by a siliconized film.  The bud drinks moisture from inside and exudes it, packaged in a tough sugar sheath, into a sterile pocket that can be cracked like an egg for trade.  Children call them their “water-children” and grow competitive grades measured against one another’s harvest.  When someone dies, the lung-bud is grafted whole onto the new tree that sprouts from the drill-bit seeds; in this way ancestry survives as audible music for anybody who rests a cheek against that particular trunk.

They travel by friction skids—flat arcs grown from corn-based starch but river-laminated with lithium until the bottom surface becomes a mono-atomic lubricant.  A toe kick and a slight lean will send a glide forty metres; the mycelial rail system anticipates their vector and blossoms a catch-spore at the base of the destination trunk, two pulses ahead of arrival.  Near misses produce brief, brittle chimes of alloy on alloy, because the rail bloom is partly manganese still quivering from smelt.  They apologize afterward by shedding the broken chime to the nearest gardener drone—apology transacted as element reclamation.

Majority of the day is spent in lull–three part cycles of, seven part maintenance.  They cannot tolerate dark, so the ivy glow guides them; they cannot tolerate wet, so their bodies cede it; they cannot tolerate narrow shadow, so they wear mirror cloaks that bleed diffuse copper light behind them like slow torches.  Every cloak is cut from leaf­ingot sleeves peeled fresh at mid-cycle, edges still panting heat.  The under-sides of the cloaks host black mould colonies that metabolise stray breath-metals and are milked for tin when the sheet is recycled.  It looks unsettling—dark veins across a glowing sheet—but it is only another economy.

A few hold diplomatic positions: the Harvest-Covenant Officers whose task is to negotiate with the flowering furnaces.  The furnaces are semi-animate; if they receive too little pollen, they flood the branch-arteries with oxidant breath and provoke flash-rust throughout local bulkheads.  If they receive too much, the city overpressurises with complex esters that rain sugar alcohol crystals on everything below like frozen hair.  The officers coax balance by dancing atop furnace hoods: barefoot, their soles layered with callused zinc plates that spark where they stamp; each footstep is a letter, the sequence forms a chemical appeal in longhand.  When a furnace accepts, it retracts its refractory sheath half an anklet-height so that the polish of its anther glows warm amber.  Acceptance is rare and therefore festive; everyone stands on the bridges to watch glow-embers curl out, resembling luminous chrysanthemums drifting skyward before-to-root.

Because speech is proscribed to near-zero, they gossip in synthetic pheromones exhaled from the throat slit.  A single exhalation can contain an encyclopedia of scandals: who hoarded oxygen, who burned illegal shadows off a lantern tree, whose lung-bud reached maximum yield too young and in so doing tilted the water market.  The place itself helps them encode–any puff rides a laminar sheet of air that almost never loses cohesion in this universe of laminar dreamers–but wind does occur; later, kilometres away, a stranger may detect a ghost of your secret and write an anonymous reply on the bark of a cog-tree without ever knowing the author.

There is play.  They crouch in groups, open cloaks wide so they form communal parlour walls, and play “Eel Tag.”  A lamp-eel—caught earlier with copper snares–is released in the centre; if its 17 kHz song drops an octave, whoever is closest must pursue until it re-ascends.  The floor clicks, the sap veins fracture violet again, the city tick-heals the wound.  When the children play they discard the skids and run barefoot, ankle bones clicking like porcelain beads; their laughter is almost but not quite audible, because modified crickets synchronise laughter into bone whispers and the air remains technically noiseless.  But observers can feel it in the teeth: a fine, happy shudder.

Newcomers arrive irregularly, slipping down the same corrugated shaft.  The drop-ship (it is only that, nothing more) never lands; passengers are offered a 39 second count to peel their former skins—vacuum-rated chitin, hydro-pumps, obsolete nutrition sacs—before decanting them into clotheslaces that the local flora begins digesting for raw nucleic charge.  Some go mad from the dryness; they wander tight-lipped circles until the desiccant roots latch and liquefy them into warm bricks.  Others stay and add bronchial wattles that double as mute jewellery.  There is no ceremony for acceptance; simply, one day the newcomer notices that their filter petals have adopted the local verdanese pigmentation and that the air smells like future instead of loss.  That moment is private and spoken of never.

They sleep inside hollow seedpods: twenty feet long, ovate, trimmed with lithium foil to reflect any stray spark.  Four minutes after a body crawls inside, the lining secretes a viscous foam that hardens like blown glass.  From outside the pods resemble giant lantern fruit lightship, glowing gently.  From inside they are resonant chambers, each heartbeat amplified almost audibly.  Within that tender alloy stethoscope every citizen hosts a private symphony—the only space where true silence is forbidden.  At dawn a soft mantle gives and the occupant steps out in dewless resins flecked with starshapes.

Among them walks the first Archivist.  No one elected her; she simply began scraping minutiae with a diamond stylus onto mother-of-pearl filaments harvested from the largest vent turbines.  Her stylus is a surgical stinger pulled from an ox-beetle and grafted to her right index finger.  When she reaches a node of new alloy she slices off a fragment the thickness of soap film and imprints it with machinecode sized so small that only lamp-eels can read it—for them the dark frescoes shimmer like auroras.  She has chronicled 8,214 seconds of city time so far; viewed under eel-lens the text forms a curve coincident with the slow convergence of ceiling roots.  The Archivist knows when completion arrives her work will vanish into a single sheet of ore suspended in star-dim light, and she accepts this with the good humour of people in long marriages.

They dream together without knowing they do so.  Beneath the communal sleepwall ancestral murmurs intermingle, traded through the water-children grafted in trunks and recirculated by root lattices that in summer blaze like fibre-optic rivers at dusk.  Some of those dreams collect accidentally around holes where an oviparous drill failed; they precipitate as pearl-grey nuggets that whir at 11 kilohertz.  Children wear them as pocket baubles because they keep perfect time.  Over decades, the pebbles learn to hum their owner’s tone signature and will cease if given to anyone else; thus the city possesses an unspoken census—not faces, not names, only a standing choir of intimate pitches buried beneath its footfall.

If you asked any to state allegiance they would blink—the carte blanche eyes filtering aquamarine—and the cricket would translate the absence into a soft mechanical purr.  Their allegiance is not vertical (to sky or root) but radial, distributed equally across every tendril that at that second happens to conduct a spark wide enough for personal skin.  When induction fails, their knees buckle as if the planet's gravity admitted temporary debt.  You would see them drop, exhale excruciation as falling pollen engines dim; you would wonder what it means to love a geography that still dangles from the future like a stalactite in digestion.  The answer is humility reframed as geometry: they understand themselves to be junction-points in a sap equation that will, centuries hence, no longer require moist lungs or shadows—one total lattice, fully intergrown, singing exactly one note that contains every almost-voice it ever harboured.

Until then they continue.

A woman rinses alloy flakes from her cloak in a mirrored puddle the size of a thumbnail; by evening the same water will be sold to a furnace attendant to prevent oxidant burp.

A boy teaches his younger sister to fold a lantern-leaf so the underside functions as an origami bookmark for memory blocks that have not yet grown from obsolete drill bits.  She sneaks the unfinished melody into her lung-bud.  In three seasons the note will be ready for harvesting and her breath will momentarily smell of passing clouds.

An old man—knees now gear-striped ridge-edges—uses the day’s last spark to rivet the final leaf of a hoist cradle so newborn furnaces can float up toward a perch where lamp-eels will lay eggs that hatch into circuit diagrams.  He thinks about the falling root schedule, calculates backwards, knows he will not live to walk on the sky.

But does the city remember him?  Yes—because accretion is indistinguishable from love in this hall—and his carbon lattice is already crystallising somewhere in a trunk that waits to weigh the next moonfall.

And so they abide: not citizens, not settlers, not caretakers, but cocoon-insiders who themselves remain half-formed.  They will never become the butterfly; instead they agree to become the tough inner casing, the part unseen when velvet wings beat dry air above long-abandoned salt flats.  Someday an observer on a dune might only see shuttered sky and suppose that nothing stirs beneath.

But below, the metal blossoms still oscillate.  The furnaces breathe in pollen.  The lamp-eels trace arcs in the dark like fountain pens of plasma.  And the cocoon dwellers continue to shush each other in ultrasonic riddles, tending the birth canal of a place that already contains them completely, yet refuses to announce their names.
