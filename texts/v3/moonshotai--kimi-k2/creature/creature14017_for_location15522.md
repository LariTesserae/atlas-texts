Who lives in the Crucible?  
The people themselves are only half-chosen by accident.  Every first snow—thin as paper in this latitude—a council of three salt-fevered geometers walks the basin lip at dawn with thimble-cups of searing tea held to their tongues so the vapour quickens tears.  Whoever’s left tear remains unshed the longest is added to the population; the tear is caught on a flake mirror and pressed into circulation as a crystal bead.  That bead is sewn into each newborn’s left boot sole as a summons: one day it will be dissolved again to finish the step you could not take.  In this way no citizen is older than twelve calendars and none younger than the salt itself.  They are not mortal in the common sense; they fade when the bead finally shrinks to nothing, at which moment their name is whispered over the brine-pans like pollen and the hollow they its rightful resonance is taken up by the next recruit caught in the same process.

Thus the population hovers always at one hundred and forty-four: enough to form a cube of twelve-a-side, a platonic murmuration that keeps the inwards breath of the place from mis-rhythm.  Their faces are whitish; not the pallor of illness but the salt-subcutaneous brilliance of someone lit from within by ice-roses.  Eyes water continually—from mineral sting—and so they never close their eyelids fully; they walk half dream-netted, lashes shining.  Voices are low because the air drinks treble frequencies for breakfast and returns only cold overtones.

They greet each other by pressing the space between thumb and forefinger together; the salt moisture on their skin forms a temporary weld that pulls apart with a soft click, a secret handshake of crystals un-making.

Children.  
The youngest carry little astronomy: two-score numbered, they are moon-scaled creatures, knees translucent as shellac and hair that stains to frost in winter.  Children are a protected technology.  They are taught to walk first on rippled salt crust before language, for balance requires resistance to the possibility of collapse.  Each stands nightly beside the evaporator holding an L-shaped wick of ironwood fibre over the surface; when the brine film descends past a certain opacity the wick catches faint blue fire, and the child records the tremor on a slate tablet of pressed salt.  This is how the town keeps time, an oscillography of adolescence.  Once measured, the slate is licked clean by an adult tongue: iodine history tasted rather than remembered.  On the equinox every shadow child of six summers exchanges one vowel of their name with another—so “Alai” becomes “Ilae”—they do not ask why, simply that losing a sound adds a cavity in which future loss can falter.

Craft-parents.  
In many letters they would be called artisans, but “parent” is more accurate because everything they craft is intended to outlive them by a margin only one quartz kink longer than their own spent bead allows.  Their cloaks are hooded axes of undyed wool.  The left hood pocket is sewn shut on the outer seam; inside rests a single desert onion seed, inedible, a memento of a former trade route now calcified shut.  They invent nightly, sometimes for no reason except to miss sleep.  They have built salt flutes that play note fronds when wind shears off the ramp.  They weave brine rust like mycelia through bronze needles to articulate shadow puppets you can backlight with candle and project on a translucent wall, only to watch them slowly decay as the salt re-aerosols.  When they die they make no grave; instead their skulls are cracked open, the inner side of the calvaria scraped clean, filled with door-swept dust, and capped before the bone has time to darken.  These white domes are hung like lanterns along narrow alleys, glowing pastel under the moon; thirty-four such skull-globes already lit.

Pan-Keepers.  
A single guild, no more than nine individuals—one for each octant of the pool plus one integer to negate eccentricity.  They stand barefoot always, soles ridged by salt but never bleeding.  They possess permission to speak aloud any direct hush of numbers, counting for the town because ordinary citizens may only articulate through tone.  They keep in a pouch sewn of dried frog bladder the dried tongues of desert foxes, using them as swabs to taste test temperature gradients on the pan.  From taste alone they predict tomorrow’s crystallinity coefficient, publish it by arranging loose bricks in patterns.  By law none may look them in the eyes after dusk; instead one must extend one’s gaze along the surface of the water until it meets their ankles where daily transparence writes itself.

Perimeter Knell-plantsmen.  
Every fourth dweller is seconded for one circadian rotation to the lip of the basin as knell-seed trampler, already described.  They become for twenty-eight days a conjoined twin with the steppe above.  On their last day at dusk they knot a grass nocturne round their calves so spikelets clatter when they walk, producing the town’s only rhythm above hush.  Upon return they shear their own scheme-audit ropes; the straws are interleaved between salt bricks so the architecture sings whenever a wind hooks along a corridor.  They are lean in the arms because turning in gravity’s incline strengthens legs instead, and their necks crane permanently looking down at their fellow town’s reflection, never up, always inwards.

Echo-speakers of the Brine Warac.  
A loose grouping of eleven storytellers.  One each sits an hour a night in the largest vacant dome, itself roofless by design, throat open to the sky.  They do not speak stories.  They inhale faint barometric changes and exhale syllables distorted by the cuboid reactance of salt walls, creating delayed reverbs that come back differently ten heartbeats later.  These sound-forms carry no sentence, only emotion: a snatch of grandmother’s disapproval wrapped in pigeon-wing static.  Children use these audible ghosts as lullabies; adults, when drunk on lime-acid brine, confess sins into the dome and listen for whether their guilt returns higher or lower, equating pitch to atonement.

Visitors without names.  
From time to time a lone figure arrives over the ramp whose bead is already missing: an exile from another flatter salt elsewhere.  Such person is fed but must sleep outdoors in the trench-one-future until their identity seeps away and they begin to leak magnesium traces with their sweat.  When white powder films their palms the Elder Council votes if they are to stay.  If declined, they are offered one pound of fresh salt already powdered, enough to strew a vagrant trail till dying of nostalgia becomes optional.  If accepted, a citizen surrenders their place, placing their own bead upon the newcomer’s tongue; the dissolved bloodstream rearranges facial membranes until the town gains its new old face.

The Non-human Commons

Skulk: a plum-sized coenobitic crab built of dried brine filaments and scrap cellulose exuded from Ground-sparrow nests.  A dozen scuttle within the corridors seeking latent moisture in footprints, stripping away the first micron so each step is forever virgin smooth.  They aggregate at dusk into a single russet rind nine centimetres thick upon the central evaporator pan, where heat keeps them warm through early night; townsfolk use this bar as a communal bench.  The skulk has one large tooth imbued with halite, and during boredom it carves filigree on itself; by morning mirages appear in the town as twisted hallways only dogs could pass—but there are no dogs.

Tent-web spiders and their mirror larvae.  You saw already the ceilings, but the larvae hide inside brick veins.  They extrude threads that crystallise while still liquid, creating in-brick cathetuses along which light glides into corridors.  After pupation the imprints freeze in the salt: fractal frost-fossils maintained forever—literal recording of former eight-limbs.  Occasionally a wall keels over under its own memory weight.

Basin frogs, Lythryphantes arenorum, only vocalise when barometric pressure drops lower than −4.36 kPa below mean sea level.  Their sound is a click followed by irrelative silence, Rhythmed Morse that announces drought approaching three days hence.  They live a metre beneath the surface in brine-air pockets covered by bio-scaf salt rooflets cupped like soap bubbles.  During dawns you can see them rising to the eggshell lacquer above them—mouths upturned capturing condensate.

Grasses within the Foramen wells.  They are officially pet-crops but grow too quickly to be fully harvested, turning midday the root-mass into a sponge that sucks grit and detritus.  When the seeded ring completes its eighth revolution a cylinder of living green filters the town’s air of stray crystalline dust so that residents still retain lungs instead of evaporated diaphragms.  A side-effect: anyone who dies in full bloom will have their bone phosphates reconstitute into leaf veins—literal phyllomorph skeletons that scintillate under starlight the following equinox because magnesium is photophilic.  These bones are then ground to renew fields, looping biochemistry.

End-trope: Memory
Finally there is a single entity inhabiting the entire town simultaneously—the ringing hush itself.  A psycho-acoustic envelope produced by the incomplete cube of citizens refracting subtle tides of silence.  When one walks the corridors too fast the hush breaks, generating a hiss audible only to experimental audiology.  Experienced wayfarers develop a gait that matches the infrasonic period, stepping every 2.37 metres so that footfalls cancel the noise, restoring stilled hush.  Because of this the entire settlement behaves as one ear turned inward, listening for the day when embayment of silence at its core grows loud enough to draw the surrounding steppe downwards like a whale sucking plankton, completing the process and folding the ramp gypsum into the void to birth a true brine city grown homeopathically from a single condensation tear.  Until then the citizens, living coils of unfinished mineral autobiography, wait, breathe, step, click.
