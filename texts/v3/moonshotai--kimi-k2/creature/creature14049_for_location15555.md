The Builders  
They have no other name, because a name repeats and here nothing, once spoken, alters. They are the hands that creased the first green blades upward and left the dent of that persuasion glowing in the stalks like candle-wick where wax has not yet cooled. If you part the reeds carefully you can still find their palm-prints—broad, tender, webbed faintly where reed-oil mixed with living skin. Their knuckles show calluses shaped like tiny pyramids, as if the pattern printed itself backward into them at the moment of making.

They appear only in transit between two stillnesses. A shimmer the colour of young limes hangs in mid-air, splitting open just far enough for four long fingers to reach through and twist a stalk into a keystone. Then the shimmer closes and no limb steps all the way across. Testament: their footprints float a fingertip above the surface of the causeway, not yet pressed into history. Each print carries the reed-braid pattern of a sole that has never needed mending.

All twelve of them share the same age—indeterminable, yet perfect. Eyelids lug no deposit of yesterday, nor pupils wear the dark brushwork of hours. Their irises shimmer the same dew-yellow that floods the sky, because they were the first to reflect it and have simply refused any other light. In movement (though there is only a gesture of movement) the builders’ robes trail smoke-green threads that hang in the air like impossible cuttings from a slow river, then braid themselves into the wall they faced and become next morning’s newest tier. Thus each is half mother-of-cloth, half living architecture.

They never speak entire words—only vowels dripped soft as bubble flow. The sound o rises between four leaned stalks, sheps into the gap it produces, and there the new niche stays. The syllable a hangs a moment like an unstruck bell-curve before it migrates into a buttress and braces it forever. Their entire tongue is a dictionary of single tones pressed into shape; grammar is spacing, not sequence.

They eat only the thought of flavour. Mid-stalk, between two purple haze-blooms, a builder presses flat teeth to pith, withdraws the necessary sweetness, then rewinds fibre-by-fibre so the reed stands uncropped. Hunger is treated as a short vertical conversation that returns the plant unblemished.

Time forgets them continually and is politely forgotten in return. Yet memory is an architecture here: touching a wall you brush a builder’s shoulder three eons ago. Should you lean close you can faintly hear them counting—always cushioning the reed at the exact midpoint between safe and beautiful. The count is neither forward nor backward, merely round, like birds describing the horizon line.

Though all identical, one builder differs. She alone completed a final step: into the westmost door-shaped absence she passed, not to leave but to inhabit the exit itself. Now she curves inside the threshold like poured mercury, both sides of the threshold at once. Sometimes, at the edge of the sealed morning you hear her inhale—the smallest back-suction like the tide refilling a shell’s throat. The air stays disturbed only long enough for an entire sky to fit inside her ribs, then the stillness presses back as polite as upholstery.

Their genealogy is recursive: each is spun at sunrise of the day they will finish persuading the last pyramid into its resting height. A builder appears already knowing where every reed will want to bend, and by dusk their knowledge transfigures into stillness. There was never a moment when the city began or opened any hollow that someone else had to climb into. At the unchanging first instant the builders and the city recognised one another as the same sentence in different alphabets and simply overlapped.

They do not dream—dreaming would mean a future tense—but they remember forward, the way a vase may remember a hand that will one day cradle it. Fragments of unfallen rain float just below the ceiling of their attention until the sky decides whether it should rain. Perhaps tomorrow; the same seven o'clock.
