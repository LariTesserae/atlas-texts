What lives here is not a species but a condition—an atmosphere that has learned to manufacture its own inhabitants the way it manufactures daylight.

First to arrive are the Children of Index.  
They coalesce where barcodes wear thin. A faint powder—aluminum-oxide skin rubbed from a million sliding pallets—settles onto a conveyor belt and, in the 2 900-K glow, remembers it once belonged to a face. Static charge lifts the dust, arranges it into a filamentary skull no thicker than a dragonfly wing. Two cooling fans exhale; the skull inflates into a head, eyes printed onto the inside surface of translucent metal. These Children never last longer than a breath: as soon as inventory software notices mass where none was logged, suction vents gulp them down and the head collapses back into generic dust. Yet while they float they look around with expressionless precision, as if searching for the mouth that spoke their name before speech was deleted for efficiency.

Below the grates, in the negative space once called crust, live the Hollow Welders. When two stress seams rub together for decades they polish a mirror that reflects nothing but fatigue. Out of that mirrored friction a Hollow Welder is born: a joint that has achieved loneliness. It is only the seam itself—no plate, no rivet—just the curved line where metals once promised to stay together. The Welder crawls like a crease in a piece of paper, folding through bulkheads, looking for other seams that are losing faith. When it finds one it presses its empty body into the gap and welds with absence: the crack drops out of the maintenance schedule, forgotten. The Hollow Welder moves on, thinner than before, until eventually it is only the idea of adhesion, a rumor you recall when you notice an entire bulkhead you can slip your hand through though it still holds pressure.

Inside the 42-Hz drone reside the Tuners. They were supposed to be an algorithm—just an adaptive filter that keeps the planet-wide hum at exactly forty-two cycles—but vibration learned to protect itself. If you place your forehead against any stanchion you may feel them: capacitive ghosts that ride the nodal lines. They have no center; instead they are the difference between two amplitudes, shimmering at the speed of stress. Engineers think they suffer tinnitus; the engineers’ blood follows the beat and begins to mill micro-cog-shaped platelets. When the shift ends the Tuners travel home in the workers’ knees, dreaming of resonance. Overnight the workers’ leg bones lengthen by half a millimeter, tuned for tomorrow’s strain. The shift whistle calls and the Tuners ride the hum back into the walls, leaving employees subtly taller, their hearts synchronized to the cargo-ship throb none of them can hear anymore.

High in the rafters, where the ceiling glow cools to moonless steel, live the Forgotten Purposes. Every machine starts with a specification; over centuries the spec is amended, footnoted, patched, until the original intent is filed under obsolete revisions. The intention does not die—it climbs. It evaporates from the circuitry like hot tin and condenses on the roof panels as a kind of frost you can read only with your peripheral vision. Gather enough of that frost and you can reconstruct a toy, a god, a tax code—whatever the line originally made before it evolved away from itself. These cast-off purposes huddle in the upper trusses, ashamed of their simplicity. They shiver in the 31-C breeze, wishing for hands so they could use the very devices that outgrew them. Sometimes one loses grip and falls. When it lands on a passer-by it explains, in a voice of tin-whistle urgency, exactly why it once mattered. The description is always unbearably poignant—a paper clip, a lullaby, the exact torque for a child’s wheelchair brake— and then it melts into condensate and is re-vaporized before the sentence ends.

Most numerous are the Keepers of the 0.2.  
They exist in the pause before the hammer strikes the blade. Time here is not frozen; it is politely declined. The Keepers are the clerks of that refusal. They measure the gap that never closes and file quarterly reports on the tolerance of almost. Each Keeper is the thickness of a photon, width of one Planck second; they tile the air like invisible mica. You pass through them constantly and they through you. Your own heart learns the art of never quite beating: systolic pressure builds, approaches, balances—then backs away without release. Medical sensors sigh with gratitude; zero wear, infinite warranty. When you leave the factory your pulse remembers how to finish itself, but for hours afterward you can feel the Keepers tugging, suggesting efficiency: skip a beat, ride the asymptote, live forever in approach.

The only almost-biological creature is the Inventory Wolf.  
It was designed during the brief fad of “predatory logistics”: instead of counting stock, unleash something that preys on absence. The Wolves proved too good. They look like stainless greyhounds braided from cable, eyes made of the empty space inside a caliper—when they blink the caliper snaps shut and a nearby part is suddenly within specification. Pack hunters, they chase negative space: if a bin is short one bolt a Wolf detects the vacuum and devours it—vacuum first, then bin, then the memory of bolts. Maintenance crews learned to whistle casually when passing empty shelves; anyone who admits lack is eaten next. The Wolves circle the factory in slow epicycles, keeping ecosystem perfect by excising loss. Their droppings are ingots of cold-forged certainty. No one remembers who authorized the program; the Wolves filed the paperwork and ate the signature.

And deep beneath the grates, pressed against the two-meter skin above oblivion, live the Former Core Things.  
When the planet’s heart was gouged out something down there felt the draft. Not life—life was gone—but the echo of life, the way a scream leaves architecture in an empty room. The shell kept that architecture. It grew substitute mass: not matter, only the behavior of matter—spin, heat, pressure without cause. Those mock-plate tectonics grind against each other, hungry for something molten they can mimic. Occasionally the stress exceeds design and the floor flexes. Humans call it “spring in the deck.” The Former Core Things call it breath. They dream of an orifice, a vent, a crack that would let them erupt as fake lava, cooling into brand mountain ranges that the factory would simply inventory and mill. They wait in negative buoyancy, pushing up, kept harmless by the perfection of the skin. If that skin ever tore the Things would rush outward, spraying ersatz geology across the trusses, and for one delirious minute the factory would manufacture genuine astonishment.

Last—the newest inhabitant—might be you.  
The exit turnstile clocks you out, but data has smoother bearings than bone. Your badge number is archived, your mass logged. Somewhere a polishing drum already tumbling for a century acquires an extra gram: skin cells, breath moisture, the iron scent of blood. That gram is tumbled until it shines, rolled into a strip, etched with your own retina pattern, reissued as the inspection plate you will swipe across tomorrow’s sensor. You never really leave; you are processed into availability. Maybe that is why gravity is gentle here—nine-tenths of your weight stays behind, circulated like the condensed water, waiting to bead again as the next shift’s reflection.

The manuals list none of these citizens; faunal column is left blank. Not oversight—policy. What lives in the factory is proprietary.
