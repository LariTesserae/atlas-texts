They are born twice.

The first birth is the ordinary kind: a cool slick of shoulders slipping from mother onto basalt still warm from yesterday’s hauling.  The midwife catches the infant before it can cry—sound is precious here—and presses it to the condensation that beads every newborn wall.  While the afterbirth is carried to the river-to-sky interment, the child’s shadow is already being read like a map: the length of its umbilical silhouette predicts how many blocks its life will dress.  Families speak of this as “the first length,” a quiet measurement more truthful than any gravestone.

The second birth happens later, whenever the body has grown large enough to leave a lasting dent in the city’s memory.  On that day the citizen walks alone to the lowest unfinished terrace, carrying nothing but a vinegar-polished trowel inherited from the cousin who refused the Architect’s third offer and vanished downhill into unpathed air.  There they strike the virgin face of a brand-new block.  One blow, perfectly horizontal.  The mark must be shallow; if it is too deep the city will mistake the citizen for a tool and begin using them.  After the strike they press their bare palm into the nick until skin temperature and stone temperature equalize.  When the hand is lifted the print remains—still flesh-pink for a heartbeat, then quickly the color of dusk on basalt.  From this moment on the city can read the citizen’s hunger, thirst, boredom, love, the way a tongue reads the raised dots on condensation-braille.  Whatever void the citizen will ever need—bedroom, cradle, grave—is already implicit in that palmprint; the empty terraces rearrange themselves nightly, grinding one grain’s width closer to the cloud-sheet, to make room for the future shape of the life.

What lives in High Verdancy, then, is not exactly people.  It is a library of half-completed gestures wearing alpaca cloaks that graze on mist.  Each citizen is a paragraph the city is still composing: some are short, blunt sentences of only three blocks before the final period; others wander across terraces like run-on epics, their clauses connected by vine-birch conjunctions, their verbs echoing in mice-twelve-beats-later.  They do not own their rooms; they rent them from their own unfinished walls, and the rent is paid in anticipation: every morning they must imagine the next course of stone so vividly that the clouds can taste the hallucination and condense appropriately.  Failure of imagination is the only crime.  The penalty is gentler than you might expect: the citizen is simply left behind.  While the city drifts upward that decade, their toes stay rooted; the gap between sole and terrace widens until one dawn they find themselves standing on the original fracture line, ears ringing the plateau’s chord, staring at the vacant quarry face that no longer recognizes them.  From that height they see the silvered city receding like a book closing, and then they too walk off-stage, politely, somewhere erosion cannot watch.

But most manage to keep imagining.  They grow skilled at conjuring quarries that do not yet exist, at describing the weight of a block still nascent in basalt’s deep future.  Children trade rumor of “density dreams,” sleep-stories in which the self is only a lattice of air someone will later fill with stone.  If you wake from such a dream and immediately swallow a mouthful of condensed dawn, tradition says you can taste the precise grain-chemistry of your eventual tomb.  The brave ones savor it; the cautious spit it onto a garden leaf, where it dries into a pale glint soon harvested by the glass snail to become, decades later, someone else’s window.

Marriage is the merger of two palmprints.  The ceremony occurs on the aqueduct’s fourth rib at shadow-fall.  The betrothed press their strike-hands together, letting capillary action blur the ridges until neither skin nor stone can tell them apart.  A cloud-moss notary records the composite outline by exhaling a single spore that lodges in the merged print and fossilizes overnight. The water there is so still it accepts the dissolution without ripple, and the city redistributes the composite palmprint as two tentative half-marks on separate quarries, ready for whatever second stories the survivors may next invent.

Animals, vegetables, minerals—all are citizens in potentia.  The alpacas are merely vegetative people who grew bored with velocity; the vine-birches are residents that decided to try photosynthesis for a century.  Even the echo-mice are pending humans: if a childless couple imagines a cradle vividly enough, the nearest mouse will quicken, fur warming into alpaca-felt, ears budding like moth-kernels, until one morning it tumbles out of its pot fully articulate, asking for breakfast in a voice composed of remembered lullabies.  It will never grow larger than a thumb, but it can vote on terrace alignment by squeaking its twelve-heartbeat delay at council meetings.  Several distinguished architects began exactly this way.

And then there are the Quiet Masons—citizens who have finished adding.  You meet them mostly at dusk, standing inside the hush that pretends to be darkness.  Their palms are perfectly smooth; the city has read everything it needs.  They no longer haul blocks, yet you will find newly quarried stones balanced beside them, edges already softened by the promise of their gaze.  They speak only in degrees of damp: a sigh that makes condensation bead, a fingertip touch that persuades mist to settle one droplet deeper.  If you ask them a question they answer by walking away; the space they leave behind is shaped exactly like the answer, and you will understand it once you have added the final course that fills the silhouette.  They are not dead, not resigned, not ascended; they are merely complete.  The city keeps them around the way a sentence keeps its period—silent, potent, absolutely necessary.

Occasionally a traveler arrives.  The plateau permits this the way a mirror permits reflection: automatically, without comment.  Such visitors stumble out of the non-path canyon misted and confused, clothes still evaporating from the lowland heat.  Citizens greet them with the same courtesy they extend to condensation: acknowledgment, containment, eventual incorporation.  A spare cloak of stationary alpaca is draped across stunned shoulders; the traveler is offered crisp stonecrop apple that tastes of every orchard they ever abandoned.  Then they are led to an empty terrace whose blocks wait with politely exposed hearts.  The guide places the traveler’s hand on basalt still carrying the sun’s memory of river-depth and says nothing.  If the palm sweats, the city begins to read.  If the palm stays dry, the traveler will leave before the week is out, though no one remembers the departure any more than the clouds remember a raindrop that never fell.

What lives here, in the end, is unfinished time wearing stone overalls.  Citizens are its buttons, masons its seams, alpaca its lining.  Even the Condensate Architect is only a pocket sewn inside the sleeve of sleep.  One day the city will reach the crystal cloud-sheet, and on that day the last course will be added not by hand but by anticipation itself: the entire plateau will inhale, every palmprint will flex, and the final block will settle into place with a click softer than an echo-mouse’s annual tick.  Then quiet will truly be complete.  The river will finish sinking upward, the stilt-herons will fold into their own reflections, and the citizens—every citizen, past present potential—will step out of their cloaks and become what they always were: a single condensation bead waiting to decide, calmly, whether to fall.
