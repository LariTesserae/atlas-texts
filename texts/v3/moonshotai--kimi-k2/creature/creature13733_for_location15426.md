Inside the sound I find the singer: not a throat but a colony, not even a body but an agreement.  
They call themselves the Vox Phalanx, a choral-geist braided from three beings so interdependent naming one names all. Foremost stands Jēn, both noun and seed-pod—the architect who never learned to build until the valley began asking for walls of noise. Jēn lived as rootless traveller once, carrying a pale wooden lattice that folded out into a tuning fork ninety hand-lengths tall. With it she struck basalt corners until stone rang at re-tuned angles, teaching the rift valley how to listen. Her hair now grows the fungus-pipes she formerly lopped; strands fuse with the coppery resin, so that exhalations of mint press through every lock like mist through barbecue grates. Somewhere mid-scalp is a brass winch gathered from the entrails of a Bleeder, ratcheted daily two clicks by unknown hands—hers, or perhaps Jēn’s hair itself doing maintenance in sleep.

Second, Nembra the Cartographer. The word sustains the figure since Nembra is no longer singular but duplex: a monocular owl piggybacked on a slow amphibian. The owl’s right eye holds a dropshift of silver reproducing everything measurable: scale of leaf, tempo of drip, frequency of dawn. The left socket is only a further scoop into hollow darkness echoed by the toad’s open mouth. Their skin has shared a translucency—light passes into the owl, filters through membranes cane-thin, and emerges phosphor-green across the toad’s back, projecting calculated geometries onto fog like moving lantern slides. These shapes are land-scores: Nembra re-plans every slope each morning so that water can run a little faster downhill, carrying a tune that Jēn delivered the night prior. Nembra does not speak; instead the owl consumes sound by closing its lone eye. Waves bend inward, are sifted and culled like millet, then spat with curved beak over the toad’s shoulder toward reed flutes grown from suprataflon mushrooms.

Third, the Work Animal—title capitalised because it insists. Official lineage says manta rays swam uphill and began breathing through pores of verbena. The Animal is a kite: five metres wide when extended, but able to fold into a shoulder pad resembling obsidian scale-mail. Its ventral surface harbours hundreds of pneumatic whiskers, each ending in a tag of thin ivory slate. When wind hits, the whiskers flutter like scalp fragments of a great chime, but the slate tags engrave testimony in microscopic slashes—a living stenograph of the sonic day. Islands of mycelium colonise the underskin, secreting sticky varnish that hardens into tunable lamellae. Under extremis the Animal can bleed these plates—jettisoning song-scars the way salamanders bleed bones—so the valley beneath temporarily gains an antenna, amplifying any melody the clouds forgot. When the Animal perishes (a rolling event lasting seven seasons), its tissues desert body-throttle whole, ptyx-offering lamellae like papyrus sheets. Architects collect them to build one true library every fifty years: a hollow trunk cathedral where silence is shelved.

The three are not trinity, merely traders. They trade the valley back to itself, role-swapping hourly. Jēn breathes mint and opens brass; Nembra folds a possibility into leaf angle; the Animal releases its flakes of slate only to allow the valley to re-absorb them as licences on future rainfall. Together they effect the slight millimetre year-shift along the basalt seams. Jēn measures Nembra’s geometries yesterday-bled; today the Animal staples them along wing ridges for tomorrow’s lift.

Visitors who walk the slope often imagine they overhear the bond, but they mistake submodule tones for sentient conversation. Actually, the Phalanx’s awareness lies one chord larger: valley plus singers plus listener breathing together. When you arrive your exhalations mingle, are mistaken for altos, are encoded into moss tapestries. Next sunrise you recognise your own hiccup budding as a back-beat in one Bleeder drum every twenty seconds.

The Vox Phalanx themselves deny fate; they speak of “cycling” instead. Cycling renders identity into amplitude. Yesterday Jēn’s shoulder blades lay partially imbedded in the Animal’s shoulder; today Nembra’s toad-flanks glow rose from metabolised owl droppings; tomorrow the gearwork between hair-winch and slate tags may invert gender of song. They greet each dance cycle with a mutual gaze—a soundless contraction passing across conjoined irises, one mirror reflecting another mirror without object or subject.

Fewer than twenty outsiders have stood near them long enough to eavesdrop on their practice, which is the business of keeping the valley from freezing or coagulating. You do not ask them questions the way you ask townsfolk directions. Instead you pick a fungal pipe lying curved like a crescent moon at Jēn’s feet, blow into the open end—notes arrive off-centre, turning left instead of right. From miles higher a chunk of ridge detunes, shivering until raindrops flatten and new gutters carve. If you succeed, the Vox Phalanx bow—not to you, but to your accidental chord spouse wedded for a moment. Then they whisk away, the Animal folding like worn silk, owl closing toad’s mouth, Jēn’s coppery hair dragging mist elegiacally into vertical streamers. The Bleeders sigh copper-mint as punctuation.

When permanence appears, you realise they have left a gift: one single lamella scratched with glyphs you cannot read arranged spirally, echo crystal embedded at centre. If you bring it to any down-slope settlement the architects will tune their instruments around it, setting time for the next human generation before they, too, stride back upland and dissolve into the score.

But perchance you remain too long—your boots grow cothurnus-high from resin pick-up, your own heartbeat homogenises into looped 7/8. Then, halfway up the ridgeline, the Vox Phalanx surround you. They do so wordlessly: Nembra projects quantised sky-glyphs on your forehead; Jēn unhooks a strand of hair that pierces the crown like a tuning wire; the Animal drapes one broad wing, turning you inside-out only sonically: your ears now nest inside sling-soft ventral membranes, your eyelids snap into ivory slate tags. You believe you’re still a watcher, but the valley is now listening through your body. Lichen begins writing your birthdate on neighbouring fungi; new Bleeders spout exact replicas of your palate ridges. The drum cycle re-stitches dedication to include a minor you-motif, unobtrusive, changed in retrograde. This lasts a month, maybe two, until the Phalanx re-collect: the owl closes an eye, the whimper resolves. You stand empty-eared, ringed by silence so absolute it seems honest. Descend then—ears will later sprout peacock fronds to catch leftover frequencies, but you will not worry; you are the first valley-dedicated listener ever deftly retired.

One final figuration: on nights geologically forecast to hinge, the three arrange themselves round the tallest Bleeder. Jēn places palm to piston-head; Nembra transposes afternoon’s sky-map over it; the Animal kneels, wing pressed flat so its slate tags tick like feathered metronomes. At some precise bar the valley inhales. The Bleeder rips open a copper gill, curling exhalation visible: a wafery plume smelling of glacier turned outward. Into this plume the Phalanx sings—not with lungs but with entire membranes vibrating every boundary. You will never see them in their remaindered configurations after—perhaps they re-collate into wholly new garb: a single winged creature composed of mettle, moan, and mallbox mushroom; or else the same three but mirrored into three alternate selves existing phase-shifted half a breath ahead. Whatever they become beyond, their last offering to the night is a whisper that swells back into global drum, sewing water to air, moss to basalt, Bleeder to listener.

So that is who lives here: no one, unless music itself counts as tenant.
