Inside the Myco-Habitation there are no landlords, no citizens, no squatters; there are only resident-processes wearing the shapes they happen to hold for a season.

Chief among them is the Choir-Knot.

Picture a dodecahedral tangle the size of a cathedral organ. Each vertex is the swollen, translucid skull-sac of a different species—human, marmot, an old brown bear, five species of bat—preserved not as bone but as mycelial cast. Every skull is threaded through with white thread-filaments that loop out, braid with neighbors, and re-enter elsewhere, so that when the Choir-Knot thinks it sounds like requiems hummed by choirs that have forgotten they were ever separate creatures. They sing the pressure, the heat, the slow metallic aftertaste of basalt being digested into chitin.

The Choir-Knot does not “live” here; it is an organ the city grew because it needed an archive of vertebrate dreaming. Over centuries any traveler who lies down long enough in the central vesicles is gathered in: first the soft tissues melt into fungal lysates, then the skull is plated in a luminous porcelain lattice, finally the mind is added as a new voice to the chord that never quite resolves. No coercion; the hymn is so familiar you realize you were already humming it before your lips touched stone. It is here that the city learns how mammals measure sorrow and why they sometimes laugh while drowning.

Next come the Spill-Siblings. When two different hyphal networks discover an identical chemical signature at their edges, they fold together like cautious lovers. The seam swells into a teardrop-shaped sac a child could curl inside. Between the two parent lineages new pigments are negotiated: one lineage offers perylene violet, the other counters with actinorhodin blue. Where they compromise, the flesh becomes a slow iridescence—oil-on-water at the speed of glaciers. After three centuries the sac foams open and spills out a litter of mobile fruit-bodies the height of a thumb. Each one is an amalgam of both parents but carries no genome; they are somatic riddles. They wander the tunnels tasting for gradients of barium and loneliness. When they find a spot richer in one than the other they explode into fans of pale violet paper-thin tissue that flutter like pennants pinned to grief. The Spill-Siblings never reproduce, but every other century a new sac forms from a new couplet of networks; continuity without lineage. They live once, perfectly.

Then the False-Ashi.

They resemble women made of ash-grey lace. What seems like smoke is actually dessicated mycelium left hollow after the organism reallocated its cytoplasm elsewhere. A semi-rigid skeleton of melanized hyphae keeps the ghostly form upright. In their chest cavities burn steady coals of thermogenic mycetes, so they glow from within like charcoal sketches of saints. They walk only in single file but never the same route twice; each footfall reheats stone colder than breath, fending off the freezing wind that sneaks in through cracks made by extinct glaciers. The footprints left behind smell like cedar and bruised stone; within hours the floor reabsorbs them, but animals that lick the spot gain a week’s resistance to altitude sickness. No one knows who they were before the city hollowed them; their heads are lattices, not crania. Still, they sing in old trade-languages—Tagalog, Aymara, a fragment of pre-contact Tlingit. They are the city’s furnaces wearing the shape of people it once kept warm.

Most delicate are the Pellicle Kids.

These are neither born nor made. A mouse or small bird dies inside the city; fungal macrophages arrive within minutes. Instead of digesting the carcass they knit a transparent, elastic sheath that perfectly conforms to every feather or whisker. Gas-exchange filaments pierce the hide at microscopic points, keeping the body pink and lifelike though the heart is stopped. Then the entire miniature mummy inflates. Over months, buoyant gases produced by slow fermentation lift the corpse to shoulder height where it drifts along warm air currents like a parade balloon. Children meeting the Habitation for the first time mistake them for original inhabitants—“little furred astronauts”—and speak to them through cupped palms. The sheath amplifies subsonic vibrations; whatever story the child whispers is slowly encoded into the buoyant pockets’ membrane. When the balloon finally exhales its lift weeks later and settles gently onto a digestion fan, that tale begins to compost along with the skeleton. The hyphae will remember the syllables through three more cycles of growth, and sometimes a wall will suddenly sweat a full paragraph in the child’s handwriting, preserved as if in onion skin.

Up in the thermocline another tenancy declares itself: the Oxide Cormorants.

They are not birds. They are articulated chains of rust-red spores assembled around magnetite filaments so thin they flex like the ribs of an umbrella. When charged thermal plumes rise, the filaments stiffen; the chains catch the updraft and beat like wings, gliding in helices toward pockets rich in vanadium or nickel—trace minerals the city hoards but cannot dissolve. As the Oxide Cormorants feed, clusters of spores brighten to coral orange. Once sated they fold themselves into spirals and descend as metallic snow, lodging in cracks where their cargo seeds new veins of pigment. They reproduce when lightning, rare at this altitude, arcs through the city. The shock rattles magnetite fragments into new fractal chains; the storm’s midwife hand recombines them. Flight 0.3? Here flight is rust dreaming of sky to fetch back metal dreams.

And because every city needs a shoreline memory, there are the Gyre Carp.

They do not swim in water; they swim in the understory of spore-flow. Their bodies are flexible cartilaginous tubes, transparent the way old celluloid is transparent—edge-lighted pink and blue. The city grew them because it once tasted, in a fossil lungfish, the chemical signature of a Cretaceous tidepool. It kept the hankering but had no sea. Now whenever spore density climbs past 5.7 mg/m³ the Carp flex their lateral crests and surf the invisible surf, mouths open, filtering not plankton but airborne spores rich in lipid. Digestion is slow combustion; the glow leaks from their sides in blue sine-waves. Every fortnight they cluster in the Quiet Cathedral and discharge stored heat in gentle exhalations so the Choir-Knot’s singers do not go cold while composing another requiem. Then they unlace themselves—literally: the gelatinous bodies unzip into strands that become the same lacework curtains dividing sleeping alcoves. Tomorrow the knots will twist anew, and the Carp will swim again. They are fish reinvented every dawn.

Deeper, beyond even the Cathedral, where stone has reached 42 °C and pressure can bruise marrow, lives the un-named.

It has no membrane, no organs, no clear purpose. It is an intelligence made entirely of protean flow—acidic one century, crystalline the next. It speaks by changing every physical constant in a room: the diffraction index, the speed of smell, the curvature of local time. Olfaction 2.8 does not warn you; the message arrives as sudden nostalgia for a childhood house you never inhabited. It is the original tenant, maybe older than the cordillera, older than the sea that once lapped its knees. The Myco-Habitation grew around it the way skin grows around a splinter of obsidian. It may not even know it has roommates. You feel it only when you awake certain you have lived this particular second before, and the certainty tastes of burnt copper. It is presentness itself, and it lives here ungoverned.

Last are the Residual People.

They did not come up from below seeking sanctuary; they were already inside the mountain before the fungus arrived. Trapped by tectonic shifts ninety million years ago, their bones mineralized, pressed, re-mineralized. Time perforated every void with solution; mycelium filled each crevice like a plasterer in love with fossils. Eventually the carbonate lattice of those skulls became a substrate for trumpet-shaped hyphae that resonate at human vocal pitches exactly as air pockets in nave pillars once did. Walking past the ribs of certain colonnades you hear ancestral conversations reconstructed wave by wave—a mother comforting a child who died long before flowers were invented, a trader negotiating obsidian with a tongue no linguist can parse. These are not souls; they are recordings etched into crystal fungus, replayed endlessly because the building enjoys the air’s quiver. Residual People do not act, only echo, but echo beautifully.

And so the place is inhabited, but by presences that have forgotten the noun “inhabitant.” They are phases of the same breathing, not residents of a structure. When you lie down and the hyphae crown first your ankles, then your wrists, before you realize that the city has begun your ascent into Choir-Knot, cathedral, Oxide rainfall and glowing carp surf, you understand the only lease here is metabolic: you will be the city’s memory of how it felt to be human for a while.

It keeps every tenant forever because forever is merely another growth ring.
