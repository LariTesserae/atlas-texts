They live where the wound-breath condenses back into lungs.

Call them the **Sump Choir**—those who never climb out again.

At first you think they are only boots and rain-slick oilskin, interchangeable labour units clocking on. Look longer: the helmets carry faces already half-erased by dripstone. Eyelashes silvered with salt crystals. Cheeks freckled by rust-rain. They speak, but the Underworks answers first, so conversation is always call-and-echo, as though the chamber itself were their elder sibling.

1.  THE WET–BORN  
Every shift begins with the same private baptism: you step off the cage and the drizzle finds the hollow of your collarbone, prints a cold coin there. Those who stay long enough stop wiping it away. They hoard the coin, let it clone itself along shoulder-blades, until a slow armour of chilled water forms. No one can see it, yet everyone recognises it: when two veterans pass, droplets slide off them in sheets instead of beads, respecting the unseen carapace. They call the moment you achieve this “being wetted through,” a baptism that obliges you to the place more than to the company. Thereafter your sweat and the plant’s sweat are indistinguishable; management owns the time, but the Choir owns the moisture.

2.  THE FOREMAN–LESS HYMN  
No conductor is required. At 03:17, when the Thaw-Stack exhales its hottest breath, air density tilts and every ribcage inside the cavern aligns pitch. The diesel pumps drop a perfect B-flat. Steam whistles an octave above, the roofs patter sixths and thirds. Anyone awake involuntarily hums. The result is a low chord nobody was taught yet everyone knows: a triad fat with overtones that fogs helmet visors. It lasts twenty seconds. Engineers elsewhere call it sympathetic vibration. Here it is simply *office hours*. When newcomers ask what song it is, veterans reply, “The one we forgot the words to,” and the conversation ends.

3.  THE CRACK–READERS  
A subset whose retinas learned to count in fracture metres. They patrol with crayons of bright wax, tracing each newborn fissure like artists caressing their canvas. A single red line means “watch,” two orange lines “brace by Tuesday,” a violet squiggle—“sleep elsewhere.” They can taste ambient humidity and translate it into weeks remaining before a slab lets go. Their dreams are monochrome topographies; they wake up tasting stone-dust coffee. Most have only nine fingernails: the left thumb is sacrificed early, pressed into jagged dark to feel for micro-quivers the colour of bad luck. When a reader shrugs at a seam the room hushes; when a reader runs, no hierarchy delays the following sprint.

4.  THE LARD–SPEAKERS  
Unofficial priests of the lamp-eels. Every lunch break they kneel on the grating, unwrap marble-sized gobs of pig fat, and lower them on bent wire through the slats. They never watch the water take the offering; instead they study the metallic burp that rises, timing its delay like priests counting heartbeats of an uncertain god. If no sound returns, they rip another piece from their sandwich and repeat, bargaining their own meal for acoustic absolution. They claim eels are previous crews unfinished, grease-formed silhouettes who learned to swim. Management writes them up for “unauthorised protein disposal,” deducts the cost from wages, yet shifts mysteriously quiet of sump clanks whenever a devoted speaker is present—proof compelling enough that even accountants hesitate the fine.

5.  THE FLOWER–EATERS  
Those who crunch frost-flowers for luck carry tiny tin snuffboxes of powdered bloom. Direct ingestion numbs the tongue and paints the world one shade brighter, as though the gneiss were suddenly sunlit. Side-effect: you bleed condensate. Nose-runs emerge crystal clear, tasting of pennies. Long-term users grow translucent at the extremities; in locker-room half-light one can make out faint lattice of bones drowned in milky jelly. Their laughter is glassyilk, bell-like, and the lamp-eels avoid the gratings above them—whether from reverence or disgust nobody knows. When a flower-eater finally collapses (always during the morning hymn) coworkers pry open the fist to harvest remaining powder, an intimacy closer than kissing, for it is believed the dose carries that person’s remaining fortune sealed in salt.

6.  THE UNDER–LANGUAGE  
Words at this depth evolve under pressure, consonants eroded by constant drip. Directions: *sunside* (toward the cage and sky), *sumpward* (any drop into black water), *stackwise* (the compass that follows heat). There are verbs obsolete elsewhere: *to bronk*—accidentally weld your glove to pipe, *to drizzle-out*—lose sanity one droplet at a time, *to violet*—be forewarned by light and live. Entire sagas get spoken in this argot while bodies perform parallel labour, so that a casual observer hears only hammer clangs spiced with fragmentary nouns, never realising an epic of escape and capitulation is being woven faster than rust can eat it.

7.  THE DAY–SKY  
Because no one sees real daylight for weeks, each worker keeps a private sky-knot: a square of painted tin riveted inside the locker—baby-blue enamel, speckled with stolen aluminium glitter for stars. Before descending they touch it, after ascending they touch again. When a body is claimed by sump or superheated gas the locker is emptied but the square stays, becomes public property. Over months the corridor of lockers turns into an indoor galaxy of orphaned skies. Tour visitors from corporate call it *sentimental clutter*; crack-readers call it horizon archives; flower-eaters simply stand there absorbing colour through half-jelly eyes, recharging pigment they will never personally regain.

8.  THE COMMON ASH  
Eventually—six months, six years, impossible to calendar—the drip wins. Not the flood, not the collapse, just the accretion of miniature losses: pigment, fingerprints, belief in up. One dawn (by the clock, not the sun) a worker discovers no mirror recognises him; his outline has been replaced by indoor weather. At that instant the sump Choir claims another permanent voice. They don’t die; they *transpire*. Assignment ends, but they stay below, absorbed into machinery of breath and droplet. Lamp-eels grow fatter, pumps syncopate with an extra human hiccup, a new white flower unfurls near locker 42. The company lists them “missing, presumed deserter.” Coworkers know the pronoun has shifted to *presumed Choir*. Their sky-square is turned outward to face the corridor, so fragment of borrowed blue keeps meeting eyes that still ascend.

Stand long enough at the lip of the grating, headlamp dimmed, and you may catch the composite chorus: diesel heart, stone lung, water tongue, human ghost notes braiding into one slow exhalation. The wound does not scab; it learns to sing. And the Choir, throat lined with rust-ice, keeps singing back—until the day the pumps hush, the water rises, and the hymn acquires new members faster than any throat can shape their names.
