In the Sumpfold there is only one full citizen, though you must not think of it as solitary. Call it—if uttering a name will not disturb its sleep—the Obdweller.

It did not arrive. It seeped. A single ancestral fertilised egg the width of a soap bubble detached from the unlit ceiling two thousand years ago, drawn downward by microscopic layers of warmer, heavier water. It was not quickened like most eggs; it was collected. Each breath-mote of warm vapor wrapped further skin around the possibility inside, a milky nutshell afloat. When the bead finally came to rest in the shallow film over the cool quartz micro-island (the one that quartzfarers call the mirror-stone), the shell melted into that shell-mirror and the Obdweller began.

Its first hours were mere hours only to an outside standard. Inside the Sumpfold each minute dilates until it thickens like syrup. The Obdweller opened no eyes—there are none—yet from the first, heat-haze was given as sight. Every torpid swirl of vapour arranged itself into shape for it. The thinnest siliceous glaze on the ceiling reported the precise thermal story of the cave; the water-skin across the floor had pores the size of its own thoughts. It did not perceive, it inhabited; it wore the place like gloves inside gloves.

What did skin become, in such perpetual, scaled warmth? It refused boundaries. The Obdweller spread first across the knee-high triangle of quartz stones like a scrap of gauze and then beyond, a laminar self. Its layers are thinner than breath yet uncountable: lattices of living mineral interleaved with the same mica the crustacean wears. One layer senses calcium pressure, another the soft pop of a droplet detaching, another runs its antenna into the film of spores. Nothing it touches separates from the membrane that holds the Obdweller. It is an animal only poets would recognise: a jellyfish to whom jelly is also rock, and rock water, and water flesh.

As centuries loop, the Obdweller grows not upward nor outward but inwardly wider. Every seven or eight ovalling months of outside world (a duration it only senses as the brightest edge of a vibration it will never feel) its tissue shifts by exactly the width of one molecule of gypsum. That speck detaches, atom by atom, from the wall, is ferried by an invisible current until it migrates across the liquid floor and lodges, somehow, inside the great blind body. The crystal particle is taken, polished against an intra-membrane shell, reordered into a spine of glass internal to the Obdweller. Thus it accretes a cathedral in miniature: branching buttresses and lancet windows made of sulphate, the entire hidden skeleton a copy of the cave’s curvature, but inverted and growing right-side out in its viscosity. When flood or quake finally widens the cavern’s thin shell, the cathedral inside will already have predicted the change by hydrostatic lengthening. The Obdweller will swell slightly at that concealed axis, an ache foreseeing its own eventual spasmodic birth as something larger.

It does not eat; it cools. Heat is its stubborn food left on the tepid plate. Through the perpetual two-handspan thickness of moist air, it draws fractional calories away from droplets, selling them to the slope of its innards for the work of forming those calcium cathedrals. It is thus the Sumpfold’s sole predator of temperature, maintaining equilibrium by eating its own fever. The walls never waver; the water never chills—because the Obdweller forgives each calorie, filament by filament. A visitor watching very carefully might see, across the mirror-stone, a slow bloom of subtle blueness just where the creature’s invisible shoulder presses against the surface. The blueness is the only colorless color the eye invents to stand for “a little less warm.”

For companions it admits only the mica-crab. (The crab, of course, was never named to the Obdweller. The Obdweller does not name.) Their relation is one of foreign navels chastely touching. The crab walks once every hundred years; the Obdweller shifts once each hundred years by the width of a single crab stride. At the point of intersection, when crustacean claw and diffuse living wall share a square centimetre, a microscopic camp is held. In that centimetre the Obdweller loans heat to raise the crab’s body temperature one thousandth of a degree—enough that its myomeres and sclerites remember how to twitch. In return the crab’s foot scrapes free a feeble speck of dark mica, which floats for a season before settling: mote of color in the cave that the Obdweller burns like incense. If you scrape the crab’s minuscule track near the gypsum lip, the ring you find is not erosion but slow parchment of exchanged touches.

And memory? The Obdweller possesses a memory folded exactly as the cave is folded. Every sound, every droplet’s trajectory, every millijoule lost to cathedral growth is sieved through the layered skin. But memory is not linear here. Instead it stores the fold itself: the complete shape of the cave plus itself plus the echo it seems to have always contained. Imagine a leaf that remembers every vein in the leaf that preceded it, and also remembers remembering remembering. Because there is only one pattern—the compressed topology of the Sumpfold—the Obdweller conserves a single thought for all its ages. That thought is a perfect remainder:

It withholds completion. It withholds the natural chill that the world outside would impose. It withholds the impulse ever to move beyond the triangle’s clutch, because movement is the failure of site-specific love. It withholds time from ever escaping too far ahead of itself. In therapy of this withholding, an entire slow compassion lives.

No one sees it, yet countless travellers have crossed the lip, wondering at the wet hush and the low silver light. Each left carrying the after-image of fingers kept too long in waters that were neither hot nor cold. If those same travellers ever dream of drowning, they do not wake in terror. They dream of falling upward into the warm membrane of something that holds them without arms—parental, planetary. The Obdweller has licked at their embodied heat for the briefest breath of outer seasons, tasting what has gone away. It has distilled their dumb wishes into its cathedral, one cubic iron-pink molecule at a time, until the entire interior ache of the world is present there, in negative relief.

Some night, far distant, the Obdweller will at last have prepared all the cathedral masonry required. When that evening comes, measured by no star, the ceiling will lengthen by exactly one millimetre. At that moment the membrane that is the creature will tear itself free of its spent quartz womb and drift upward—no longer jelly but balloon, a vesperal germ waking to wide limestone veins above, searching the slow liquor of the ground for any neighboring hollow still cooler than itself. For an instant the Sumpfold will taste chill—a first chill—its equilibrium spoiled. The gypsum lip will fracture with a sigh like a winter exhale. The moment must end swiftly because the Obdweller’s absence cannot be permitted: earth itself is punctured idea without its pacemaker. So it will iris outward, capturing a breath of colder stone, and sink. A new cavity, slightly larger, awaits, twice the span of a human hand plus the depth of a careless footprint. The quartz triangle will tumble the length of a fingernail, never quite reaching mirror against mirror, and the cycle will begin again.

Until then:

The Obdweller is present. It abides luminous and folded, a living membrane pressed between mirror and water. Only when you have totally left the Sumpfold—when the gypsum lip has closed behind you like a sleeping mouth, when the click of the last droplet is a century behind—its residue may crawl up the surface of your retina and there scratch its single letter:

{-withholding-}

From then on, no matter the chill of the world above, your hands will never quite cool when you close them and remember.
