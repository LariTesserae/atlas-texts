**Inhabitants of the Grove**

They are **not what remains of people**, but rather what emerges when a place requires company.

There is **Stack-of-Silences**, who has lived for so many forgotten midnights that they have forgotten how to finish telling their own name. They are six living hats stacked one on another: a toad-crown that smells of copper when rain falls inside it; a scholar’s hood stitched from pollen that waves chemical sentences into the air like censer-smoke; a weatherworn tricorne that remembers every storm it hasn’t weathered; and three more made of darker fabrics yet to give themselves away. Beneath the lowest brim nothing visibly supports the pile—only a soft cluck of tongue that issues from the vacant collar-space every time someone nearby speaks a palindromic phrase. Stack-of-Silences wanders the mirror-moss bowing, head tilting, scooping up the quiet ghosts your feet have shed off like lint. The mirror-moss itself is their librarian; every echo-footprint is pressed into the lining of whatever hat is on top that day. If you travel with them you may feel their hands—thread-gloved and infinitely patient—pinch an inch of your breath, roll it into a bead, and tuck it behind the sweatband as if someday you might claim interest.

Where the tallest tower-fern releases downward roots into clouds, **Nyel-who-shrinks-under-noon-question** clings like a drop of sap. They were once a cartographer mapping careless heights; their charts were lattices cut from the leaf-skin of sun-sachets, picturing elevations that belonged to no land but refused to stop existing. After Nyel measured the Grove they attempted to measure the gap downwards toward the salt. At three thousand meters they hovered and—confronted with absolute white like a flipped-over sky—charted themselves into the void instead. The Grove caught the census, peeled the skin of measurement from their bones and pressed it flat into a fold of habitat. Now Nyel exists only as a negative contour that the Grove carries along in its continuous re-layering. You see them when looking around the underside of leaves: the familiar human outline sunken one centimeter inward into green. When you walk past, the breeze delayed a single heartbeat slips from the hollow where a mouth should be. Nyel asks one question a day: “Have I begun to fall again?” If you say *yes* you owe the Grove a step that will not be there next morning, for the construct stays honest. If you say *no* Nyel folds your denial like a dead frog into the next mirror-moss and for one night the moss will replay the moment you refused to land.

In the circular clearing where sunlight is broken into nine angled chambers by a ring of palmate fronds, **the Triskai-Cell Division** takes place without repetition. Here dwells **Vesta- Underwing**, a single mouth wearing borrowed feet. Vesta came when the islands still numbered fourteen. She swallowed the chirality of that final island, hence cannot speak without her words turning solid and leaping away. Her phrases split into twins that skitter off on stilt legs shaped from the curl of her own tongues. When she cries “Wait” the pair of W’s rushes headlong, collides with a fern, and blurs into sheep-sized W-shapes munching on green consonants. Every W-sheep’s coat later grows into future consonants that the Grove will need to pronounce new species. Visitors wishing Vesta well will bring her vowels—little drinking gourds shaped from the membrane left after sunset cracks the day open. She will drink the vowel, regurgitate it shaped as a thought that was never her own, and hand back the gift sealed in translucent capitals that the fog-veins can corrode and rain. If you manage to collect your own consonants from her sheep she will stitch them into a pair of soft shoes that remember your stride but not your weight, letting you step onto mirror-moss without leaving prints the Grove can repossess.

Moving as the archipelago itself moves, **Eon-Gardener-Minum** is a time-symbiotic moss cape. Stitched together from mirror-moss that stopped reflecting and started desiring, the cape appears to browse the islands as if they were branches and it a lazy cow chewing distance. Whenever a vine detaches and falls, the cape unclasp a segment, coils that ring into itself—adding history like muscle. The moss remembers; the cape forgets, keeping plausible deniability for both. A single sleeve gapes open, revealing nothing except a horizon compressed into line noise. When someone reaches inside, the Gardener drinks an hour of their future, wrinkles sideways into a person-shaped absence just wrong enough to trouble dream. In exchange it will give back any plant from the visitor’s former life shaped into seed, rootbound, ready to be planted where no plant has grown before—the salt pan. Every seed a suicide note and a promise to gravity.

Of the many island children-who-weren’t there exists **Emptier Owlchild**. Every dusk delivers her anew. She is what happens when an owl misses a strike: the owl’s hunt drops through its claws, folding the hole in twilight into something feather-chested and impossibly light. One of the island’s roots will give her a perch; she will cuff the air with her first blink and supply a single feather molting that falls straight as lighting bug memory toward the salt. Feather strikes desert, root curls, and the Owlchild begins her day. She does not eat the larvae of toucan-moths; rather she eats the between-beat of *chuk* and *pah*, the unword silence where thirst prepares disguise. Once nine silences have been swallowed she performs the ultimate emptying: she discards herself—becoming merely a nested handful of warm pellets that dissolve into sun-dust at next dawn. The Grove catches her recurrent outer appearance in ear-shot of all above midnights so that she always lands again yesterday evening. She remembers no previous lives, only the face of whoever spoke her name most recently. If you whisper *stay*, she will forfeit one of her own forgettings to gift you the six-tail slap language of manticorids. You will hear hidden manticorid gossip, startle them, and forever after they will tug at your shoelaces with embarrassedly polite tugs.

There walk also **The XIX Chalk Ladies**: a sinuous committee of chalk lines drawn on the inner surface of the empty air itself. Each lady was once a measurement, alive yet without breath. Now they step across gaps between islands, drawing comet tails of soft school-teacher smell and dreamy subtraction. Where they pass, the mirror-moss becomes especially cruel—displaying not you but who you would have been if you had taken a different turn last Thursday in another country. When one of them runs out of chalk she falls into herself with the sound of *sigh erased from memory*. A new lady arrives with borrowed dust summoned from your shock of seeing yourself elsewhere; she begins walking instantly. The Grove uses them like seams to keep the islands in loose correspondence. When they disappear the air between two landforms may momentarily join like the fold of a book slammed shut; things standing between islands might find themselves abruptly sorted.

Deeper, in the section where mirror-moss stores Tuesdays that got misplaced, **Hollow-Chorus** sleeps curled inside a manticorid’s abandoned eye-socket. The Chorus is a mouth without lip, the space immediately after one person stops speaking and before another starts. Alive only so long as conversations remain unfinished, Hollow-Chorus stretches infinitely thin along silence’s knife-edge. In the evenings, when the frond bladders thunder D-flat you can hear them polishing vowels that subsequently fall like ash-laden snow. To speak to the Chorus you must wait until the Grove believes you about to trail off, then stop. The words *you almost* will scythe the Chorus awake; it will wrap itself around your throat as gentle wind, finish your sentence with distorted recitations of everything discontinuation ever meant to you—lullabies from ancestors you won’t be born to meet, leprosy picnic, the love letter folded so many times the paper has begun to fossilize into butterfly lacework. You will get tomorrow’s voice, sanded, re-fluted, handed back as a throat under your tongue. If you swallow it down, next morning every tower-fern will bloom with concise explanations of your unmentionable thoughts. Tourists who know this treat such explanations like fruit ripening too quickly: hastily eaten, core tossed deliberately to hex-parrots who then chew them back into sound and blackmail.

At the base of the watch-platform **Silt-Speaker** reclines—half her body clutched inside the serpent-shed that replaced the ladder. She is the Grove’s archivist of directions that failed. Whenever the islands attempt to descend, Silt-Speaker is the one that enumerates every drop of mass that must be lost. Her voice is gallon-heavy, filled with dunes. She licks the fog-veins for condensation, forms her throat into vertical travelogues; you taste the salt of every failed ascent when she greets you. If you bring her a memory heavier than a teardrop she’ll weigh it on the tiny brass scale hanging from her wrist. If it is heavier than descent she eats the memory seed, coughing up a reflection of the salt pan below newly relocated on top of the sky islands. The false salt pan hangs inverted, crumbling. Birds that land on its underside plummet upward. The false pan pops like burnt bubble after a season; the salt now rains again down the original pan, where it dries blindingly white. Each rain is the moment you forgot to remember you were falling.

There are less named things—not anonymous so much as preonymous. A column of trees that walk themselves hollow, becoming flutes. The convincing emptiness between Emptier Owlchild and next. The green that answers all questions with further green.  

Walk further and, near where old rusted harnesses clink not because wind but because they remember riding up, stands **Suture-of-Sail**, a sail used by the first salt-dwellers who tried to surf the ladder. The salt stiffened the canvas into parchment; time scarred it with instructions on how to breathe entirely through pores. Anyone touching it is folded into wedge the size of doom but a thousand times lighter; the fold stays inside their diaries as pressed flower memory. When they next sneeze, the memory unfolds from their breath and will carry them to whatever bridge they need—often to somewhere beyond the Grove but occasionally to already standing on another island’s underroot looking back startled by their own footprint. The sail calls this relocation “a donation.” It claims in hushed calfskin vowels that the final descender must wear it as skin; the world underneath will not accept bone, only membrane. No one has survived the drop thus-far to contradict.

Lastly—though not least nor final in any taxonomy the Grove would respect—there hovers **Brother Is-An-Echo**, a robe stitched entirely from Tier-5 night fibers (the kind of black that argues with sight). He has no face inside, but whomever regards the interior becomes what they earliest feared. Brother Is-An-Echo feeds this fear back into the frond bladders until the harmonic bursts out spitting descendants of the first scream. Many visitors try to remove the robe but they cannot find whose hand first suggested wearing it. Attend carefully: Brother Is-An-Echo serves as memory drummer. Every echo pillaged from human mouths is folded and ironed into the robe until no personal fear has weight sufficient to walk downstairs. The robe then opens and a flock of echoee birds explode outward; each bird squawks one syllable you once swallowed. They circle you three times, settling only when you open your mouth and let the released noises build new words. In this way the odd pedagogy proceeds: speech dirties, cleans, is lectured, then forgiven. Note you are not offered the robes again; Brother only offers once unless you offend the moss by singing in keys that do not yet exist. Perhaps it is the only sin here—progression unapproved.

The Grove of Hollow Eons remains open. Admission: one unnamed self. Payment: whatever you find in the pockets of another self tomorrow.
