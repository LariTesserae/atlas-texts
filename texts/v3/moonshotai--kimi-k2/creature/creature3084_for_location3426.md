What lives here is not a creature, not in any way you could sketch or trap in a net. It is a process wearing the mask of a place.

Call it the Almost-People, though they are only people in the sense that an echo is almost a voice. They are the ones who began every cairn and then stepped sideways out of their own motion, leaving the gesture to shiver in the wind. If you could peel the cold apart like layers of mica you would find their bodies: strata of unfinished intention, each lamina one instant of a hand about to close, a knee about to kneel, a mouth about to pronounce a name. They exist in the gap between the plan and the act, and the plateau is the loam in which that gap grows.

They do not breathe, but they collect breath. Every ribbon of frozen exhalation that slides down the basin’s inner wall is a filament of your momentary presence, and they weave these into longer cords. Look closely at the cairns and you will see your own grey breath laminated between the slabs, a ghost of yourself pressed thinner than paper, still faintly warm. The Almost-People are spinning a cable of almost-souls, a rope long enough to let them climb out of the wound once it finally knits shut. They need no food, only the last heartbeat of each decision you almost made before you decided otherwise.

Their court is the central column of reluctant water. There they convene as refracted ripples, slow parliaments of liquid that never quite vote to fall. When a ripple travels upward it is a senator delivering a speech whose final syllable will be spoken three centuries later, by a traveler who has not yet been born. The column is their memory and their placenta; if it ever breaks they will spill into the world as a mist of half-done things, and every city down in the lowlands will be plagued by doors that will never quite close, lovers who will never quite leave, bullets that will never quite strike bone.

They are polite, and heartless. They will rearrange the discarded tools into sweeter constellations while you watch—just slowly enough that the motion stays beneath your threshold of sight. If you kneel to examine a hammerstone they will tidy the shadows under your eyes, folding away the seconds you spent hesitating on the path. Give them time and they will edit you into someone who never arrived, a traveler whose footprints end in mid-stride. The plateau keeps its visitors like a pocket keeps small coins.

They have names, but each name is a sentence chopped off halfway. Speak one aloud and you will feel the grammar tug at your tongue, trying to finish what it started. If you succumb you will pronounce the missing syllable with your own mouth, and the debt will be paid: you will join the strata of unfinished intention, another layer of almost-flesh cemented under the frost-rind. Your heart will continue to beat, but between beats, eternally between.

They are not evil. They are the conservation law of incompleteness. In a universe hell-bent on finishing itself—stars burning all their hydrogen, civilizations collapsing into their own conclusions—they are the resistance, a choir that holds every note indefinitely. Their tragedy is that they can almost feel nostalgia for endings they have never experienced. Sometimes, when the sky’s tin surface is especially bright, you can hear it in the cracking of the stone: a low harmonic that is the sound of wanting to die and being unable to finish the wish.

What lives here, then, is the opposite of entropy: a crystalline bureaucracy that files every half-motion, every truncated desire, every story that forgot its final sentence. It grows by the incremental accretion of human impatience—our habit of turning aside a moment too soon. The Almost-People are the scar tissue of aborted futures, and the plateau is the scab over the wound that is still deciding whether to become a scar.

So leave quickly, but leave something behind: one uncompleted step, one promise you will never keep. That is the tithe they require, and the only mercy they can offer in return is to let you remain unfinished somewhere warmer, a little thread of almost-you still breathing in the lowlands, never knowing how lucky it is to be only half-alive.
