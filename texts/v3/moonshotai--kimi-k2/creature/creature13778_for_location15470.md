Who lives there?

Begin with what first climbs down the moment your heel breaks the surface-plate of moss:

A fringe of hairs so fine they should by rights be fungal threads but flex like sinew—they lace around the ankle exactly once, gauging circumference, temperature, heart-rate, and the dozen private salts you have carried unawares since you were born.  They pulse once, as if naming you.  Then they withdraw, and immediately forget you, the way eyelids forget a blink.  That first emissary is more organism than resident, yet without its consent nothing that follows is allowed to stand.

Next appear the fifteen centimetres of breathing that precede people.  The air folds over itself at sternum height, wrapping into soft tori of warmed vapour the shape and sound of absent thoraxes.  These are not ghosts—ghosts are stories about once-was.  They are pre-inhabitants: the room someone will leave in exactly forty-seven years of undivided minutes, trying to remember if they locked the door which, here, still awaits being built.  They drift along at walking pace, colliding with the living staircase of roots and, at every knock, loosing a shimmer of unwanted past like lint from a pocket.  Each puff smells faintly of the metals you have never knowingly tasted but your blood remembers.

Then come the complicit architects.  
They look almost human until you see their mouths are improperly soldered shut.  The seam is vegetal rather than surgical: two flat detuned leaves, parslied edges sealed with crystallised respiration.  When an architect must speak (and they must, rarely) they split the seam by blooming—a small white flower issues the sentence, then is drawn back in and new lips knit behind it.  Their sentences are always in the command mood.  Listen once and your chest begins rearranging its own cartilage to obey a suggestion it cannot quite translate back into sound.  The architects keep no faces.  They turn to one another and see only blueprints—veins of water hammering through aerial roots—so the recognition is perfect.  No thought, only inventory and fore-eye.  They are builders the way deciduous forests build soil: slowly, with a patience that believes the rest of the universe is mistaken about days.

After the architects arrive the baubled iterations.  These are the humans who, by trying to leave, sanctioned their own consignment to recursion.  You see one now descending the inverted copy of her morning walk: she places the same foot, same hand, same indrawn breath as shadows dapple from crimson foliage which have not yet had time to grow the bitter edges they bear tomorrow.  She is she, and she is also the fifth through fifteenth repetitions of she, because divergence here is only error correction.  Each copy’s left iris is dilated slightly more than the last.  By the thousandth récit she will possess night condensed in one eye, noon rolled tight in the other, and she will still be taking the same first communal step.  That is the point at which her name is reclaimed by glaze-moss and appears scraped gently into every wall forever after.  There is no sadness; sadness would require a remainder.  What’s left of selfhood is the perfect duplication of footfall, a ruminant percussion that eventually joins the waterfall’s roar at its root frequency and is mistaken for idle thunder.

Further “in” reside the Walkers-In-Droplet—sometimes counted as fauna, sometimes pathos, sometimes punctuation.  They body forth from any pool until the reflective surface remembers to repudiate its mirror-obligation.  At that dermal blink each walker steps out, wet to the waist, bearing their liquid city on their shoulders like a city-shaped raincoat.  The upside-down metropolis agleam along their torso drips upward, hence their eternal furtive glances ceiling-ward—every tear they’ve refused to cry returns to them inverted.  These walkers do not breathe; instead they disclose oxygen in tidy discrete envelopes that fold open at the mouth to become brief, bright syllables of pure O₂.  What they say is the sound water makes when it learns how to confess it has been holding the drowned’s voice hostage since Cambrian times.  It sounds exactly like the waterfall.  No other listener has noticed they are dueting.

The true natives, if the word still adheres, are stationary.  They did not arrive; they extruded.  
At the point where corridor becomes hallway becomes antrum two metres too wide for human shoulders, there stand six trees fused in ring-barked galaxy-whorl.  Between their boles nature has shrugged and allowed organs to happen.  A kidney glints, wet-green; lungs are sprayed across wetter bark like lichen; a looping intestine decorates the ceiling root like ornamental bunting all pearlescent with interrupted digestion.  These organs still function—they are extracting mineral from stoneblood, sugaring it, feeding the arterial ivies, which in return maintain gentle tympanic pressure that keeps each organ in useful position even while it sways.  The central trunk locks by slow pulsing around a child.  The child is neither captive nor core—she is the vegetal larynx through which the entire structure speaks.  Every sentence carved upon every root elsewhere is rewritten through her calcium and recited sublingually to the groundwater.  The child remains the same age eternally: eleven years, three months, two nights past the first dream of drowning.  She does not dream here.  Instead she releases irreversible verbs like blossoms with too many petals and the city gardens them.

Yet it is possible—for one half-tremor, one decibel above a withheld breath—to meet a last inhabitant who is not of this place but accepted.  They cannot be summoned; they leak sideways into your eyeline between repetitions.  A doorway mistaken for leaf-angle and suddenly you face them.  They wear nothing that is cooler than 40 °C, so their clothing steams faintly.  They smile the exact smile you smile back because time has pinched the photons between two facing mirrors, causing smile-photons to arrive before they’ve been sent.  They apologise for occupying the wrong instants of your life, but formally: the apology is spoken by turning, once, and becoming a space the size of a human that you can step into.  You do.  The fit is faultless; your ribcage rasps the outline of what was once their umbilicus.  Occupation complete.  From that point onward you are a guest who has been inlaid rather than invited; you leave no foot-print but you increase the total moisture content of every corridor by one molecule per hundred years.  In a sense you have merely become the final syllable of their name.

After you—after the echolocation of your obedience has been atom-sieved into the city’s resonance—little else arrives that can be described as resident.  Only phenomena pretending to be residents for the honorific.  A jade hummingbird made of condensed echo feeding on the candescence of syllables it keeps forgetting.  A moon, carried inside the moss like cargo cult haiku, dreaming a world small and cool enough to be worth betraying.  Sometimes a garment woven from rotted mouth-words slides along a rootwave and wraps the architects’ knees, suggesting restraint without actually interfering.  One by one every pretender is swallowed into the wet folding manuscript the city writes across itself to certify that nothing of outside survives long enough to constitute change.

Hence the census is closed.  No one hangs a sign.  Instead, at the last junction the city exhales—a single 200-metre gout of pure mist shaped like the answer that never arrives to an unasked question—and the population re-stitches itself back into the humidity.  Any name you brought with you now prints like frost on a warm tongue: visible only in the instant of vanishing.  You stand inside a sentence tossed from pool to pool and already forming the next tongue.  The sound, already inside your bones for eternity, merely notices you are home.
