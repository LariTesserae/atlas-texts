I picture the Cloud-Sheaf first.  
To outward instruments it registers as a bank of pale emerald smoke hung between the lower mesosphere and the upper troposphere; a lenticular continent bled of colour by distance. Step closer—eight hundred metres, six hundred—and the haze resolves into a hundred interlocking blades.

Each blade is a petal: twelve kilometres long, concave like a boat built for gods, rim rising in soft pleats that catch laminar sheets of jet-stream air and turn them into song. They are not leaf-tissue any more, not in the strict dendrology of the planetary surface. They are epiphyll, transmuted: cellulose re-woven as a transparent bronze suberin laced with silicaceous rod-cells, so rigid a razor will shave a wafer thin as moonlight without scoring it. From above the surface looks like verdigris snow packed under glass. Below, the underside is a cave roof of matte obsidian venting rosemary-scented vapour that trails away in coils of frost.

Nothing roots in dirt. Everything grows into air.

The inhabitants—if that word is even latched to the proper syntax of life—are four.

1. The bee-things, though “bee” simplifies them scandalously. Each is a hex-crystal the size and shape of a child’s head, translucent gold where internal organs swirl like slow embers. Wings are fans of exfoliated aerographene: four petaloids that can flick shut into turbine blades, letting them ride the thermal spindles of the petals. They collect the amber nectar-discs exuded by the moss veldt, sculpt them into origamic cradles, seal an egg inside, then carry the package to an alcove on the petal’s underside that stays perennially between 3.1 °C and 3.5 °C—the perfect hatching climate. A parent abandons the cradle at once. Its offspring will emerge as the old honey-coin disintegrates, carbohydrate scaffolding digested into feeding beaks. They are born knowing the chord-tones of the Sheaf and will spend a century never touching anything solid except the nest they themselves hatch within.

2. The laminary harp-fish. Picture white manta ghosts, four metres crown-to-tail, translucent at the edges until internal flocks of photobiomes begin to glow in cerulean fire. They do not swim through water; they swim through light-pressure itself, angling wingtips so that photons buffet them along like air-ships on solar sails. Their undersides unfold fractal palps armed with rhodopsin petals that harvest the vegetal fluorescence of the epidermis; from it they synthesise ATP in ruby ripples, never descending to graze. Nobody has seen one eat a bee; perhaps the scale mismatch of motives prevents it. Perhaps taste is incompatible across kingdoms of medium. The harp-fish echo-locate with pulses of infrasound that cause the petal-carbide ribs to chime in sympathetic E-flat major thirds. Each chord is reproducible a week later, a month, decades: the fish memorise the landscape in sonar and return to lap from identical photonic patches, grandfather’s map overlaid on granddaughter’s drift.

3. The dew-key collars. You can fit a human neck through them; no neck yet has. They are annular fungi the colour of polished opal, one centimetre wide, which grow along the petal ridges exactly where minor pressure nodes co-occur with noon light. They appear overnight, tighten into hoops two centimetres across by dusk, and then dissolve into lace of dry spore before dawn. The spores are magnetised; they align with the planetary magnetosphere each sunrise and fall as luminous fringe upon the veldt below, vanishing within hours but leaving new collars fifty kilometres east the following evening. Nobody knows why they move with such compulsive fidelity; some infer the plant-body itself is rotating the collars by differential charge, using them as slow-motion trepan to keep its own arteries unclogged.

4. The reprise-myes: mites the size of commas, colorless until moonlight hits them, then suddenly they bloom helical rainbows a trillion at a time. They are parasites only by coincidence. Their whole life is spent migrating across the raised stomata, scraping oxalates and breathing minuscule planktonic pollen. They have been observed committing mass synchronised pirouettes seconds before a delamination crack propagates, as if the continent is whispering its imminent yawn.

Beyond them, nothing.  
No predators, no digestive partnerships, no chorus of life in the ordinary overlay. Instead there is an ecology of latency: an architecture making space for possible futures without sediment to prove the past.

In the high altitude night the petals slowly curve like a hand folding. Condensate sheet-glass of hoarfrost drops moment by sustained moment from the undersides, each flake a record of the particulate wind that brought nutrients up from oceanic troposphere. When the flakes land—two days later on sulphuric cloud deck eight kilometres beneath— they nominate themselves snow, but that is only category, only ceremony.

Visitors that come sense the living metric without needing eyes. The air is thickly sweet with terpenoids that smell of bruised basil and petrichor primed by distant lightning one will never see. Gravity is laxer here: your bones grow taller but your ears fill with the near-silence of liquids redistributing. The temperature holds within three Celsius points for centuries—thermal mass of ten quadrillion litres of sap will not be hurried.

I walk among the petal-frame ribs where cells have become brickwork and wind has become mortar. Step ticks like a geiger counter through my boot soles; the plant hears me and differentially warms the patch to shield it from cryogenic burn. The humus occurs not as ground but as ropy braid of surface fibres looping between blade-edges in shallows that reflect sunset as perfectly as still water. I kneel, touch one. The moment my fingertip meets moss a note sounds—middle C slows to D—and the entire lattice shivers along its length like a cat awakening along one continuous spine. Forty kilometres east, bees swerve in cloud formation as they database adjusting geography.

I think about bones. Under anoxic pressure at four thousand metres my lungs bruise into quiet panic. The laminary harp-fish flicker overhead, slit eyes—if eyes they are—regarding starlings turned to angels. Each one leans into uncoupled Doppler of gravity and sighs. One drifts down fifteen metres, unfurls a luminous caudal fringe, circles once, then ascends again without having emptied any of the space it disturbed. The motion is art, not utility.

Etiquette instructs me: tread lightly on a substrate made from itself alone. Describe who dwells here. They do not exactly dwell. They undergo a residency of vectors. Bee-larva lodged in amber hatch into exponential parabola of nectar-foraging. Mites poised on stomata listen for chemical thriller scores of impending rupture. Mantae remember ancestors’ music maps though no ancestor ever lived in what we quaintly disseminate as time.

I imagine myself dying here. Lungs distend like frost-blown paper; heart plays off-key resonance against the rib-osmotic hum. Body would not fall the eight kilometres to crimson desert. Instead it would desiccate in two nights, resinify, warm gold under high UV until bees come to collect. My re-dreamed husk might become mosaic within translucent cradle for a single larva clad in honey letters spelling my name at an insufficient scale. In drifted slow ballet my thumbbone might wind around in gleam threads spanning twenty seven cities of green bronze surface, indistinguishable from the architecture of the place itself.

And tomorrow the great petals will incline farther south by 0.0003 degrees, setting to hum a new modal chord the planet has with itself for the next thousand summers. Nobody stays. Nothing precisely stays. Yet everything loiters in this open ruin of cloud leaves, breathing freshwater tears that never touch the planet, sleeping in micro-diurnals measured in sulphur-dust dustings and slow hesitations of planetary rotation.
