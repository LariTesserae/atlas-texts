They are the Rafters, the people who were born beneath the floor of the world.

A Rafter’s first breath is taken upside-down; the midwife steadies the infant while blood drains the wrong way out of the umbilical. Because of this, their hearts are offset two finger-widths to the left of where atlas makers expect. A visiting anatomist once drew them as mirror humans and was fined for counterfeiting reality; he left town minus one reflection and plus one kidney he hadn’t arrived with.

On naming day—celebrated at whatever hour the air-river rolls past the home tower—each child is suspended by the ankle from a brass hook bolted to the masonry. They hang until a drifting trout brushes their cheek; the fish’s first scale to come loose becomes their syllable of anchor. A copper scale gives you a name like Klim or Serr. A scale of silver, rarely caught, grants a name that whistles when spoken: the child becomes Axhh, a vowel of wind. A scale of rust is refused; the baby is lowered immediately, wrapped in indigo, and told to try again tomorrow. Three rust scales in a row and the infant is renamed “Probability,” issued a government coat stitched from leftover dusk, and employed as living ballast on the maintenance crews. Probabilities never speak, but if you walk behind one you hear the canyon arguing with itself in their wake.

Adult Rafters move with the patience of people who trust the ground to betray them. They stride the catwalks boot-first, soles clacking like typewriter keys. The rhythm is etiquette: left-click-pause-right-click means “I see you, no harm.” Double-left-left-right is a warning of loose railing ahead. Anything faster is poetry or insult, depending how the listener’s pockets weigh. Because their inner ears have recalibrated, a Rafter can pirouette on a bar the width of a broom handle over a mile of black air, but place them on ordinary soil and they fall immediately—too much sky pressing up bruises their sense of up. They vacation by lying belly-down on the balconies, dangling arms into the abyss so blood can pool where memory should be.

Work gangs are named for the sound their tools make. The Clangour handle iron, retrofitting balconies before hematite reclaims them. The Hushpaws harvest silence bricks, gliding in felt slippers so the Works never notice the theft. The Chaw-crew speak only in consonants, lubricating gear teeth with expectorated phonemes; after shift they cough up vowels and sell them to poets who paint the sky with biodegradable graffiti. Every fifth year the crews exchange members by lottery, so no citizen remembers how their original gang sounded. This is considered social hygiene.

Children apprentice early. At six they’re given a spool of carbon rope and one bat wing, already glassy and dead. They must sew the wing to the rope before the fibers delaminate. Success earns the right to fish for trout shadows: you cast the line into the air-river until something tugs backward, then haul. The shadows arrive oily and two-dimensional, flapping like pennants. Rendered over a low probability fire, they condense into edible night—black caviar that tastes of starless sleep. Fail the sewing test and your rope is cut into shoelaces; you join the groundless, running messages along catwalks forever, never allowed to look up or down, only sideways.

Rafters keep two households. The first is their “up-home,” a rented shelf bolted to the tower underside: cot, cooker, a cistern that drips yesterday’s weather. The second is the “down-home,” a private volume of air tethered by piano wire and habit. They speak to it, warming the cavity with breath until condensation pearls; later they drink those memories as water. A down-home can be willed to heirs but rarely survives probate; usually it collapses into fog and seeps into canyon accounts, increasing local rainfall by one sigh per square meter. Architects broker new ones, pricing by buoyancy and regret index.

Courtship is vertical. A suitor lowers a bucket on a silk thread; inside lies a single gear tooth engraved with a riddle. If the recipient recognizes the tooth’s original machine—often their own heart—they bite down, returning the gear upthread. Engagement lasts until the tooth wears smooth from nightly gnawing. Marriages are celebrated on the maintenance cables: bride and groom clipped to opposite sides of the same wire, leaning out until their combined center of gravity settles between. They kiss across the braid, exchanging blood that momentarily forgets which body it belongs to. Divorce requires cutting the strand; whoever plummets acquires the other’s nightmares. The winner remarries immediately, weighted by ghosts of ex-inlaws; the loser's memories are fished out of the abyss by the bear-things and sold back as souvenirs.

Religion orbits the concept of Later. Their single deity is called Eventually, an presence believed to be arriving just after the instant you stop waiting. Icons depict an empty coat holding a lantern of horizon. Services are held in echo: worshippers stand at the gorge lip and shout a sentence into the dark, then leave before the echo returns. Whoever stays hears the echo answer a question they hadn’t asked; these unfortunates become clergy, titled Echokeepers, doomed to collect unanswered sounds. They spend life stuffing echoes into sacks woven from the city’s obsolete rules. When the sack ruptures, a thunderclap rebirths the canyon by a millimeter; maintenance crews invoice the clergy for the expansion.

Diet is mainly vertical produce: downward-growing beans whose roots seek sky, albino cabbage that forms spherical voids you can butter and eat like bread. Meat comes from the pale bears. Licensed yawners are sung lullabies in Chaw-crew consonants until drowsy; at the yawn’s apex a marksman fires a silence brick down the gullet. The bear freezes mid-roar, digesting nothingness until it shrinks into a wallet-sized cube of tender protein. Hunters pry the cube free, leaving the bear to reflate on next season’s condensation. Vegetarians exist: they grow their shadows in boxes, trimming them nightly like bonsai, sauté with river photons. The dish tastes of guilt and iron; they wear extra pockets to weigh down buoyant conscience.

Old age is diagnosed when footprints begin hovering one millimeter above the catwalk. The elderly are fitted with soft iron anklets filled with donated memories—usually secondhand childhoods purchased from departing immigrants. The ballast keeps their soles grounded, but each borrowed memory leeches colour from their eyes until irises bleach the shade of canyon twilight. When the last pigment drains, the citizen becomes a Seeing: a walking viewpoint who can spot which reality bricks are flawed before they set. Seeings inspect freshly poured probability, tapping with white canes that ring like tuning forks. A rejected brick—one that would have birthed cancer or composed an awaltz in f minor—gets shattered and sprinkled onto the hydroponic balconies. Plants absorb the shard-music; ghost melons learn to taste of minor chords, a flavour prized at funerals.

Death is negotiated. The dying lease their remaining weight to Works accounting; mass is money here, and a corpse is pure ballast. In exchange they stipulate orientation: some choose to fall forever, others to rise until they become constellations of maintenance lights. The contract is signed on the donation tower saddle; the guillotine of forgetting takes only memories unwanted by kin. Whatever is left—an eyecolour, a talent for minor chords—ascends or descends accordingly. Survivors plant a perforated lantern on the trajectory; its beam is really just a question posed to Eventually: “Will we meet again, or is this the echo I must keep?”

Outsiders sometimes ask: what do Rafters want? The answer depends which pocket they carry. Those with heavy pockets wish to lighten, so they trade metal for music, guilt for geese that migrate in perfect straight lines across the canyon fog. The light-pocketed crave ballast; they’ll swap laughter for wrought iron, recite their grandmothers into iron filings, pack boots with imported soil so tomorrow might feel firm. Everyone agrees on one desire: to remain long enough to see the canyon decide whether it is wound or womb. Until then they walk upside-down, inventing gravity with every step, manufacturing the sky they cannot see, storing daylight in the hinge of a yawn, handing tomorrow to whoever leans furthest out on the wire between one breath and the next.
