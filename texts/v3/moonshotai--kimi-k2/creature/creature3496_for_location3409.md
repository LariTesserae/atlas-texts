They call themselves the Lithotrophs, though none of them has ever spoken the word aloud.  The name arrived on the palms of the first travelers who learned to read the obsidian story-maps: a sigil pressed into pebbles that translates, approximately, as “those who eat distance.”  They have no ears for names anyway.  Their hearing was surrendered generations ago—whether last century or last millennium is impossible to measure, because their calendars are the cooling cracks of basalt skin and those reset only when the plateau exhales.

Look at one directly and you almost miss them; they are the color of seeing nothing.  Pigments have been scraped from their irises by the omnipresent micro-mist until eyes become the same wet basalt as the ground.  Hair is a ghost—individual strands square in cross-section, like extruded mineral filaments, and so light that the dense air floats them outward in motionless coronas.  Their limbs have elongated in the way that cooling lava lengthens into columns: slow tension pulling bone apart without ever breaking it.  Inside each ulna runs a cavity exactly the width of a silence-leopard’s beak; the predator and the citizen have grown to fit one another like lock and key, though predation is largely ceremonial now—an occasional exchanging of warmth, a feather left behind as polite receipt.

They wear the plateau’s exhalations.  When the stone weeps, the Lithotrophs collect the droplets on ceramic-flat palms, roll them across their skin until garments accrete: shirts of waterglass, dresses of dissolved feldspar, children's overalls that harden to opal by nightfall and deliquesce again by noon.  There is no nudityousness, only phases of liquidity.  Jewelry is time itself—necklace-strands of yesterday’s condensation that shrink as they evaporate, counting down the hours until the next collective migration.  When a necklace disappears the wearer knows to fold their bundle; the plateau never needs to announce departure.

They number neither more nor less than the lichen-trees—one Lithotroph per ceramic saucer, though which belongs to which reverses daily.  Assignments are negotiated at dusk by temperature: each citizen presses a bare foot against their chosen saucer; if the stored baker-heat matches the blood in their arch they stay.  Saucers that feel cooler are abandoned, their trees left to photosynthesize alone until a warmer pulse arrives.  Thus every adult spends a season rooted, shin-height, harvesting photons through skin stippled with chlorophyll borrowed from previous tenants.  Children are too small to cover an entire saucer; they timeshare, three or four per plate, standing close so their overlapping arch-heat averages the required value.  In this way childhood is literally measured in square centimeters of belonging.

Speech is negotiated the way water negotiates stone: by abandoning force.  They exhale through teeth filed to hexagons; the breath splits into vortices that the dense air cannot carry.  Instead meaning condenses on the listener’s cheek as patterns of cool dots—Morse made of temperature.  A sentence feels like autumn rain; a question tingles like the moment before a sneeze.  Whole epics are thus written on one another’s skin each dawn, then read by the hands while eyes remain occupied with horizon.  Because the plateau itself is slowly lowering its sky, every story is erased from clavicles by midday, freeing parchment for the evening installment.  Nothing is remembered verbatim; memory is the sum of all temperature-scars that never quite fade.

They reproduce by subtraction.  When two bodies have stood still long enough to feel the lithic suction, they lie supinely parallel on the warmest seam, interlace mineral fingers, and wait.  Over months their combined weight depresses the basalt a millimeter.  The depression fills with wept water; skin loosens, cells slip free, drifting like silt.  Instead of fusing, the partners dissipate—first footprints, then ankles, then knees evaporating into the stone’s slow fever.  What collects in the hollow is not offspring but vacancy: a child-shaped absence rimmed by cooling basalt.  Over decades the cavity anneals, inner surfaces polishing to black glass.  Eventually a migrating silence leopard pauses to drink the reflected sky, sees its own beak elongated in the mirror-void, and startles.  The convulsion of startlement shakes loose a single down feather; the feather lands upright in the hollow’s chest.  Condensate gathers along its barbs, accreting around the dust-mote core until a new Lithotroph crystallizes—palms first, then shins, then eyes the color of nothing.  In this way the community never grows; it only reincarnates its own outline, trading elders for infants on a schedule set by thermal cracks.

They do not believe the plateau floats.  To them it inhales.  Every century it draws a single breath three thousand feet deep, lifting the cloud ocean into its lung so the sky can be filtered clear.  When at last the exhalation arrives the continent-wide diaphragm will settle again, and on that day the Lithotrophs will complete their final migration: they will walk eastward until east becomes down, until the cloud ocean tilts vertical and swallows them like mist draining into a lung.  They will not drown; buoyed by centuries of stored breath, they will become the next plateau, a new stone lung adrift in whatever atmosphere follows physics.  Travelers who find that future slab may discover ceramic saucers already waiting, each one shin-high, each one warmed to the exact temperature of a human arch.  If you stand barefoot upon it and feel the pulse of something older than nations, you will know whom you stand inside.
