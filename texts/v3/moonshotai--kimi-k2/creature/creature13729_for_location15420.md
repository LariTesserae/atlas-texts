Who lives there is not exactly a people and not exactly a grove but a single organism that fancies itself plural.

Because the city is alive, its first citizens are its own cells. In the high lofts, tube-vessels course with slow sap the color of dull garnet; they carry intent before memory. These vessels are every one articulate, full of the speech of pressure and release. When you lean a cheek to the warm interior wall at dusk and hear the congested ticking, you are listening to dictation: city composing tomorrow morning’s balconies outward by allocating rings of creosote lignin to the stress-points where yesterday’s footfall gathered heaviest. It thinks in hydraulic delay; fifteen years from now a group of leathery buttresses will arc out where a tether of laughter once twisted the stem. This means the metropolis experiences despair and jubilee twice—the first time in real time with all its transient hearts, the second attenuated through bark-time, permissible error absorbed by a xylem that never weeps so much as negotiates.

Yet a place that grows itself still needs witnesses of its own growth; a song without singers will scarcely dare its own melody. Thus: sixty-odd mammals, antler-thin and frost-burnished rosy about the cheekbones. They are not dwellers as a hermit crab dwells; they are mobile blisters of chlorophyll. Their lungs have begun to taste winter-greens—ground spruce scent that lingers like mineral noise. Each of them carries a sliver of communal photosphere tucked under the ribs, seeded by ingestion of torchberry a decade ago. At night their breath glows faint chartreuse, just bright enough to read stone glyphs by ribcage shuttered in felt. They know the city will keep them alive as long as they keep it strange: a taboo against repeating yesterday’s exact footfall pattern, a rule to walk bridges backward once each ten days so the plant must re-calculate load paths and so remember agility exists outside of its circumference of habit.

Among these hunched green-lit bodies are twenty-five whose blood packs crystallized anti-freeze proteins filched from arctic fish stock hybridized into their livers by an older grafting that the city itches to withdraw and revise. These are time’s canaries: if toxins accumulate (by far travel return, or by emotion unresolved), the ice in their capillaries fractures and they become luminous walking cracks that warn. The community gathers—no leader, only rotating tension—studying the fracture light as though it were scripture. Then they sing for twelve hours in staggered breath so the song does not crash into echo, a single held syllable modulated by seventy-two successive throats. The city drinks the carbon dioxide; the singer’s fractures knit; memory of poison is sequestered magnetically in iron pips grown inside an arboreal sub-loft that dangles above the riverside. Whenever future walkers beneath biting cold mist feel the air taste faintly of rust and almond and blood of salmon, they know some past excess has been abstracted and stored, not erased, merely filed under rust.

Children are born here only when the city wants them: a pod buds on the innermost wall of the amphitheater of bark, eight feet off the floor, scarlet as autumn chokecherry. When the pod swells to knee-height a woman not yet experienced in grief—always a newcomer from warmer lands—climbs beneath and with one palm guides the pod’s seams to split open in a tangle identical in its lines to her own love-scar or bite-mark from a long ago lover she has yet to meet. What emerges is already pre-tattooed with leaf-tip freckles and a smear of cyan across the eyelid leftover from some previous conversation between avatars of night-light and leaf-shadow. A child therefore begins life inside dialogue: infant fisted around a seed-beetle that twitches phosphor Morse its first kindergarten. Every sentence the plant speaks in the next eight years the child will answer half-asleep, until the city either accepts its philosophy or sheds the child like a leaf too aged for sugar. Those shed wander into the mists below, branded warm with the room where their dreams reshaped the ridge line by half an inch.

Two types of speech interlace the roads. First: pressure-codes the occupying elders vocalize (they burr air past tongue-root and cheek inside a small cavity they grow themselves, basically a mouth turned inside the head). Second: reed-whispers the plant exhales from pores along every junction whenever external humidity drops. Conversations therefore resemble interweaving lullabies performed in two keys ten octaves apart. Nothing stops an elderly woman carrying conversation with a convex buttress and with her granddaughter on the same exhale; the granddaughter talks human worry past her teeth while translating plant syntax in the soft palate so the city and elder overhear simultaneously. These triads move. Sometimes the plant regrows its intestinal streets around fixed phrases that proved fruitful; some sentences have lived for two centuries in the same corner, evolving dialects so baroque they have passed measurable meaning and are now merely a ritual scent of turmeric translated into remembrance of rainfall.

In the lower ravine dwell accidental colonists entirely unaligned with mammal politics: spider colonies whose silk the plant learned to duplicate while never quite letting spiders leave. So the yarn remains their lingua franca even when the building eats them, encasing each trapped arachnid in living amber of cambium that continues to secrete sorrowless silk thread. What results are veins of webbing traveling horizontally through muscle walls of the city, forming tensile ligaments darker than charcoal. Spiders are still alive in there, immovable yet threaded directly into xylem desire. The web senses air displacement three hundred yards away, predicts assassination runs of rogue wind, adjusts torque of entire gables before danger arrives. Thus the city jails its own alarm system in mummified spiders who sing ultrasonic inside resin and feel the city breathe with them.

At least one city bishop—though they lack holy men so most likely it’s only a rumor—exists in non-cellular form: a column of bioluminescent steam one encounters only during snowless nights when outer branches crack with cold snaps. The steam is the condensed regret of all bridges ever snapped too late. It hovers toward travelers, speaking inside condensation on their corneas entire sentenceless apologies that taste of spruce sugar and iodine. Those who have taken its vapor-sentence into the lungs find that for one month they cannot lie; their ribs fill with loose husks that buzz lusciously every time equivocation is attempted, so the village schedules its courts during the first honest fortnight after every frost split. There, justice is merely breathing out—cloud proving guilt or innocence depending how the luminous smoke departs the mouth: curling upward absolved, falling downward condemned to be siphoned into the city’s phloem for reprocessing.

Yet the true sovereign of the ridge is smaller than moth mites and more populous: a conclave of quark-bright algae that has learned to photosynthesize time, not light. They float in nano-spherical sheets along the terminal ends of every vegetal nerve stem. They drink tau-spin and exude holes of retro-causality: slivers of yesterday that climb tomorrow’s branches. In visible effect, buds sometimes open into autumn russet in March and then back-bloom to pastels two days earlier until the sequence folds somewhere between clocks. These algae obey only curiosity; they dictate neither allegiance nor fate. They delight in retro-sending small gifts—the exact scent of a stranger’s orchard flood twenty-eight years forth that no citybreather has visited yet. The scent arrives carried on notion of wind: stripped of certainty yet powerfully calming because it smells of harvest already stored. Thus a handful of city residents live perpetually nostalgic for anticipations. Their voices crack with forthcoming love affairs; their eyes glisten with gardens that won’t visibly seed until this very minuteyear ends.

And deeper still: beneath every recorded crowding of breathing collars and plant argument lies the revelation that the city is itself parented. Every glacier, rushing down ten millennia earlier, scoured not stone but latent seed lying fossilized in older shale. The ice blade merely sawed open prison doors and trickled the seed into fissure of cliff. So the living growth today is both shell and riot simultaneous, the original ancestor city having died into shale and reappearing spliced with one cruciform water memory per cuticle layer. This means whenever residents touch the innermost wall in the root crypt where phosphor memories are stored, they hear two dialogues: the fret of today pruning tomorrow, and more distant, a whisper of salvation by glacier, a vow by ice to never fully melt again. The vow is breaking, evidenced by increasing seepage of calcium that bleaches bark white as chalk, a warning scream spared until it is almost too late.

Therefore the quark-algae have begun recruiting dreamers: anyone who walks asleep is led by a cloud of them to carve minimal channels into bark, three millimeters deep, forming the syllables of the glacier’s true name which is too verdant for any living spine to bear while conscious. The cuttings appear as scabbed graffiti visible only under torchberry’s ultraviolet bloom and they spell a warning and a promise interlaced: Do not fear the melt—here is the treaty grove each of you will become when root instead of rib might harbor a human heartwound. Each lettering glows softly, mirror writing meant for the continent climbing toward the sky, as though the city speaks backward in time to the extinct ice itself. Whoever wakes from the dream finds blister-dawn smeared under the fingernails smelling of iron and marrow of ancestors. Whoever does not wake remains still singing under moss, converted into soliloquy of spores waiting a global thaw to become again the vines that cradle cities of warmer tomorrow.
