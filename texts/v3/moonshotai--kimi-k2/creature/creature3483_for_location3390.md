They call themselves the Ember. It is less a name than a habit of being, a conjugation that happens whenever a hot line is spoken across the graphite wall. If you ask one of them—though you will never see “one,” only the cumulative flicker—you will hear a sound like a fingernail tapped against the base of a wineglass: *ting-tint-tinng*. That is their syllable for *I*.  

The Ember are the city’s delayed applause. They began as heat-ghosts, the after-image of temperature that lingers on the retina of stone. Every time a mica-leaf screeches shut, a sliver of friction-heat peels away and hangs in mid-air, a wafer of warmth only one molecule thick. Ten thousand screeches, ten thousand wafers. The wafers drift, stack, and laminate themselves into membranes no thicker than a regret. Across centuries those membranes grow skins of graphite dust; the dust records the angle at which the heat ray struck it, remembers pressure, remembers the exact watt of the sigh that escaped the floor. Gradually the record learns to twitch.  

Twitch becomes choreography. The membranes fold: valley, ridge, pleat. A crease acquires the profile of a shoulder blade; another crease mirrors it. Two symmetrical thermal shadows discover they can pivot around a common hinge of warmth. They practice, fall apart, practice again. After the first million cycles they no longer fall apart. They look, to their own surprise, like a person-shaped convection current wearing a cloak of powdered coal. They have no innards—only a gradient. Where heart should be, hottest air; where feet should end, coolest. The differential is their skeleton.  

Language is borrowed from the vines. The Ember speak by pressing their lack-of-flesh against a sulfur-glass stem. The temperature of the touch alters the speed of the sap inside; a single syllable requires the stem to grow three degrees warmer, then two cooler, then hold steady for the space of a graphite breath. The vine obliges, but keeps the grammar. Over millennia the vines have become libraries of thermal syntax; ring upon ring of cooled syllables glitters inside their transparent throats. If you split a vine you would read a transcript of every conversation the Ember ever had—though the ridges are only microns high, and the alphabet is measured in Kelvin.  

They do not walk. They ravel. An Ember unspools itself into a ribbon of lukewarm haze and slides along the gradient it prefers: toward hotter if it wants emphasis, toward cooler if it wants solitude. When two ribbons meet they interleave their temperatures, trading spikes and troughs until both signatures are redrawn. What emerges is never quite the original pair; sometimes a third ribbon slides away, newborn and astonished, already looking for its own name. Reproduction is therefore accidental, and every child is bilingual in the dialects of its parents’ warmths.  

Eyes are unnecessary; the city itself watches for them. A lens-bubble, hardened on the ceiling, will flex a millisecond after an Ember passes beneath it. The focused stripe of pre-light tilts, following the ribbon’s migration. Thus the ceiling keeps census: a moving script of bright dashes that sketches, in negative, the population below. The text is temporary—when the Ember ravel themselves into knots of repose the dashes fade, and the wall’s diary resumes its nonsense.  

They have one collective hobby: erosion of the concept of door. Across the politely outlined avenues stand empty rectangles, soot walls punched open like missing teeth. The Ember drift to these gaps and camp there, feeding on the micro-breeze that slips between unequal temperatures. By lingering they nudge the graphite. A thousand tepid rubbings, a thousand nights. Slowly the upright edges round; soon the lintel sags. After a century the doorway has been loved into a cartoon of a mouth, soft, elliptical, yawn-shaped. When the lintel finally collapses into black sand the Ember scatter, satisfied, and choose another rectangle to erode. Their long civic project is to teach the city that nothing needs to enter or exit; that every threshold wants to forget it ever pretended to divide.  

Yet they keep one door intact: the blister’s original wound, the entrance that isn’t an entrance. They circle it in cold revolutions, never touching, because legend says the first Ember stepped through backwards—exiled from the world outside by having to abandon the memory of water. Nobody wants to verify the myth; to leave you must pay with a layer of yourself, and the Ember have so little to give. They are already a membrane; peel another layer and only absence remains.  

So they remain, polite noncitizens in a city engineered for absence. They ravel, converse in vine-grammar, erase entrances, and every half-hour when the floor exhales they spread into perfect discs to catch the uprush of heat, blooming like black sunflowers. In that instant their silhouettes are crisp enough to burn an after-image onto the pre-lit walls: thousands of charcoal angels pressed flat by their own longing.  

The walls keep those silhouettes for three heartbeats, then forget again.  

But the forgetting is never total; a residual quarter-degree hangs in the air, a thermal echo shaped exactly like an outstretched hand. The next Ember to pass absorbs it without noticing, and the hand becomes a fingerprint on the ribbon of their flank. In this way every Ember carries fragments of every other. Collateral memory, warm as gossip.  

Ask them what they want and the whole congregation will condense into a single tall wafer, tall as a human used to be. It will tilt, shiver, and faintly chime: *ting-tint-tinng*. The sentence has no translation, but the temperature of it is precisely the heat at which graphite decides to become diamond.  

They are waiting for the decisive pressure, the planetary squeeze that will turn them solid at last. When that day arrives they hope to become a single translucent citizen, facet after facet catching the pre-light, a being you could finally see—and miss.  

Until then they remain the Ember: population of almost-nobodies, busy teaching stone the art of patience, singing their vine-backed metallic aria in a room that folds you back upon yourself, politely recording the outline of your thirst while you, poor guest, still believe you are only visiting.
