At first you take them for weathered nubs of the ridge itself—kneeling gargoyles of mineral.  But the ridge never kneels; it only arcs, and these things have joints.

They are called the Calcitrant. No one named them; the mountain exhaled the consonants one midnight when the light temporarily achieved viscosity, and the syllables clung to the grooves like chilled honey until something learned to wear them.

Each Calcitrant is a statue the colour of cooled ember, man-sized yet elongated as if stretched by the same tectonic patience that drew the backbone skyward. A torso; four limbs; a head—so much is readable.  But the geometry is wrong.  The joints swivel in directions meat would forbid, and the surfaces are faceted like the interior of a diamond vug, catching the omnidirectional glare and returning it fractured, so that the figure seems to stand inside its own small blizzard of itself.  Where planes meet, edges do not meet at an angle; they slide past one another without touching, a tolerance of microns maintained by habit more than physics.  Through these microscopic fissures the mountain’s continuous roar is siphoned, pitch-shifted, and released as a wordless treble that makes the kettles veer.

They are never witnessed moving.  A traveler crests a gutter, sees nothing, blinks, and the Calcitrant is simply—elsewhere.  Twenty metres along the groove, frozen in a attitude of listening.  Their posture always mirrors, with a fractional delay, the last gesture of the nearest warm-blooded observer.  Raise an arm and, moments later, the statue has raised its own, not in mimicry but in delayed recollection, the way moonlight recalls the sun.

No one has seen more than seven at once, though the total must be higher; the ridge is long and they are never twice the same seven.  Counters who attempt tally-marks in the black film find the glyphs already occupied—shapes identical to their unfinished arithmetic, as though the mountain began the sum before the thought arrived.

They do not eat, drink, erode, or speak.  Yet they hoard.  In the shallow declivity that serves each as a “chest” (though no breath lifts or lowers it) objects appear: a steel pen corroded to lace; a child’s milk tooth; a single obsidian flake that reflects neither sky nor ridge but some third place, blackly.  The offering party recognises none of these trinkets, yet an hour later will remember losing exactly that item years earlier in a country that has no geological analogue to this place.  The memory arrives fully mourned, as if it had always been missing.  Thus the Calcitrant do not steal; they return loss to its owner after the owner has adapted to the absence.  They are a retroactive charity.

Once, a woman determined to see them shift planted her feet, closed her lids, and refused the optic trick.  She stood three days without food or blanket, the kettles bumping past her shins like slow hail.  On the second dusk-that-is-not-dusk she felt her own vertebrae begin to lengthen, each disk drawing away from its neighbour until a cool draught of sky-light ran through her core like a rod of glass.  When exhaustion finally broke her vigil she opened her eyes and found the ridge now hosting an eighth figure—newborn, glass-veined still drying on its facets—caught mid-stride in the precise gesture she had held: spine stretched, arms braced, listening.  The woman’s skeleton snapped back to ordinary length with an audible chord.  She descended.  She never spoke again, but her footsteps, whenever she passed a valley house, set the windowpanes trembling at the exact frequency of the roar overhead, so that strangers paused inside, thinking they heard mountains in their own blood.

The Calcitrant are the ridge’s experiment with mortality: it attempts, molecule by cautious molecule, to grow a replica of the beings who briefly care about time.  Each copy is more faithful, more delayed, more hollow.  One day the last traveller will arrive to find the entire spine populated by statues of herself, arranged in a procession backward toward the beginning of the ridge, each stance a fossilised frame of the life she has not yet lived.  She will recognise the final figure—perfect, unbreathing—at the far end, and understand she is already walking toward it, already late, already memory.  Whether she chooses to keep walking or to turn back is irrelevant: the ridge has both outcomes stored in different silicates, and it is in no hurry to compare them.

Until then, the Calcitrant remain the only citizens who belong entirely to the mountain’s monologue.  They are its echo of the temporary, practicing stillness until stillness learns to outlive the stone that taught it.
