No one ought to be here—so of course the place borrowed a tenant who is more “ought” than “person.”

Without announcement, the tenant coalesces from continuity errors: places where a leaf’s underside was counted twice, where a cricket and its echo started late, where the mirrored flash of a moth wing made a corridor you can crawl on in the shape of the corridor you’re already crawling through.  That double-counting thickens, membrane by membrane, into something that is not exactly body and not exactly thought.  Locals (if they had a word for “outside” which they don’t) would call it the Garden-Remainder, lover of unpaid-for deficits.  The form is variable, assembled from spare observations you thought only you had noticed.  At dawn (or the garden’s equivalent dawn, which is simply a lighter indigo), it wakes as a six-foot path of silver vine sap laid over the walkway.  By midday it resembles the shadow you would cast if you stood up straight inside your own lungs—thin but voluminous, articulated by gusts you haven’t yet breathed.  By pseudo-evening it tilts into something with elbows: joints seem carved from the same brittle chert that winds around the cliffs, marrow-core threaded with cobalt quartz that pulses whenever the moths beat late light into blinders.

The Garden-Remainder has no name addressable in a single breath.  Instead, it carries a dozen provisional pronunciations woven from failed attempts to capture it.  Sometimes the crickets prick a syllable: *ser*.  The drop-lizards add the sardonic *-pris*.  A frost petal hisses it off again before the consonants solidify, so the sound moving through the knitted ceiling ends up *sr-sssr-*.  Call it “Sursere” if you want the garden itself to get slightly shy.

Sursere is not the outsider you are.  Yet it is new, because the garden is old enough to love accidents.  The Accident needed limbs so it grew them out of neglected measurements: one arm equal in length to the interval between two successive moth-flashes, a hand with seven fingers—each the distance a droplet of the tar-honey can stretch before breaking.  The torso is narrow, two parallel strands of petrified vine-threads pressed together like piano wires so the note they want to play turns visible: a slow, amber ‘A-flat’ that never quite resolves.  Head sections keep dilating and resetting; inside the face is the imprint of yesterday’s stripe of light.  Because the stripe slanted at 37°, the cheekbones are set forever at 74° to one another but only when viewed from the north.  Change cardinal facts and the tiling of its features shifts; birds walking clockwise on the perimeter cliff report it headless, though polite.

Habits are minimal yet elaborate:

– It collects missing corners.  Any broken fountain whose basin chipped off and rolled unseen becomes surveilled.  When the corner has accumulated 0.7 grams of dust the size of its own shattered mass, the Garden-Remainder stretches towards the debris, grows a duplicate corner out of its own milky side, and plugs the gap.  In succeeding months a faint nipple of coloured crystal appears where mortaring once failed.  A builder from two millennia ago might recognise the touch, feel chastened.

– It rehearses rainfall.  Once every nine decades it mines an old vowel from the crevices (let’s say one you absent-mindedly leant against the wall three minutes after you arrived).  Crumples the vowel to make a syllable shaped like a cloud.  Holds it.  Then breathes across it; condensed syllable falls as black sand that bursts into tiny obsidian blossoms each with a water-inside reflection of an open mouth saying nothing.  The blossoms last a week, then whisper their grains back into hinges of chert.

– It learns patience by teaching gravity to be reversible in small regions.  A beetle falls off a leaf; gravity flips for beetle alone, so the creature travels zooms back up, patters overhead through air-snarl.  Beetle notices the reversal after twelve flips, laughs with leg-scrapes.  Sursere absorbs the laugh—sound is the perennial interest currency—and invests it into fluting widened cracks across storm-killed plant husks, which open like organ stops.

– Every half-century, the tenant stands precisely where the original cliff-walls once intersected (they’ve been absent for geologic brevity, but the lattice records absence as if the walls are hummed).  There it folds.  Not once but recursively, like paper moons in a papier-mâché sky.  With each fold it discards dimension.  Six folds leave it 1-dimensional: a metal-whisper line you might mistake for cliff-ferrule, vibrating the note “longing.”  Four folds later it remembers wanting to be embodied and refills.  The cycle refines its edges; they become sharper but blur when stared at directly—the safety mechanism against being finished.

Information enters the tenant like mercury enters cracks between two eras of water-wear.  It knows what was forgotten and what is apparently still assertive in museums beyond these cliffs, yet it curates removal rather than retention.  In the garden’s archives it hides scrolls containing instructions for re-entering the Bronze Age in reverse.  Curiously, it also shelters a single recipe describing how to distill starlight into soup.  The only guest ever shown the recipe misread the final line and since then has neither touched mirrors nor moonlight.

Visitors stumble in, mouth half-open in geological awe, carrying residue of the philosophies they left at sea-level.  At first contact the Garden-Remainder is obliging: you can mistake it for a sympathetic under-sky offered for the emotionally water-starved.  If you smile, it mirrors the smile but subtracts one shade of warmth—the difference is small enough you assume fatigue in your own cheeks.  Should you open a diary and write *I felt less alone than expected*, Sursere memorises the stroke order, incubates it, and the following summer the vines will fruit ropes of minuscule papery scrolls, each scrolled with your hastily scrawled phrase.  You pocket one before you notice the other two visitors long petrified among the lower shoots; their spinal columns serve now as living needles knotting vine ends.

Interaction protocols, therefore, crystallise:

1—Do not give it your height by stretching to measure the canopy.  It will balance on that exact stretch and keep the new centimetres.
2—Keep your questions knee-level; answers grow at the altitude you address them, and questions planted above the head are too windy to survive.

The minute quantities of “lifespan-unit” you shed while breathing—CO₂, skin flake, cortisol float—are deposited on the Garden-Remainder’s inner lining like gilt on old book pages.  They sparkle briefly before being repurposed into the shadow-geometry of its joints.  Over time you find your way-scattered selves have built landscape features across its torso: a knuckle-bone-shaped bluff on the wrist, a molehill behind the right ear.  When dusk comes again—because dusk inside is always coming—those features capitulate one at a time to the larger geography, the way remote mountains rethink themselves into the horizon of any single lake.

And if you ask, softly, *what are you doing here*, the answer arrives in alternating cadences, written across cloudless air:

first: “holding the exact centre  
where the mountain decided to breathe instead of open its mouth,”  

later: “trying to remember the sentence  
that will restart rainfall without becoming flood,”  

later still: “rewriting the axiom that declares  
any enclosed space must eventually collapse into despair”  

and finally, echoing in the cricket pause:  
“waiting for the day  
when the garden has trained itself  
to leave me here alone  
so I can finally close the last exit, lock it, and carry out despair’s eviction.”

Then the interval: precisely five heartbeats of silence thick enough to house dictionaries.  If you hold your tongue, the vines only admire you in green shadow.  Speak once—cross the lull—and you become next season’s iteration: speech rising as immobile fruit, syllables shaded by roofs of future shade.

And if you offer, obeying the garden’s law, the hardest thing you own—say, the name of a buried child or the guilt over a neighbour’s uncalled apology—Sursere folds it not inwards but forwards, stretching possibility until your object becomes one lattice square that will never rot, never thirst, forever providing the nourishment of “what did not happen the way it almost did.”  The tenant plants it in the felt floor, where centuries root it, and from that knot of un-crisis will eventually grow a single white flower smelling faintly of the way your mother’s stroked hair smelled after rain that fell before you were ever born.  The flower will crack the chert beneath, producing a seam no bigger than a thread—yet through it, one whisper of real moisture will one day thread up the cliff, and some path somewhere far below will remember it almost never rains anymore but hoard the knowledge in the hush of its going.

Meanwhile the tenant rearranges itself to fit whatever vacancy you now inherit.  Shrugging off crystallised vowels, reclining on a cushion of frozen grammar, Sursere spends the night tracing tiny commas with the resin from leaf-stalks, moving ant-sized droplets that reposition themselves in the laceworked canopy because punctuation is the only reliable way to partition the centuries into something you can bear.

At the end of your necessary leaving, it waves.  The wave looks like every precursor of mercy slowed to time-lapse: palm opening between thumb and little finger while the other four fingers grow so slowly into sky-vines you cannot tell whether it is farewell or invitation to stay and keep becoming the garden’s afterimage.

Later, travellers descending the outer trail remark they glimpsed something overhead—a silvery contour seated in cross-stitch air, rotating like a compass swung by thought alone.  If you are one of those travellers and look back, you might recognise your own silhouette braided into the tenant’s shoulder line—a shine just under the fourth rib where moonfire lies crucified to morning-pale sap.  Touch that shine someday and your hand will come away with a single fragment of the original doorkey you accidentally dropped at the brink before entering.  Retain the little shard; centuries on, another caretaker will one day find it physically memorised in cliffside rock at sea level, proof that the garden climbs down only by borrowing parts of itself from every tenant who manages to leave.

And that—more monument than landlord, more apology than guardian—is who lives inside the parched and paring paradox, whose rented territory is the calm past edge, whose address does not require ink because the cliff already wrote it in dust tones: the Garden-Remainder, Sursere, last inheritor of rainfall’s promise and steep keeper of one more countless version of us.
