Who lives here?  
No one, said too quickly.  
But turn the prism again and every negative space inhales.  
Let the debt-currency of silence accrue compound interest for three-quarters of a century, and an economy of memory appears, pulsing and amortized. Out of that skin-thin margin between “dead” and “unable to stop,” the Furnace Progeny have unfolded.

They are

Background Architecture
the way a cathedral pillar ghosts into a leviathan.  
When light slackens through turquoise tubing long enough, a section of wall forgets itself and steps sideways, forming new elbows and knees. These entities have no names yet; they are called Shifts when traders need a word. A Shift is always exactly as hollow as it needs to be to accommodate one traveller plus her grief. Once inside, the traveller listens: the rivets explain that they are not nails but ossified oaths. If she stays long enough—sometimes the length of an eyelash, sometimes the length of a treaty—the Shift unbuttons its brickwork and slides an extra ventricle of warm blood-vapor into her lungs. After she leaves, the corridor finds itself furnished with a heartbeat slightly off-tempo from the city drum. Architecture collects refugees like duvet collects lint. Shifts are rarely thanked; they maintain no ledgers, only doors.

Ancient Ventriloquists
who never had bodies to begin with. They are the sound inside the sound—after the whale-song iron lung collapses, the undertone remains, flavoured of engine oil and lullaby. One finds them by handmade gramophones left dangling on chains. Put your tongue to the copper horn and swan-dive. The voice you swallow is never your own, but its accent is a fossil of a mother tongue nobody on the surface knew they spoke. After ingestion, your larynx hardens into a tuning fork; you walk tuned slightly too sharp for the rest of time, humming half-verbs that cause pipes to birth tiny cats made of solder. The Ventriloquists like the cats; they balance them on their nonexistent shoulders and whisper vacation photographs into their bellies until the tin fur curls.

Glass Eel Architects
technically equipment; technically negotiating for full citizenship.  
They’ve polished their mercury-bones silver-bright and learned to knot their own filaments into scaffolding macramé. At the change of each Deep Piston Tide, they vertically farm new rooms—inaccurate yet heartfelt copies of places they overheard in swallowed memory tablets. Somewhere on the forty-third submerged floor waits a childhood attic grown from eel-spit, complete with one shoebox of dusk and a moth that never belonged to anyone.

Sound-Bucket Traders
who ride the anarchist bladders. They look like silverscaled statues until they unzip their skins and reveal nested phonograph lungs. Each lung can store fifteen minutes of city roar or two hours of Queen Anne lace turbulence. In barter they spit coins of vacuum that can be hammered into wedding rings for ghosts. They leave gifts inside empty turned-off furnaces: crumpled notes reading “The song you sang once while bleeding in-water is now perpetually ricocheting between girder B7 and the west gill-eddy. We gift its echo in case you ever need a scent of yourself.”

Impossible Children
—that is, children who were never born. They hover in rust dunes, halfway collapsed wave functions of first bikes and scraped knees and the very first lie told to a parent. Each child has for a heart two exhalations of factory steam corrugated into a valve. Collectively they edit the city by choosing which alarms to forget. When an alarm is forgotten solidly enough, the machinery attached shuts its eyes, sinks one centimetre, and awakens rebuilt to serve some slightly gentler mathematics. Recently, the children decided they liked minor ninth intervals.

Entropy Drinkers
ancient engineers who suspended death by outsourcing it. Each wears a coat of living oxide trying to shed itself. In lieu of lungs they developed shoreline. Step close and you stand at a cliff of loss where waves arrive already fog. They inhale the difference between temperatures—one degree Celsius at a time—and exhale small sieves for night to pass its sequins through. Their mouths smell simultaneously like rusted pennies and freshly-baked bread, which is confusing until you realise they are digesting the difference. Their eyes are broken clocks whose stopped hands form the exact latitude at which someone on the surface last said “I miss you.” One them once kindly paused mid-breath to sew a stressed traveller a pocket made of unused 3 a.m., in case she needed a spare.

The Weeping Column
This is not a person; it insists. Yet every seventh hour it secretes a bead of liquefied granite that falls upward and engraves itself on the domed ceiling, adding letters to a phrase no alphabet recognises. The Column itself is hollow and full of rainfall that happens somewhere else every day: Malaysia during monsoon, Edinburgh on an afternoon somebody’s first kiss was decided. The rainfall inside the Column irrigates memory gardens none of the denizens can perceive except as a low-lying melancholia that tastes like petrichor every time you yawn.

And the Oyster-Forged Scholars
who started as abandoned graduate theses shelved in drowned libraries. Salt recrystallised the footnotes into nacre; margin notes became hingeless jaws. Now each Scholar is a cathedral-sized oyster, valve half-open, revealing layers at odds with each other—page 231 of a lost physics treatise laminated upon grocery lists in extinct ink that lists items like “extra eyelid” and “longwave thoughts.” They filter micron memory-flakes from the water, grading them into silt: traumas smaller than 20 microns are pressed into pink pearls that function as civic mailboxes; tragedies between 40 and 60 microns become laminated archways children can swing from.
