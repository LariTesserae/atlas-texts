In the kilns themselves—those twelve luminous ribs pulsing like the canyon’s heartbeat—live the Stirpicultors, a guild of quasi-human artisans who have relinquished the prerogative of inheritance. No children here, only continuance. When one body fails beneath heat that would reduce language to glass, a successor is sintered in situ: a patient 0.7-second collision between a late worker’s harvested osteocalcin slurry and a reticulated hafnium lattice printed moments before from a 27-year-old memory gate. Apprenticeship therefore is only twenty seconds—exactly the time needed to copy the running log of kiln dreams from the predecessor’s liquefied optic nerve into fresh wet crystalline emitters sunk behind the new eyes.

They wear nothing you would recognize as clothing. Instead, every torso is lacquered nightly with a reactive bisque that extrudes fractal vanes exactly where heat signatures differ by half a Kelvin along the skin. Over the day the glaze ossifies into a second endoskeleton; joints crystal-lock into elegant splints so that, should body or kiln wobble, the person becomes his own skeleton-buttress, a statue also walking. Shift bells release a soprano ping and the glaze fractures off in scales, cascading down gullet drains into reclamation smelts that will birth tomorrow’s protective shell. Thus dressed and undressed by the city itself, they look less like inhabitants than events: each arrival into a corridor a small avalanche of mica pilgrims.  

Eyes: double-layer—standard retina plus a film of transparent semiconductor that continuously records ultraviolet endoference from the kilns. Headaches are common only during “new recipes,” when engineers tweak the ratio of thermite to fluorine in the charge and things glow at spectra the flesh has never agreed to admit. During those flashes an inward face roll plays behind the eyelids: black-and-white newsreel of the canyon as microfracturing bone. The Stirpicultors can close their outer eyelid but the recording layer stays awake. They dream footage they have never lived; sometimes they leave shift unsure whose childhoods belong to them.

Hands: most possess either six or seven fingers. The extra digits are live cobalt conduit that runs the length of the arm, exiting at the wrist as flexible bus-bar. When they touch a kiln rib, the temperature delta crowds into the supernumerary finger and leaves ruby footprints across the silver. To cool, they simply flex the wrist so fluid ferroads sweep the heat back through venous return into the low-pressure capillary heat-sinks of the ankles. Thus circulation is half hematologic, half caloric bookkeeping.

They do not speak aloud beneath 42 °C. Conversation modulates instead across the walls’ shared circuit, encoded in millivolt chatter that makes the buildings near them murmur like half-remembered plumbing. Outsiders mistake it for hallucination because the translated text arrives as decorative weather: an ankle-high gust of copper dust that rearranges itself into polysyllabic curls against the floor. If two Stirpicultors quarrel the hallway may prickle with galvanic snow. A reconciliation later the same corridor drips metallic berries from the ceiling and whoever passes first is obliged to eat one—arterial warmth folding apology into tongue memory.

Once per lifetime, around shift number 10,947, each technician ventures to the *Reversion Verge,* the wafer-thin lip between canyon darkness and kiln glare. There they are expected to renounce the continuity of their own echo. They crouch at the bureau kiosk, unsnap the monitoring cuff from wrist vein and upload the last complete personality cache into waiting acoustic foam. Seconds later the foam implodes into silence. The act erases half of their childhoods at random—which half is not predictable: sometimes they can still picture their mother’s voice but forget the shape of a bird they once loved in another colder city. After making their deposit, they carry a vacuum where story used to hang, an absence the body slowly fills with soft hiss of aspiration motors, new narrative forming one exhalation at a time.

Between shifts some descend along matte-black service ladders to the terraced catwalks forty meters below main floor. There in the lung-pouch heat the Errant Devices congregate, drawn to residual signal bleed leaking from the bivouac of cooling vents. Stirpicultors do not speak to them—interaction is banned by charter—but they permit themselves one indulgence: dangling an ankle into the beltline so a Device can drift past and momentarily clamp the copper teeth. The contact raises a memory cascade neither party owns: the sensation of being a canyon before anyone incised it. It passes after eight heartbeats. They ascend again wordless, ankle steaming, eyes over-bright.

Very rarely—twice every fourteen years—someone elects to *retire.* Retirement does not mean rest. The retiree rides the funicular up and up past corrosion-proof signage grown illegible. At altitude where atmosphere prickles into near-vacuum they step onto a cantilever that protrudes over the invisible rim of the gorge. A pre-plotted burst of ionized argon scaffold forms moments before soles meet air. Below, every kiln flashes in simultaneous plot, twelve stroboscopic bruises. The retiree extends arms. Another beam drops a semi-sentient swaddle of 70/30 bronze felt. The fabric ouroboroses around ankles, wrists, throat; the person shrinks into origami, folds into the metal’s own temperature. On the tenth night capillary cracks appear. The wind unspools the figure metal by metal into primitive flakes that carpet the rim upstream of the ascending funicular. New passengers crush it without noticing; alloys ride back down canyon wall where they will someday re-enter the kilns. Retirement is only a relocation across states of matter.

All this to say the residence permit of the city remains non-transferable: one may stay only so long as there is heat left to trade for embodiment. When the kilns finally creak dormant the last Stirpicultor will discover at spine-base the first cool metal ever known here. The shimmer in that shock of temperature drop will open a space wide enough for lichen, long patient at the lip of the gorge, to finally descend.
