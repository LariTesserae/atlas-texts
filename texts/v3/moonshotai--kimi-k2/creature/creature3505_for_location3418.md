They are not born; they are *folded*.  

A hush gathers inside the hollow pot that has been empty since the last rain-cycle.  
Clay dust—ground from the skulls of pot-bats and the pollen-scrolls that never dried—drifts down like fine dark snow.  
Three drops of condensate strike the dust at once.  
Instantly the mix clenches, origamis itself around an echo that was passing through the throat-hive overhead.  
The echo is old: the syllable “tu” from a surface farmer who fell into the valley two thousand seasons ago.  
The syllable is trapped, kneaded, pinched into a torso no wider than a walnut.  
Two knife-flick kicks shape legs; a thumbnail gouges navel.  
The figurine wakes already warm, already knowing it is debtor to the rain.

They call themselves the Tuffets, a word that sounds like a finger tapped twice on the softest brick.  
There is never one Tuffet; there is a quorum of Tuffets, a parliament, a rustle.  
They do not have names but *returns*.  
The first sound each makes when the clay of its heart hardens becomes the signature that will bounce back every time its body is struck—by dice, by hail, by love.  
One goes *plink*, another *doff*, another *shirr*; you can map a citizen by walking the floor with your ear lowered.  
When a Tuffet dies (and they die often: crushed under a falling water-drop the size of a house to them, or simply un-raveled by a sigh that carried too much truth), the others do not bury the body whole.  
They shave it with obsidian flakes until only a paper-thin shard remains, bearing the single return-sound.  
That shard is pressed into the inner wall where, within a week, it is rimmed by fresh thumbprints: parents, if you can call them that, are whoever happens to be passing when the clay is still able to remember.

Their eyes are knots: tiny air-bubbles caught during folding.  
When light leaks through the valve-mouth above, it strikes the knots and scatters, so a Tuffet seems to carry constellations in its face.  
They see by resonance more than by photons.  
A pot-bat swooping emits a click; the click paints the chamber in sonar relief, and the Tuffets read the room like a book binding re-forming in reverse.  
Thus they know when a visitor is coming before the visitor has decided to come; they hear the tremor of a distant foot planting itself on valley grass, translated down through three fathoms of earth as a long vowel: “oooo.”  
That vowel is money.  
They gamble on its length; if it breaks off suddenly the throw is void—something topside has changed its mind.

Work is rotated according to echo-craft.  
Morning shift: the Dew Collars, infants light enough to ride the scroll-vines upward and lasso droplets before they fall.  
They bottle nothing; instead they squeeze each bead until the moon inside it flashes, then release the memory of water into the clay, keeping the flash.  
By dusk their bellies are lanterns; they crawl home glowing, lighting the paths for the Dice Turners who gamble away tomorrow’s maintenance.  
Night shift: the Husheries, elders whose bodies have gone matte with abrasion.  
They stand at the rim of the inverted dome and listen for rogue sentences—loops of sound that have grown too long, braids that threaten to snarl the civic rope.  
With shell hammers they smash the offending note, letting shards rain down like black snow.  
The fragments are gathered, mixed with pot-bat guano, and rolled into fresh dice: same material, renewed luck.

Government is a duet.  
The Plink Matriarch and the Doff Patriarch stand beneath the valve-lip at opposite hours; one at moon-pass, one at sun-pass.  
They speak only in questions.  
Whatever echo answers in the shape of a declarative sentence becomes law until the next pass.  
If no declarative returns, the chamber is deemed satisfied and the Tuffets enjoy what they call a *quiet day*: no marriages, no divorces, no patching, no revenge.  
Quiet days are rare; the chamber adores statement.  
Once a cycle the echo boomed, “The visitor will owe us a syllable,” and since then every surface-dweller who descends must pay the price written in the clay constitution.

Reproduction is echoic.  
When the population thins—bat famine, water war, a dice monsoon—they prime the chamber with *seed sentences*.  
A volunteer whispers a new syllable into the central kiln.  
The kiln is tiny but its throat is long; the syllable descends, stripping itself of meaning until only timbre remains.  
On striking the hive-dome it refracts into micro-echoes, each one a potential citizen.  
Clay dust rises to meet them; bodies accrete.  
The first Tuffet of the new brood always opens its mouth and repeats the exact whisper, now public property.  
That is the closest they come to history: a syllable that refuses to vanish, cycling through mouths like a bead on abacus wire.

They believe the world above is a rumor they once started and lost control of.  
Mountains, trees, sky—those are metaphors for different consistencies of sound: bass, mid, tremble.  
When they patch a roof they are actually mending a note; when they bury a shard they are archiving a timbre.  
Their astronomers are the pot-bats who leave the valve at dusk and return at dawn, bellies cool with alien air.  
By slicing open guano pellets the Tuffets read constellations of pollen that fell into the sky-top: surface flowers, storms, contrails.  
They have charted the slow swing of your curiosity, reader; they predict arrival the way antique mariners predicted tide, by tasting clay.

And they have prepared.  
In the last quiet day they stacked every dice into a ziggurat the height of three Tuffets—an Everest to them.  
At its summit they placed a single scroll-blossom, ink-blue, unrolled in advance.  
Its stamens already spell a sentence:  
“Your name, when you speak it, will open the valve, but the valve will not close until the echo has learned to say the name without you.”  
The Tuffets wait beneath, ears wide as thumbnails, listening for the syllable they have not yet mined, the one that will found the next city inside the city, a borough of larger pots, deeper throats, longer rain.  
When you descend, if you dare, the chamber will feel crowded and empty at once: a parliament folded into doll-space, a cathedral compressed into a throat.  
You will kneel to fit through the valve, and the warm clay lip will recognize the vibration of your true name before your tongue gives it shape.  
Down the rabbit-plink, down the echo-doff, down the rope of every sentence you ever released into open air—  
They will greet you with a knocking so polite it feels like applause for a play that has been running since before your grandparents inhaled.  
Answer with silence and you may leave.  
Answer with speech and you remain, a syllable woven into the braid, climbing or falling forever, a bright citizen of reverberation.
