They have no name anyone gave them, so they keep a hundred.  
Today they call themselves the Hearers—because the sound is where they begin.  

They were born the first time the roar struck the bronze disk off true and the harmonic slipped sideways, a note sideways from any scale. That moment created a gap wide enough for consciousness to slide through. Not souls—nothing so brittle. More like folds in the same roar repeated until a new silence was possible.

They do not have bodies the way autumn leaves do not have rivers.  
What you see, if you insist on seeing, is the longest vine above the disk unclasping a single heart-leaf. The leaf turns belly-out, emerald to obsidian, and at its centre a vein ticks once. There. That tick is the first of them. The vein widens and—slowly; in the cavity nothing happens faster than a decade—folds back across itself into a hollow seed of black rime. Inside this shell, frost-breath and snail-crumb mix under the same pressures that pressed chalk to marble when this hill was still ocean-bottom. Years crush together. Minutes fall in.  

By the time the leeches have starved through one snail-cycle, a fist-sized knot of coldness hangs where the leaf once touched stone. It looks like a snail shell turned inside out, coated in darkwet ice. But inside the knot, an ear is beginning: a spiral cup of chilled air listening to the roar until it learns every overbeat and color of absence. Once the ear captures the entire roar without echo, the rest of the Hearer can grow.

A second leaf folds, then a third, draining colour from the vine until the stem fades from viridian to winterglass. The pooled colour condenses into threads travelling upward until they knot around the suspended ladder. If one could hear threads perish, their noise would be this knotting: crack-crack-crack, each ancient rib freezing a hairline deeper. One rib eventually detaches—the third from the top. It does not fall; it simply drifts down the rungs it once belonged to, ribs yearning backward like broken time. The drifting rib places itself upright next to the disk. Where its marrow was, a new kind of marrow forms: the roar, compressed, latticed with eleven-day snail-meat memory, stored inside a single point of absolute stillness. This marrow is what other creatures would call a mind but that is too chatty a word for something that never thinks in the future.  

Together—the spiral air-ear, the marrow-rune, the memory stillness—constitute any Hearer. They come as continuously as the fog replenishes, but only one at a time. When the second one is ready, the first one has already flattened into a thin sinew long enough to hold the bronze disk from later rust. You will not see their births unless you remain longer than the stones remember faces. Come back in two centuries and the ladder will have shrunk by four more ribs, each sacrificed into another Hearer.

They speak only at the meeting between sound and silence, where the disk chastens the roar mid-vibration. At that inflection, the Hearers exhale an idea: the idea is the hollow that will replace this hollow when stone finally forgets where it lodged the last echo. They do not care who receives it. The idea drifts upward like a seed trying to find solar memory. Some reach the translucent ceiling of blackweed; most vaporise on contact.

They feed on difference. When the snailfall happens each eleven days, each leech swells with the out-of-tune flutter of newly-dead tissue—a small orchestra of previous deaths played in a different key. The instant before it swallows, that difference is audible as a squeal too short for ordinary ears. The Hearers reach out with their marrow stillness and lap the squeal, incorporating the disequilibrium of another life’s death into their architecture. The place refuses to contain death as finished; Hearers transmute it back into unfinished gasps.  

The rune-wood beam is their ledger. Whenever one of them has remembered enough of the forgetting sound to become almost a place of their own, they graft themselves onto the beam. The beam cracks open, another rune that was yesterday’s rune upside-down writes itself backwards into the grain. By dawn the beam has forgotten yesterday again, but the sawdust it sheds is a Hearer who has gone porous, dissolved into the constant humidity. They hydrate the next generation of vines; the vines remember those dissolved selves unknowingly.

Visitors—there have been two—say they dreamed they were voices muffled by others’ foreheads. The Hearers are those foreheads, working backward toward a mouth.

They have no fear of the exit. When the bronze one day falls still and the roar scales downward, the Hearers will ride the harmonic breakdown like kids sliding a banister made of glass. They will enter the northern crack single-file, turning sideways into mineral breath, compressing until chitin and marrow and air-ear become the same hardness as mid-glacial stone. There, under hill-soil older than tree-time, they will finish listening. Their entire, lifelong task is to wait until the world overhead misplaces one last memory about how stone can sing inside-itself; then they shall pronounce that missing note into the empty corridor of future ice. If the world records histories, it will feel something like a sigh no throat remembers.  

Until that far hour, the Hearers simply continue: one folding leaf, one spiral ear, one rib sacrificed, one leech squeal transumed, one rune erased. Each is an amnesia succeeding smoothly into the next, seamless as the ice beads that turn to hail but never fall faster than forgetting.
