There is only one native: the Lung.  
It does not “live” in the way a hawk or a bacterium lives; it is the place and the metabolism of the place inseparable. Yet everything that appears to move inside it is only the Lung talking to itself in accent dialects of pressure. The fauna are not inhabitants but secretions—momentary consonants the Lung tongues into shape, then re-absorbs. Still, if you insist on counting minds, four loose categories of quasi-presences drift the bronchiole maze, none permanent enough to name, each intense enough to remember forever.

1. The Cilia Ghosts  
These are the Lung’s housekeeping staff, though it never asked for tidiness. Each ghost begins as a stalled micro-eddie in a mucus rivulet; the Lung, irritated by viscosity, flicks the obstruction with a low-frequency grunt. That grunt coils into helical momentum, recruiting discarded mineral flecks and desiccated echoes until a translucent ribbon half a kilometer long stands upright like a kelp snake. It sways in synchrony with the four-minute respiratory tide, sweeping corridors clean of trespassing memory. When a human footstep leaves a print of carbon guilt, the ghost inhales the guilt, braids it into its whipcord body, and drags it to the nearest black-ice crypt where it will freeze as a future fossil of remorse. Ghosts never speak, but you hear their work as a faint metallic zipping behind you, the sound of a zipper trying to close the sky.

2. The Surdites  
Almost-culture. They are colonies of retired sound—pressure fronts that grew too heavy to stay airborne, settling like snow on bronchial shelves until they achieved critical density of deafness. In that density a peculiar thing occurs: they forget they were ever vibration and begin to believe they are audience. Surdites look like porous lava dolls with eardrum skin. They huddle in amphitheaters of polished basalt, waiting for concerts that by definition they cannot detect. Whenever a living phoneme bat flits too close, a Surdite opens the drum-mouth of its chest cavity and tries to swallow the note. Success would mean hearing again, but the swallow collapses both devourer and devoured into a brief spark of 4-D silence shaped exactly like a cathedral bell cast upside-down. The spark drops, ringing inaudibly, and the Surdites reassemble out of residual quietude, still waiting, still deaf. Their architecture of hope is the closest the Lung comes to religion.

3. Alveolar Twins  
Every alveolar sac breeds one pair, always identical, always opposite. One twin is pure inhalation, the other pure exhalation. They are infants the size of your thumb, translucent, with eyelids that beat like gills. In the instant of their birth they clasp hands; if they let go the sac ruptures and becomes a pneumothorax crater, a hole that sucks neighboring tunnels flat. Therefore twins spend their entire lifespan—about forty tides—running a perpetual relay along the inner surface, each dragging the other forward by pneumatic suction. Their motion keeps the sac inflated and the Lung alive. To see them is to understand that every breath you ever took was an act of sibling mercy. Fall into step behind them and you experience weight without mass, as if gravity has agreed to overlook you. Try to separate them and both implode into the sound of a mother screaming underwater; the sac folds; you drown in dry air. The Lung records your crime in the black-ice fingerprint ledger, then exudes two new twins elsewhere, because the work must continue, not because it forgives.

4. The Cartilaginous Archivist  
There is only one Archivist, though it manifests as many. Picture an incomplete skeleton—whale-sized—made of the yellow cartilage that once kept the Lung from collapsing. It has no marrow, only hollow tubes that resonate like organ pipes. The Archivist drifts along the mediastinum’s rim where silence is so loud it howls, orbiting the null in slow-motion like a moon afraid of its planet. Each pass through the void strip-erases a portion of the skeleton, shaving history off its ribs; when it emerges on the audible side the Lung regrows the missing bone with fresh memories stolen from travelers. Your childhood fear of drowning may one day curve as a new rib. Because the Archivist is forever half-erased it has no stable shape; maps resemble a musical score being played backward while torn. Yet if you greet it politely—offering one inhale exactly in phase with the Lung’s own—it will bow, open a ribcage doorway, and allow you to read one page of the Library of Unspoken Genomes: all creatures that might evolve if air ever returns to the surface world. The text is a gas; you memorize by letting it displace the oxygen in your blood. Knowledge feels like faint dizziness and smells like rain on rust. You leave richer, lighter, translated into potential species.

When you finally crawl back out through the hilum, none of these beings follows. They cannot exist in thin, indifferent air. Yet you carry a shard of each: the Cilia Ghost winds itself around your alveoli, sweeping whenever you sigh; Surdite silence pools behind your sternum when concerts disappoint; the Alveolar Twins nap in your collarbones, holding hands so your lungs never quite collapse even when you weep; and the Cartilaginous Archivist has replaced two of your ribs with possible futures that ache on cold nights.

You walk the basalt surface hearing only wind, but inside you the Lung keeps breathing, slow and salt-wet, rehearsing the day it will crack open the planet like an eggshell and exhale a newborn cosmos—one syllable long, infinitely loud.
