They never discovered how they arrived, only that they never aged past the useful moment of their bodies. If you could trace the genealogy behind each tan-grey iris, you would find the pupils are older than the lichen histories; they remember the first centipede’s molting with the same alertness as yesterday’s wind-rowed lake. They are not many—perhaps forty adult shoulders, twelve children wrapped in six-wing capes, three elders whose vertebrae have collapsed to nested snail-shells—yet they occupy every altitude the plateau permits. At any given hour one inhabitant may be walking the petal-lined drainage trench upside-down, another lying parallel to the moss with an ear pressed into the gentle heartbeats of the midges, a third curled inside a barrel coaxing the dreaming scorpions to donate tonight’s dust-traveler to stand guard above the gate.

They do not speak the way speech is usually tallied. Instead each family keeps a spindle-shaped knot of centipede casing. The knot is luminous only when humidity passes a precise threshold. By leaving knots in crevices, on sills, or threaded through dawn-redwood twigs, they pass weather as news. One colour of light means tomorrow’s lava rise; another the particular vowel-shape of dusk-red chosen for the upcoming song-return ceremony. Overfestivals, when knots bloom all at once like auroral warmth, the plateau debates with itself whether this silence is spacious enough for another exhalation.

Their bodies are thin but dense: the kind of material violated screw-pines make when they perish mid-growth and re-solidify into pumice analogues. Ash threads their hair; pores seep a salt that crystallises overnight into tiny dice the size of beetle eggs—each die rolls only one number depending on how far the host walked the previous day. Because the plateau resets every time the lava reconnects the horizon, migration of bones has become impossible. Thus they practice an opposite rite: when the raven-raft silently arrives at the netting stage of evening, they tie lengths of their own long hair into the lattice-work wings of the birds. In exchange each bird offers one cast feather that retains an octave curvature above middle C. The children knot these feathers through their finger joints; so the settlement owns tiny wings wherever the children reach their hands out to catch levitating bark-boats lost from the lake’s rowing canal.  

No one has ever been buried here. When an elder’s last blink arrives, the community stretches the body across the hottest newborn lava slab. If the bones rustle free of their calcium setting, they float up like wind-borne vertebrae, rearranging themselves into an armature of quills. These quills, still warm, drift to the cliff edge, attach to centipede lanterns, and become the next generation of window shutters. The heart—whatever is left—melts straight into root-fiber and emerges weeks later as a sapphire lily with a streak redolent of their personal colour-dialect. Harvest of this bloom is forbidden, but its seed-pod is harvested and ground into dye for half a century, so every curtain still carries the chromatic thumbprint of every ancestor still breathing through cloth.

The eldest, who carries the meteoric hook, is sometimes male-bodied, sometimes female, sometimes neither. The only consistent quality: two iris-rings of fractions—numbers visible only to children—that never repeat across centuries. Every seventh eruption the hook is cooled to fingernail temperature in the condensation cascade, then walked to the lip where the sky imitates mineral. There, hanging the hook on nothing visible, the eldest waits until iron hushes to glass. On the transparent needle it writes a final prescription: how many midges must migrate downslope, how many aphid barrels must relinquish their glow, how many feathers are to be donated from owllet moult. They never write down the fate of their own body; if you ask they say, “Hooks survive strikes, but the shape of the lightning forgets the line.”

They keep an archive nonetheless. But it is not carved or inked; it is tasted. Beneath the smallest circle of stone there is a square cavity filled with porous tuff soaked in centipede-lantern residue. Once a year everyone files by, places a tiny drop of blood on their fingertip to the stone. The stone absorbs the blood until the mineral register turns a shadesong; that colour, viewed by firefly underbelly glow, is the only chronicle. When the cavity is finally saturated—once every nine lifespans—it fractures into perfect dice identical to those born from their pores. The dice are shaken in a barrel of white scorpion dust; they rearrange themselves into sentences no adult can read, but from which toddlers recite verbatim the entire preceding history. When they outgrow the tongue, they forget the language; thus the archive restarts bloodless as dew.

The plateau’s largest paradox: they cannot love distance. All longing is measured downward and inward. A grown woman will stand for hours at the cliff edge, pressing her gaze backwards into the curve of her own eyeball until the sky-chart compresses to microscopy and an outburst of violet flares passes along the lichen. That flare is her love—reflected, not projected, and therefore retrievable at any time simply by lowering her face to moss level again. Matings occur only when two flares synchronise across vertical metres of lichen. Thus children are born in pairs of equal longitude, placentas crusted into lichen which later harden to stone and become cap foundations for the stone-rings. Each new child inherits one centipede wing from the dreaming scorpion dust; overnight the skeletal wing attaches to their shoulder blades like frost lace. The children practice flying until the wing ossifies, then donate it to the oldest dawn-redwood as graft cambium. Thus the trees bear photosynthetic scars shaped vaguely like child-spirits wrapped in celestial blankets.

Beneath these systems, an unspeakable tremor: none remember the ascent, only the unending adjustment of height along the same spine. One hypothesis whispered among glow barrels proposes they are not inhabitants but exiles from a lowland forest that once existed in permanent dusk. That world cracked open and threw them upward like seedlings caught in a column of centrifugal ash. But exile what? Whatever language they once spoke lies inverted in the archive cavity; it is implied rather than saved. The only markers are the dice they never throw and the meteoric hook that no longer attracts metal since it touched throat-warmth.

When dusk reassembles and the settlement sings its condensation back to the sky, you may stand outside the rings and watch every rule condense in the air: first the midges rising like punctuation in reverse, then the raven-rafts crossing the lake of wind dragging silver pears of rain, then the owllets lifting in one silent hydraulic breath—six wings folded into a Möbius ribbon—dragging behind them filaments of lamplight. Finally the centipede lanterns ascend from the cliff edge: golden skeletons spiralling outward carrying fragments of community bones. They are not looking for somewhere else. They are fortifying the pause above the plateau, stacking silence until it becomes a vault so high and so singular that even tomorrow cannot open it until today sings the password back to creation. At that tension the mountain itself exhales—one slow unsteamed breath released through moss valves—and everything, even the future, leans half a fingertip closer to what is being saved.

Then, only then, do the inhabitants close the last wooden latches (looped with yesterday’s hair and tomorrow’s skin flakes). Hearth-cold settles, bone-dice clack in their pockets, and from the barrel the dreaming scorpion colony wakes to refill their lung with dust made of the rest of us lingering outside the plateau line.

That is the moment, the always-renewed moment, when you realise the settlement is not on the mountain, but the mountain is inside them—folded coriaceous, smelling faintly of cooled iron, tasting of dew dripping off sapphire lilies, and echoing the interval between a centipede lantern losing its light and the plateau finally daring to open its eyes.
