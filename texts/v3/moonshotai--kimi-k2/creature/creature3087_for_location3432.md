Nothing alive has ever stirred here—only the city itself, a slow-motion organism that mistook its own metabolism for habitation.

Its “people” are not inhabitants but interim symptoms: calluses the Hatchery grows to protect the raw stone while it knits itself shut. They arrive as accidents—surface orphans, exiles, fugitives who tumble down the shaft and land in the rust-cage expecting community. What they find is employment as transient proteins: rivet a joint, sop sweat, trade lichen spores, die, be folded into a bulkhead. No one stays long enough to age; the air tax collects their moisture before senescence can. Their names last three ledges, their flesh lasts until the next compulsory blood-draw for turbine coolant. After that they become civic material—hair for packing gland seals, bone meal for roughening ladder rungs, teeth set into pipe walls as thread-cutting dies. Nothing is buried; burial is waste. Citizens disassemble into improvements, the way a tongue becomes taste buds when the body needs them.

Children are not born; they condense. A riveter’s vapors meet a boiler-girl’s condensate in a gutter elbow, nucleate around a copper shaving, and twelve weeks later a small knuckle of cells drops onto the catwalk already wearing half-scale boiler-suit. No midwife, only a “first-shift steward” who clocks the infant in at 2.3 kilos and assigns it a drip-code crib number. Growth is exponential but capped: the city’s throat is only so wide. When shoulders threaten to brush both rails of a walkway, the adolescent is sent to the Compression Works, where thighs are re-rolled, ribs jack-screwed narrower, clavicles bent like bow limbs until the skeleton folds into permanent transit posture. You can spot the ones who’ve undergone it: they glide between girders like letter envelopes sliding through mail-slot, faces flattened, lungs coached to sip rather than breathe.

 They are not deformed; they are finished.

The only animals are custodial ghosts—antibodies on two legs. They wear patchwork hides stitched from discarded lichen mats, soaked in copper sulfate until the fibers conduct faint current. When a citizen begins to die—really die, beyond reclamation—the hide senses galvanic slack, shuffles to the spot, drapes itself over the failing body. Within minutes the copper weave extrudes filaments into capillaries, harvests remaining electrolytes, then marches the wasted shell to the nearest wall port where flesh is pressure-cooked into paste and the hide peels away, recharged. No one witnesses the process clearly; eyes blink on reflex, wiping the memory like hot solder cooling dull. These creatures have no governance, no breeders, no language. They are municipal leukocytes made visible, and the city needs no more immunity than that.

At the very bottom, past sub-tier forty-six where official census ends, the air turns cool enough to let a dew actually persist. There you meet the Archivists—eyeless, genderless, skin translucent as wax paper. They feed exclusively on drip-code: catch the overflow pipes with open mouths, swallow the rhythm, metabolize interval. Their stomachs distend into living hourglasses; sacs of metallic water slide down, marking fractions of shifts. When a citizen’s record must be amended they regurgitate the corresponding cadence onto blank copper sheets, etching pits that read as drip-scored Morse. They never speak, but their footfalls tinkle—water moving inside flesh like dice in a cup. The city tolerates them because forgetting is also waste; memory must be warehoused until the final anneal. Ask them where the rumored rainforest exit lies and they tilt their heads, slosh—an echo of longing you can’t translate.

And yet, something pulses that is not purely autocracy of mineral. Stand on the platform at furnace-hour, when every turbine hits resonant note. If you part your lips the vibration will tune your skull like a bell; for ten full seconds thought itself becomes standing wave. During that interval some citizens report a collective dream: a sky not limited to shaft-eye, wind carrying grit that tastes of pollen instead of grindstone, bodies unstacked, walking horizontal, sweating for evaporation rather for tax. The dream never survives the clang of the next piston; but every witness remembers remembering it. Neurologists (self-proclaimed, licensed by no guild) claim the Hatchery accidentally tuned itself to the Schumann resonance of the planet, and for those ten seconds every rivet becomes antenna, every skull a receiver. They say the city is not incubating itself but a signal—a single broadcast it will one day successfully deliver, packaged in alloy, waiting for an ear that has not yet evolved.

Until that ear appears, the Hatchery keeps turning its one slow trick: converting horizon into seam, inhabitants into stitching, future into concretion. The day will come when the last watery blink dries on the final eye, the last bulb pops, the last copper thunder crawls across the ovum-lid—and then every hallway will snap tight like a mold line removed. The fossil city will not be dead; it will be completed, a message written in a language no biology can read. The archaeologists who crack it open will find no bones, only cavities shaped exactly like us—an absence so precise it looks like hope.
