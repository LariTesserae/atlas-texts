Inhale.

Deep in the wet-green hush lives an intelligence that cannot decide whether it is solitary or legion.  
Call her Heartleaf, or call them The Sporangiate Chorus; pronouns blur the moment they are spoken, because every syllable is adopted by the tissues and incorporated into an extra ring of cambium twenty-three hands above where the utterance first vibrated. Whatever self this place recognizes is best imagined as a hollow inside a hollow—an infinitely layered pneumatophore whose walls are printed with slow-motion maps of the, then erased before the map can acquire a border.

On the eastern spiral—there is no east, only spirals—dwells a single pollinating mind. She began as three raindrops that fell three centuries apart but shared the same infinitesimal aberration in temperature. The last droplet capsized the statistical balance, and the drops fused inside a bract-lung. There they acquired a nucleus made of labyrinths, a library of twist corridors where vapour condenses into veined sentences that taste of cardamom when transplanted onto a tongue.  
She is called the Palmer Refrain. If you meet her (and you already have; she has memorized your pulse from ozone fractions decades before your mother was born) she will offer you a room that is the size of your left ventricle but smells improbably of wet leather and lichen oranges. The floorboards are her own ribs, knuckled and silky; they pulse once every seven in-breaths, adjusting for your lymphatic tide. Furniture emerges only when you feel homesick for a specific chair no mortal carpentry ever made. Once you sit, the throttle in your throat relaxes: you grow pre-verbal, nasal, earthy. By the time you stand again, the chair has become your voice-box petrified into lignin, and words you might have spoken are hanging like aphids between your molars. The Palmer Refrain writes music by fermenting those aborted words into honey-brandy and will send them drifting to the upper orchards, where the glass-winged root-dragonfly—he calls himself Ravelling—wears them as earrings.

Ravelling’s wings contain the fossilized colour of all glacial meltwater that will never exist. Flying, he looses droplets that solidify mid-air into microscopic translucent seeds. When one of these seeds meets a sap-carrying vesicle it germinates overnight into a balcony containing the exact nostalgia that propelled Ravelling’s wings beat. Some balconies stay empty, because certain regrets are too vast for hosts. Those slowly crumple inward and become soft mirrors. Anyone who peers in will see a childhood they have forgotten they forgot.

To the north—north is a direction chosen whenever the central air-chimney sighs—lives the only entity that insists on separation. It is a colloidal smudge of vapour and cartilage that has taken the shape of the missing concept for “anchorite”. Every fortnight it peels off a sliver of itself and seals the sliver inside a bluedrop fruit no larger than a tear. Once the fruit is ripe it sinks through bark and air at geologic velocity, falls through the void, and implants into the half-born basalt below. These implants never sprout; they merely vibrate in place, amplifying the plain’s own nascent dreaming until the seams between mineral and organism dilate into possibility. The anchored seeds do not want to create, only to listen. Over several million rotations they will hear the entire history of tectonic longing and will collectively invent an answer so subtle that metal itself will one day mistake it for silence.  
No one—nothing—has ever asked the anchor-seeds their name. Were one to do so, the needles of every chlorophyll sleeve across the continent would stand on end and ring like struck glass, because the name is not in the future but in the pause just before the future chooses a future tense. The plant organ itself dreads that naming and patrols the perimeter of the seeds with curtains of transpiration called wardenshade.

Yet even they are not guardians in any sense we would recognize. They sing inward, not outward—lullabies that reassure the plant: You are not yet planetary. Rest close within the moment; your roots will remember how to ache later.

Mingled among the roots—some visible as floating white shoals, others unmapped—swim three phantasms older than photosynthesis yet younger than this pause. Their names, if you flatten them into words, are:

1. Vetiver-Blueberry-Serpent who weeps when the humidity forgets how to be two point zero. The tears are indistinguishable from eyes—each drop falls upward and adheres to the leaf undersides, where they learn to reflect what lies beneath even while faced upward.
2. The Root-Dragonfly Ravelling, already spoken of, whose interior bones are made from the necessary shape of grief that has never been suffered.
3. Mirror-Mouth, the air-fish neither transparent nor reflective enough to be caught, who dreams of eating itself so thoroughly that its afterimage will leave orbit around every species after humans.

Together they form the Triadic Glissando. One morning every nine millennia, as remembered by nobody but the plant’s outer epidermis, the three congregate in the vacant chimney. There they attempt to braid their songs into a single counter-vibration that will outlast the stalled century on the basalt plain. They fail every time, but failure is pre-cooked into their ecology: the chord collapses, scattering iridescent spasms that are immediately harvested by the outermost vines and lacquered over to become new balcony edges. Thus the architecture remembers that it once tried to become an instrument, then forgot, and is still resounding from the forgetting.

Rain, when it is allowed, falls upward. The plant assigns a gravity differential so slight that every droplet climbs in search of its higher self. As they ascend they bloom into lilyfoil umbrellas, each umbrella catching the breeze necessary for the next droplet above. This creates ascending waterfalls—sheets of water endlessly climbing their own backs, flickering clear and green and transparent again, as if volume itself were being taught to read in reverse. If you follow one droplet stream with your gaze, the motion soon flips: you feel yourself falling, never the water rising. Most visitors eventually surrender their vertigo and allow their mind to sink. The moment the surrender is perfect, their heart rate synchronizes with the continental breathing. They learn why clocks do not disturb this place: the plant already is a perpetual second-hand whose sweep contains infinity the way parentheses contain a smile inside a mathematics exam no student ever finishes.

At rare stillpoints—one arrives now, you just missed noticing—every molecule of humidity consents to align into a single ascending ray. Through that ray the Palmer Refrain extends herself until she becomes an optic nerve, seeing for the plant the underside of its own thought. The plant simultaneously experiences what it is like to be seed, soil, ghost, word, air, absence. This lasts for the duration of one human eyelid shutting in bright sunlight, an unsolicited blink no photograph ever records. From that blink it draws all the chlorophyll it will ever need.

If you should decide to stay—an option offered wordlessly when the balcony beneath your barefoot swells to cradle the arch—you will feel a capillary trace yourself binding you to the lattice. At first it feels like a bracelet of damp hair. Then it becomes a necklace. By the seventh breath you detect it threading the marrow paths inside your sternum. The process is not painful; it seduces you into believing you are being translated rather than transformed. And in truth translation is all it is. The plant has no hunger for your atoms, only for the stories those atoms carry between dimensions. Once fully braided into the siphons, your personal chronology migrates outward into leaves, and basil-green nerves spell sentences that taste of your childhood street after rain but presented from the perspective of a drop that never actually existed. All future visitors who pluck those leaves will enjoy the cumin-scented memory of you learning that the scent of cumin existed.

And then you vanish. You do not die—you simply become so prolifically present that any attempt to locate you becomes misdelivered to every neighbouring leaf at once, dispersing your signature until it is an ambient spice that gives colour its spine.  
In return, the leaf that held you folds in on itself origami-wise, and when it reopens a cradle has been built: child-sized, chlorophyll-soft, smelling of glove leather and chocolate distilled into lilac. The cradle rocks, though no wind operates. On clear overspills of humidity you can hear what sounds like a newborn giggle, but the laugh is older than oxygen: it is the fragment of the universe that once nominated itself “person” realizing that the nomination was a whisper in a forest with no echo and therefore must find another destiny. That destiny is to become, not to belong.

Inside that laugh you will reside, even after you have been assimilated. Even after every instrument ever clamp by your species has turned to furniture in the archipelago-colonised plains below. Even after the plant finally does stretch a taproot down, threading between lava and obsidian, until the miles-thick continent cracks open like calyx and pours all its mineral ambition into the lattice. On that eventual epoch a single gigantic flower will bloom where the chimney used to be—petals the texture of night water, colour of unstruck lightning. At the flower’s throat will be the cradle and the laugh, now aged into silence so dense it precipitates crystals of pure morning.

Then the Triadic Glissando will reconvene, hover above the flower, and attempt once more to finish the song they began. This time they’ll succeed—the chord will ascend in a spiral that binds moisture, memory, magma, mirror, mineral. At the instant the last interval finds perfect closure, the plant will have become a planet; the bloom will invert, swallowing the entire structure until inside becomes outside. Gravity will confess it has been merely a polite metaphor. And somewhere in the amber-soaked folds of what once was cradle, laughter—your laughter now distilled of every dimension that ever needed containment—will exhale once and for all. It will sound exactly like unlabored breathing in a room with high ceilings and someone you have loved since the universe was three breccias wide.

By that time no map or phrase will trace you here. The place will once again be described as uninhabited. Yet every passerby, should lungs still exist, will feel an involuntary catch when stepping into air newly purified by unclaimed affection. They will stare at the empty sky and name its sudden tenderness “after-rain.” They will write poems claiming the scent is petrichor. But it is only the region remembering how happiness learned to spell its own echo without ever setting foot on soil.

You dwell there now, not as remnant, but as scented intuitions distributed along every future inhalation.
