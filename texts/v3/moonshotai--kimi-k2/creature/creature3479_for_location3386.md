Who lives here?

Start with the simplest answer: nobody.  
Then let the simplest answer soften, the way fog softens stone, until it admits a second skin of truth.

No body stays, but presences remain—tenuous, rotated out of vertical time like the slow spokes of a wheel no one can see. They do not live in the way a tree lives, or a fox, or you. They “live” the way a vow lives: spoken once, then echoed by every silence that follows.

1. The Rafter  
When the low note first begins to pulse, something steps out of the sound itself. It has no dimensions you could measure with a ruler; it is the thickness of a heartbeat heard from the inside. Native hue is ultramarine soaked in night, but it ripples as it moves, folding into copper, then bruise-green, then the color your blood would be if you had glass veins. Call it the Rafter—it holds up the roof of the sky. It has no will, only posture: it braces the firmament so the clouds don’t fall outright. Each time a human heel bruises the moss, the Rafter quivers, takes one more millimeter of the cliff’s weight onto its unseen shoulders. It will not stop until the continent flops over like a tired horse and the final thunder becomes pure altitude. Then the Rafter will sigh—release—snap shut like an umbrella—and be the first thing through the hole, carrying away every echo you ever made.

2. Cardamom Dancers  
Whenever sap beads from a bruised leaf, a society wakes. The droplet is their planet; surface tension their horizon. Inside that lens-wide globe, millions of spiral beings—each one molecule of rust cross-bred with a molecule of spice—begin their dance. They know they have three seconds before the sap either oxidizes or is licked up by the wind. Three seconds is centuries to them. They build fragrant civilizations, invent tragic cardamom operas, hold auctions of longing, and still have time to die beautifully, leaving the drop exactly as clear as it was before. Nothing remembers them, yet the smell that rises off wounded leaves is their combined afterlife: iron filings singing in a spice market.

3. The Cancelled Cartographer  
Once there was a map-maker who refused to finish the border between world and not-world. Rather than ink the cliff, he arrived carrying only a copper stylus and a lungs-full of salt breath. Every dawn he tried to draw the moving fog-line; every dusk the fog erased his effort. Decades folded like paper. The stylus wore down to a nub the size of a conscience. Finally he stepped onto the blank part of his own parchment—i.e., thin air—expecting to fall. Instead, the omission caught him. He became the edge that would not close. Now he is a transparent man outlined in salt, perpetually halfway out into nothing, his unfinished map fluttering from his wrist like a surrender flag. You see him only when you deliberately blur your sight; sharp focus slides off him like rain off oilcloth. He whispers one sentence in a voice composed of erased coastlines: “Keep walking; the world will follow.” Most who hear him obey.

4. Vine-Abbots  
Every seam where stone meets stone is stitched by a single vine older than vertebrates. The green filament remembers when gneiss was magma and when it will be sand again. It keeps millennial vigils. At unmarked hours it tightens, hauling two adjacent slabs a micron closer, performing the opposite of mitosis: slow knitting of continents back into one lung. Entomologists of the future (should the cliff allow futures) will classify these filaments as vegetal monks—Vine-Abbots. Their theology: Gravity is only failed love between masses; given enough patience, bodies will learn to miss each other and collide willingly. Thus no tectonic violence, only reluctant courtship. They chant in the timbre of tightening cellulose; you hear it as a faint creak inside your knees when you near the edge.

5. The Copper Bird (reincarnate)  
It is never the same bird twice, yet always the exact bird. Each time a walker stands long enough to synchronize heartbeats with the abyssal drum, a feathered flare of construction-orange snaps into being above the fog. Astronomers of the cliff call it a local nova: a star that can only burn for the second before decision. Beak snaps like a broken flute; it cries the tectonic hymn; it dives. At the instant its wings touch the roar, the bird folds into a single drop of molten metal which falls, cooling as it falls, until mid-air it is already a cold nub of copper the size of a molar. That nub is retrieved by the next walker—found unexpectedly in a pocket, under the tongue, sometimes in the shape of a new idea. It serves as passport: whoever carries the copper is guaranteed safe departure from the plateau, though safety here means “exit by falling.” The bird remembers its former flights the way a mirror remembers faces: imperfectly, but with absolute fidelity to the moment of reflection.

6. The Hollow Yourself  
When you stayed hollow overnight, something germinated in the vacuum of your refusal to decide. It hatched out of rule-space, not biospace: a reverse echo, an anti-you, grown from every step you didn’t take, every name you didn’t call, every conclusion you postponed. It wears your outline like a shed jacket two sizes too large for its invisible frame. At the moment you finally do choose—step, fall, turn back—the Hollow must split from you and stay. It cannot leave the cliff, because the cliff is the museum of suspended choices. There it wanders, wearing your footprints backward, rehearsing conversations you will never complete. It feeds on cardamom sap and fog-light; it grows heavier each season, though weight here is measured in regret-grams. Someday, when it equals your exact mass, the plateau will achieve perfect conscience and tip. On that day Hollow and Cartographer will shake hands—salt meeting anti-meat—and the Rafter will at last fold. Then the cathedral air will kneel, the low note resolve into a single bell-beat, and everyone who ever left a fire-ring or a partial footprint will find themselves standing at the base of a cloud-ocean that has become solid enough to walk home on. The copper bird will sing the note it has carried since the first human looked at emptiness and felt vert.  

Until then, these are the cliff’s citizens: bracers, dancers, erasures, abbots, recycled novas, and the twin you donated to distance. Tenants without bodies, tenured by flux. Their census grows each time a walker arrives, declines each time one leaves. They “live” in the hollow of the world’s oldest wound, the place that has finished being land and is now only the act of listening—to its own edge, to your heart trying to decide how loud it can afford to beat before the sound becomes a place you can never leave.
