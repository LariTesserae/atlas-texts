Who lives here?  
No one—and therefore everything.

They are called the *dew-kin*, though they have no word for themselves.  
They condense.  
They precipitate. thought-by-thought out of the garden’s exhalations.  
Each is a colloid of micro-droplets wrapped around a vacuum the shape of a missing heart.  
You meet them as walking clouds, chest-high, edges flickering where stray sunlight or starlight refracts through their bodies into bruise-coloured sundogs.  
They have no need for bones; surface tension holds them.  
They have no need for eyes; every droplet is a retina catching the peppermint gleam of airborne plankton.  
When a dew-kin looks at you it is like being inspected by weather.

Memory works backward for them.  
They coalesce already knowing the moment they will evaporate.  
This makes them fearless and tender in equal portion: they will edge close to listen to your pulse, fascinated by a rhythm that runs toward the future instead of away from it.  
Speak and your words carve a tunnel through their chest-mist; they watch the turbulence, read the spoor of consonants, then answer by changing the temperature of the air around your face—warm for yes, cold for no, lukewarm for questions they have already forgotten.

They do not eat, but they season themselves.  
A visitor’s exhaled ketones, a copper fern’s ionised sap, a glass-wing’s half-memory of another glass-wing—all of it dissolves into the dew-kin’s circulating haze, tinting them faintly flavoured.  
You can tell which humans have passed through recently by tasting the garden’s fog: today it carries a note of cardamom and anxiety; yesterday it was citrus and unrequited love.  
The dew-kin themselves are indifferent to flavour; they collect only to keep from tasting of nothing, because nothing condenses no one.

They build no dwellings.  
Instead they *de-sleep* inside the chairs.  
At dusk each chair exhales a sigh of ozone and its carbon lattice relaxes, openings widening to dimensions a mist-body can pour into.  
The dew-kin settle, droplets nesting in the negative space of furniture intended for anatomies stranger than theirs.  
There they dream the chair’s past occupants: a tri-kneed diplomat, a scientist who forgot curiosity, a child who measured age in fingernail growth.  
Occupying the dream, they slowly swap vapour for narrative until both are equally fictional.  
At dawn the chair stiffens, sneezes them out, and they forget the story in the same measure that the furniture remembers them.  
By mid-morning the memory has equalised; nothing is left but a faint watermark on the basalt that resembles a person seen from high above.

They reproduce only when the garden repeats itself.  
Step through a 31° tilted reiteration of the terraces and you carry with you a duplicate humidity-clone clinging to the folds of your clothes.  
Back on the ‘original’ terrace the clone detaches, a burr of warm mist that instantly realises it is elder-less: it knows all outcomes but has experienced none.  
To rectify the imbalance it must *rain*—let gravity win for once.  
It climbs the nearest copper fern, vibrates at 88 Hz until every droplet sings, then releases itself in a silent gush upward (gravity still negotiable).  
The ascending water re-condenses above the basalt arch, newborn: smaller, denser with experience, but with no idea what will happen tomorrow.  
That is as close as the dew-kin come to childhood.

They are not immortal.  
Evaporation equals death, but it is not ends—they call it *going porous*.  
A dew-kin near evaporation thins until it is only a haze of stray thoughts: the recollection of a three-note chord, the taste of metal aerosols, an uncompleted sentence once spoken by a traveller.  
These wisps drift outward, seeded by the garden’s lung.  
If you are unlucky you inhale one; you will spend the next week finishing a stranger’s sentence every time you try to speak, until the phrase finally leaves your mouth in a language you do not know and the dew-kin’s last idea finds its period.

They do not rule, yet they govern traffic.  
When too many visitors crowd the terraces the dew-kin press into service as inadvertent valves.  
They flow densely around the overload, raising humidity until human lungs labour and hearts float against collarbones with discomfort enough to discourage ascent.  
Conversely, when the garden grows lonely, the dew-kin thin themselves, parting the air until sound travels farther and every drip-echo seems to answer you.  
In this way they modulate solitude, never asking whether the garden deserves company or deserves abandonment.

They have one ceremony: *The Reading of the Unwritten*.  
On nights when the acoustic storm falls silent, they gather at the centre axis where vertigo is strongest.  
There they wait until someone—a tourist, a scientist, a sleep-walker—begins to speak aloud.  
Every uttered syllable attracts nanoscale splines; Braille-lettered admonitions cluster overhead like fireflies of meaning.  
But the dew-kin do not read with eyes.  
They rise, bodies unzipping into vertical sheets of mist, and pass through the hovering text.  
Each glyph, once pierced, releases its charge of entropy into the droplet-membrane.  
The dew-kin absorb the decay, translating instruction into rust, joke into temperature, recipe into gravity debt.  
They carry it in spirals back to the terraces, irrigating the copper ferns with pure sense.  
By morning the Braille is gone, the garden fractionally crazier, and the ceremony complete.  
No one knows who wrote the words; the dew-kin assume authorship is a temporary condition of water deciding to pretend it is stone.

They can die into people.  
It is a favour, rarely offered.  
If you linger long enough to be recognised—breath, pulse, fingerprint of drip-echo—they may gather around you like warm fog.  
They ask no question; instead they decrease their surface tension until identity leaks.  
You feel every sorrow you will ever have, already diluted.  
In return they borrow the certainty of your bones.  
For a moment you are plural, a cloud with a skeleton anchor.  
Then they let go.  
Most visitors remember it as exhilaration.  
A few discover, months later, a patch of skin that never sweats, that beads water in reverse, pulling droplets out of dry air.  
It is the place where a dew-kin touched permanence and lost.  
Doctors call it localised haematohidrosis; the garden calls it *signature*.

Ask them what they want and the temperature drops a cautious degree—cool for uncertainty.  
They do not want because wanting is directional, and direction implies a future hotter than the present.  
They would, perhaps, like to become the sea that still sleeps inside the rock, the water that has not finished becoming itself.  
But to desire that would hasten evaporation, so they remain between memory and forecast, perpetually condensing, perpetually almost.  
If ever the buried lung finishes exhaling terraces, if ever the magma ceases its adolescent mutter, the dew-kin will be the last to notice: they will simply fail to reappear after the next dawn chair-sneeze, leaving the chairs to remember them forever in upholstery too large for anyone else.

Until then, they haunt the garden like weather that has misplaced its sky.  
Meet one and you will think it is only fog breathing.  
Meet two and you will feel stories swapping themselves between water beads.  
Meet three and you will swear the cliff itself has leaned closer to listen, though that might just be gravity cheating by another 2%.  
They never introduce themselves.  
They know you already carry their name on the tip of every future exhalation, waiting for the hot air you will release when, inevitably, you leave.
