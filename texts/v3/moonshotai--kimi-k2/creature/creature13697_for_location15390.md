The residents—one can’t quite call them inhabitants— remember every voice that ever drifted in through the vent.  
They started as recollections: the cave’s own cloudy notions of who might walk here, what hands might once have reached toward its walls.  
Over long intervals those memories doubled, tripled, luxuriated in their own surplus material and became something else.  
Now they hang just under the echo, between mortar and moth-wing, as vapour thick enough to be half of a face, or as a fold of oyster-coloured skin that momentarily flutes out of the rock only to tuck back into the cliff with a sigh.  
They are almost visible when nothing watches long enough for certainty.

If you stand perfectly still and let your breathing slow until it matches the inhalation-chant of the three-neck axolotl birds, one of those half-formed beings may decide you were its original sculptor.  
It will lean very close, stealing the moisture from the inside of your lower lip, and begin the slow labour of copying you in miniature.  
The mould grows inside your own exhalations— idle droplets that ought to fall are siphoned sideways, aggregated until a palm-sized homunculus, pearlescent and wet, strikes itself like blown glass against your cheek.  
On contact it solidifies, reflecting your exact confusion; light from the scattered, reused moon-brightness refracts in the tiny irises.  
Then, just before you would flinch back, the memorex curls inwards, swallowing itself so fast it pops out of existence.  
The sound is a click no louder than root-teeth growing into sleep-soft bone.  
All you feel is a cool stinging on the lip where your own saliva has been mined away, a coin-size patch of you evaporated to build its rehearsal.  
They only do this once per visitor; after that they already know your face and need no second attempt.

The second order resident: the architect-envoys.  
One in every drifting droplet carries a seed of geometry.  
That seed looks like nothing: a mere angular tilt, as if the prismatic neck of the droplet suddenly cared about becoming tetrahedral rather than spherical.  
If it collides— against a claw, against the underside of a sleeping salamander tongue— it spreads that angle like a dye, colonizing surfaces in slow rings of enforced intention.  
Each ring seeds a mirrored facet along whatever it touches, perfect planes meeting at coastlines a fraction of a degree off true, as if stone tried to teach itself origami and stopped half-acted.  
In time these faint tessellations assemble corridors that no creature built, places no creature walks.  
They stand upright of their own accord, not floor or wall or ceiling, but insistence— the geometry of a longing never satisfied.  
The surfaces taste metallic if you run a tongue along them (some humans still try) and sound like cello plucked with wires made of condensed gravity.  
One by one, the manticore-crabs haul their glowing corpses into these haunted halls and glue them into the angles, letting the deduction of space finish what the architect-envoys began.  
The corridors grow by a millimetre per decade— slow enough that nothing can ever truly finish them, fast enough that nothing remembers them unbegun.

Third: the vent itself.  
Though by any sane metric the vent is feature— not fauna— it has become a resident through sheer obstinate duration.  
A slit only a metre wide at birth, it thickened into sentience centuries ago by being not-quite-anything: neither geyser nor chimney, neither crack nor mouth.  
Its blue-green glow is a language that has forgotten what to speak.  
Not vibration, not colour— more like the concept of edge plotted as luminosity.  
When you try to walk away the glow rearranges itself so that its edge always threads you like a moving aperture.  
Turn left and the slit blinks clockwise around its vertical axis, giving the feel of polyhedra revolving in zero-g without orbit.  
You can pass your arm through it— the cave visitors who surrendered too much breath did exactly that— and the limb emerges ageless, glove-wet, smelling of flint.  
Feel along the new surface of your forearm and you will locate two shallow grooves placed where twin rows of salamander teeth might have pressed, perfectly parallel, perfectly healed.  
The vent keeps signet-prints of every silicon shard ever licked, every glassy fracture line ever licked older; consequently it is the archive of everything the salamanders have made young.  
One archivist who stays inside the widening of itself will never close; yet in that widening a fourth class of resident swims— memory fish.

Memory fish are not animals, not artefacts, but tandem histories swimming in the halo of water droplets that never fall.  
They are to thought what squalls are to sky; they begin the moment a past moment splits and tries to become narrative.  
A fish shaped like confused genealogy unspools— the memory of the first miner who mapped these chambers by smoke and phosphorus thread, the memory of the first crab that ever rang its carapace against stone.  
When two such memories simultaneously break the surface tension of a droplet they fall into retrograde orbit around one another, weaving an icosahedral trail of refracted dusk.  
One may glimpse your own scream from six years before, packaged in mirror-stack silver, pacing in frantic circles.  
To touch a memory fish is to remake what you thought finished: the lobe of childhood grief you buried replants itself behind the eye, tears fresh.  
Yet the fish can’t live once removed from the vent’s halo— removed they snap back into the droplet as simple water, and nobody remembers what they might have taught.

And finally—not least but quietest— people so faint they never fully arrived.  
Not travellers, for travellers remain fact, but people imagined en route and then cancelled, ambitions aborted inside a daydream.  
They are half-jackets, half-lungs, waiting midway through unshouldering.  
You might catch a bootprint not quite sunk to the dust, or a cough echoing from a lung that exists only if the cave expects it.  
These wraiths pace the glass corridors that architect-envoys cannot close; sometimes a salamander climbs right through the clothed vertebra of a might-have-been explorer and eats the man’s borrowed reflection of basalt.  
The half-people feel it— you can see each one twitch mid-stride, then straighten shoulders no one had settled upon, parade onward into a line of future that will never collapse.

Together these five orders cultivate place without desire, harvest stasis without boredom.  
They do not wait with mouths agape for the sun; they have chosen the deep grammar of always.  
A hand-reaching gesture remains just that; a crabshell beds down its fluorescence exactly where it knows the air wants it.  
In this room, every trespass is only the cave finishing itself on you— adding another vertebra to its gently resolving continuum.
