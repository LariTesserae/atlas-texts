They are called the *damp-born*, though none of them speak the name aloud—it would require inhaling the city’s breath, and their lungs are already full.  

Each arrives already dressed: a caul of translucent basalt thread, knitted by the moth-tailors in the instant the pod splits. The garment is a map of the city’s future fractures, glowing faint violet where stress will collect. They wear it like a second skeleton for the first seven years, until the thread learns their personal curvature of gravity and loosens. On the morning it sloughs off they stand naked in the plaza of out-tilt, arms out, letting the aberrant vector pull them uphill until the last filament snaps and they fall—slowly—back into vertical life. That drop is their only birthday; they count age sideways after it.

No damp-born owns a face yet. Features here are cultivated, not inherited. Infants leave the pod with a soft basalt mask, featureless as an unthumbed lump of clay. Over months they press it into shape with whatever touches them: mother’s collarbone, father’s singing-hammer, the belly of a drifting droplet, the edge of a vine that wants to know if they are earth or sky. By the time the mask hardens it is a diary of pressures, not genes. Cheekbones remember the weight of a caterpillar’s foot; the chin bears the fingerprint of moonlight that once got lost between altitudes. Visitors who meet them later feel an uncanny recognition—*I have touched that curve before, in a dream, in a tidepool, in the moment before deciding who to become.*

Their bones are still negotiating mineral content. Calcium is begged from the rain, but the rain also wants calcium back, so their skeletons grow inward, not outward—lattices of internal coral, weightless and resonant. A damp-born child can be carried on one palm; an adult can be lifted by the sound of their own name sung at the right pitch. When they die (which is rare, and temporary) the body does not fall. It levitates to the altitude where its personal time-zone equals zero and hovers, fossilising in mid-air. After a century the stone grows a womb around the husk; a new pod forms, and the cycle tries again. Death here is just the city learning a new way to pronounce you.

They speak by condensation. Words are grown on the inside of the teeth like pearl, then released as calibrated droplets that ascend or descend until they find a listener whose internal pressure matches. A sentence can take three days to arrive; a quarrel lasts a season, each retort drifting between courtyards that no longer occupy the same hours. Love letters are engineered to merge with the receiver’s lung-damp, becoming part of their bloodstream, so to reread you simply exhale on a cold morning and watch the memory fog your reflection. The most intimate act is to borrow someone’s hourglass and let their private clock drip through your ribs for a while; when you hand it back you are partially inside each other’s past.

Their government is a committee of postures. Every seventy-two hours the citizens gather in the plaza where gravity points outward and lie down at angles that sum the vectors. The resultant tilt is read like a compass: if the net drift aims toward the ocean-root that hasn’t yet descended, legislation is considered *ripe*; if it aims skyward, the law must be *fermented* longer. Once the angle has been recorded, everyone stands and forgets what they decided, trusting the city to remember in the way stone shifts. No minutes are kept; the record is the thermal scar left in the basalt, a hieroglyph only moths can read when they repair cracks at night.

They have no mirrors, only *echo-pools*: shallow saucers of still water suspended in mid-air by surface tension. You look sideways into one and see the back of your own head, but from the vantage of a droplet that will exist tomorrow. The image winks out if you try to touch it, and you feel the future moment when that droplet will strike someone else’s skin. Narcissism is thus a form of prophecy; to admire yourself is to eavesdrop on a stranger’s fever.

Tools are persuaded, not wielded. A citizen who needs a ladder sings to a young rib of basalt until it feels the hollow absence of height in the singer’s joints; it grows rungs shaped like the internal profile of longing. After use the ladder reabsorbs into the mesa, heavier by one wish. Eventually the entire city will sag under the accumulated ballast of unmet desires; that weight is expected to pull it down the last few fathoms until the roots taste ocean and the cycle floods upward. They call this future event *The Embrace*, and they prepare by practicing drowning in each other’s exhalations, gentle as lambs.

They eat only what volunteers. A fruit must release its stem with a sigh that matches the eater’s cardiac rhythm; if it does not sigh, it is not yet your death to taste. Meat is unheard of, but sometimes a caterpillar offers a flank of its own fountain-lattice, crystallising a steak of living salt that regenerates before the swallow is finished. To refuse such a gift is the gravest insult—you imply the donor’s metabolism is not beautiful enough to reincarnate inside you. Acceptance means you spend the next week glowing faintly with borrowed bioluminescence, a walking apology.

They have one holiday: the *Night of Unfinished Gravity*. For six hours every balcony rail is loosened so that leaning becomes flight. Citizens step out carrying small stones encoded with sins (a disobedient angle of growth, a word spoken too fast). They release the stones into the neutral buoyancy between ocean and city, where nothing decides which way to fall. If a stone ascends it has been forgiven; if it sinks the sin returns to sender, manifesting as a new drip-castle growing from their spine overnight—an external conscience you must carry until it crumbles. The city records no crimes, only topographies of regret.

They aspire to vertical orchards: forests that dive upward, trunks descending like roots into the sky, feeding on starlight the way seabottom plants feed on snow of dead plankton. The project is centuries shy of fruition; seedlings have been planted upside-down along the mesa’s underside, irrigated by moths who carry dew against gravity. When at last the first leaf breaks the ocean’s mirror-skin, the damp-born believe identity will invert—people will walk on their shadows, speak in solid instead of liquid, and the city will finally know which way is down. On that day they will dismantle their hourglasses, pour the sand into the sea, and begin measuring time by how long it takes a single drop to forget its own reflection.

Until then they continue: soft-boned, postural, provisional. They greet one another by placing a palm over the place where the other’s mask is still warm and unfinished, feeling for the pressure of tomorrow’s thumb. They exchange no names; instead they share a droplet of lung-damp, tasting the years the other has left to evaporate. The flavour is always bittersweet, like a sunrise arriving six hours late—an echo that remembers it was once a sound, a citizen who remembers they are still a city learning to breathe.
