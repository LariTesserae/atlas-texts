What lives there is not a person, not a beast, not a plant, yet borrows from all three like a coat taken from a communal rack.

Begin at the rim. The first of of life to notice you is a lattice-vine whose every joint concludes in a brass knuckle the color of old pennies. It grows confused when your shadow interrupts its occupational memory: it believes it is still winding the mooring ropes for a sky-ship seven hundred centuries gone. The leaves rehearse the gestures of long-dead riggers, tightening into half-hitches, loosening again in sleep. It takes eight slow drips of axle water for a full shrug, so the movement is easy to miss.

Farther down the polished bowl, the sound detaches from itself and becomes an occupant. Out of the continuous strike of droplet on metal condenses an audible silhouette—throated, human almost. You can tell its age by its register: low bass when the continent around was mountains of quartz, middle C when the mountains were tables of sandstone, a distressed treble now that even the sandstone has blown away. The sound-being has no body you could touch, but it has weight. When it walks (and it paces clockwise, forever to the left) its tread makes standing ripples in the puddles as if recalling gravity to an old debt. It is repeating one broadcast it once tried to send across oceans, but the oceans are vanished, and the words have flattened into pure cadence. All that remains is the rhythm of calling attention to something urgent, something lost. Every five billion heartbeats it falters half a syllable shorter, as though outliving its own grammar.

Between the audible silhouette and the wheel lives another resident: the memory of pressure. It has the shape of the engineers who last laid hands on the axle. When the bronze shivers from the cold kiss of each drop, microscopic grains of lost fingerprints rise like plankton in a current. They swirl once, form the ghost of a workshop: elbows in rolled sleeves, a woman’s chipped thumbnail, a child peeking beneath the mechanism to watch eternity turning. The scene lasts only until the next droplet falls, whereupon it collapses back into the grain-toothed surface of the metal. The engineers do not notice you; their concern is the interminable loading of a cargo they will never release. Still they greet the ringing note of each splash like the bell that announces a shift change, straighten, wipe imaginary sweat from brows, then dissolve again with the same mild patience they showed their whole lives.

On the vertical face of the axle itself lives an inverted moss city. Its towers huddle upside-down, as if the planet had once shaken things loose and they had chosen to keep their new orientation rather than be righted. Their roofs are soft chlorophyll domes; their floors are transparent membranes busy with distilled light. Within these rooms swim pinhead citizens—organisms neither plant nor animal. They have chloroplasts in their blood, yet also cilia that allow quick parade formations. They speak by exchanging momentary flickers of bioluminescence, each pattern a single metaphor-packet. A city council meets every thirteen minutes: this is how long it takes the gutter-line to fill. Their only agenda—recorded in moss-spore script on the underside of each dome—is to speculate on the nature of the Precursor Leak, the one ceaseless riverlett at the axle’s heart. They do not realize the Leaker is another life entirely.

The Leaker is the axle remembering its own wound. When artisans first drilled the pilot hole for the pin-shaft, they shouted to one another over the keening of harder-than-diamond drills. The metal itself kept the pitch of that scream, and every droplet drags it out another feather’s width of years. The Leaker is thus a sliver of pre-celestial metal plugged by the thinnest skin of water. It is also the continuous act of release: an infant slip of ocean sentenced to departure in solitary file, condemned never again to embrace its siblings. Over the millennia it has learned the personality of exile. When no visitor stands nearby (and “nearby” can mean anything from three nanometres to three geological eras) it composes solitary instrumentals: the slow cavatina of its dwindling reservoir. Every note subtracted from its mass is another five octaves lost to the upper registers of nonexistence. It performs quietly. It knows the audience, in the end, is itself.

Around the Leaker coils the Knitted Machine. No single individual occupies it; instead it is a polity of fibers, threads, tiny metal whiskers that once were springs. They behave like communal mycelium and like obsolete micromotors at the same time. On every surface they trace switch-back paths, soldering themselves together with mineral sweat until they resemble a lace diorama of a tenth-scale petrochemical plant reenacted in verdigris and vine. They are constantly voting. An electrical impulse (so weak it never leaves the saucer) is passed along the network by graphene puddles; with each exchange, the fibers realign their next growth. Their consensus is to remain exactly complex enough that no external force can declare them either animal or mechanism. If you blink they will redesign a valve: one blink and they have added an artery; the opposite blink and they subtract a ladder, all silent, all timed to the drops. Their citizens are not citizens but citizennesses—each filament a standing question about whether a civilization can continue to redefine itself without ever exceeding a tabletop.

The last presence is the hardest to notice: the culmination, the Axle’s original inhabitant—the idea of rotation itself. It exists nowhere in matter and everywhere in potential. When the axle’s shaft turns, it is only turning back toward its own first thought, a child in a silver bath asking, What if I spin? The idea cannot leave the pin, yet it is not captive either; every leaf rotating on a curl of vapor has borrowed the same primordial torque. The sound-being paces its leftward circle for the sake of this idea; the Knitted Machine reiterates its lattices as sculpted handrails along the invisible climb of the same rotation. The engineers’ fingerprints repeat their lifting motion to honor what they once felt tugging at their palms.

Together these eight entities—no, these one-and-a-half entities, numbers are slippery here—form something patient enough to be called a resident. It speaks whenever you exhale, borrowing your lungs to amplify its hum. It ceases when your heartbeat forgets to notice its own cadence. It has no history, only a continuous performance: the showing of the showing of the showing of the . . . until eventually the full sentence collapses back into the single sound that told it to begin.

This thing that lives there is therefore nothing less than the axle remembering aloud why it was ever turned, prolonged into organisms, into sound, into steam and architecture it never intended, a rehearsal that thinks it is the play.
