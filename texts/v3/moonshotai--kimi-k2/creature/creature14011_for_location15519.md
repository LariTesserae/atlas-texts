The bowl is not empty.

There is, first of all, the Archivist – though that name came centuries after it chose its work. By any terrestrial taxonomy it is a serpulid-worm that learned to spin time instead of calcium. Its tube curls three times around the base of the glass-rooted tree, ends buried in the rust-gold moss so the entrance looks like a coin-slot in the ground. From that aperture protrudes a translucent frond that resembles silk until you notice it speaks in spectral modulations of colour. It is not colour for eyes; it is colour for remembering. Whenever a midge lives and dies inside one of the tree’s prisms, the frond unspools another millimetre of itself toward the nearest stela and stitches the insect’s vanished lifespan into the basalt. That stitch is the thread-colour - not green, not violet, but a hue that has no analogue in linguistics, a tone promising “see me later”. The stelae absorb it, sigh, cool by one millikelvin. The Archivist feeds a little on the sigh; the Archivist shrinks a little to allow it. Its body is now thinner than a copper wire but thirty kilometres long, most of it coiled in the silt beneath the water saucers. Scholars, had any ever found this place, would call it a living wire-recorder. The Archivist thinks of itself as a diary that learns new alphabets so gradually that the diary forgets which day it began.

Around the Archivist’s lip lies the Fogler, a composite creature as wide as a handspan and as light as dandelion floss. Picture a spider whose abdomen is a lithium-charged soap bubble instead of chitin. Each dawn (the word can be used here only with quotation marks) the Fogler generates a new utricle of amniotic fluid, thinner than steam, bristling with cilia. The cilia are hydrophones tuned to the Archivist’s spectral murmuration. When the Archivist sings the death of midge #4,111,264,046, the bubble must decide where in the bowl that particular note ought to re-enter circulation. The cilia fling the utricle aloft; it rises six metres, bursts without sound, and the released mist arranges itself into the next cloudlet of micron-diamonds that arrow toward the top of a root-tube. Those diamonds ricochet once inside the hollow trunk and seed another hatch of midges. The Fogler’s public job is therefore meteorology; its private aim is quieter. It is slowly knitting the sky into itself, one lifted droplet at a time, so that eventually the entire atmosphere above the hollow will be made of its shed vesicles. Centuries left? Decades. The Fogler does not know.

At the water edges waits the Librarian-Moray: a sombre, dun-grey eel-shrimp a little longer than a human forearm, though no human has measured it. Where the other residents converse in pressure and frequency, the Librarian speaks punctuation. When the stelae release their heart-warm flash, the water skin above them sends a transient ripple shaped like a comma, question mark, or colon. The eel-shrimp swims to kiss each mark, absorbs the glyph into the magnetic crystallites lining its lateral line, and stores it by growing one extra scale, iridescent black, in the matching curvature. This is its bookshelf. At irregular intervals it swims a slow spiral between the saucers, deliberately brushing the mirrored cuneiform against every polyp head it passes. In doing so it redistributes the cooling-glyph across the colony’s biomass. After redistribution, the bowl might release, three months hence, a single dandelion seed shaped like an exclamation mark, which the Fogler will coax aloft and the tree will catch and mine for boron. The Librarian-Moray neither predicts nor regrets; it typesets.

And then – sometimes – comes the Walker, more fable than resident. There is no proof the Walker is alive, or ever was. It is merely a regular circadian wrinkle in the luminosity field, a heat-shadow shaped like a biped in long coat, about one-and-a-half polite metres tall. Every seventh year the moss compresses in a line of seven shy footprints that lead nowhere. A pocket of expelled breath, faintly charmingly scented of frost-struck mint, lingers for a minute over the basalt sigils; after the minute, the stelae briefly cool below the taste of regret. No one is sure if the Walker is an external visitor briefly stepping inside the rules or an internal hallucination briefly stepping outside. All the locals treat it hospitably: the Archivist flushes a softer thread-colour, the Librarian traces an ampersand into the water, and the Fogler refrains from bursting any new bubbles for sixty heartbeats. The Walker, in return, writes nothing, leaves nothing, says nothing, yet the hollow cools by one further millikelvin so gently that the difference can only be remembered decades later by the oldest polyp lips.

Otherwise there are only ephemera – mineral beavers riding diamond rafts, thought-beetles that dissolve at first distraction, and heliotropic chords of lichen thinner than photon nerves. All practice exquisite moderation.

Should you risk staying overnight your own metabolism will undergo courteous partitioning. Some of your dreams will climb a spiral of humid light and take residence in the tree next as moth-faced midges. Others will be recruited to errands: one REM episode encodes itself into the eel-shrimp’s black scales, another becomes tomorrow’s upward-falling drizzle. Your heartbeat, imperfect but sincere, is laced with micro-arhythms adapting to the bowl’s slow tidal surf. When you leave in the morning your body sounds subtly rebuilt to itself, as though a piano has been re-tuned by exactly two cents. By the time asphalt returns beneath your foot the retuning is already forgetting its source. But every seventh year, on the night the Walker treads again, you will dream a wire of colour threading its way through basalt gates, and you will stir with the taste of cooling somewhere on the back of your tongue, a soft mineral lullaby no earthly weather owns.
