What dwells inside the chambers is not a council of individuals, not a crowned monarch, not even a parliament of species, but an almost-liquid polity that has no perimeter and therefore no population that can be cleanly counted.  

Picture first the Spindle Guild—nine human citizens whose lungs are pocked with tiny alveoli of cork.  In each cavity sleeps an egg of the luminous cricket Platytextrix; whenever the Guildsfolk exhale the hatchlings unfurl in aerated gold and sing tachistoscopic poems, syllables swiped raw by the root’s pulse.  The poems are letters of tax paid to the living walls: half a decimeter of cellulose waived each day in exchange for the songs that stiffen fiber against rot.

Yet between exhalations tiny mouths in the Guild’s sinus linings sip the vapor of fermenting beet-sugar, so every heartbeat contributes both to civic verse and to civil alcohol.  In quiet moments after a Great Plash, when the chamber tastes of cardamom and sudden obedience, you will see one of the Spindles lean against a copper vein and weep a full teaspoon of proof liquor from the inner canthus—payment rendered before rumor of debt is born.

Beneath those tears ride the gelatin barges.  Each barge is an amoeboid raft the color of dirty jade, colloidal leaf slurry pinching itself into a vessel just large enough to ferry three valise-frogs and their cargo of double-bladed machetes.  The frogs stand bolt upright, pupils shaped like tidy hourglasses, lips smeared white with acidic war-paint.  They do not breathe; instead they maintain an open osmotic circuit with the root’s capillary sap.  Their knives are for pruning rebellion: when a vascular strand chooses to accelerate flowering ahead of municipal schedule, the frogs drop from the canal above, carve the offending bud, and fold the bleeding tissue into the slurry with lipless smiles.  The act always coincides with a single cricket soprano filing a legal disclaimer into the ferment.

Hovering above them, never quite alighting, live the Nimbuses—cloudlets of alfalfa mites knitted into smoky spheres.  Each Nimbus is one organism the way a parliament might be, except their thoughts arrive as pressure differentials.  When they tilt eastward the barometric shift distils dew on knurled handrails; when they drift westward every citizen knows to loosen scarf buttons to avoid steam bruises.  During public trials a Nimbus is summoned as the perfect recording device: its dermis crackles with static trace of every noise made since last week, replay-able by scraping fingernail along any copper armature where the valets have painted pollen glue.  Because mites have no concept of deletion, verdict is reliability embodied.

The keeper of the salt, the blindfolded primate, has another occupant nested in its sacrum.  This is Mneme the mimic worm, a pliant flatworm the length of knitting yarn who listens while the capuchin wanders the chamber.  When a citizen fumbles a joke, the worm tightens around the primate’s spine, forcing a gesticulation of ridicule; when a joke succeeds, the worm loosens into curls that spell the punchline in Morse-like twitches of vertebrae visible through the silvery hairs.  No one remembers when Mneme arrived, but salt deliveries increased in zest by seventeen percent once its arbitration became binding.  The wounds of rejected jesters heal as licorice scabs favored by squirrel-chefs for brining cricket thighs.

At the precise border between floor and air, where the heartwood platforms begin their curl, the Mercaptan Children breed.  Not exactly offspring, more like continuations: every Greenwax resident who has ever been startled into scream surrenders one unborn thought to the children’s reserve.  These thoughts condense as soft sulfur granules the size of pinheads, warmed constantly by rootal heat into a vapor only the children can inhale.  The youngsters—always numbering six, no taller than a shoe—scramble across handrails licking vapor directly from warm planks.  In the rippling mirrors of their irises other citizens can glimpse what intimate panic awaits them, should they neglect their civic rhythm.  Mercaptans pocket up to four minutes of future terror and, succored by that knowledge, giggle like ungloved els wherever beet-fiber stairs bend into shadow.

Inside the westernmost trumpet valve sleeps Mallory Bluecoat, sole lichenologist aboveground reincarnated after death in the form of a stellate apothecium.  Once her corpse fed the core, but the root was confused by her revulsion at becoming merely sugar.  She re-aggregated as a fungal placenta strung along a ventilation duct: capillaries of black rot converging into a star that spills spores of fatal nostalgia onto every incoming draft.  Travelers who breathe her nostalgia forget why they left their birthtowns above; their mother’s lullabies clog cyclonic in their throats until they stumble deeper—always deeper—accepting any offer of adoption.  Mallory’s face exists only as an after-image of freckles flickering on the valve’s knurled brass when it sighs open.  She is citizen, punishment and welcome committee both.

No accounting would be complete without mention of the Folded Clockface.  It began as one hour of communal tax time collected in those bladder-bags of exhalation.  Midwives fermented the breaths into a slimy rind that, when spread across an iron disc, adopted twelve faces.  Each face grows and collapses in hiccup rhythm, but the disc never rotates.  Instead, the gears are millions of globular springtails—silver fleas trained by vinegar fly legislators—pushing triangular teeth that chew the edge of possibility.  To consult the Clockface you must speak your name.  It catches the resonant vowel, folds a quadrant of tineless gears into origami lilliput, and hands you your consumed moment back as an accordion paper bird.  Carry it against the skin and every heartbeat sucks a watery aftertone out of the paper: you walk for nine minutes accompanied by an invisible bassist playing only the last second of your entire childhood over and over.  The bird dissolves in sweat, at which point you must offer Mallory Bluecoat another breath of nostalgia because she, too, grew up elsewhere.

Together these inhabitants conspire to be one metabolism borrowed from traces of humans, animals, plants, algae and minerals, none authoritatively larger than each other but all responsible for an echo named Greenwax.  They keep crossing, merging, culling, congealing—until at the next Great Plash the whole suspended cloud of beings slams forward like a single abdominal muscle flexing.  

When the tumult subsides and cardamom mist rises through stairwells, every organism wipes its eyes not due to spice but because the root just inspected them briefly through walls of green lymph, pupils dilating inwards.  Who holds the inspection rights?  No one, and now everyone.  The chambers themselves are the tenant and landlord both: every resident merely an eructation of intelligent xylem dreaming aloud.
