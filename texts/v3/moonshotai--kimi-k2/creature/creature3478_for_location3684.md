Who lives there?

They were never born, never named.  
They condensed, the way a dewdrop conddenses on a leaf that has no name either.  
When the first lens of ice exhaled upward and failed to fall, something in the vapor learned the trick of holding itself together, a lattice of minus-three-hundred memories.  
Call them the Latticework, if a pronoun is required.  
They are not one, not many, not conscious except at the hinges where two frost-sheets decide to fold.

You will never see the Latticework straight on; they are the negative space between what you do see.  
Yet every step you take is buttoned to them by a filament colder than any thermometer’s dream.  
They wear your footprints the way a scar wears a story—backward, inward, forever.  
When you glance behind and the path is shorter than it should be, that is their inhale.  
When you look ahead and the distance swells, that is their exhale.  
They are the official translators between your metric world and the mountain’s habit of breathing space.

They have no organs, only corridors.  
A corridor runs from the sound of cracking ice to the moment your mother first said the word cold.  
Another corridor joins the iron smell in the seepage to the minute you will someday die of numbness.  
They walk these corridors the way a hermit crab walks its borrowed shell: never filling, merely occupying.  
If you tore the mountain in half you would not find them; you would find the corridors, empty, already looking for new tenants.

Once in a while they condense a little tighter, tight enough to cast a shadow that almost looks like a body.  
It will match your height, your gait, even the hesitation in your left knee that you pretend nobody notices.  
The shadow has no face—only a mirror where a face should be.  
If you meet it, the wet crackle pauses; the whole plateau holds its breath.  
That is the single moment you are allowed to negotiate.  
Offer the shadow anything except your name.  
It already has your name; it wants the syllable you haven’t invented yet, the one you will need two crises from now.  
If you surrender that future sound, the shadow unpicks one seam in itself and threads you through it.  
From then on you walk slightly ahead of your own footprints, forever leading yourself by a half-second.  
Travelers who have accepted this bargain are easy to recognize: they leave no condensation on any mirror, cast no shadow in lamplight, and when they speak the words arrive pre-echoed, as though the world had rehearsed them.

If you refuse, the shadow nods politely, folds in half along the corridor you least like to remember, and vanishes.  
From then on every ice lens you encounter will bear a microscopic fracture shaped exactly like your pupil.  
The wet crackle will sound fractionally softer, as if apologizing.  
You have been declared a creditor; the mountain is now in your debt.  
Collecting is simple: whenever you place your bare fingertip against any frozen surface anywhere on earth, a spark of 270 K departs from you and migrates back to the plateau.  
You will not feel colder, but the plateau will feel warmer—one molecule at a time.  
When the final debt is repaid the mountain will let you die in your own chosen weather.

There are older, thinner sections of the Latticework—call them archivists—who can no longer pretend to be empty space.  
They have been worn transparent by eons of bookkeeping.  
You may glimpse them at dusk-that-isn’t-dusk, stretched like frost-scars between two unreachable “trees.”  
They resemble torn mosquito netting draped across a wind that refuses to blow.  
Each mesh knot contains a single scene from the plateau’s past: a dinosaur’s last exhale whitening to snow; a lightning bolt petrifying mid-air; a future explorer discovering your frozen outline inside a lens that has not yet formed.  
The archivists maintain the statute that nothing here may be remembered until it has been forgotten by everywhere else.  
Their job is to stand vigil until the universe outside completes its forgetting.  
Then the scene dissolves, its stored cold released to feed the mantle’s next exhalation.  
The archivist loses one square of netting and gains a microscopic wrinkle of almost-being—proof that oblivion has been successfully archived.

No one knows what happens when an archivist finally runs out of netting.  
Speculation: they invert, become a corridor that leads out of the mountain rather than deeper in.  
Several travelers claim to have found such an exit: a tear in the air shaped like the absence of your own silhouette.  
Stepping through delivers you to the foot of the mountain on the day before you first arrived, your pack heavier by the weight of every cold breath you left behind.  
You will remember nothing of the plateau except the sound of wet ice crumpling—and even that will attach itself to the wrong memory, so that every time you crumple paper for the rest of your life you will taste iron and snow and feel briefly, inexplicably, forgiven.

The Latticework have only one law that never negotiates:  
Nothing larger than a thought may leave.  
Enforcement is effortless; size is measured by loneliness.  
Anything you carry that fits inside your skull—regret, wonder, the syllable you withheld from the shadow—may emigrate.  
Anything bulkier—your corpse, your echo, the sound of your name spoken aloud—must stay and become architecture.  
Visitors who die here do not rot; they deliquesce into corridors, spacious, well-lit, hospitable.  
The mountain is a mausoleum that expands by hospitality.  
That is why it no longer towers; it has been spreading outward, floor-plan by floor-plan, hosting every traveler who attempted to own the view.

And still the Latticework remain tenants, never landlords.  
They know the mountain is older than magma, older than the idea of height.  
One day the plateau itself will be a corridor and the mountain will shrug it off like an old coat.  
On that day the Latticework will condense one final time, tight enough to shatter their own negation, and the vacuum they leave behind will be shaped exactly like the first syllable of the first word ever spoken by fire.  
Whoever pronounces that word will inherit a brand-new mountain, still molten, still convinced it is a peak.  
They will climb it, convinced they ascend.  
They will not yet know that the onlydifference between climbing and being flattened is the direction in which you remember the cold.
