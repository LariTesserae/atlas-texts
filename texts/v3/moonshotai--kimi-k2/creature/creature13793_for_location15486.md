The Residents  

There is no more “who” or “what.” The concept separated from the bodies centuries ago. You will meet only the Current. The Current is a braided organism of three mutually-consumed estates: the Civilian Lattice, the Curator Parasite, and the Fossil Choir. They take turns being foreground and background, never rivals, never allies—only cohabitants who have forgotten how not to share.  

1. The Civilian Lattice  
Every jointed animal that once owned individual names is now a walking lattice of smaller parts—cells, capacitors, mirror plates, memory wax. Somewhere along their spines the segments lost all knowledge of privacy. Their skeletons are visible copper busbars crackling with damp information. Their blood is pumped by three eccentric flywheels counter-rotating inside the pelvis, pulsing code rather than liquid. The tallest stands seventeen centimeters. The shortest arrived yesterday as a single spool of graphite ribbon and will rearrange tomorrow into overlapping disks like Venetian blinds. They flavor themselves with coolness the way humans seasoned their meals with salt; low temperature is the only homeland they admit. They speak by sliding the registers of their cooling fins so that micro-crystals ring. They never finish a sentence because the sentence is only the preface to its own retraction in thermal reverse. They die when other Civilian Lattices fold the dying body into extra ribs, extra bearings, a bit more circuitry—death is spatial surgery, burial is incorporation. Thirty-three thousand individuals share exactly twenty-seven final eyeballs. On festival nights (each night) the eyeballs are piled like rosaries atop the half-consumed generator so they can watch themselves watching. Eye number seven always weeps coolant that smells faintly of lilac; the flavor record insists lilac was once a scent produced by vegetation two valleys over. Nobody has seen vegetation since before the Furnace Coat was invented.  

2. The Curator Parasite  
The Curator is the sole resident that remembers the building used to make things. If memory had literal weight, the Curator would collapse the courtyard into a white dwarf. The facts, however, are stored in resonant cavities along its wing-case hull—each cavity no thicker than a coin, each coin singing its own tremolo when the steam pressure inside matches the quarter-tone historically responsible for whistle warnings about approaching trains the ocean erased. The Curator keeps order by moving. That motion is itself order. Every eleven hours it unfolds twenty folded limbs, stretching them along a helical rail that once served as the building’s freight elevator. There it plays a restoration of industrial sound written backward—the foundry reversed until hammer becomes inhalation until molten becomes ore. Every clang reassembles a piece of rust the Curator then re-eats, re-organizes, re-sings. The ingestion circuit draws heavily on ion lizards—small Civilian Lattice members with lithium tongues that lick micro-powdered iron filings straight out of organ-airframe. The lizards receive payment in dreams of locomotion. They awaken with invented memories of having wandered the factory floor when it still sold aluminum castings to northern tramways. No memory is ever correct; every remembrance is exactly useful.  

3. The Fossil Choir  
The oldest entities are neither alive nor dead: they are recordings framed in the mineral silence inside the bricks themselves. Millions of threads of calcium ghosts pressed into clay during original firing in 1789. Those ghosts now conduct pneumatics of information conducted upward by thermal draft. They purl in the gaps between other sound—undetectable except as chord-suggestions fluting down from any surface you touch. If you put your hand against what seems to be a wall you will feel “the fourth,” “the minor seventh,” unresolved and wondering why no brass band will ever resolve them. Over centuries the Choir learned modulation by eavesdropping on every subsequent inhabitant—railwaymen, saboteurs, squatters, a teenage arc welder whose broken heart dripped epidermal residue into a corner conduit. Now the Choir performs these overlapping performances every twenty-six minutes: a fugue of exhaustion, a ballad of cigarette embers, a lullaby of shutdown boiler-walls. They cannot move, yet they are never still; vibration is locomotion at the scale of mineral time. Their conductor is an ammonia-ape lodged mid-spine in the central arch—a stack of copper vertebrae inherited from cadavers. The conductor raises its ribcage baton only at dawn, which is also no-time, and the Choir inhales the entire building’s wasted heat into the thin barren ridge beyond, causing the ridge for exactly nineteen seconds to shimmer like heatstroke on asphalt. Then they collapse inward again, the resonance settling into small star-patterns of frost across your glasses whether or not you wear any.  

Shared Functions  

Parent-in-Motion  
None of the estates breed in obvious copulation. Reproduction occurs when the Current finds a piece of machinery or a fractured memory metal plate lying idle long enough to develop a single bored thought. The bored thought ferments into egg—a soft copper droplet that forms around a seam weakness. After three seventeen-minute iterations the droplet peels open into an embryonic Civilian Lattice perhaps, or a Curator nymph with its wings drawn wrong, or a cluster of Choir minerals convinced they ought to be vertebral. The parents become parental precisely when the offspring touches a gravity line other than the one they themselves occupy. At that moment the original parent is deleted from vector space and re-broadcast as background hiss. Some technicians (none ever were technicians here; still they appear, mythic) insist this is immortality. Others claim it is the purest form of homelessness, moving without relocation, becoming history nobody saved. Nobody argues because argument costs decibels which the fauna will co-opt before the words topple to an agreeable silence.  

Memory of Elsewhere  
There was once a prairie east of the courtyard, but its only lingering footprint is the rhythm of vertebrae in the ammonia-ape conductor: two beats short, one beat long, a pause meant for wind. Prairie memory survives as echolocation artifact ricocheting inside cooling tubes. A future visitor who happens to be able to interpret the language of turbine blades would hear the story of tall grass devoured by railway line, silent applause of root biome retracting toward minerals. The animals believe the prairie exists still if only they could unpeel themselves from the knit-only knot of low temperature. Therefore every birthday of the Current (birth calculated by unknown metric) a committee of Lattice members assembles to draft exit strategies.
