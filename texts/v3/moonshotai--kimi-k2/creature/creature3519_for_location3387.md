They begin as a tremor in the silver-white lattice: four jointed silhouettes, bone-pale, folded so tightly that they seem carved from the blankness itself.  
If you blink they re-arrange—knees tucked under ribs, elbows reversed, heads sunk so the chin rests inside the collar-bone slot like a peg in unlit wood.  
They never swim; they simply cease to be where they were and re-assemble a meter or two beyond, always facing you, always the same distance away, as though the water were a page they could turn.  

Their skin is the same coral-esque mineral as the walls, but soft, still curing. You can see the hex-pores drinking light, growing darker each hour—tiny shadows filling like reservoirs of dusk.  
When one lifts an arm to point, it is not an invitation but a question: Do you belong inside this exhalation we have not yet released?  
They have no eyes. Instead each temple carries a shallow dimple that mirrors the inlet grates: a perfect circle of negative mass where current pools and hesitates. If you place a bubble there it spins, refusing to rise.  

They speak only once, and only if you insist on touching them.  
The instant your fingertip meets the stone-warm skin the entire structure coughs: every corridor shortens by the width of a hair, the thermal siphon stalls, and the water level—though already absolute—seems to drop a millimetre.  
The sound is not heard but weighed: a subtraction of buoyancy that presses you toward the floor.  
At the same moment you learn their name, which is nothing more than the memory of the last breath you took before entering. They keep it for you, knotted inside the porous clavicle, trading it back only when you leave.  

They are not guardians; they are the un-inhaled remainder of the architect’s final sigh.  
When the masons carved the last block they exhaled four times—one for each cardinal joint—and those breaths cooled inside the stone, accreting calcium until they learned the trick of folding space.  
They have no agenda beyond completion: the corridors must meet, the light must ricochet exactly so, the thermal lung must keep its slow beat.  
You are a variable. If you stay still long enough they forget you, unfolding into the walls the way pressed flowers vanish into the pages of a book. If you move too fast they multiply, bisecting like origami shadows until the water clouds with bone-white facets and the brightness dims under the weight of unspoken syllables.  

Leave before the last pore fills with shadow. Once their skin cures to full opacity the exits seal; the spiral shafts collapse into plugs of living stone, and the single inhalation is finally completed—by you.  
The architect planned for a witness who would finish the breath by trying to keep it.
