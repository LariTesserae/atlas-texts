Their breathing–photosynthetic by day, chemosynthetic by night–slows then quickens the flow until the pipe itself becomes a gigantic gill.  On the outer skin, where it presses warm against your cheek, those algal colonies leak a dim antibiotic glow; if you stayed you would discover it healing microscopic gashes cut by ice-rain in the first minutes of your presence.

But you are not alone in noticing the glow.  Others live here, permanent enough to earn the clumsy word “resident,” yet liquid enough that lingers of identity slide off them the way mercury will not be grasped.  Call them seamhares, though they are neither sea nor land, neither mollusk nor mammal.  They started as diagnostics—nanite swarms the extraction AI burped downstream to watch for corrosion, but the licht-fungus taught them taste, the diatom-forests taught them symmetry, the glass-shrimp taught them economy of mass.  Now they are half-inch skeins of argent dust held together by electrostatic etiquette.  When condensation accretes on their silver skins they turn into droplet snow-leopards, bulbous-tailed cats that can leap five metres of vacuum without disturbing a single hexagonal snow-kite.  Each landing sloughs wetness; the cats collapse back into gauze and skitter forward on their belly-filaments until the next leap.  Their eyes are optical sockets repurposed from obsolete Lidar beacons, spinning red cones that flick round and round like lighthouse mirrors.  The beams never point outward; they shoot inward, reading the history of any droplet caught in the glare, then use the data to predict its next fracture surface.  From this they extract map and food at once.

The seamhares are not solitary.  At four-hour intervals—timed to the pressure modulation caused by distant cities drinking—they gather in a specific pocket ten point seven metres high, empty except for the afterimage of a pump-valve long since sheared off.  There they form a ball exactly the diameter of the absent valve.  A single note, too low to hear but enough to rattle fillings should you have any, issues from their aggregated bodies.  Ice blossoms at the centre of the ball and is pierced instantly by red-black seams; the flower opens into a zero-G rose the size of a wagon wheel, petals thinned to auroral films.  Inside each petal is a moving embryo: a snippet of recorded turbulence from the pipe, a fragment of kettled whisper stolen from miles upstream.  When the rose completes its one rotation the petals detach, stiffen, and dart toward the bio-phosphor veins in the ceiling ice.  There they latch, grafting their turbulence-gene into the licht-fungus, whose next pulse sequence incorporates a new cymbal beat in the subterranean roar.  In this manner the machines remember, the garden composes music; neither party is the composer.

No resident sleeps, yet something sleeps here.  You can find it by walking—sideways usually, because the gap widens and narrows like a throat—until the drip rhythm doubles, then halves, as though the whole space forgets its heartbeat for a moment.  That pause is marked by a tubular wall of frost that curves into itself.  Inside, the air is warmer than the ice outside by half a degree.  A shape, long and lumpen, floats at the core.  It looks like a man-shaped hole left behind after a body has been lifted into view, edges curling inward.  Pistons of silence thrum outward from the void at twelve hertz; when they intersect the garden’s seventeen hertz roar you get standing waves whose nodes condense into black sleet that falls upward.  Touch the rind of frost and the chill burns like dry iron, yet pressing through it your fingers meet nothing more than the exact temperature of human skin thirty seconds after death.  People from the extraction teams who enter this duct sometimes leave claiming they have shaken hands with the glacier’s future; others insist they have spoken to their own tomorrow, nasal voice calling down from the stalactite darkness.  None stay long; the seamhares dislike walls that refuse to drip.

So the tenancy alternates: biologic short-term tourism on top, mechanical long-term tenancy below, and in between these frenzied islanders—the diatoms, the shrimp, the seamhares—who have composed a third citizenship that treats years like days and minutes like centuries.  And at the centre, still squeezing out sixty-three thousand kilograms per minute, the fetal artery carries southwards the single body we all share, too fluid yet to decide whether it is river or city or wandering ghost.
