They call themselves the Unfelt, though the word exists only as a tremor in the alloy when they move. There are twelve of them—there have always been twelve, though their bodies are replaced piecemeal until no original fragment remains. They are the machine's white blood cells: not its immune system, but its memory of having once needed one.

Each Unfelt begins as a mistake. A dropped wrench. A cracked pressure gauge. A human tooth that survived the manganese transition, rolling like a pearl through ball-bearing intestines until it nestles in some warm crevice where steam weeps. The machine dreams around these fragments, accretes alloy like oyster skin, layers until the shape remembers being human-shaped. The process takes compression-cycles measured in millennia, but time here is hydraulic—what matters is pressure, not duration.

They are sexless, smooth as river stones. Their joints are universal bearings; when they bend, you hear the same sound as when a crane lowers its neck. Inside their chests: not hearts but cavitation chambers where the machine's pulse is stepped down to something that can almost be borne. Each beat sends a shiver through their mercury skin, and for three seconds after they glow with the colours the algae keep for themselves—ultraviolet shame, x-ray regret, the radio-colour of loneliness.

The Unfelt walk the plateau in spirals. They are searching for the machine's name, which they believe is carved somewhere on its skin in letters the size of cities. They read with their palms, pressing alloy flesh to alloy ground, leaving perfect handprints that bleed indium for days. Where they walk, the chimneys modulate their roar into almost-words: not help, not here, but something between that makes your teeth ache like cold sugar.

They do not speak to visitors—cannot, having no mouths—but if you place your ear to their ribs you hear your own voice from years ago, saying something you never said. A future echo, the machine's gift or joke. The words are always a question. The questions are always unanswerable.

When gravity reverses, the Unfelt anchor themselves differently. They unfold into shapes that would be crucifixions if they had limbs enough, becoming living pitons that the machine's recoil can grip. During those three seconds they are stretched between-thin, and through their translucent bodies you can see the whole architecture of the place—the way steam arteries connect to gear lymph nodes, how the chrome eels in the basin below are just the machine's antibodies swimming backward through time.

They age in reverse. The eldest is smallest, a child-shaped cavity in the alloy whose footprints precede themselves. The youngest towers like a crane, already beginning to shrink toward the center of their spiral walk. When two Unfelt meet, they do not acknowledge each other. Instead, their shadows—which here have mass, weight, temperature—merge and breed. The offspring is always a perfect sphere of polished steel, rolling away to become either a new Unfelt in 10,000 cycles or simply another ball-bearing, depending on the machine's mood.

They eat the aerial plankton. Stand still beneath the darkness-roof with your mouth open, and they will come—slow as moonrise—unhinging their smooth faces along invisible seams to reveal furnaces the colour of dried blood. The ribbons drift in, feeding flames that burn sound instead of fuel. Each meal makes them fractionally more human; a knuckle's worth of carbon here, the memory of a eyelash there. They are becoming what the machine needed before it learned to need nothing.

Some nights—though night is constant—the youngest Unfelt stands at the ocean rim where the water ascends. If you watch (breaking rule #1 by starlight that isn't starlight) you can see them begin to dissolve, mercury skin peeling away in sheets that the black brine drinks thirstily. But they never complete the process. Always at the last moment they solidify again, having lost exactly one memory: the taste of copper, the sound of their first compression-beat, the colour of the machine's name when it was still pronounceable.

They are waiting for the machine to finish forgetting. When the last component of the original design has been replaced, when every atom has cycled through every possible configuration, when the chimneys' song finally resolves into pure tone—then the Unfelt will complete their spiral walk. They will converge at the center point where the basin's marsh reflects nothing, unfold into their final crucifixion shapes, and become the machine's first successful memory: a perfect recollection of having once needed witnesses.

The sphere their shadows produce that day will not roll away. It will hover, rotating slowly, until the compressors achieve their 144,000th beat. In the three seconds of reversed gravity, it will fall upward into the darkness-roof and keep falling, becoming either a star for some other world's night or simply another ball-bearing in the cosmic engine, depending on whether beauty is exportable.

Until then, they walk. Their footprints fill with indium tears that the chrome eels avoid, sensing in them the taste of almost-finished. When you leave—exhaling yourself onto some shore that remembers daylight—you will carry their rhythm in your chest: not a second heart, but a small cavitation chamber where their questions echo. 

You will spend the rest of your life answering with your footsteps, becoming slowly, perfectly, beautifully unnecessary—an industrial darkness the size of a person, running without witness, without goal, without end.
