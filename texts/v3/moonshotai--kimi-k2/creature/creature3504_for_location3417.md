They are the Capsized, the rain-salted, the root-fed, the bell-fed.  
They have no collective name for themselves; when they need a pronoun they borrow the tree’s first-person plural—“we” means larch and mind at once.  
Outsiders who reach the dripping umbra sometimes try to label them—Drowners, Upsiders, Choir-kin—but the syllables never stick; they slide off cedar cambium the way moonlight slides off wet slate.

A Capsized child is born in a cocoon of air.  
The mother climbs a yew rope until she feels the exact tug where gravity forgets itself, then she knots her ankles and lets the inverted fall open her womb.  
The infant emerges into a sphere of rain that never quite touches it, held by surface tension and old habit.  
Midwives call this first bath “the listening.”  
While the child still swims they press its fontanel against a bell-root; the carillon enters the skull and tunes the plates of bone.  
From that moment the child’s thoughts keep time with the bronze that is still falling, still soft, still somewhere in the xylose corridors.  
They learn to speak by echo before they learn to shape words; their earliest syllables are simply the grove talking back through a small wet mouth.

Their dead are planted, not buried.  
A corpse is wrapped in nettle-fiber and positioned inside a hollow larch so the heartwood grows through the rib cage like a slow embrace.  
After seven years the tree extrudes a single wooden knuckle that bears the deceased’s thumbprint; relatives harvest this knot, slice it paper-thin, and ferment the rings into a spirit drunk at winter solstice.  
To taste someone’s grain is to hear their final dream recited in the key of sap.  
Murder is rare because killing a person also kills the tree that holds them; the grove would lose a buttress and the city would list.  
When it happens the offender is tied to the damaged trunk and left to argue with the composite mind of victim and larch together.  
Most arguments end in reconciliation; the grove is patient, and guilt is porous.

They age at the speed of heartwood.  
Forty human years roughen a Capsized face only as much as one growth season roughens bark.  
A ninety-year-old looks thirty by old-world metrics, but their pupils are cross-hatched with annual rings; if you look closely you see light divided into concentric seasons.  
They do not count time in circles—no clocks, no calendars—yet they know exactly how long ago the marble was dropped, how long until the next echo of a council meeting returns, how long since the rain paused and the city applauded itself with bells that might never have existed.

Outsiders who stay begin to grow secondary phloem under their fingernails.  
After a decade the cambium replaces the lunar half-moons with living seams; if you clip them they bleed sap and faint music.  
These migrants are not adopted; they are grafted.  
They learn to walk the ropes by letting the tree’s own sense of balance pour into their ankles.  
The first time they successfully hear their pulse echo back as civic carillon they cease to feel expatriate; the grove has added their rhythm to the slow chord that keeps the city from twisting loose and falling the rest of the way into sky.

They tell no stories about the world before the Capsize.  
Memory, for them, is vertical: it descends or ascends according to the same etiquette that guides rain.  
When they need counsel they speak a question into a puddle and watch the ripples climb the water’s underside; the answer arrives as a pattern of droplets re-entering the sheet, reversed but legible.  
They believe that every city ever built is still standing somewhere in the column of water that connects cloud to buried mountain.  
Eidenvein is simply the one that agreed to wait inside the wood rather than inside the stone.

They have no word for hero, but they have a verb—*to bell*—that means both “to rescue” and “to resonate.”  
One who has *belled* is someone who, at the moment of civic imbalance, added the exact tone that kept the grove from snapping.  
Such people rarely know they have done it; the echo tells them years later, in the return of a speech they had forgotten they gave.  
They walk lighter afterward, as though one buttress of their own skeleton has been replaced with resonant bronze.

They do not fear drowning; they fear evaporation.  
A day when the rain pauses longer than three heartbeats is met with communal stillness.  
They sit on their cedar cambium cloaks, knees drawn up, and listen for the hush where the city might evaporate upward into unremembered air.  
When the drip resumes they exhale together, a soft susurrus that smells of fermented root and limestone sift, and the grove starts humming again, low, cool, inverted, rain-washed, root-held, bell-fed, sea-dropped, grove-dreamed—waiting patiently for the next palm that will eventually close around the river stone of it all.
