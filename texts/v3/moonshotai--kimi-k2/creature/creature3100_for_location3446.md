Who lives here?  
No census taker could survive the humidity long enough to finish the list, yet the place is densely tenanted.  

First: the Root-knit parliament.  
Under every flagstone lies a braid of hair-fine rootlets—larch, plane, catalpa, elder—each braid carrying a single vote written in calcium oxalate. They meet every dusk when the photosynthetic margin shuts down and surplus sugar can be spared for politics. Their debates are slow, chemical, sweet: one faction favors keeping the town “open” to human lungs, another pushes for complete cork-seal. They have been arguing since 1887; the minutes, if laid end to end, would ring the town like a wedding bangle.

Second: the Humidors.  
They were once human factory foremen, but the settlement’s lung-weather entered them through the stitch-holes of their coats. Moisture pooled in the pleats of their brains; lignin precipitated in the marrow. Now they are soft, man-shaped colonies of spalted wood. Their hearts are fungal biscuits; when they speak it is with the small creak of timber drying. They still wear the copper watch of their appointment, but the numerals have become lichen and always read “half-past spore.” Their job is to remember the way machines sounded—so that, if the town ever forgets itself, it can play the recording back through hollow trunks and relearn the industrial lullaby.

Third: the Cambium Maids.  
No one knows whether they preceded the town or germinated with it. They look like adolescent girls until you catch the collarbone: too smooth, a ring count at the throat. Each grows outward one millimetre per year; when the radius reaches thirty-seven rings they hollow out and become Door-Posts—the very ones you must thumb for residence. Their song is the smell of fresh-cut plywood; it makes outsiders drowsy and suggestible, but to locals it is merely the town’s evening bell.

Fourth: the Edison Fruit Choir.  
The glassy bulbs that hang from tram-lianas are not fruit; they are voice-boxes. A berry-shaped capsule of electrolyte grows around whatever syllable was last spoken beneath it. When the weight of unsaid things becomes too much, the bulb drops, shatters on the root-cobble, and releases a single word into the fog. Visitors often collect these fragments—“perhaps,” “tinder,” “return”—tucking them into pockets as souvenirs. By nightfall the word has usually eaten through the cloth and crawled back up the root system, so the choir never depletes.

Fifth: the Mycorrhizal Banks.  
Because coins corrode too fast in this weather, value is stored as phosphates traded through fungal lace. A citizen down on starch can mortgage tomorrow’s sunshine; a squirrel-steam engine (there are three) can be leased by the hour if you surrender a promise of winter bark. Default is simple: the fungi stop answering your knock, and overnight every door forgets you. You remain visible, solid, but no threshold will open—a ghost made of solvency.

Sixth: the Kingfisher Mayor.  
It is the same bird mentioned in the guidebook, but the entry is already outdated. The bird is no singular creature; it is a relay. Every decade a nestling hatches already wearing the exact cerulean mask. The old mayor feeds it the town’s map-moth, thereby transferring the deed engraved on the wings. For one bright moment the bird contains two maps—yesterday’s and tomorrow’s borders—and in that overlap it pronounces city ordnances in a trill that sounds suspiciously like dripping water. Then the elder kingfisher plucks out its own cobalt breast feather, drops into the fog, and dissolves: pigment becomes vapor, duty passed. The town records the succession as a simple weather note—“blue rain at dawn, no wind.”

Seventh: the human remainder.  
Perhaps you hoped for them earlier. There are still twenty-three lungs counted in the municipal ledger, though they are registered by spore-print rather than surname. They live inside the warm factories, wrapped nightly in cedar-scented root-shavings. Most arrived as hitchhikers, surveyors, lovers of the unlikely; the fog took their wrist, guided them in, then forgot to guide them out. After a decade they grow porous: breath rhymes with leaf, skin gathers a green undertone. They become clerks of the Humidors, nannies to Cambium Maids, auditors of the Mycorrhizal Banks. Eventually they step outdoors barefoot often enough that the root-knit notes their CO₂ tithe and enrolls them as citizens. From that day on they are visible to but not importunate of, visitors—like the background hum of a valve that remembers it was once a prayer.

Exit clause:  
The settlement never refuses departure; refusal would require desire, and desire here is diverted into annual rings. You will feel the tug on your wrist only when your personal humidity matches the town’s—when your breath weighs the same as the fog you borrowed. That moment is awkward: you suddenly realize you have been breathing loaned air. If you accept the debt, you may turn around and the door-paths open wide as cambium lips. If you decline, the fog reclaims what it lent; your lungs empty with a soft cedar sigh, and the last cobblestone finds its straight line again beneath your heel. Either way you leave carrying one token—sometimes a blue feather, sometimes a word that hasn’t yet shattered. It will sprout in whatever dry place you reach, unfolding a faint industrial sigh, reminding you that manufacture is only habitat seen from the outside, and that every lung, eventually, negotiates with trees.
