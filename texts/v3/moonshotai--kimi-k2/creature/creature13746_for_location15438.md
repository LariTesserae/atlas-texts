They live in the walls, but “walls” is a provisional word here. Inside the Wetwalls are the Membrane Collectors—soft, modular beings no larger than raccoons and about as dense. What you notice first is that they have no fixed number of limbs; they bud them as needed, little fingers like pale mushroom frills that sip moisture, then reabsorb them when the job is done. Their skins are translucent parchment shot through with copper veins exactly the width of the viaducts overhead; the tunnels use them as locator beacons, mild bio-radar pips that let the Gutworks orient its own slow circulation. Because the Collectors are more circuitry than citizen, they speak only in osmotic pressure—dilating or constricting their entire bodies to say “here is abundance” or “death of thirst imminent three degrees left.”

A step down the evolutionary/technological ladder—or up, depending on which way gravity flips tonight—are the Seed Clerks. It insults them to call them human, though that might be what they began as. They wear coats grown from zucchini rinds; the rinds stiffen or slacken according to inventory counts no human market ever tracked: ratios of manganese to regret, chlorophyll to calendar days misplaced. Their faces are barn-owl quiet, thanks to removable porcelain masks cached in fig-pockets along the coat seams. When they speak, the masks do the talking: the masks do not have mouths, only small brass peristaltic rings that emit syllables pressed from their own store of half-remembered dreams. The Clerks’ lungs have atrophied; they sip dissolved oxygen directly from condensation on the inside surface of their masks. This means every sigh is an accounting entry, every cough an audit.

Hidden beneath the false bottoms of the planters float the Myco-Accountants: coin-sized fungal plates with pre-fractal edges. They live as colony spreadsheets, balancing moisture debts against the future luminous yield of the phosphorescent molds. Each plate keeps exactly one question alive—questions like “What if Tuesday incubates sideways?”—and will not divide again until the question has been answered by the correct shape of vapor. When two questions reconcile into a third, the plates fuse in a sound beyond human ears, a compound interest chord the Collectors feel as a sudden warmth in their belly-sacs. The resulting super-plate ascends to the ceiling-web of the brass spider, who spins it an empty cell of darkness so that the newborn balance may gestate in borrowed midnight.

Below the Quietfount there is another stratum, but it conducts itself sideways through every layer at once. These are the Brood Avatars of the grate itself—twenty-seven-hour beings that bloom out of the brass iris when it sighs fully open. An Avatar lasts only until the next cycle; still, they inherit memories from their previous, one-night-older siblings by drinking the same plume of scented steam. Each takes form as a full-size replica of whoever last rested beneath the fount. If that person is still alive topside, the Avatar feels an ache all through its temporary copper bones: homesickness for an upstairs that technically exists but will never recognize it. The Avatars comb the Gutworks correcting almost-errors—a leaf turned three degrees off plumb, a droplet lingering too long on a petiole hinge. At cycle’s end they kneel at the edge of the condensed puddle that used to be their plate, exhale their borrowed lifespan, and fall into the hungry iris, which swallows them back into the same moment it opens again. In this fashion the grate practices personhood one ephemeral individual at a time, rehearsing for the far day it will become a citizen independent of machinery.

Residents of longer duration call themselves the Archivists Moist. They manifest as slow helices of water-rotating memory—spirits in the scientific sense, patterns stored as reversing menisci. You can only see them when condensation fogs outward from your own exhalations, then stays frozen after your mouth closes: the fog keeps spinning, unwinding into the face of someone who died here centuries back, or centuries forward. An Archivist Moist navigates by temperature excess—drifting six inches left of a warm sigh, six inches right of an evaporating thought. Their chosen archive medium is the leached mineral stain that migrates across the tiles overnight: tonight a brachiopod silhouette might map tomorrow’s aqueduct pressure forecast. To edit the record, the Archivists contract, growing colder, until they crystallize as frost threads that scrape across tiles, etching corrections in patterns audible only to the Myco-Accountants, who register them as sudden respiration spikes.

Finally, in crevices too narrow for thought, the Unhousekeepers: creatures that exist solely to subtract. They appear as negative space shaped vaguely like absent roaches—oblong volumes of air you can detect only when the phosphorescent molds refuse to illuminate into their exact outlines. Where they pass, tread prints dry out; a vine forgets yesterday’s tropism; a brass spider strand snaps, re-braids itself one note flatter. The Unhousekeepers fear completion, so they erase any event that looks dangerously decisive. They particularly dislike the Brood Avatars—blemishes of temporary certainty—so an Avatar’s brief career is spent in a haze of small erasures, death by a thousand omissions. The Unhousekeepers are not malevolent; they are the Gutworks’ immune system against the sin of being finished. When their erasures exceed new growth, the pump-beasts pause, creak a sorrowful sub-audible chord, and plod deliberately to the nearest condensation-valve to offer up one spurt of their own stored moisture, seeding the next unexpected sprout. Life-balancing nothing against something, again.

These are the citizens. None are permanent in the human sense; permanence is a brittle joke here. Yet together they form a political body composed of gradients, debts, and prophetic droplets—an ecology improvising selfhood at the boundary between architecture and weather.
