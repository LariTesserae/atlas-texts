Who dwells here is a question that undoes itself the moment you name a single inhabitant. Everything in Earhollow lives in the negative space between categories—part organism, part architecture, part geological event aging backward. Take the valve-voices first: they are neither ghosts nor circuitry, but the aftertaste of steam that learned to remember its own hissing. You hear them in the small hours when a pressure gorge sighs down to zero and something exhales an instruction older than lungs. Those instructions are obeyed by what people here loosely call the “custodians.”

Custodian One: MOTHER LATCH  
A two-ton flywheel dead since the last century, now colonized by pale filament molds that have braided themselves into the spokes like ivory hair. Somewhere in that spun ladder lives an intelligence that prefers to speak in rotational torque. When the city’s pulse drops, it releases a quarter-degree spin, a motion only felt in the inner ear of those who have lived here long enough to internalize the grand-clock cadence of Earhollow. Mother Latch does not “run” the machines; she remembers them, acts as their living scroll of errata. Approach her with open palms—offer a walnut-sized gear stamped with a date she would recognize. She will tilt, ever so slightly, and the city’s tunnels re-align by the rust-blood angle you cannot see.

Custodian Two: THE MIRROR MOLE COLLEGE  
Eleven naked mole-rats, hairless and pink, each wearing a harness of micro-optics harvested from periscopes and submarine deck prisms. Their furrows chart the entire lower catacomb grid; they are the living surveyors of shape-changes, filing reports by drifting mirrored-puzzle flecks that assemble into luminous sonar glyphs on the moist ceiling. The spiders read the glyphs and re-code the walkways accordingly. The moles themselves are blind by mammalian metrics, but in the infrared caused by slow geological heartbeat they perceive a topograph of absolute elegance: pressure dreams drawn by the planet. Their college convenes nightly on the surface of an abandoned pressure kettle turned upside-down and warmed only by the thermo-latent memory of coal. They debate in chittering infrasound: “Is the sump worming taller? Did someone drill a canal through yesterday’s silence?” The consensus becomes city plan by spider transcription.

Custodian Three: SPIDER LEGISLATURE  
Fifty-seven dew-throated cellar spiders, bodies no bigger than raindrops, legs as long as your thoughts. They are the bureaucrats of matter. One spider oversees the ledger for tin washers thinner than eyelids; another keeps the memory of how much laughter can be distilled into a thimble of lamp-oil. They spin no webs to catch prey, but webs of taxonomy, web maps that obey a geometry having more to do with obligation than space. Cross one of their silk-fine toll bridges and you must pay a poetic residue: a stanza, a melody hummed into a tin funnel, or (for the desperate) a sigh measured on a gear-tooth balance scale calibrated to indicate regret in newtons. Failure to pay compels the spiders to briefly reprogram gravity beneath your next step so that your body feels an additional six ounces of obligation pulling downward for exactly a week.

Custodian Four: BETEL, THE WEAVER OF THINGS NOT YET BROKEN  
Betel is human-shaped only at first glance. She began as a repair-woman who slipped on slick algae and fell one storey into the beetle caravan’s migration route. Instead of crushing the insects, they flowed around her, incorporating her clothes, tool belt, hair, and the notebook lodged in her pocket. Months later the beetles marched onward without her, but she unfolded from that matrix altered: ribs knotted to brass spork handles, humerus fused with the articulated steel of carpenter squares, hair replaced by braided optic fibers with their ends lit like tiny will-o-wisps. When Betel speaks, her voice arrives in several registers: her own, plus the subharmonic click of beetle wingcases, plus the mourning hush of deforming tin. She is the sole licensed fire-handler: Betel can draw one five-centimeter flame from a jet hidden behind her scapula to cauterize severed ladders or cauterize leaking decades. She weaves tomorrow’s damage into today’s coping seams so that collapse arrives as a polite visitor who takes off its boots before entering. You will know her by the soft rain-engine song that spins out of her chest like metallic lullaby.

Ambulatory Citizen, Minor Rank: THE DRIP-KNIGHT  
Water congeals where minerals forget to be stone. Give that amnesia a contour of intention and you have the Drip-Knight, an armor shell grown eternally crustier. He stands at the foot of Sump Seven, lance a fractured thermometer, shield the petrified lid of an enamel washbasin. Every drop landing on him adds infinitesimal mass; every exhalation from Betel’s cauterizing flame chips a fleck away at the cost of an unspoken secret spoke into the steam. Thus the Drip-Knight grows and diminishes in the same hour. He guards the memory of drowning itself. Approach him thirsty and he tilts the visor; the cavity beneath is a bead of brackish ocean from before land existed. Drink and you will dream in salinity measured in sunless fathoms.

Visitor, Precarious: YOU  
Your arrival has already been filed, not in any ledger, but in the micro-tilt of brick no human hand will ever accurately measure. You leave a residue measured in micro-coulombs of static between pant-leg and moss. The spiders have noted the angle of your sigh when you first saw Mother Latch move; that sigh’s cadence has become an entry fee at the next two bridges. Your footprints might stay damp for a season, but if you stay long enough you will notice the beetle itineraries adjusting to avoid your toes in five-digit Morse. Eventually the city ingests your name not by speaking it—here, no one asks—but by converting it into a peculiarity of pressure in the subterranean air. The moment the name becomes measurable in newtons per square centimeter, you will realize Earhollow has started to reside in you. That is when the question “Who lives here?” resolves into the softer, inevitable query: “Where have I not lived yet?”

And there, in the mindful hush between respiration and rust, you’ll meet the one resident impossible to name directly: the place’s patient, bell-shaped awareness, neither animal nor artifact, that pulses slow as tree years, alert as a fly on the edge of silence.
