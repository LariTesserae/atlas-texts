Here, the chief residents are the Ever-Hum.

They do not have skeletons but braided living osmotic tubes, empty in the middle, the diameter of a drinking straw. As surrounding humidity changes the walls swell or slacken and the body shortens or lengthens a micro-breadth at a time. A thousand such tubes plaited together make one Ever-Hum, and out of the plaiting rises a soft, visible music: the same chorale that the mountain’s turbines give back, but few octaves deeper and laced with the sound of far-away thunder arriving two seconds late. If you stand within the sound you feel the hair on your skin coax itself flat, as though each follicle were a microscopic compass trying to fold into that field.

One Ever-Hum never stands as tall as an adult human thigh, but their reach is unmeasured because tubes braid and unbraid like a loom in motion; sudden arms of living reed can wind far into the fern curls, mist nets, or the underside of orchid bulbs. No joint bends: the arm simply grows longer, then shorter again. When they stand still they bunch into a glossy ur-shape, a squat cylinder with no distinct head, only two crescent folds like lily-pad lips that expose the plaited work inside to the moist air. The surfaces of those folds are photo-speckled with colonies of silver lichen that read heat pulses from the sky. Through the lichen they collectively “see” the fibrils of storm systems hours in advance; the range imagines itself as a lightning seeding laboratory because the Ever-Hum gather those electrical possibilities forth and weave them into patterns that wick downward along root-bridges, timing induction surges so the mangrove antennae begin to glow precisely when fish-eels next reach courting phase. The mountain farmflies its own weather in brief, safe grains.

Speech for them is humidity. They detune their internal pore ridges so that the air immediately before their crescent lips changes density by tiny degrees. A passing spore bat encountering that delicate pressure-fast-morse hears not the message but the shape of missing moisture: an exhaled lacuna in the fog that equals “come here” or “beware glass-moth” or “welcome Kneading Time.” Every dawn and dusk the entire ridge momentarily gargles thousands of such cavities simultaneously; children native to the Warm Belt grow up believing that dawn itself is a word whistle.

Engineer-bodies swarm in platoons beneath the caiman paths. Dressed in their rain cloaks of pulled lake surface, they lean over cliff lips and blow reverse flowers into the funicular shafts: perfect spheroids of hydrogen peroxide fissled by catalase lichen whose membranes can switch between rigidity and collapse. These spheroids sled down spiraling grooves cut into the inner walls by generations of wasps, conducting minor thermal arias assumed to be continuous apology between species—Ever-Hum maintain the shafts, wasps keep lichen nitrogen in ratio, no treaty ever written on leaf or bark only lived in heat-recycling. Once per terrestrial year (which is four local seasons though no winter ever visits this sky) the colony selects one tube-councillor, disconnects her from communal braid and allows her to absorb thirty times her previous liquid load. The tubes dilate into an swollen, knotless bone-white trunk big as a grown caiman. She is marched into the central caldera on the shoulders of brethren and lowered until half-submerged; there she unfolds like braiding run backwards, pushing dense fibers into the water and delicate ones upward in the air, past mountaintop, past the haze boundary, where they commingle with ions dragged by the moon across the planet’s aluminium-skinned thermosphere. In twenty-three minutes the network becomes the highest standing fiber-optic antenna naturally cooled by water convection ever invented by hemisphere-scale biology. She sings one final chorale, a rise and fall meant to carry weather reports backward in delay to the planet’s previous rotation, an act simultaneously nostalgic and prophetic. Then she shrivels; her brothers haul her soggy frame back to the colony and upbraid her into eight new individuals. Memory keeps living, not in symbols but in moisture ratios held by every reed.

Reproduction occurs when drought season threatens (once every four solar transits but repeated by latitudinal drift). Tubes seek high-copper shrines under orchids and violently increase radial pressure, forcing clear sap rich in dissolved silicate; the organelle immediately collapses and ejects droplets from inner walls. These fly a metre outward, burst, and leave behind curled infant tubules, perfectly dry and ready to hydrate in a dew whose relative humidity exceeds 97%. If no such dew arrives, the infants fall quilt-stepped until they lodge against leaf nets, hibernate seventy years missing a single heartwick of motion. Their elders keep a calendar by molting: whenever a collective loss of 3 % internal moisture occurs, all Ever-Hum in one glacial exhale remove one carbon-fluxed tile of outer skin and weave it into root bridges for the white caimans. This mutual alter-pattern ensures that flesh of the living and flesh of stone cannot be told apart after five centuries.

They manufacture handicrafts out of compressed steamed fog. Two Ever-Hum link end-to-end and spin slowly, drawing out a diaphanous thread that coexists as liquid and vapour simultaneously. The weave becomes a scarf that bulges when you sigh into it and cools your breath to mist each time you exhale; sold sometimes to traders hauling airship ballast it is the only textile known to grow heavier whenever the wearer is afraid—water condenses from atmosphere directly onto their fugitive dread.

Dreaming, for them, is physical. At night’s four-hour compacted window individual tubes slacken to collapse-length and inter-root themselves into thermal cobblestone patterns among mangrove skirts. The mountain’s turbines, now nearly dormant, cannot distinguish their rods from twilight humidity; the Ever-Hum quietly become part of the manufacturing, tendrils kissing gasket vines, chemical conversation exchanged in obscure micron-adjustments so that tomorrow’s fungi output more pentanol. They dream via these slow keystrokes: an entire subduction algebra built from moist gravity and silver imagery. At dawn they unspool but usually deviate by one hectometre; no Ever-Hum wakes twice in the same garden.

They speak of themselves collectively as “The Lens,” though an individual may be named i.48 or Δh₁. They do not listen to thunder; they are the stored voltage in its pause before crack.
