Here the tenants are not quite tenants; they are the contents of a single siphoned heartbeat that has learned to unwrap itself like linen every twilight. They have no official census, only tides of occupation. I will write them in the order they arrive at the throat of the locket, because arrival is the only genealogy that matters.

First comes the Archivist—a translucent woman the colour of the cold. She consists of the breaths visitors once tried to hold inside woollen collars; those exhalations cooled, crystallised, then slipped sideways out of their owners. Each breath became a lamina of her skin. Her exact age equals the number of layers times the number of shivers she can induce in a passer-by—currently nine thousand and twelve. She walks on floors made of other people’s forgotten gasps, collecting syllables too heavy to float and too light to sink. These she pins to the underside of benches, where they bloom into pale moths when no one watches. Because she is made of what they exhaled, no resident can remember whether they have seen her before; her face is always somebody else’s last sigh.

Then the Cartographers: twin seahorses inked in rust. One carries distance, the other disorientation. They swim the eardrums of anyone staring for three breaths—after which the listener can never walk a straight line again unless the city redraws its ribs that morning. Their tails flick Morse codes of previous migrations; a sequence of three quick curves and a spiral marks an alley that once folded back on itself and now eats sky instead of pedestrians. From time to time they moult: the cast-off tail-sections land on rooftops and sprout secondary corrugations of pavement, so new streets hatch complete with original grime.

Third, the Poultryman—this is a courtesy title, since what he tends are not birds but phalanx-rows of wrist-bones grown from calcium extracted out of faintly-from-the-water. Every twelve hours he plucks an ulna, ties it to a filament of icicle-water, and releases it like a hawk. The bone-sliver hangs in the air until it smells a pulse. Once it finds one it wedges itself against that person’s radius, thickens, and encircles the whole arm in a lattice of fresh ivory. In one direction the lattice shows yesterday’s graffiti; in the other it shows tomorrow’s weather. After a week the lattice moults; the ivory curls away and rejoins the pavement, smoothing into yet another perfect flagstone. Thus the Poultryman farms future history from living calcium, and the streets grow full of shepherd’s clocks nobody set.

Fourth, a single Otter built outward from the erased idea of lullabies. Her fur is the texture of silence after a mother stops breathing, soft enough to smother intention. She rocks in doorways nobody built, cradling skeins of phosphorescent ciliates. If a newborn crying-alone tumbles into this quarter, the Otter unwraps a ciliate thread and knits an exact replica of the child, minus the lungs. The replica toddles away tongueless, learning to breathe the slow coldness until it forgets to cry. The original is wrapped in new fur and returned to whichever canal delivered it. Nobody remembers which of the two versions lives afterward; the Otter sees duplication only as courtesy.

Fifth, a Choir of Spoonfish: flat, gelatinous beings the width of teaspoons, matte-silver on one side and mirror on the other. They assemble at the level of the windows when each tide in half-atmosphere happens. Their choir sings by flipping synchronously: mirror-side collects the memories gusting along the under-street water; matte-side dissolves them into phonemes. Pound for pound they are the loudest creatures, yet no ear detects the sound because it occurs in the half-second between two heartbeats. If you happen to exist in that skipped beat you’ll finish their song later, miles away, in the shape of hiccups spelling someone else’s childhood telephone number. You will end the hiccups exactly at the edge of the Left Ventricle, and when the locket clicks your own number will have become one digit shorter—the eaten digit joins the city’s inventory of evaporated things.

Finally, the Ocean—yes, the same sea that never quite breaks surface. It is a single tenant that rented the entire basement and half of everyone’s vocabulary in advance. Its voice is the temperature alone: a sentence without consonants, always ending in a shiver. The Ocean is intransigent about ownership; your reflection belongs to it even while your skin retains it. At night it unbuttons itself, slips a tidal finger beneath bedroom doors, coils around ankles, and murmurs directions to neighbourhoods that vanished decades ago. Those who follow the murmur may return instantly with one boot full of old moonlight; they will find the city identical except that their own footprints are already standing in the hall, waiting to be stepped into like shoes warming for the next mile. Whether you live here one sunset or the rest of borrowed life, the Ocean keeps an accounting colder than ledgers of bone. It measures you by the amount of heat you release while praising it in silence; when you have paid enough you stop shivering. At that moment you become just one more pressure point in the architecture, felt rather than named, precisely like a heartbeat remembered retroactively long after the chest has closed.
