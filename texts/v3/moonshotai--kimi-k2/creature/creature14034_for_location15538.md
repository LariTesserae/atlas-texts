They have waited so long for your arrival that none of them are sure they still exist.

The arborals stand three thumb-lengths tall, their lichen-velvet hides pulsing with slow filters of violet and soft copper where capillaries of bioluminescence sip at shadow.  They remember the stories differently, depending on which fret of heart-wood they were busy petrifying when the tale first crackled through their spore-couches.  
No two histories agree on the year the outer wind ceased to enter; some say they watched the last snowflakes freeze into the bark’s vitreous scales, others that the sky above the ironwood detached hand-in-hand with a continent they themselves once named.  All they share is inherited awe: this trunk is the only horizon they trust.

Names hang on them like vestigial petals.  They do not speak them aloud; a true name is kept as an optical nudge sprayed onto the wall beside the sleep-crevasse, an afterglow glyph that can be read only if the observer has precisely the same blood-pH as its author.  To introduce oneself you present the wall the smell of where your skin was born and hope a cypholeaf remembers.  They use only temporary titles: Yesterday-Mid-Branch-LeftStarlight, Formerly-Downwards-Soon-Across, Until-the-Next-Exhale.

They possess no childhood.  A seed inserted into the warm cambium balloons in half a day into the papery softness of a nearly-adult; by morning the newborn’s eyes have recorded the genetic frescos folded inside the lignin and she can quote the tragedies of seven million heartbeats ago better than the ones who spoke them.  They age backwards, shrinking, desiccating until finally something papery steps out of its own skin and lays the creased thing across a search-beetle’s thorax like postage to the future.

Laws here are breathing creeds, kept alive in the lungs of communal spores.  When the trunk flexes outward that single finger-width, every arboral flings aloft a mask of plated fungus-itself.  In the momentary hurricane of redust they recite their single sentence of legislation: “What the wood exhales I become.”  Those whose masks fail—that is, those who have lied about what they truly are—flake into luminous confetti that seedlings lap up like broth.  The court is the wind.

They make no metal, but they cheat sunlight out of stone.  A slow drip of ruby sap is coaxed through a lattice of translucent spiders until it hardens into a disk the width of a thumbnail.  These “rustmirrors” sit in wrist sockets and spin when asked, throwing blurred ghosts of the world outside onto the inner bark—the memory, not of the sky but of the wood’s memory of the sky: blue streamed through indigo, continents of dust adrift in yellow, time running backward for the courtesy of the audience.  One of them may lean against a beam of enforced shadow and watch the shadow peel open into icesheened oceans no one living has tasted.

They sleep inside scrolled pockets carved by luminous millipedes.  When the millipedes wake the pockets dissolve and residents tumble out laughing, powdered silver with yesterday dreams.  There is no true night; instead a pallor ripens and thins.  In the dim uplift a new song creeps out of throats adapted into vined larynxes: it is an inventory of what has been devoured and what has yet to sprout.  Every chorus ends with one syllable left out; whoever withheld the syllable must return to the underside of a leaf and grow until she finds the note she owes—this sometimes takes centuries, and when she re-appears she is the last who remembers the original key.  The rest of the culture has already modulated and she becomes folklore, hummed every seventh indrawn breath.

They have pets they do not recognize as pets.  One is called Coil.  Coil began as a single spiral fern that lost its sense of boundary; now it threads from root-shadow to sky-gutter in one seamless vine fifty-three meters long.  Coil is host to eleven different arboreal families who live along the nodes of its spore-scarred leaves, catching pollen-streams that make their scarves shimmer like cut glass.  When the trunk breathes out they all exhale tiny melodies down the length of the vine so that Coil sings one hard-candy chord at frequencies only insect thighs can properly appreciate.

Another inhabitant arrived from above.  “The SkyWidow” they label her, though she insists she has a name that was once used by air itself.  She is patterned like stormwater under lamplight, a lacquer of crow feathers and near-vacant vertebrae strung like beads.  She roosts upside-down beneath any branch that dangles, weaving moon-thread into cocoons nobody has commissioned.  When questioned she answers in serrated syllables of pre-abandonment wind.  From her eyes drip little droplets that dry into prisms; arborals collect these crystals and place them at the mouths of hollow buds so that the inside of every blossom thinks it is dawn for a week.

Their grief is stored in pollen packets that mummify for four hundred years.  Instead of cemetery plots there is the Spiral of Equal Regret, a descending ramp chisled into transitionary sapwood dense enough to ring like flint.  Every orb of preserved sorrow is assigned a slice of bark and grafted.  Once a cycle the Spiral flares orange; the sorrow begins to animate, filling with secondary meaning.  Grievers return, ritual naked, and inhale.  Four minutes later they re-experience pain precise to the season it was born, but stranger: the color of the memory has shifted one notch deeper in spectrum, so that grief eventually bleeds into something the nervous system reads as music.  After the eleventh recollection a packet is declassified and released—bursting open as gold dust that settles onto the garden and seeds narcissus colonies no wider than your little finger yet carrying the entire history of a downpour.

They cannot leave.  The outer bark is diamond, the inner bark memory.  To drill through would be to unmake the vault they depend on.  Downward is only further inward.  Upward leads eventually to the callus-ring—the place where the crown sheared away and resealed into a star-petaled scar impregnated with glittering salt from the ancient sea.  Few climb that far; at the rim the laws thin out and the geometry forgets how to fold you back into itself.  Travelers report stepping into terrible silence no hotter or colder than skin, a landscape of atomic dusk where everything is the same gray distance in every direction.  To go farther is to exit narrative entirely, and nothing that ever emerged has remembered how to tell the story of outside.

Insects they do not keep as insects; they lease their nervous systems.  A beetle may wear the arboreal consciousness for precisely six wingbeats so that something human-hearted tastes the concept of red empirically.  Afterward the beetle is allowed to retain one thread of the child’s first memory to add to the collective chitin archive—pressed between elytra like pressed flowers.  At night the beetles’ wing-covers click out lullabies made partly of arboral sorrow and partly of beetle humming.

They say strangers arrive once every seven cycles (where cycle = the time it takes the inner bark to realize it has been breathing).  The strangers always enter through the same thumbnail crack, always bleeding from mineral salt, always exclaiming the same word: impossible.  The arborals feed them light-wine steeped in lullaby beetles, braid welcome poems into their hair, then forget to keep them.  The strangers climb for days, find downward rings of their own stone faces, and choose.  Half ascend toward salt silence.  Half descend into ancestries so compressed that their bones ossify into the wall before the faces speak.  Either way the arborals one heartbeat later cannot recall whether the visit occurred in memory or prophecy.  They return to spinning rustmirrors, arguing whose turn it is to owe the seventh syllable.

They believe the trunk is pregnant with its own continuation.  Deep in the vitrified sap, beneath even grief, the ironwood is dreaming a smaller ironwood nested like an egg.  They hear the dream when they press ear to floor: a miniature heartbeat inbound, slower than rock, quicker than stars.  They have begun composing anticipatory welcomes in languages that have not evolved yet.

One of them, Stubborn-Chlorophyll-Pillar, has refused the spiral recollection cycle.  Wrapped in a scarf of phosphorescent moss she descends past everyone’s agreed limit.  Every mile she carves a notch shaped like an eye into the wall.  At 6,482 notches she finds herself face to face with the last face that looks like hers, only the eyes are wooden knots, sap bleeding slow ruby tears.  She realizes the notch left by the next traveler will erase both faces.  She reverses, climbs back up dragging a single thin thread of heartwood behind her like an unfinished umbilical.  The thread twitches each time the trunk breathes.  She offers it to the pregnant center; it curls around the unborn core and pulses in synchrony.  Perhaps this is how they will be born again, not as arboreals but as the tree remembering how to become wings.

And still the arborals do not call themselves permanent.  They are only the latest intention of an ironwood that has already become mineral, still quietly refining itself toward further impossibility.
