They are the conditional citizens.

Some began as marginalia in architectural blueprints: half-erased circles, unused arrows doing nothing but pointing. The draftsman set his pen down to refill coffee, and the inked doorways filled themselves with impatient silhouettes, people-shaped gaps arguing whether they’d rather be thresholds than bodies. When the draftsman returned, the building had tilted nineteen degrees to accommodate their dream of leaning. Between worlds, the outlines ossified. They stepped out of the drafting film wearing nothing but notional overcoats. They bear no scars because events still hesitate to pierce them all the way.

Others are footsteps that escaped their owners during rainstorms. When the town’s gravity soaked horizontal, those prints slid sideways, skimmed the pavement’s meltwater like skipping stones. Each left behind a faint graphite quarter-note of identity—the heel that once loved jazz, the toe that used to stand on a subway grate to feel the updraft of passing trains. Once the prints discovered the freedom of ten directions at once, they elongated. Foot-long to meter-long to forever, now they drift as strips of biped origami, lanky bogeymen with laces still scrolling down the sides like loose script. They talk mostly in pressure and release, Morse of stance.

There is a baker woman whose bread does not rise; it elaborates sideways, producing infinitely layered flats. She opens the oven, and entire topographies scroll out like unfurling scrollwork, the scent curling into algebra. Customers tear off a corner and ingest a tract of prairie. After eating, their shadows take prairie shapes for the rest of the day—long and waving. She remembers each field because it used to be her dream while commuting to a downtown that evaporated the day she wanted it too badly. The town hides that city folded inside her loaves; former coworkers walk by sometimes, wondering why the rye tastes like fluorescent lighting.

A watch repairer winds the city itself. Every 47 hours he climbs the embryonic buildings with a watchmaker’s loupe and a pendulum the size of a scarf. He places the pendulum against frost-coated walls, aligning its swing with the agreeable humming of the gutter rails. Slowly, reluctantly, the whole block agrees yes, it is indeed exactly four minutes until nonexistent sunset. His sleeve contains flocks of missed seconds—tiny rust-colored birds that peck at the air, looking for leftover hours from yesterday. Sometimes they roost inside the static satellites and startle shoppers who reach for drizzle.

Two children hopscotch along a cloud-high balustrade. They are the only ones who arrived together voluntarily, stepping off a dream their parents were having simultaneously in two distant cities. The dream narrated a town square turned ninety degrees; the parents woke up to clipped, symmetrical heartbreak—young ones missing, home quiet like an unplugged lamp. In negative city the siblings don’t age; they accumulate games instead. They have collected 312 hopscotch grids, each stitched onto the foam with chalk borrowed from vanishing curbs. When they finish a round, the chalk scuttles home, returning to the sidewalk it originally abandoned. The boy recently introduced a rule: if you land in square 7, you must breathe in a color nobody has named. His sister invented “umblu,” a halfway between ultraviolet and apology; it tastes like mint left too long on the tongue.

At night an archivist comes out. She has no fixed address; she is the embodiment of the population census no one carried out yet. Carrying a satchel filled with nearly full pages, she glides from slack building to slack building, listening to the masonry confess what persons still need introducing. Each confession births a line on her ledger; ink blooms like frost. Once a name appears, a corresponding person gains solidity. Some newcomers stumble into the city fleshed only elbow-deep, needing to wait for the next sentence. Others arrive in duplicate, two copies debating whose sigil belongs on the mailbox. The archivist neither mediates nor hurries. Her favorite moment is when a citizen reads their own entry, discovers their middle initial had been leaking into the frozen moss slogans all along.

Most astonishing are the cartographers of voltage: colonies of ex-lightning bolts who were frightened by open sky and folded themselves into the circuitry under the pavement. Their bodies look like calligraphy violently un-orbit: zippers of blue-white fire tapering to graphite tailings. They mine the weather for spare emphasis marks. When controversy arises—such as whether that pockmarked plaza should retain its new title or revert—“the volts” congregate, drawing new schematics across the darkness. Their debates are visible as sudden temporary auroras. When they vote, the decision appears in the moss; their signatures smell like ozone.

Three neigh-sounds live in the tram rails that never were. They nicker in Morse. Riders who mistakenly translate their gossip often arrive everywhere at once: a laughter occurring in several directions until the body fetches one. They feed on torque and stunned wonder, but also on lullabies sung under breath on cold platforms. If you hum to them while waiting for a train that silently implies it will arrive, they sometimes coax the track into briefly hugging a different era—two minutes of pre-photograph dusk, or a Thursday which menstruated only once and was retired in embarrassment. Passengers step off into coats that remember different wars.

At the town border, sitting on the curbstone that is also the edge of the sky, you can sometimes meet the Stray Paragraph. Written long ago in a novel that dreamed of submitting footnotes to itself, the paragraph extricated itself during composition and arrived here seeking a body. It appears as adult-height serif text, ivory ink, walking on stems that look like typewriter arms. It speaks in descriptive clauses, offering visitors glowing fragments of someone else’s unfinished intentions. “The scent of burnt lavender, 1913 Vienna, tram bell slouching—” it begins, then stops tasting the air like a sommelier of tiny futures. Touch its last word and you’ll feel the page warmth of ink not yet cooled. People ask it why it left its novel. It answers, “I was the moment she almost decided to love you; the author felt it best postponed. Cities wait better than sentences.” It sleeps folded in the scrap bin behind the bakery woman, dreaming of era and comma smoke.

The conditional citizens are varied but share a single job: to slow the city down just enough so it can realize it is alive. They wake daily in rented thoughts—memories borrowed from yesterday, lent to tomorrow. They whisper lullabies to the unfinished second floors so the embryonic bricks do not miscarry into the meltwater. In winter they knit sweaters out of abandoned not-quite decisions: sleeves for choices almost made, collars for paths almost taken.

Above them the magpie pivots the sky around its stillness, watching their stewardship. Under them the ladder forgets its altitude precisely to teach returning pilgrims that departure is another move in the same game. The conditional citizens applaud in falling drizzle and seed weather with every leisurely blink.
