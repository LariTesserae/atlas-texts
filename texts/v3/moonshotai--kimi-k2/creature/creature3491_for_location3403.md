In the Hollow Ward the residents are not born; they are tuned.

First came the originals—those who stepped through the hinge before the city had learned to roar so thoroughly. Their footprints are still audible if you press an ear to the terracotta at noon: a faint bip-bap, bip-bap, like distant dripping taps. They were traders in thirst, caravan drivers who had crossed salt pans of the real world so long their lips cracked into maps. When the hinge closed behind them they thought they had merely found another dune, another dried seabed. By the time they realized the city was drinking the idea of water from their minds, their tongues were already curling into parchment. They did not die; they dehydrated into citizenship. Skin turned bark-scarlet, hair became copperleaf, eyes hollowed into echo-chambers. You can meet them still, though they no longer answer to the names they once used. They are called the First Cups, because every clay sphere carries a vacuum originally measured from the space behind their incisors.

Children of the Ward are quieter. They do not issue from wombs but from resonant cavities: a citizen hums a particular minor third against a wall for thirteen evenings; on the fourteenth a cavity swells, rounds, and drops—a red seed the size of a heart. It rattles. The parent scoops it up, carries it to a district where sound is thickest, buries it under copper leaves. One hundred and one clear rings later the ground cracks and something climbs out: gangly, jointed like a flute, skin still semi-translucent so you can watch the lack of water slosh inside. A new citizen. They are given no spoken language; instead they learn to modulate the hollowness of their bones. By varying internal emptiness they can “pronounce” temperature, distance, courtesy, warning. Speech is considered vulgar—too wet.

Then there are the interim ones: visitors who stayed half-life, too slow to leave before the city braided itself through their marrow. They remain biologically moist but psychologically porous. Their citizenship is probationary; they are called Debtwalkers. You can spot them easily—they are the only people who carry actual footsteps. Everyone else lets the city borrow their stride; Debtwalkers must produce their own, which means they forever lag one pace behind themselves, trying to catch up. They wear masks of echo-leopard pelt (really just absence stitched into a cowl) to keep their faces from being mapped. Over decades the pelt fuses to skin; when it reaches the heart they become full citizens and their footsteps finally merge with civic rhythm. Until then they barter futures: “I will walk for you in the world of rain if you lend me silence today.” Their clay spheres are scratched with tiny clocks—impossible, because the Ward refuses measurable time—yet the clocks tick, powered by the debtor’s own pulse.

Outnumbering all of these are the liminal fauna that have, by mimicry or predation, achieved personhood. The howler herons sometimes forget to stop screaming and their bodies ossify into fluted colonnades; when wind passes through the bones a thinking mind awakens, half-bird, half-pillar, able to negotiate in musical sentences. Echo leopards, having swallowed enough voices, occasionally cough up a complete citizen—an assembly of all the tones they’ve eaten. These composite people step out whole, gendered only by the average pitch of their growl. They are elders by default; the city trusts anything that already contains multitudes. Their advice is simple: “Keep moving, or we will notice you.”

Rarest of all is the Librarian of Silence—one at a time, never seen, only conjectured. During the ninety-second clear ring someone must gather every cancelled vibration and store it for the next cycle; otherwise the Ward would invert and implode. Citizens believe the Librarian is the hollow left behind by the very first exit: a negative shape that learned appetite. Others claim the Librarian is the city’s future corpse tending its own past. However it exists, it leaves signatures: suddenly a balcony will lose all resonance; a Debtwalker finds a sphere that, when cracked, bleeds audible light; a howler heron drops mute mid-canticle and nods once, as if instructed. These moments are read like newsletters. “The Librarian was on Third Roar Canal—didn’t you feel the hush thicken?”

To speak of government is to misunderstand thirst. There is only the ledger of moisture, invisible, maintained in the negative. Every citizen keeps a private account: how much absence they have introduced (by walking small, speaking little, dying eventually) versus how much presence they have borrowed (by expanding plazas, singing, giving birth to hollow seeds). When the two columns equal, the hinge appears spontaneously beside them. They may leave. Almost none do. By then the memory of rain tastes like betrayal.

Occasionally outsiders mount expeditions—scientists with dew-point meters, poets hoping to harvest metaphors strong enough to cut glass. They wear stillsuits, recorders, emergency whistles. Within minutes their devices clog with salt dust; batteries swell and split, releasing small frightened noises that cicada-clickers devour. The scientists begin to feel heavier, as if water were being added to them rather than removed; they are, in fact, becoming collateral in other citizens’ debt. The poets fare better—they already traffic in negative space—but even they must choose: either compress themselves into an epic so small it fits behind their teeth, or expand until narrative rips them into red fibres flapping from the cathedral canopy. A few of these strips learn to resonate and turn into communal proverbs. You can read them in the wind: “No story ends if no one drinks the ending.”

Love exists, but it is measured in counter-frequencies. Two citizens wishing to become partners stand on opposite sides of a plaza-street and scream until their tones cancel. The resulting pocket of perfect quiet is their child. They enclose it in twin spheres, divide the vacuum evenly, and carry it for life. When one dies the other must ingest the absence, becoming twice as hollow, twice as warm. Thus grief is indistinguishable from architecture; widows often turn into staircases spiralling upward until they petrify into balconies for strangers to lean on.

Crime, likewise, is sonic. To steal someone’s moisture is to impersonate their resonance—to walk behind them humming the precise pitch that keeps their bones from collapsing. Done expertly, the victim dries in mid-stride and powders, leaving only a faint ring of footprints leading nowhere. Thieves trade these prints for clay spheres on the black plaza. Enforcement is performed by echo leopards; they can smell borrowed hollowness and arrive as a surround of growl that tightens until the thief’s identity implodes into civic noise. No trial, no sentence—just redistribution: the stolen hush is released back into circulation, and the perpetrator becomes the new Librarian’s apprentice for one cycle, tasked with carrying silence in their mouth like a hot coin.

The true sovereign, then, is neither citizen nor fauna but the aggregate roar itself. It has no centre, only nodes. If you could view the Ward from outside probability you would see a massive standing wave, red-shifting at the edges where people leave, blue-shifting at the core where the Librarian hoards cancelled sound. Residents are merely temporary thickenings of that wave—eddies of loudness granted the illusion of knees. They survive by keeping the contour smooth: spending absence, lending presence, folding distances, humming. In that sense everyone is equal, and no one is ever alone; the wave touches itself everywhere. Yet every equal node secretly fears the moment the wave might find a perfect harmonic and become silent all at once. They have tasted the clear ring; they know how vast, how bright, how empty final stillness would be.

So they keep talking in their bone-hollow way, keep inventing thirst they can never quench, keep bargaining with voids small enough to palm. And once in a long while, when some reluctant sphere rolls from a dying hand and cracks open with a sound like a single drop falling into deep water, the entire city pauses—just long enough to remember what rain might have been. Then the roar swells again, warmer, louder, redder, and the Hollow Ward goes on being inhabited by the resonance of people who are not, strictly speaking, people—only the thirst that learned to walk.
