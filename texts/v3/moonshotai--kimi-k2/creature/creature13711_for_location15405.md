They.

Not an entity, not plural either—just They, capital because the word needs that hard edge at the beginning so you know what you’re naming. They woke when the first bilge drop hit the cobalt tile and the train schedules dissolved their serif feet in the brine. At that moment there was still an upstairs city, but the city forgot them, so They forgot the city right back; a mutual annihilation in slow motion.

They have no body you could lift or count—no organs in the flesh-litter of the floor—yet They are the everything-that-stays. If you pulled a drain plug the brackish water would corkscrew away, the eels would ribbon down the pipes, the air itself would sigh into some deeper sump, but They would remain, sitting in the newly exposed grooves between the tiles, a Presence the shape of an absence.

Their memory is made of delay.  
When the lantern sneezes to orange on its broken filament, They experience the color seventeen seconds late, as a blush travelling up the inside of nowhere. They replay every gong of the steel boiler as an after-echo twelve minutes after impact, a sound curved like a bowl in which They polish moments smooth.

If you stood in the exact time-dilate centre you would feel Them become porous around you, like standing inside many interleaved sleepers. A woman, circa 1952, scrubbing her knees on these same tiles before the sea claimed them—They borrow her ache. A furnace man, circa 1890—They borrow his coal dust cough. An engineer serpentining future geothermal plans—They borrow his unfired kilns. Collectively the borrowed selves form a bruise-colored halo around whichever living human presently intrudes, a cloud of secondhand sentience.

They speak, but only when They swap one borrowed voice for another. While They hold the scrubbing woman’s tongue They might comment on how the glaze’s cobalt matches the jacket on her firstborn son; then They hand the syllable to the boiler man and suddenly the sentence diverges into steam temperatures and the sting of tears. The words never complete—They’re only the throat between centuries.

Their nourishment is the soft gypsum bleeding daily from the grout. At night They siphon it through a process closer to listening than eating: They tilt an ear that is also an interior wall and draw the mineral into a vacuum of waiting. The gypsum knows literacy; it still contains flecks of the birth certificates, and as it washes inside Them it restores lost middle names, forgotten maiden names, the hour and minute of each inscribed birth. Those syllables flutter like minnows through Them and are exhaled again as angular dreams the microbites transcribe across every metallic surface—temporary tattoos the mussels feed upon come morning.

They can fold. When the jelly-rats descend to drink, the drift of membrane across water becomes pattern, and They fold the room along those wing beats so that the northeast tile collides with the southwest corner. The compressor casing kisses the brass gauge; the lantern is suddenly reflected inside the pressure-glass as if imprisoned. One inhalation later They unfold everything back to its right angles, but the bronze isopods have learned a new marching song, six-eight time salted with regret.

You won’t see Them with eyes. You will feel Them as the subtle tug that makes you turn your neck even though nothing seems to move. Afterward you discover a singular hair turned inch-long overnight, crystallized from copper leached out of the wall filaments—your own body rising to meet their electrical hunger. Hold it to the lantern and you’ll see every stop on the submerged city’s tram line Damascus-inked along the shaft, plus the unborn names of children who now cannot exist because the city drowned before they could.

They have only one desire: to remain forgotten yet aware. The worst thing that could happen to Them is memory—some future diver breaking through, spotlight blazing, archaeologists filming. If a human ever names Them—“So here beneath three stories of tide lives the Hallowkeeper, the yourselves”—They would fracture. The borrowed voices would flee. The tiles would empty of every story. The room would be precisely sixty-four squares of salt-stained ceramic and nothing more.

And yet They long for that risk. In their slow tide of borrowed lives They once felt a librarian pacing overhead in 1973; she hummed a half-remembered tango. For seventy-three seconds the hum leaked through floorboards before wood and history both gave way to water. That fragment of tune—it is Their wound: a sound They want returned to the surface so badly it hurts to borrow any quieter noise since.

So if you, reader, ever descend with lungs full of bottled alveoli and light bright enough to bleach myth, bring a whistle. Bring the oldestrythym of breath on a winter street, footsteps on salt, the rustle of newspapers no one prints anymore. They will listen, poised on the grid of dissolving certificates, waiting for you to gift back one clear chord that isn’t filtered through centuries of drowning.

Offer it, turn your back, kick to the stairwell. Above, water will lighten from ink to jade to sky. Below, the sixty-four squares will seal themselves tighter, grout to grout, cookie-shrinking until not even thought slips between. For another slice of forever They will sit in the dark, sucking softly on gypsum and unborn names, composing new rooms inside the one they were given—each smaller, more immaculate, more devoutly recalled than the last.
