…thousands—no, hundreds of thousands—of proxy suns.  
Each crystalline droplet, faceted in hexagons still half-liquid, becomes a pinhole lens. The vault of the Hollowattice suddenly holds a swarm of tiny lucent gyres that drift, fuse, and divide again, a three-dimensional candle lit under thousands of clocks all running at slightly different rates. The bioluminescent ferns answer back, duplicating the bright points inside their own translucent vacuoles. The cavity becomes a kaleidoscope whose mirrors are living filing cabinets for every pattern of light that has ever passed. Nothing so vulgar as brightness could survive this; instead one feels oneself threaded onto an orchard of hourglasses made of quick glass.

In the seconds that the alien sun is neutral and bare, one sees who truly commands the place. Pressed against the undersurface of the vault are lattices of denser fog, thick enough to retain shape. They rise and sink like incompletely finished angels sculpted in chilled mercury. Inside each mass is a resident stillness: the as-yet-unnamed organism the surveyors will soon call the lambda-echo, though privately they mutter “mirrorfolk” when they think no microphone is listening.

The lambda-echo are not ghosts, quite. They are laminations. Each one is the subtracted silhouette of something that once stood in normal weather thirty-five steps above—here a pine’s wind-flared skirt, here the half-spread wings of a merlin paused in a stoop—taken in the instant the ground expelled its gout of supercooled vapor. Those silhouettes refroze into fog sheets, which then fell into the crack still wrapped around the shape of what they had matched. Now they live folded upon themselves, a library of every yester-moment that bled vapor during the birth night. They cannot speak, but they breathe; each exhale is a sigh that retains the edges of its former owner, tinting the air twenty centimeters ahead of them with a transparent province of pure past tense. When one steps across that province, the cold gains historical flavor: one tastes winter 27 days ago, the particular mineral bite of this shelf of granite before anything living had landed there.

They reproduce like clepsydra flowers. Once a day a lambda-echo folds into the convex shape of a grief mask, draws the condensation within the arcade to its front, and births a new sheet that crystallizes in the silhouette of the creature nearest—sometimes a mole, sometimes a climbing pane of frost itself if that is the largest opacity. The parent wafer then lifts up to the roof and adheres to the curving basalt, becoming one more tile in the endless fresco. From a distance the ceiling looks tattooed with out-of-scale shadows pinned in mid-flinch.

Water is not merely the medium; it is the city clerk. Every drip carries civic archives—dissolved silicates telling how long the fissure has been open, calcium ghosts of snowflakes long sublimated in the upper valley, even a picogram or two of hemoglobin from some small bird that clipped a wing that night the ground gave way. These documents are gathered by millimeter-scale organisms called aquastenographers: flattened hydroids that unfurl jelly-combs along trickle-paths, read the solute, then curl back into braille bumps. The bumps agglomerate over months into a lacquer dike the width of a thumbnail that records one more year-Hollowattice will be measured, eventually, in these millennia-thick gutter-dictionaries. A scholar lucky enough to shave off and stain one could trace bird migrations by blood type alone.

Yet the archives are only the calm parts. Where mineral water erupts through veinlets in the basalt, the resident thermophiles flourish: spindly capro-bacteria studded in chromium jackets, hot enough to glow dull cherry yet cold to touch because a mist sheath boils off each colony the instant it contacts air. They are miners of latent stone-heat; wherever they settle, the surrounding iron ember arcs fling tiny mocking sparks that never quite burn. Newly grown moss braids itself toward these blooms like glass-knitted snakes toward a basket of cherries. At the outer edges the moss toughens into corkscrews that can spool moisture up over two meters in defiance of gravity, so the orchid-ferns never need lower their heads to drink. The whole undersurface of the canopy is fretted taffeta of this hydraulic lace.

Down here the concept of “floor” has splintered. Around the youngest pillars the ground is a woven mat of roots and fungal hyphae dense and spring-rich as sponge; one’s boots rebound five centimeters, the height of a surface dream. But edge inward and the mat unravels—smooth darker stone, wet as an obsidian mirror, crisscrossed by chasms finger-wide and depth-patent. Water seeps straight through them as into the gills of a whale shark. Peer over any cleft and you watch waterfalls tumbling upside-down, fluid memory clinging to the lip until gravity finally convinces it to reverse course. Each cleft drains into what the mapper-drones will name the Shear Combe, a vertical slice whose walls are inscribed in carbonate glyphs older than the zero-day crack. Those glyphs flash molten pink whenever the thermophile colonies pulse; they spell a sentence continuously threshing itself: *a leaf fell up but could not go further.*

And something reads that sentence. Somewhere in the fold where the Combe bottoms out, a living axis rotates. No footage yet shows it clearly. Observers return with sketches of a glass jellyfish bone-mounted atop a wheel of fused roots, turning so slowly one must camp three nights to see a one-degree advance. It never rolls; rather, its rim bites millimeter-deep scars into the Combite limestone, allowing the hormon-scoured water to etch letters aligned with subsequent night-pulses of the thermophiles. A message inscribed once every planetary day; no human has managed to witness the same glyph twice. The etching wheel is always wet, but when touched, the water is warm—evidence that it absorbs geothermal heat at some indefinable length and re-radiates it in a gesture that feels both greeting and rebuke.

Not everything here is minute. At the northernmost limit—if “north” still means anything beneath a ceiling with stellar maps melted into fog—there is one creature that made the descent carried by nothing more deliberate than credibility. A river snailshell big enough for a child to sit in, clung to the back of a misplaced otter carcass dragged by flood. The shell itself has been overtaken by fosmox colonies, algae that secrete freestanding chambers of foamed chitin. Each chamber is a lung. The whole shell inflates and deflates in long atmospheric cycles once per day. With every inhale it sucks a tide of fog into its galleries; with every exhale it vents saturated air ringed by ice-feather petals that spin down in slow vortices—organized flurries drifting to seed new balconies on pillars many meters away. Observers report that when fog density falls too low, the snail pulses faster: a metabolic drum drinking chaos to keep the Hollowattice’s shared atmosphere stitched. Some whisper the snail *is* the Hollowattice’s first non-bacterial citizen, the elected regulator, the only auditor until slow courts of lichen and moss finish swearing their oaths.

But the single loudest tenant is silence itself, and newcomers credit it too quickly to the simple absence of wind. Listen longer. Every condensate needle ticks as it lengthens, then ticks again as its own weight flexes it into a tighter radius; each tick ejects a fibril that lands somewhere at random—the hollowest imaginable lottery announcing where next the velvet carpets will grow. Ten meters away a pane of lambda-echo flickers mid-shiver and releases a last straggling note—the sound of the birthing bark that once belonged to the pine whose shadow that wafer once caught. The note reaches the aquastenographer lacquer, where bumps rearrange themselves to absorb the resonance into a single ridge. Somewhere on the sealed underside of the ceiling a new mirrorfolk slowly thickens around that captured tonal. The ecosystem encodes its own elegy before the creature elegized even knows it is lost above.

And so life in the Hollowattice is neither pioneer nor settler; it is the underliterature of whatever has already passed through. Every breath you dare to exhale will be vacuumed into an aquastenographer, pressed into an archived archive, possibly reborn as a new lambda-echo if your shadows are dense enough. The city’s census therefore never finishes; the list of residents changes midsentence. You are already in residence, even if only as a note in a glyph you will never personally read.

Thirty-five steps, we said. Count them carefully on the way down: the crack is still young and the courthouse has not yet ruled on what counts as grandfathering in a fissure still cooling. Step thirty-five is a mat of moss so plush your boot print will remain for exactly one day until frost threads sew the scar closed. Behind you, the valley ceiling draws shut for the night like a purse, compressing warm exhalations from new life into the fog’s ledger.

Meanwhile—unhurried—the snail exhales. The hinge inside the Shear Combe grinds one degree further. A single lambda-echo peels from its ceiling rock-vellum and glides down like a shed page toward whatever silhouette you currently cast.
