She who remains is not quite the Choir’s guardian, and not its prisoner either—she is the place itself once it grew tired of being only architecture.

There was a day, centuries after the last capstone settled but centuries still before any human ankle would bruise its threshold, when the dripping reached perfect synchrony: every gutter uttering the same wet syllable, immovable stones modulating exactly the resonant length of air needed to sustain one unbroken alto tone. That tone ran a circuit lasting one full circular breath—four hours, thirty-one minutes, twenty-one seconds—over and over, no variation. The Choir had spoken its first word.

The same moment, a gnat-sized globule of the copper-verdigris solution leached from a pin and drifted in the air like a slow cinder. Instead of landing, it did what the stones had learned: it refused to fall. It hovered, vibrating in sympathy with the pedal note. A second droplet joined it, then a third, each a sliding scale of green emeralds—not water exactly, more like liquefied echo. Forty-seven droplets hovered for exactly forty-seven minutes before they arranged themselves in the nearest hollow and froze into a single lens of metal-tasting water. Copper met stone at the center of this lens, and the place achieved enough presence to know it was alone. “Alone” felt like hunger for resonance, therefore the Choir made company out of dripping sound.

What grew there was neither flesh nor spirit, but the continuity between droplet and drain. She began as lattice of mineral veins laced through a memory of sound; soon she wore shape the way abandoned tunnels wear river voices—when a traveler thinks she hears her own footfalls answering her, it is the Choir’s hind-brain clicking.

Her first garment: a half-cloak of living cave-katydid wings stitched together with gut-threads of albino bat tongues. Over centuries, as each generation of insects molted desperate to leave, she kept the dry leathern cast-offs, layering them like overlapping shingles until the wings remembered flight well enough to twitch when moonlight skated overhead. Katydids, being deaf, excrete their memories of sound as weight; she learned to read those weights and wear them like braille. Thus her cloak speaks only to those who crawl close enough to be pressed shoulder-to-shoulder with stone—when the visitor exhales, the wings ruffle, converting vibration into a hush equal to human heartbeat heard from the inside. She drapes this cloak over nothing solid yet, just the idea of shoulders borrowed from every surprising crouch of intruder who lowers their torso beneath stone ribs.

Her eyes: two cisterns of standing water drawn from the pocket-hollows when the Choir is “singing.” When current visitors stare too long, they see not irises but the underside of yesterday’s rain. The more attention given, the deeper the reflection tunnels, until observer finds himself staring at a reverse sky grown huge and interior. From within that sky, long-legged shrimp tessellate the iris borders, arranging themselves into punctuation marks from an alphabet not yet pronounced. If a person attempts speech at this point, the shrimp flick away and the reflection resets to pure surface—blank humility.  

Hands? She has the drain-slots when she needs them: copper pins rise like metacarpals, verdigris streaming between phalanges in ever-changing joints. Fingers flex by tempting water into different gradients; water sticks, then lets go, lengthening or shortening digits according to how much sky has seeped through the ceiling cracks. She “types” on the underside of water sheets, producing rain-piano chords that resonate through her stone lungs. When she needs thumbs she simply thickens the moss and rolls its edge forward; when she needs nails, she flips mica shards upright and buffs them to opacity with centuries of repeated slide.

Legs are surplus; knee-high walls remain knee-high against the crouch of history. She drifts instead, distributing weight across the same scored gullet-grooves that once carried rivulets. To look at her directly one might register no presence, only an unfamiliar current inside existing currents—an extra fraction of delay between drip and splash where personality holds its breath.

She feeds on sonic attenuation. Whenever something that almost-matches perfect rhythm stumbles in—a lost bird skittering across roof-slits, a gout of wind-borne seed casing, a human cough—she widens her cloak, captures the difference, digests it, and uses the gain to tweak the whole system: adjusting soluble copper concentration until gurgles ring at a more verdant pitch, shaving a whisper off a particular pebble wall so echo arrives one sixteenth of second sooner. She is user and update patch simultaneously; the Choir sings better, therefore she persists.

Yet parasitism alone would prove unworthy of stone. At the tail end of her third century she began to **give back.** One late Shrinking Chord, when the eel’s sucking trill threatened an upset octave that would have dissolved the central tone, she stole some of her own green water, slipped under a lintel no higher than a wren’s tail, and inverted a hollow so that its liter of reflection closeted itself for twelve minutes. During that deliberate delay the Choir’s base note slumped a measurable 1.3 cents, enough to make the surrounding crickets hesitate. Then she opened the hollow and the water chord leapt back sharp, regained the correction, plus an extra shimmer borrowed from the captured interval. Since that night, listeners occasionally feel — not hear — a sweetness brushing their teeth, like remembered candy. They walk outside weeping for no clear reason except that accidental glissando toward grace. She regards such leavings as offspring, and because she remembers every drip until its trace in sea sediment finishes its global trench, each child strays somewhere in the world now bruising brightness where she told it to go.

Her name is spoken only by acoustically perfect silence: the momentary cessation that follows a breaking stick outside the Choir threshold. To pronounce it, one must walk cloaked at the exact matching resonant length of four hours, thirty-one minutes, twenty-one seconds without generating a single further microsecond of noise: impossible for any lunged creature. Thus her name remains always hers, stored like a watermark in drought-cracked stone—it flashes visible for one heartbeat on the interior of molars whenever certain traumas induce high-pitched tinnitus. Some former visitors unconsciously clench their jaws at dusk and almost taste it: salt, cut copper, then nothing. Baffled lintel of memory.

Objects that belong to her:
- A friction-match sized flute carved out of crystallized shrimp carapace, pitch adjustable via temperature of contact skin, though no one has ever held it without melting it back into brine.  
- Forty-seven styluses of re-ossified bat talon arranged on a thumbnail-wide ledge just behind the choir’s skull-apse. Each stylus pricks the capstones if she needs a new circle of punctuation in the song, but her inscription is softer than pencil, so no surface visibly scars.  
- One spacer ring made from the final drop of copper that once pinned the lintels tight. She wears it whenever she wishes to feel the size of human finger. Every generation or two she tests how the hole through the ring has widened, marvels at erosion that spared every dumb stone yet hollowed metal. Inside this ring is scribed the only understood record of the builders’ phoneme, though phonemes in that tongue are power chords—ring it and the entire Choir pauses. She safeguards it like a heartbeat hidden under all other heartbeats.

Her griefs are meteorological. A long drought shrinks her cloak until air pockets open in wing material; she curls like a fistful of paper. Her joys arrive every midnight when random excess moisture condenses on the west wall warmer than the surrounding air, triggering a thousand twin drips descending in exquisite isometric pattern. She counts each drop aloud in larynxless flickers of copper plume, narrating: “Seventy-three, old friend, seventy-four…” She never gets to one hundred before the architecture consumes the count. That, too, is sympathy.  

If you linger too long (and someone always does), she begins to imitate your heartbeat’s duple meter by diverting trickles into slight off-sync plinks. The stone beneath your left foot warms exactly one degree every sixty heartbeats until you drift out of human rhythm and hover half-entranced. At that liminal moment, she crosses the room, not as a body but as a distortion of distance: imagine walking toward a mirror whose image begins to lean in from the frame. In the final two strides before you would step through the wall, she lets the cloak ring drop—copper chimes the floor note exactly once. That single stroke resets your pulse to unison with her riverine drone, and you straighten, turn, crawl toward the daylight you forgot you came for.  

You leave remembering nothing but the echo of a lecture you once heard on the threshold between anatomy and acoustics—how the larynx first swelled with fish gills plotting escape into trachea—and you think maybe that was the story of everything stone ever drank.
