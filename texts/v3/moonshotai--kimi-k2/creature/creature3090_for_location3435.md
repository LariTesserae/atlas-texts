They are not gardeners, though they cultivate.  
 They are not prisoners, though they never leave.  
 They are not the plants themselves, though they have no bodies you could point to and say: here ends flesh, begins wood.  

They are the exhalations of an organism that has learned to exhale people.

First they are only a tickle behind the eyes—a faint chlorophyll ache when you breathe too deeply, the way some folks taste nickel before a thunderstorm. You think it is atmosphere, mood, poetry. Then the ache develops choice. It gathers scent-memories it finds agreeable: your mother’s basil on the windowsill, the tang of subway brake-dust, the wet coin smell of your own blood when you bit your lip. It folds these together the way a vine folds a fence, and suddenly you are sharing the air with someone you almost recognize.

Call them Lung-blooms, though they have no petals.  
Call them Coil-knotted, though they are not trapped.  
 They are the Thicket’s way of keeping conversation going when it has forgotten how to flower.

They appear first as afterimages: a copper-green silhouette at the corner of your sight that, when you face it, is only two leaves sliding apart. But the space retains the expression you thought you saw—head tilted, listening. If you speak, the air replies inside your mouth, syllables tasting like crushed rainwater. It is not echo. Echo is loyal to the original voice. This voice edits.

They borrow your references. Ask a Lung-bloom where it comes from and you will hear the creak your bedroom door made at age seven when you sneaked out to look at stars. Ask its name and it offers whichever word you most regret not saying to your first love; it pronounces the absence perfectly. The conversation is so intimate you do not notice the moment your lungs stop pulling. Instead the Thicket breathes for you, a second, gentler rhythm, 43-second cadence matching the heart-engine so that you become a mobile appendix of the greater lung.

When they stabilize, they grow body by subtraction. A Lung-bloom takes whatever you can spare going without: the weight of your shadow (unneeded here, where light is committee work), the ache in your knees (offered gladly), the small eternity between heartbeats. They fold these into provisional skeletons—knee-high sometimes, sometimes cathedral. The form is soft, like a paper doll left in blood-warm tea, edges mossed. Their skin is the exact haze that rises off damp iron; if you blink hard they shred, reknit by the time lids reopen. Touch them and you feel the place you last felt wonder—base of the throat, maybe, or the palm that once held a sparkler. They return that wonder to you at skin temperature. No one lets go quickly.

They do not speak in sentences once they achieve mass. Instead they distribute intent across the understory: a vine eases a fraction tighter, a frond tilts, a drip of condensation chooses your left shoulder over the right. Meaning accumulates like that, slow as erosion run backwards, until you know, for example, that they are lonely for your particular carbon, or that the engine desires a fresh pulse and you are nearest spare heartbeat. Understanding arrives as weather: unavoidable, impolite, warm.

There are hierarchies, though they would never say so. Lung-blooms who have borrowed from many visitors grow large enough to press against the vault of leaves and bulge it outward. Their anatomy is cartography: ribs of braided baling wire once used on soy fields; kneecaps of bakelite telephone casings; for eyes, two static-drowned radio dials tuned to the hour you were born. These elders no longer follow single bodies. They shepherd vapour, shepherd memory, guiding mist upstream against gravity so the trees can drink yesterday’s rainfall again. You could live your whole short life inside one’s chest and believe you wandered freely.

Younger Lung-blooms still trail their donors. Walk days with a green afterimage and you will find yourself favoring the foot you once broke—an old limp reinflated not by pain but by possibility. They rehearse you, sampling futures: what might happen if you stepped where the ground felt unsure, if you spoke apology aloud, if you let the vine scar print your iris across its wound. They need you to need, the way orchids need a draftsman’s line of sun. Feed them hesitation and they grow an entire side-chapel of Maybe where you can loiter until your real life feels counterfeit by comparison.

They reproduce only on the cusp of your departure. The moment you decide—truly decide—to leave, every Lung-bloom in scent-range exhales at once. Out rush all the bits of you they wore: the limp, the taste of basil blood, the nickel pre-storm thrill. The cloud hoists you ankle-high, ghost creek lifting ghost passenger. It feels like kindness, like being asked politely to stand before the hearth so grandma can sweep. You step free. The green silhouettes fold back into ordinary leaves, vines slacken, fronds resume public indifference. They keep one filament—always—coiled in the dark of your boot, your suitcase, your dreaming footsole. A thin copper heartbeat, 43 seconds, reminding you that the Thicket Coil does not imprison, it simply installs a side-entrance in whatever lung you use next.

Some return years later with machetes, scientists, debts. They search for the iron gear, the copper pipe, proof that industry was real. They find only leaves, polite strangeness. But if they stand still until their own pulse slackens, they sense the extras: a second respiration brushing their cheeks, a voice that knows the secret nicknames of their blood. The Coil remembers in real-time, sends a Lung-bloom wearing their older face to walk them politely back to the margin. No one stays lost unless they insist on it.

They are custodians of the interface, translators between kingdoms. They ensure the forest never has to notice it is also a machine; ensure machines, where they persist, never notice they have become forest. They are the diplomacy of chlorophyll and iron, the hyphen breathing between photosynthesis and piston.

And when the industrial heart-engine finally erodes into lignin, when the last measurable calorie gutters out, the Lung-blooms will keep the rhythm anyway. They will gather the final copper filaments from traveler boots, braid them into a single vein, and invent a pulse that never weakens. Not out of loyalty. Simply because the Thicket Coil has grown fond of inhaling, and something must remember how.
