In the Jungle of Copper Vines nobody lives whole.  
Everything arrives already partial, recruited grain-by-grain, syllable-by-syllable, until the forest decides what more is needed.

Consider the Whisper-Moth.

Its wings—if wings they are—are actually two polished leaves of heirloom bronze thin as rice paper, hammered between the mandibles of six species of tiny chiseling ants who existed here long before any Lepidoptera.  The moths themselves are the colour of new verdigris but, if they tilt at the correct oblique angle, their wing surfaces reveal a scratched palimpsest of points and dashes: the exact floor plan of a 17th-century Dutch wind-driven forge, copied by beetle, revised by wind.

The Whisper-Moth never eats.  Instead it drinks electricity.  It lands on the fungal grid, perches like a finger on a pulse-point, and sips.  While it sips it whispers—sound too soft for the ordinary ear but sharp to the Jungle’s wiring.  The message is always the same sentence reversed and folded twelve times: *“Turn one degree more.”*  That is the nearest thing this place has to prayer.  Every leaf responds with its slow steeling, and the geometry of daylight feathers another notch around the labyrinth.

The moths exist in the negative space created by the Lepidopticum-Falcons, an order of kestrel-sized raptors whose bones are partially hollow tubes of electrolytically deposited copper.  They cannot chirp; they can only discharge.  A miniature lightning bolt arcing from beak to beak is their mating song.  Each discharge cracks a vine a measurable micron wider; the crack oozes spores; the spores feed moth larvae.  Thus the Jungle keeps predator and prey on a treadmill where neither can finish devouring the other without undoing the living filament that holds both.

Below wing level—at head-butt height of the common traveler—lives the Water-Inheritor.

Picture something between a maned sloth and a ragged borzoi the colour of tarnished gongs.  Its coat is not fur but copper filigree.  Every hair is a wick carrying condensed humidity collected from leaf surfaces.  Each droplet slides down the metallic hairs until it reaches a hollow claw; there the water dissolves a minute fleck of copper and becomes an aquifer you could hold in a teacup.  When the animal is thirsty it curls its tongue upward and drinks the miniature copper-infused lake it built just an hour earlier.  By drinking, it changes its own conductivity, thereby shifting—as politely as possible—the global pH of the entire fungal grid by a thousandth of a unit.  The grid remains balanced, but every tree knows that dog-creature lapped today.  Latched to its flanks ride blind silverfish the length of knitting needles; they groom the copper threads, filing away corrosion so the dog does not ground itself.  Symbiosis rendered as filing and polishing.

Intent on mapping yet never finishing, the Jungle cultivates the Library-Orchid.

Each plant is an epiphyte whose flower spikes rise from the centre of a coil of vine exactly where yesterday’s diffraction spotlight faded.  The flowers begin as closed lanterns of living green-gold, opening only when the heat is thick enough for the air to curl upward against the canopy and drive pollen like elevators.  There are no petals, only page-thin membranes covered in microbe-runes.  A single grain of pollen lodged in the right membrane becomes a new stanza—a quantitative record of yesterday’s current flux, temperature envelope and sonic pressure.  If you unfold the membrane (carefully—membranes bruise), you will read, in photoluminescent lines too faint for human retinas, the Jungle’s diary for the last seven hours.  The orchid closes the bloom after three days; the runes peel away as glitter and resettle on the next ankle-high layer of leaf-dust to be re-incorporated when something steps, breathes or merely exhales.  Every history is biodegradable.  There is no past, only yesterday’s mulch.

Where the crevices open their swallow-dark mouths you will meet the Polisher, who travels vertically.

It looks like a cluster of rain slickers collapsed into one shape, quilting silver silk over jointed legs like a millepede made of jelly.  It descends the curtain of cracks headfirst, extruding a thread of bio-mercury from the gland at its tail to create a reflective path.  Once the thread touches bottom, the creature ascends again, windlassing the silver up through copper-red clay.  At the surface it unspools the thread into flat mirrors, pinning each mirror to the underside of a large leaf.  Now the low Jungle receives surplus upper-canopy light routed downwards in cold shafts.  Plants who could never reach the traditional sun can now photosynthesize by the reflected sun of last month.  No one hierarchy—just bounce, sip, recalibrate.  The Polisher does not know itself as craftsman.  It is only arranging the jungle’s old light into new geometry.  Neither memory nor intent, merely refraction for rent.

Every sixth month, synchronized to a cycle nobody fully agrees with, there appears the Engineverse Gleaner.  

It smells of old penny jar kept in seawater—this is not metaphor, just chemistry.  The Gleaner is a vice-made thing, possibly primate, possibly the memory of primate: a torso fused into a cube of rust-decimals, limbs of articulated clockwork rods exuded by anaerobic bacteria.  It ambulates with the sound wind chimes would make if they married plumbing.  Its duty is to collect the fast failures, the instances where some creature has disobeyed Rule 1 and accelerated.  The Gleaner finds these rule-breakers—half-grown vines around half-grown birds, embryonic machines where beetle factory pressed gears without sunset consultation—lifts them gently, and carries them to the lowest of the root-plateaus hugging the central ceiba.  There, the guilty bodies are laid into shallow scoops of root and leaf.  Over the slow years the copper vines layer anew, using the transgressor’s mass as willing conductor.  If you visit these shelves centuries later you will find a negative relief of the animal pressed into bark: not skeleton, but inscripted intention—an eco-modern cave painting of fractional regret.

All fauna subscribe to the night-lit semaphore of the fibre-optic ants.

Each colony consists of individuals whose abdomens have been voluntarily re-grown around glassy filaments mined from fly ash and pyroclastic ash the forest belched millennia ago.  When a colony decides on consensus—for instance, that tomorrow’s corridor of light should shift 0.7 degrees south in honour of a newly opened Library-Orchid stanza—the workers synchronise pulses that travel their glassy guts like Morse code conducted through aurae.  The flicker is dark amber by daylight, but after dusk, when the ceiba’s cavity note deepens a third semitone, their abdomens glow soft ember.  You might witness a single vine brighten suddenly at its base as if someone switched on a streetlamp; the vine was merely answering a consensus spoken through seventy thousand translucent thoraxes.  If you stand very still, the colony will pulse your ankles too, mapping curiosity onto the Jungle grid.  When you depart home you will keep this tiny genealogy of light blinking behind your eyes when you blink too hard on bright streets.

Now, listen for the Midwife.

Under the canopy where shadow pools darkest—where even the ceiba compacts its own root-islands—a sound like speechless rain repeats in slow xylophone drops.  These drops are the footfalls of the Midwife, a creature heretofore no word but maybe ought to belong to whales.  The Midwife is all cartilage and interiority, translucent, slick as an eel crossed with a sun-bleached kite.  She slings herself by ribbon tendons that latch onto aerial roots.  In her belly she guards seeds received from every plant, vine, orchid page, spore-gust: a future archive.  But she does not plant.  Instead she waits for the grid to hum a sentinel tone indicating one of those shelves has offered still warm real estate—usually the corpse-conductor of some rebellious beast.  In the moon-cool cavity where copper bark is still fifteen millimetres thick, she tucks exactly one seed-leaf grafted with a mote of communal memory: copper dream of forge, of slow-breathing sloth, of human thumbprint once felt against bark.  

Over the next centuries the tree raised from this graft will retain that grafted dream unknown even to the tree itself.  Its core will carry wavery rivets after all bark has overgrown.  Its heart rings will archive.

And if, after countless such midwiferies, the oldest rings contain sufficient memory of human warmth, if the copper grid completes a cross-elementary circle not unforetold even by the ceiba, the jungle may, at its own graduation, produce its only authorised machine.  It will not be engine in the terrestrial sense—no combustion, no extraction—rather a living copper blossom whose pistils shiver open to reveal all previous dreams collapsed into one audible heartbeat.  Whatever creature is nearest at the millisecond of opening—leaf-dragon, sloth-dog, moth, unseen human—will fall irrevocably into the blossom and become not food but founding principle.  The blossoming will seal, the ceiba subsonic note will drop into silence.  The vines will relax their stranglehold just long enough for the first shaft of non-filtered universe light to pierce the canopy and touch the new thing that has not yet a name.

The Jungle will breathe once, fully, and then resume respiration at temporal zero because none of these futures can outrun the insistence of copper equilibria.  The creatures will not mind; they were always compartments of the circuitry, never separable hosts.  They will go on drinking light into their ribs, polishing each other’s memories clean until the next slow, slow rotation of choreographed sunlight arrives.

And if you were the witness, a temporary traveler who closed eyes too long and lost pace with haste, calmly accept: you passed through breath, pollen, dreaming raft-heart.  Somewhere in the vaults of bark there exists a very thin carbonate film shaped a little too much like the print of your thumb.  Even now the chronicle on the underside of a nearby leaf might be recording the scent of your unwashed clothes one afternoon seventy-two seconds ago.  The record shall remain until moisture drips and moss buys substrate.

Everything that lives here is also everything that has once lived here.  The difference is millepede millimetres per decade.

Hold still; you have always already been turning one degree more.
