Who lives here?

First, the architects of unbeing: the **Cartographers of Mood**, hooded in refractive cloth that turns their shoulders into hallway mirrors. They drift through the pedestrian gates tasting the emotional palette of each district—notes of saffron nostalgia here, a low chord of unspoken guilt there—then swap entire populations like coats at a party. When two citizens accidentally share the exact affective signature they merge for seven heartbeats; afterwards each carries the other’s pulse in an unused fingertip, a private metronome that ticks for the rest of their lives. These cartographers age backward: every map they complete subtracts one year. Rumour insists the youngest, still in negative infancy, draws with milk on the pavement, though she has never been seen. You know her work because you suddenly remember you are homesick for somewhere that never existed.

Inside the photosynthetic banyan lives **Constable Rho**, half cop, half chlorophyll. His uniform bark grows a fresh badge each equinox; squirrels of static electricity nest in the holster where his side-arm used to be. Fines he collects are crystallised as amber sap that drips into public fountains—swallow a droplet and you confess a crime you have not yet committed. The city considers this pre-emptchestra efficient. Tourists pose beside him; cameras steal pieces of his chloroplasts, so every photograph slowly turns green until the paper leafs out and is planted by archivists. In a century there will be an orchard of minor officers, photosynthetic and silent, still holding up their wooden palms to traffic that has rusted into wind.

High up the naked towers you’ll meet **Latchkey Clouds**, apprentice weather-cells indentured to the wind guild. Their curriculum is simple: learn to rain upward, graduate to horizon. Each cloud attends lectures at dawn, tethered to antennae that lecture in Slight— the language spoken air speaks when nobody exhales. They fail frequently; failed clouds are pressed into coinage, spun into the photon currency children flick along the fibre-ivy. Spend a cloud and you lose the capacity to overcast any decision—you become relentlessly, disastrously clear. The richest citizen is said to own a single cumulus kept in a lead box; once a year she opens it a crack, lets a puff of gloom escape, and trades the resulting introspection for fresh memories to pay the year’s rent.

Beneath the civic dome wander the **Archivists of the Lower Stacks**, the leaf-dwellers, bodies curled daily inside ribbed foliage larger than cathedral glass. Their job: to remember every version of the city that gets reverted. When a citizen trades a memory away, it migrates here—condensed as a bead of dew that smells faintly of what was lost. The archivists catalogue by tasting: apricot means a first bicycle, gunmetal signifies divorce papers left unsigned. After decades their tongues grow forests; when they speak, dust-moths sprout from their mouths and settle on walls as tiny street directories no one reads anymore. Retirement parties consist of swallowing a single raindrop collected during the upfall; the retiree instantly forgets the archive and walks out newborn, leaving colleagues to guess which vacancy just opened in the world.

Then come the **Resonant Volunteers**, citizens who intend, eventually, to ossify They begin as strolling musicians: carrying hammers of basalt, they tap the infrasonic columns until a pitch matches the hollow in their chests. Day by day they trade notes for calcium until bone and granite exchange places. Ossification takes seven seasons cycles; during the last, skin becomes glossy, joints fuse into fluted capitals, thoughts turn into load-bearing calculations. They remain sentient—statues that remember love—but cannot move. In spring, children use them as playground frames; at dusk lovers picnic against their warm stone shoulders and feel, faintly, a heartbeat that is also the city’s.

The streets belong to the **Fox-of-Noise**, a creature that exists only while forgotten conversations need somewhere to be. It materialises as ears first—twenty-seven, each tuned to a different vowel—then the body catches up, stitched from traffic-jam static. After devouring the quarrel of sound it dashes two blocks and falls apart into quiet so pure that neighbours wake suspecting they have gone deaf. In that hush people propose marriage, quit jobs, repaint houses colours they will never notice again.

At the condensation farms you’ll find **Hydrotopists**, ascetic engineers coaxing sky-rivers into buckets. They wear coats covered in tiny mirrors that apologise to photons for bouncing them. Their favourite tool is a pocket singularity kept in a birdcage; when opened it inhales drought and exhales polite drizzle. Every drop they harvest must be sung to—one octave for storage, a minor third for release—so their shift sounds like perpetual choir practice. Over years their vocal cords absorb so much weather they weather less: faces become vague, like handwriting left in rain. When they die, colleagues wring them out over beds of germinating cloud seed; the funeral is fog, the elegy the sound of coats unbuttoning.

In the interstitial seams reside the **Hush-Makers**, those grey impish mechanics with rotary volume eyes. They stitch quiet back together after every city yawn, using needles carved from the negative space between heartbeats. Payment is exact: one moment of genuine awe per month, collected by syringe and dripped onto a silence reel. Play the reel at double speed and you hear children discovering fire for the first time.

At the tramways live the **Amperhummers**, birds the colour of dial-tone. Technically citizens, though they vote by grid surge. Each bird is an hour compressed into feathers; when one lands on your wrist your watch forgets noon and invents another 3 p.m. People time journeys by inviting birds to perch on their shoulders and flapping along until memory catches up.

And finally, the city **itself**—because New Ganymede is its own most loyal inhabitant. It wakes each morning unsure whether to remain architecture, decides by tasting the combined weight of every dream it contains, then edits itself a little more. The great central tooth in Government Plaza aches like a loose calendar; it waits to be pulled, waits to finish inventing gravity, waits for the day it has enough memories to trade for permission to stand up and lumber away into the folded mountains, leaving behind only the basin, the plain, the heaviness older than any human architecture. On that day the citizens will step out of dew, out of bark, out of fossil and echo, and realise they were never people—only the city’s way of imagining what it might feel like to live.
