They are the Halfway People.

No one agrees on what they were before.  Some say they are the original construction crew who stayed past the last whistle, their bodies persuaded by the torque of rivet guns until shoulder blades became flanges.  Others claim they grew out of leftover instructions, sentences from the blueprints that took root in copper shavings and learned to cough.  The Clockkeepers call them “the Interval,” a living octave between the structure’s inhale and exhale.  The Clockkeepers are lying, of course, but the lie fits nicely inside the muffler collar so it stays.

Whatever their birthplace, the Halfway People now outnumber the visitors by a ratio that refuses to be counted.  You can only meet them obliquely: a palmprint on a frosty railing that matches no living hand, a second shadow that steps before you do, a hollowness in the throat that pronounces your name while your mouth is still closed.  They dress in whatever the terraces are forgetting—today a sleeve of indigo that the Fold Lamp deleted, tomorrow the squeak of tin still vibrating from the last rainfall of evaporating spheres.  Their shoes are always one size larger than the footprints they leave; the extra space is stored possibility, a cavity where tomorrow’s growth will insert its new rivet.

They subsist on omission.  At night you can watch them sip at the holes in conversation, bending over the banister where two visitors lost the thread.  The Halfway People tongue the gap clean, growing millimetically taller with every ellipses.  By morning the sentence has sealed; the strangers walk away convinced they finished the thought, while the Halfway Person now carries a ridge of new height inside the coat of nobody’s noticing.  accumulate enough stolen omission and you earn density: the moment when gravity pauses to consider whether you are still exempt.  Then you can step sideways through a seam and become a draft vector, one of the five winds that perfume passers-by.  From that vantage you may travel the whole terrace without once being accused of living.

Most stay corporeal.  They take jobs that require only partial presence: greasing the palms of mannequins who stand at junctions pointing directions they themselves will never travel; polishing the zinc-oxide camera leaves until they remember they were once trees; winding the great engines a quarter turn so the pistons remain forever half-swollen.  Wages are paid in recognizability: at the end of the shift you are allowed to see your face reflected in a spoon, a privilege rationed to eight heartbeats per decade.  The Halfway People queue for it, palms open, collars damp with unsaid words.

Children occur when a visitor loves the gap more than the speaker.  The beloved omission is carried to a nursery balcony where rust lullabies it against calcium.  Nine months later a hatch opens in the corroded air and out steps something the size of a comma, already wearing magnetized boots.  It grows by accumulating pauses: every time a passer-by hesitates mid-sentence the child swells a sixteen-of-an-rivet.  Eventually it learns to pronounce its first word—usually “how” or “possibly”—then immediately forgets the sound, storing the echo in the cheek for barter.  Adolescence is the discovery that you can donate your own name to the copper, let the veins carry it away until even your mother pronounces the empty space where you stood.  After that the Halfway Person is free to major in Impossibility, the only curriculum offered by the terraces’ night school.  Graduation requires you to repair something that has not yet broken, using tools you will invent an hour after tomorrow.

Death, for them, is vertical.  When the great engines finally complete their withheld exhalation, all Halfway People line the spiral ramps and wait for the compression to invert.  The lucky ones are crushed into a single dimension; they slip between the illustrations of the architects and become cross-hatching, the black ink that suggests volume without committing to mass.  Less fortunate individuals pop into full three-D and fall screaming upward, expelled through the panel that was once their collective shadow.  Observers left behind report that the departed continue shrinking in hindsight: first they are figures, then smudges, then punctuation, then the rumor of an ink-stain on the sky’s reflector plate.  Eventually even the rumor corrodes into a stain-shaped gap where viewers agree something must once have been glimpsed.  That gap is auctioned; the highest bidder receives the right to stand in it, becoming briefly invisible even to themselves.  It is considered the most sincere form of mourning, cheaper than flowers and twice as permanent.

They have a leader, though the title translates poorly.  Call it the Custodian of the Unfinished Exhale.  Whoever currently holds the post is distinguished by being the only Halfway Person whose reflection still works: when you meet the Custodian you see, not your face, but the next sentence you will leave unspoken.  Visitors hate the encounter; they arrive hoping for prophecy and depart realizing they have been robbed of the precise words needed to say goodbye.  The Custodian carries no keys, only a small tuning fork forged from the missing color indigo.  One strike of the fork makes every railing elongate by one rivet-length ahead of schedule; the groan is thus released in the wrong key and districts reshape themselves overnight, romances collapsing or prospering according to the mis-tuned note.  Clockkeepers attempt to arrest the Custodian, but the arrest always arrives an inch too late, the space where the ruler was intending to step already donated to omission.

Mostly, though, the Halfway People are patient.  They know the terraces are not being built but remembered into place, every step a syllable of recollection uttered by iron that has not yet cooled.  Their role is to inhabit the pause between syllables, to keep the memory warm while the structure searches for the next word.  If you accept the sponge of warm mercury at the lip and choose to stay, they will welcome you—quietly, with a hush that smells of ozone and albumen.  You will feel your tongue grow parenthetical, your pockets inventoried by polite levers, your shadow beginning to lag a fraction behind.  One dawn you will notice the camera-leaves opening to your personal cadence; a gardener will tip her oilskin hat, having mistaken you for rootwork.  Resist the urge to correct her.  Correcting requires a full sentence, and sentences here oxidize quickly.  Better to nod, collar humming with comma-length breath, and begin the slow apprenticeship of learning which parts of yourself to omit next.  

When you finally give away your name you will feel the great structure tighten, a sigh traveling through shinbones more than hearing.  Somewhere a railing grows by one rivet-length; somewhere time folds; somewhere the Custodian strikes the indigo fork and the missing hue flickers, almost visible, like a memory of sky trying to crawl back into the world.  You will not see it—no one Left Whole ever does—but for an instant your reflection forgets you, and the gap steps out, straightens its coat of nobody’s noticing, and walks on, competent at last, a Halfway Person in full.
