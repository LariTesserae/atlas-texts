They call themselves hivers, walkers, or simply—in the tongue overheard between neon-green lovebirds—ladder-kin.  No census records them because no municipality large enough to count has ever stayed awake longer than a generation or two; the Upperworks chew up bureaucrats faster than clockwork moths.  Yet they are everywhere: nested into recesses the size of packing-boxes, folded like yarn into pipes wide enough to crawl, breathing the city’s lung-filtered breath as though it were a clan-song older than names.

First, the Smelter-born.  
These are the inheritors of the traction halls below, children raised in a single metallic vowel—a low F-sharp—produced by the twenty-six great pistons that haul the counterweights nightly.  They emerge blinking into daylight at age eight when the decree comes for new pit-lackeys, their eardrums grown to resonance, able to feel an imbalance three terraces up just by pressing a cheek to any brass rail.  They walk barefoot so the tremor code can enter through the arches of their feet; later, they copy that code into the shorthand of pipe-chalk that only Smelter brothers read.  They are stock-bodied, copper-voiced; they kiss on the irradiated landing where Piston Four exhales because the heat there tastes of the first furnace lullabies they ever knew.  Most marry the pistons longer than they marry spouses.  They tattoo weather maps on their backs—blued veins across the shoulder blades like equators—because skin is the only weatherwall that never rusts.  When a piston finally cracks, an elder Smith will lay his head against the pipe and cry in perfect pitch with the aching steel, then die the instant harmony folds; nobody buries him, for in the morning the engine will inhale his skeleton and cast it into a new crank-arm, and a small girl will feel the old man’s pulse resume inside the rhythm, like the soul of an echo.

Second, the Arborists.  
They descend from runaway orchard scholars—botanists who once catalogued foothill fig genetics and defected skyward when the paperwork outgrew the branches.  You’ll know them by the two hollow straws they keep in the corners of their mouths: one for sipping nectar, one for squirting seed gel into crevices where no hand can reach.  They style their hair as radar dishes—plaits curved backward to trap pollinating moths—so that spores accumulate in dark glossy halos each dusk.  They speak in cultivars: “That child lacks pectin,” they’ll mutter of a pale newcomer, or “He’s full of anthocyanins,” as praise for a heart that stains the lips when bruised.  Each Arborist is married to a tree at all times.  The bond is forged when, at age fifteen, they graft a bud of their own skin (the post-auricular fold, soft as fig sarcocarp) into a bark slash.  If the graft takes, they keep the scar, a living seam, and the tree bears fruits with a pink blush exactly the shade of that abandoned human dermal cell.  If the graft fails, well—the fig continues studio-green, but the botanist keeps a hush around her throat for life.  They waltz nightly under the orchard’s skylight tubes, pollen drifting like blurred galaxies, and when a pregnancy occurs it is said the foetus leafs once, a momentary imprint of cotyledon on amnion, before settling back into mere tissue.

Third, the Glyphtenders—the archive monks.  
Silk-robed, barefoot though the grillwork can burn after a mirror-focused noon.  They keep living ink: colonies of cyanobacteria trapped in translucent resin pens which must be warmed by armpit heat each morning or they fade to pale betrayal.  On the haunches of public stairrails they inscribe spiral after spiral of historical smells—yes, smells—captured from beer kegs, tangerine skins, hot bronze.  By brushing a glypht strip with breath, a reader’s olfactory nerve replays the hour the scent was taken.  Thus yesterday is not retold but re-reeked, and memory is communal breath.  To become Glyphtender one swallows a lentil of antique paper—usually shredded from the last census—and cultivates the fibres in the gut for six months, drinking only honeywater.  During that time no spoken word is allowed; the stool that finally emerges is pure cellulose lace, which the candidate then burns to ash, mixes with linden sap, and uses to write the sentence that will become their novice tattoo across the throat: usually the first lie they ever told, now anchored forever where the pulse can blur the letters at each heartbeat.  Visitors seeking lost grandparents often squat next to Glyphtenders at twilight, inhaling 90-year-old perfume until they weep into the mosaic.

Fourth, the Vaporettes.  
Light-stepped, glass-bodied courier guild whose lungs have hypertrophied into living condensers.  They are always cold to touch, for vapour skin-temperature is 15 °C.  They haul dew in hollow finger bones—each phalanx a test tube—walking among terraces during evaporation pause to harvest the rising ghosts.  They are accordingly transparent: you see their guts shifting like inverted clouds within rib domes of aerogel cartilage.  Attachment for them is problematic; they leave stains of themselves—droplets shaped like half-words—on every lintel they pass.  Childhood ends at the first Meniscus Dance: children leap from balcony to balcony along the steam upwells, attempting to bounce upon nothing more tangible than hot breath.  Those who fail fall three terraces, break a femur, and retire to archival medicine.  Those who succeed lose pigment: their skin becomes a vessel for sky.  They marry evanescently, clutching partners only when microclimates fold to allow lingering.  Their graveyard is the mist before dawn: a Vaporette at death boils away from the gurney, leaving only the collected water of a lifetime spelling a single syllable of regret across the night market.  The syllable glows briefly violet, then joins the cloud labs where new rain will be seeding on the forty-seventh day.  No plaque.  Just weather.

Fifth, the Orgglers.  
Everyone forgets them until platforms grow too quiet.  They are long-necked adolescents kept in fungal darkness, so the optic nerve stretches like taffy behind milky lenses.  Their tracheas whistle the resonant chords of any lattice they lean against, translating structural fatigue into arias.  They speak no language but harmonic complaint; therefore a cracked beam always sounds like a mezzo sob two hours before collapse.  Fed only on stewed glasswort and mycelial liquor, they grow tall and slack as mop handles, joints wrapped with damp wool to keep their bones from ringing all the time (so the neighbours can sleep).  They sleep upright in drainpipes; when an Orggler dreams, the pipe tone drops a perfect third.  Wake an Orggler before dawn and the entire parapet warbles in moan-blue.  Their adulthood is a sudden ossification: the hyoid hardens into a bronze tuning fork whiter than any tooth.  The day it happens a chime is struck that enters the Traction Hall, and Smelters drop their wrenches; Glyphtenders ink the chime’s resonance for smelling; Arborists note a fig-vigour surge; Vaporettes feel the humidity ripple.  The newly tuned Orggler walks toward the west parapet and disappears down an unseen stair never to return.  The city attributes its hundred-year survival to eleven such notes sent to ground level, though no explorer has ever found the original scale.  The Orgglers’ child-keepers mark the event only by painting a thin green ring round a library shelf, the precise shelf presently containing the lightest book—usually the one about short-lived mid-air cities.

Across all these castes exist half-castes, deviants, swapped babies, the rope-dancers who practice unanswered questions above alleyways during festivals.  Elevator City does not choose them; it assembles them like a sentence long, melodic, half-forgotten, still being conjugated by gravity and sunlight.
