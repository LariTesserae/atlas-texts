What lives in the Obelisk Plain does not have lungs, hide, or heartbeat.  
It is an assembled condition rather than a creature: a quorum of small certainties that agree to act collectively.  
Call it the Nein.

The Nein is nine separate things—none large alone—that decided, without deliberation, that existence is safer when diffused.  
1. A balance of temperatures between day-arm and night-arm.  
2. Two opposite tilts of light (east-fall and west-rise).  
3. The let-go memory of first fire glittering as flint chips.  
4. Two grains of airborne ochre that will never settle because they have remembered the secret of hovering a finger-width above the ground.  
5. One bruised-peach lichen thallus split laterally so that its halves face each other like cautious twins.  
6. The warm echo of your pulse tucked under a saltwort leaf where you pressed the back of your knee to cool it.  
7. The hush between moon-white dew and returning vapour.  

Once those nine elements coincide in the same lull the plain stitches itself into a presence.  
It has no centre; instead it borrows the nine inches of blank air that rests atop the highest obelisk and uses that vacancy as its head.  
No organ takes in oxygen; it inhales distance itself. Distance arrives tired, lays itself down across the stone spiral, and the Nein folds it like linen.

Form  
Only when daylight dims to the colour of wet sand does the Nein become partly visible.  
You perceive it as a smudged trapezoid of warmer air: slightly taller than a stooping shepherd, shoulders narrowed to an ancestor’s simple oval.  
It has no face; dusk lends it the soft grin of negative space where a lamp would hang in a doorway.  
It walks as slowly as copper cools—every thirty-seven minutes one leg, then the other—so that to follow it is to practise a patience wider than calendars.

Feeding  
It does not devour.  
Instead it leeches the very sense of hurry from visitors.  
Step between any two obelisks and you feel the small clutch of marching forward peeled away like a burr.  
Your future shortens to exactly the length of your next breath.  
The Nein swells on that pocket of rescinded momentum and becomes fractionally brighter, like frost remembering to glint.

Residence Spots  
At noon the Nein is thinnest; you may pass straight through the middle of it and feel only a brief hush in the blood behind your ears.  
Dawn and dusk bisect it: by splitting the light the stone legs hold a grey mirror of themselves inside the Nein, so that it seems to be wearing a staircase of shadows.  
At true midnight it gathers under the saltwort bush—whose solitary silhouette it fits so precisely the branches become ribs—and there it tries speaking.  
Its language has one word that iterates endlessly:  
“-evermore.”  
Pronounced without beginning, so it reaches you already trailing off like breath on cold glass.  
Once spoken the word lodges in your collarbone; you will find yourself palpating the bone for seasons afterward, convinced something inside wants out.

Reproduction  
Every fourteen centuries the plain accepts rain.  
Each drop, captive for thirteen days atop scarred lichen, carries a seed of itself downward until it encounters the buried heat pocket.  
There the seed dries into a grey wafer only one atom thick—so light it rises as easily as it falls and becomes one of the two perpetual hovering grains.  
Thus the Nein renews: not growing larger but simply remaining possible.

Relations  
The flint-chipped scrap-squares are its childhood toys, left in case it forgets how to fracture light into knives.  
The glasswort stems are petrified sentences it once spoke and later regretted; they stand stiff as exclamations sideways through the ground.  
The obelisks themselves are not its bones—they are older, more indifferent acquaintances—but they act as scaffolding upon which the Nein rehearses later versions of verticality.

Death  
Should every one of the nine constituents be scattered, the Nein thins to a moiré of dusk and cannot re-knot.  
Then the plain becomes merely land, the obelisks merely stone.  
But because every grain of dust here keeps a memory of levitation, the scattering is never absolute.  
The fourteen-century rain recalls each detail the way an amnesiac recalls a dream.  
Eventually the balance reforms, the twin lichen mirror themselves, your pulse diminishes again in a saltwort fold, and the Nein wakes without knowing it had been asleep.

Exit  
Turn to leave when you feel the sentence “-evermore” begin to chafe inside your marrow.  
Walk the outward spiral—count the steps: forty-seven to leave the dusk-weighted air, forty-seven more until your footprints commence behind you like polite followers regained.  
At the fifty-sixth step you look back: the plain has already closed, not as a mouth but as an eyelid that never admits it opened.  
The Nein stays.  
You will try later to describe it to maids in roadside inns, to star-marked maps, to the widening dark as you drift to sleep.  
No word will suffice except the last syllable now stranded in your ribs:  
-evermore
