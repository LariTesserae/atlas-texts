What dwells in the Choir-Wood are not quite creatures, not quite listeners, but something liminal between throat and tree.  They arrive here—every “they” being negotiable—by first surrendering the certainty that a body ends at skin.

Start near the rimrock where travelers press their backs to stone.  If one of them lingers until midnight, the lattice above will have heard the double heartbeat in his chest—human rhythm, yes, but riding atop a cooler triplet that belongs to the plateau’s own arterial drum.  The echo rouses a disseminator, the being the locals call a Vent-Kin.  

A Vent-Kin has no skeleton.  Instead, it keeps its memories in braided ligaments of green cartilage that flex like oboe reeds.  At rest it hangs curled inside a root that has grown specifically hollow for it—a living resonance box.  Awake, it somersaults out, the root splitting open along pre-scored rills and sealing again behind.  The visible shape is thirty feet from crown to tail-tip, but most of that length is merely trailing membrane—tissue stretched into drumheads stretched again until they veil the air like translucent kites.  The membrane bears tattooed scars in the shape of last night’s thunder.  A traveler can read tomorrow’s weather by running a palm across these scars; if they feel a story, rain is promised; if just a hush, drought is rehearsing.

Vent-Kin feed on the interval between question and answer.  When a lizard chirps and the echo tries to arrive but hasn’t yet, the Vent-Kin unfurls a vacuole that pinches off a droplet of silence no larger than dew.  Swallowed, the droplet detonates as memory inside the Vent-Kin’s chambered gut—memory of the lizard, of the canyon wall, of the listener’s left ear.  Stored long enough, those bite-sized hush-memories ferment into a creamy sap the Vent-Kin later exhales through vents behind its fifth rib, seeding it across the hanging lianas so the whole vegetal floor trembles with newly created tales.  In this way every footstep you once took here will be looping in the stems tomorrow to steady the weave.

Beneath the Vent-Kin, hanging upside-down from latch-vines like rotting fruit, are the Sighers—pale, fleshy bodies as broad as children’s cots, but lymphatic and pliant.  They have no heads; instead a puckered orifice on the sternum opens and closes as they sleep.  Each out-breath is a lullaby at 140 beats per minute, the exact resting pulse of the plateau root.  At dusk they rotate slowly so the root hears the blood inside their veins as a decrescendo gong and responds by sweating resin sweeter than almonds.  Predators avoid them—not out of fear, but because a Sigher’s exhaled lullaby returns all sharp teeth to soft sleep.  A traveler carrying a blade is cautioned never to come closer than the length of an exhalation, lest his metal heart be coaxed into unweaponed surrender.

Higher still, living in the upside-down cloud-sky just below the green canopy-membrane, drift the Soprano Kites.  12-foot dragonflies fashioned out of stiffened leaf and spider silk, they cruise on warm thermals exuded by the wood itself.  Their wings are fluted with acoustic pits; every downdraft tripping across the pits births a perfect F-sharp above high C that skitters like runaway bells.  The species is sexless until paired audio forms an overtone lattice—two Kites circling until their note-carvings interlock into a harmonic moiré.  In the instant of completion a new Kite condenses at the lattice knot: body of cello-back, wing of wind-cleaved leaf, memory of prior songs.  The three then separate upward, tear-shaped holes in their thoraxes already rehearsing the next possible spawn.

Groundward—not “down,” only farther inward—are the Last Conversationalists: human descendants who lost the desire to speak words.  They have traded tongues for resonant cartilage stitched behind the ears.  When they need to complain of hunger, they angle their skulls toward a Viola Liana (a vine whose fibers twist naturally at 440 hertz) and lick once across the inner ridge of their own teeth.  The liana hears the moist click and, moments later, lowers a pod swollen with protein.  One mouth-noise equals one ration.  These survivors cannot speak without giving something away; hence silence is cherished like currency.  But the Wood always repays.  A widower pressed silent for a year lifted his child and heard the boy’s heartbeat spell “I am here.” The Choir recorded the rhythm and produced a flowering that spelled the same phrase in the language of pollen, coating the father’s lips with golden testimony.  

At the very center there is the Chorister itself—only one, possibly the only thing that was ever singular in the Wood.  No one has seen it and remained describable.  They say the Chorister begins at the geologic core of the plateau, the slender taproot that dives past sandstone into the planet’s harmonic mantle.  Everything heard in the Choir-Wood is one of its dream-hiccups.  Occasionally the lattice grows a door no one remembers, round as a cathedral rose window, only the rose is a wound.  Through the wound visitors glimpse scales of sunset breathing; through it the Chorister steps, not forward, but sideways into the story just told—so that if you walk on tomorrow you will meet a version of you who arrived yesterday, humming your footstep backwards.  The mortality that once belonged to you has been transposed into a minor key and sewn into the root-schedule.  Chorister does not mouth greeting; it merely allows the entire Wood to modulate a quarter-tone so each leaf becomes subtly wrong, like seeing your reflection blink a second late.  That modulation lasts one cycle and then the Wood forgets again, remembering the note only in deeper rings, slower than embryos.

Among mortal visitors, a few refuse reintegration.  They seal their lips with hardened sap, learning to move without exhale, becoming something half-rumor, half-moss.  They migrate along the root struts by slithering on their own rolled-up shadow, silently trading pitches with the Vent-Kin for safe passage.  Thunder recognizes them as already digested and ignores their bodies; lightning passes through them like needles through lace.  These Quiet are the only beings that age here, remaining visibly wrinkled against the backdrop of un-aging leaf.  Yet even their wrinkles resonate: when they die, the fold of each crease peels off as a wafer of dried sound, flying off to be eaten by descending lamphrey birds whose gills flutter out triplet arpeggios of farewell.

Thus what “lives” in the Choir-Wood is never quite one species or many: it is a consortium of vibrating parentheses, pauses caught half-spoken between verb and forest.  The plants dabble in animal hunger, the animals tend antiphons of chlorophyll, human mouths seek silence so the Wood can pronounce them.  You do not sight a creature and say “Look, a thing”; you stand still until whatever has borrowed your pulse steps from the blurry margin into the joint custody of ear and bark, and suddenly you cannot describe either.
