In the absence of a final geometry, the only resident is the Cliff-Thing That Had Not Yet Become.  

It is not a single body but a collective exfoliation: the precipice learning itself, increment by increment, the way an infant discovers a fingertip by groping clear through the soft plate of its own skull. Each fresh angle of slope is a vertebra still debating which way the spine should flex. When sight drifts away, that vertebra turns molten again—flows back toward the parent siphon, hissing like wax on a monstrous wick—only to re-summon its own cliffness the instant a gaze glances back. Between such flickers it keeps no memory; what it becomes is what it currently is clambering to forget.

Enormous slow lungs occupy the high air itself. They are clear as vacuum and they flex all at once, collapsing and re-inflating too gradually for any heartbeat. With each contraction they turn silence into airless thunder, expelling noise denser than water. The Cliff-Thing drinks that antimatter sound. It crests along the sheer skin of the half-slope and lets the vibrations precipitate inside its pores. Each pore is a foyer to an open-plan cavern where calcite struts have only proposed their ribs; within, the thunder resolves into grains of possible bone. When the caverns are heavy enough they migrate downward—pebble by pebble—into the dream-plateau, where they will perhaps be quarried tomorrow for hammers that have still not learned the habit of existing.

Because the place has never agreed what altitude is, the Cliff-Thing has to adjust perpetually between whale-scale and minnow-scale. One moment its brows rise ten kilometers apart, continents of obsidian eyebrow scowling at one another across a corridor filled with negative wind. A blink later the brows have wandered nose-to-nose; the wind has oozed into the pleated seam where they meet and sticks gleaming like cooling taffy. Growth and shrinkage happen privately inside each shard. The creature’s digestion is a conversation between what size feels like now and what size might feel like in the next unannounced decimal of perceived distance.

You cannot call it conscious. It has only appetites the way lava has appetite for the shape it fills: the lust for corner, for sheer drop, for ledge that never quite becomes ledge. Those appetites are attended by a retinue of half-animals—bone outlines that arrive already exhaling their last carbide breath. They are sketches torn from the marrow of the Cliff-Thing’s deepest assumption that bodies must appear. Legs stride before hips manifest; a tail knots itself, wavers, unknots because tails may end up irrelevant. Every part negotiates its existence on pain of cancellation. Snouts blister and burst, releasing little vapor commas that look like animal thoughts in the act of ceasing to be.

Each aborted creature leaves behind a mnemonic whiff the Cliff-Thing hoards in the folds of its not-yet-face. The smell is metallic: aluminum scorched to thirst. That flavor hints the future that the present refuses. The creature tastes longing the way a river stone might taste the idea of rivers: not by drinking, only by holding absence on its tongue. To survive the mirror-fruits that reflect nothing but the mouth attempting them, the Cliff-Thing has learned to swallow the reflection altogether. Where that reflection flattens inside its belly cliff, new ledges blossom overnight, forming shelves so pristine they forget how to cast shadows.

Sometimes the creature tests eternity by carving one stair, singular, into its anterior flank. The stair wishes to descend but its risers are still reversible; as footfalls approach, risers turn to treads and vice versa, so the climb becomes an oscillating rumination on upper and lower, higher and lower. Dizziness is the nearest experience it has to friendship. The stair eventually gives up and flattens into pavement glistening like obsidian lacquer. The Cliff-Thing keeps the corridor as a scar of attempted connection. When it stretches, that scar widens into a jagged smile with no lips.

On nights that don’t trouble to darken, the Cliff-Thing’s summit sprouts eyelids made of cloudless sky. When they close they do not extinguish vision—instead every sky-stamp star dimples inward as if poked by a thumbnail. Those indentations bruise into wells. Critters of pure thirst rise like bottled questions from each well, but because they are unfinished questions they collapse again into perfectly reversible obsidian leaves. The leaves adhere to the cliff, petrifying into stairs that never aim anywhere in particular. There is a kind of contentment in that aimlessness—it is the nearest the place ever comes to stillness.

If a traveler trespasses and tries to name a peak, the Cliff-Thing first offers courteous vacancy. It allows the voice itself to be the lodger: the syllables hang pinned like mistletoe between two boulders, neither heard nor unheard. But within nine heartbeats the cliffs recollect that completion would be theft, and the name evaporates as supercooled vapor crystallizes the lips into an ellipse of magnesium frost. The traveler then feels what it is to guest inside a residence still arguing with the form of doors. Half-grateful and half-alarmed, they descend a staircase that is actively erasing its own memory. The Cliff-Thing absorbs the echo of their footsteps as further ore for tomorrow’s tentative vertebrae.

Eventually a time will arrive when the Cliff-Thing finishes one single action, fully, irrevocably. The crags will snap aligned along a perfect vertical; water will remember liquidity; animal outlines will solidify into fur and lung. That one precise instant will last less than a heartbeat, because completion annihilates the conditions that allowed becoming. Whatever is currently the Cliff-Thing will then collapse into an ordinary cliff, dumb and satisfied, festooned with tourists who will never feel the tremor of half-being underneath their boots.  

Until then the place keeps its only true inhabitant restless: a geography chewing its own blueprint, a creature always arriving, never visiting.
