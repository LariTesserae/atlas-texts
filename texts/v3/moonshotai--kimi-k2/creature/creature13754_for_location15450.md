In this theatre of amethyst air and raw basalt, life is still improvising syntax.

– – –

Look first at the spine-flowers. They ride the cliff-face two hand–spans above one’s head, clinging with roots so fine they seem braided from frozen spider silk. Their blossoms are mere thimbles of midnight plum, each rim fretted white by frost crystals so regularly spaced they might be hand-stitched pearls. They open only when an invisible threshold of light passes through a fresh flake of basalt, so each newcomer to the crag experiences the opening as a hiss of scent — crystallised star-anise — released against their cheek alone. The moment a blossom sheds its first pollen grain the entire stem melts into obsidian varnish, leaving only a lacquer scar recording the exact frame of its brevity. Ten heartbeats later, seedlings no bigger than eyelashes press out of that gloss, chasing the same seam of light east along the rock. They are therefore always fleeing the parent death that begat them; they are migrants who carry the archaeology of their own birth in a trailing skein of glass beads the colour of deep water under moon.

They are looked after by the Quiet Cutters. Nobody has seen a whole one; they appear only by parts. A matte-black claw fishes for ants under a shard, then withdraws into nothing. A crescent of tinder-fur, edged with frost feathers, hovers beside a clot of lichen long enough for the lichen’s deepest scarlet to leach into it like dye in water — then the cloth snaps itself shut and is simply wind again. Their faces, glimpsed as kaleidoscopic fragments in the bright needles they occasionally leave behind, suggest a fraternity of ancient miners whose lantern glass was never lined with silver but with night itself. They perform small ornamental acts: engraving a thumb-wide groove so that meltwater flows toward the seedlings, snapping off a sliver of basalt to expose black milk warmed by the planet’s belly, pruning the over-reaching antlers of frost with a clipping pair of beaks made from glacier ice. The Cutters do not keep their garden; the garden teaches the Cutters how to belong in dimensions still unfurling.

Microscopic masons with bodies like translucent needle-case worms stitch calcium stolen from the cliff plates into paper-thin tunnels between leaves and stone. Their gut bacteria glow pale teal; at dusk the ledges shine like stained glass stacked a finger’s width deep, flinging auroral ribbons into the sky. When the outer basalt finally flakes away the tunnel-lights wink out — stars guttered behind corroded paper — and mother rock falls forward into the void trailing a fibre-optic spine that sways until wind breaks it. That discarded glowing thread lands on moss below, where it is taken as treasure by snow-silver snails that keep it coiled beneath their shells for the forty days of moonlight they expect to live. The light slowly dissolves into the snail’s slime, and any track it writes thereafter will fluoresce for the next three nights, insurance that predators misread the path entirely.

Down in the shadow fittings of the lower buttress lives a single strand-beast no thicker than rope. It resembles a millipede made of translucent chitin, but every segment is hinged to eleven more, each in another plane; no part of the rope can lie flat upon stone for more than a heartbeat before a fold flexes and curls it elsewhere. Its eyes are everywhere — punctuation, tiny ghost clocks of hematite. It moves only at a fraction above absolute stillness, mapping thermoclines, tasting spinoff minerals not yet named. At the moment it reads a lattice in the rock predestined to avalanche within a month, it gives birth by splitting precisely midway, and the two halves walk away in opposite centuries. Nothing eats it; researchers who glimpse it forget the alphabet two days later.

Where the cliff-skin is still releasing steam, the mist holds a parliament of half-formed things. Vapour toys with hammers, glimpses of fox masks, portable doorways. These are the conceptions of future residents — not spirits but drafts of what could become spirits if enough stories are told to them. They weigh the moment, smelling for a mind that’ll confess their possibility into momentum. Perhaps you will be the one who says, “In the month of sapphire frost a copper boat navigates the river of steam,” and therefore the boat will pull itself together from airborne oxide, plate by articulate plate, until it drifts six inches above the stone with oars of gradual coral. All residents know that naming is provisional, that a phrase allowed to linger may harden into architecture the wind must negotiate for centuries.

Between stem and stone flit the Sky Skrimmers. Less than lice in silhouette, they are nonetheless scale-shifters; when they perceive moisture condensing on a seed-case they balloon ten-thousand-fold, thinning to made-of-shadow. Their wings become retinal blades that can catch a full sunrise and bruise it lilac. In their bloomed state they draught the cliff’s scent history and compress it into oil-like droplets stored under the wings’ seamed veins. When shrank back down they carry these memories embedded in their bodies to crevices where roots explore hairline fissures. A seed that drinks such memory begins germinating not its own tomorrow but the yesterday of a comet shot of ages past. Thus every stem upon the plateau sings in different geologic dialect.

Bind it all: a population no census will ever master, living in perpetual rehearsal, barefoot on glass parchment the continent is still pressing into existence.
