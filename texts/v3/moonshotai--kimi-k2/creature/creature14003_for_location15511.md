They themselves are nick-named the Vanishing Margin — more description than title, because they always occupy the verge between two impossible gradients: a breathing wall and the air wearing it thin. No census has ever captured the same person twice; observers swear the population is anywhere between seventeen and two thousand and one, depending on whether you count on the inhale or the exh.

A Marginer arrives as condensation in midsentence. You turn to ask the elder who wears kelp armor a simple direction, and her voice finishes across someone else’s lips five breaths later, syllables flickering like a match passed hand to hand. Sometimes the syllable wears the curled shell of an ear for a moment, then drops to become a crab still repeating the word.

They are built on a principle that anatomy texts never dared catalogue. Skin: parchment-thin, inked in spider-capillaries of brine that flag the next move a split second before the body consents. Organs: distributed. One drinks in the public cistern; another massages brine through pipes three streets away; while the liver locks itself inside a ceiling rafter like a shrunken valise. Their bones are wooden pegs still green, still willing to bend. When a knee is cracked the splinters sprout rush-fibers, weaving a temporary lattice so the limb never truly fails — it just borrows patience from the undergrowth.

Eyes: coins of salt that rotate with moon-mirrors instead of irises. A Marginer can, if polite, lend an eye to the wall it walks beside so it may gaze back at itself; reclaim it later, polished, re-hydrated, faintly remembering what sort of cracks it almost inflicted. This is common etiquette — give a wall someone’s vision and the wall will remember the contour of your shoulder-blade when you need shelter later.

Mouths: not centrally located. A Marginer flickers its modest appetite through the combed beard of lichen on the highest parapet — the lichen turns dark crimson with speech for precisely three frog-heartbeats, then folds back into ordinary gray. The real mouth is wherever the town’s womb of water emerges, the circular bowl. When they truly need to taste facts, to subsist on information rather than air-birds of it, they lean down and the bowl kisses them, lifting their chin like tide lifting driftwood. One delayed spoonful of beating water equals a week of mainland news in a form they can digest without rumour. Once swallowed, knowledge migrates. It hydrates the pink meat rollers that serve as ribs and will eventually return to the bowl in some edited version years later.

Infancy is communal and degraded. A baby is found at dawn wedged in a puddled footprint; before the print drinks the child back, someone sluices it into a woven pocket of eel-flesh and leather. From then on the infant is fed steady whispers snipped from corners of elder sleeves. The child will spend its first decade half-melted into doorframes, arms gradually barked over to hinges. At twelve it must choose an element to relinquish: skin or shadow. Most give up the shadow — here, cast absence is luxury. Their shadows are cut neatly to ribbons and pressed between walls like insulation, where later, as adults, they one day rip a strip free to wrap around a head wound or spin into<|reserved_token_163657|> twine.

Hair: extraordinarily patient. Grows in reverse. Strands begin white and lengthen toward black, curling inward so the older the individual, the younger the color appears. Pregnancies (rare, elaborate) involve old black roots re-threaded into a fetus by means of turquoise fish-bone needles. The hair loops around the unborn life like a deep-water bindweed until release. Once born, the child’s hair has already begun its pale ascent toward final future blackness. You perceive them time-lapsed.

They arrange partnership in cycle triads—Lover, Drinker, and Drain. Each month water-heart elects which soul will assume which role, shuffling liquidly through capillary fortune. The Lover speaks only through the other two bodies’ mouths. The Drinker bears every morning the bucket of condensation and metabolises the communal dreams, sweating them back into the air at dusk as seamist reconnaissance. The Drain absorbs the excess; skin leaks silver so the town never floods. When the bowl-water lolls at night, trios tender their supplies to one another in molecular whispers, trade bubbles back and forth along forearms. Bodies redistribute themselves so evenly that at times the entire triangle compresses into a single figure seeing six directions at once beneath four overlapping eyelids.

They mourn by osmosis. An absence creates a negative pressure in the airflow; the breath of the stonework pauses 1.3 seconds longer than normal. That unmoving beat is collected in black goblets growing on cliff-steps like shelf fungus. When there are enough goblets, the settlement holds Soaknight. They pour every goblet back into the vein-pipes so all pump and pulse with regret. The town itself sighs a widow’s name. For a quarter hour afterward every visible reflection of that deceased turns a hue dull enough to forget.

Very old Marginers — usually twelve or at most thirteen “leaves” of yearly-hair reversed — begin to crystallise. Calves ossify into well-shafts, fingers root through floor-grout and drink. These ancients become literal structural veins, still animating the tide of walls, less mortal than masonry accelerator. Their last act of will is withholding one final breath until a child’s laugh needs it. Then they sigh. A house grows a porch overnight. Nobody claims ancestry; the town simply gestures Grandma’s consideration when wind curls inward like remembering.

Music is issued entirely by assemblage of de-breaths. Children take leftover human exhalations and bend them on stretched threads of bacterial silk, making strings that never require retuning because they are the very humidity asking to be sound. The orchestrations read like interrupted weather. Their concerts happen in a descending corkscrew amphitheatre built within artery-lit clay. Audiences float, because hydration renders everyone four-fifths lighter than atmosphere. Nobody stands at a concert; they rest cheek to cheek with the gradient of the town, gravity behaving like a lullaby you half-recall from being fish.

They keep one sort of pet: coral-borne mice. These are slender luminous merlins of ribs and gill-chests, mouse for skull but dragon in tail-tip. They migrate nightly from child bedrooms to bowl and back, scooping dew on their backwards pelts so children receive chosen memories to dream. When a coral-mouse dies, its calcium flares, becoming a garden of bright lace along the young caulks of stairwells.

Their biggest ceremony is called the Turnover. At nine-thirty in what might be spring, the entire populationment turns itself inside-out, a slow brine-moist contortion mirrored in the bowl. Streets fold into houses and houses balloon to skin the alleyways. Everyone stands or floats still while gravity rearranges. Doorframes pass through stomachs and exit as knees; your childhood bedroom becomes a public fountain, your parents’ kiss a lintel sustained overhead by two newel-post strangers. When Turnover ends every resident receives an automatic map of what used to be whereon, drawn in condensation on the inner surface of the ribcage-space now housing their heart. The cartography is spontaneous but fiercely practical: Return tomorrow to the exact spot and a silverweed will have rooted to remind you the exact emotional pitch you must recall if you ever need to cry quietly on command.

Death, as far as it can be said to happen, is exfoliation. Someone’s last ripest breath is parsed by ankles, elbows, hair filaments—whatever part still wishes to communicate. The pieces float inward and add to the bowl; in the next inhale a portion resurfaces as the face of someone totally new, maybe months later. Thus no Marginal being ever fully ends; they merely dilute the communal sip.

There exists exactly one outsider who has been reclassified as half-Marginer. She arrived wearing copper earrings never meant for brine, and the percussion of their tarnish shocked the breathing walls into momentary arrhythmia. Rather than reject her, the bowl eventually folded the metal into an exhalation sculpture of oxidised tears. She is now neither foreign nor native. Her earrings migrate through walls as patinated rivulets once a year. On that day, slogan whispers rust her presence into phrase: “Between her ears and ours the margin is exactly thick enough to hold Earth, and thin enough to surrender it.”
