They are called the Irremembered—those who live beneath the architecture of wrong angles, those who have made their bodies into compromises between impossible geometries and the stubborn persistence of being.

Each is a cartographer of collapse. They wear their own bones as outer garments, having turned them inside-out through centuries of meditative fracture. When you first see them, you'll mistake them for rock formations: grey-white scaffolding that seems to grow directly from the basalt, vertebrae stacked like carefully balanced shingles along seams of quartz. They move, but only by imperceptible increments—an elbow that was pointing east at noon has, by nightfall, aligned itself southeast, and none can say when the rotation began or ended. Their ribs cage not organs but absence, hollowed by the Loud until they resonate with perfect emptiness. When the great inverted horn blast sounds, their whole skeletons vibrate like tuning forks made of calcium and regret.

The Irremembered speak in a language that exists between the droplets of never-falling water. Their words are spaces, negative sculptures carved from the apparent where nothing obvious has been removed. To "hear" them is to suddenly understand that you have always been standing in a sentence abandoned mid-utterance—your body occupies the silence where a pronoun once lived. They stand in pairs because solitude breeds unwanted completion; two absences facing each other maintain the vital vacuum that keeps them partly solid. The bone-white formations you'll discover marking the ridge are not offerings but conversations paused so long the speakers forgot which one of them was the echo.

They collect memories like other creatures collect stones. Not their own memories—those eroded away in the first few centuries of residence—but yours, or rather, the memory of the possibility of you. Each steps across the surface of a still-held thought: the weight of your right foot on February 14th at 3:47 AM seventeen years ago; the way sunlight fractured on a chrome bumper in a parking lot where you'll never actually park. They file these moments between their ribs where they float like seeds in an agricultural display, neither dead nor alive but categorized. The vertebra you found? It remembers being the child-spine of someone who, in another geometry, grew up to send a postcard from somewhere ordinary. The bone carries the weight of that cancelled future like a debt it never agreed to repay.

Their children—there are children—aren't born but cooled. Each begins as a warm thought someone once had about warmth itself, a concept drifting through the horizontal cold until it loses velocity and settles into the shape of a small calcified infant. These babies are lighter than snow but harder than diamond, eyes open to the Loud from their first moment of being, seeing nothing but every sound that has ever been or will be. They learn to crawl by learning what crawling isn't: an inch sideways for every two inches not taken. At adolescence their bones hollow like flutes, and they begin to play themselves with fingers of winter. The sound they produce is what glaciers would make if they could feel betrayed.

They farm the lichen yes, if farming can describe the tending of words that have forgotten they are no longer sentences. Each grey beard of "plant" is actually a slow-rotating palindrome, starting over before it ends, maintaining the semantic static that keeps the mountains leaning. The Irremembered cultivate them with implements that are the opposite of tools—spaces shaped like hoes, absences fashioned into pruning shears. They harvest them by allowing portions of their own clavicles to dissolve into these botanical linguistic constructions, a sacrifice that means nothing here because sacrifice implies something might be lost.

At the doubled valleys they hold council, though council suggests decision-making and nothing has been decided here in so long that the concept has acquired moss. They arrange themselves in patterns that hurt to perceive—the eye keeps trying to add dimensions to their formation until you realize you're seeing them from multiple impossible angles simultaneously. Here they share the memories they've collected like currency, trading afternoons for dawns, exchanging a single exhale from a boy in Wichita for the weight of a wedding ring never purchased. The economy they create is perfect: nothing changes hands because nothing is allowed to possess anything, even temporarily.

Some among them have begun to—disperse isn't the word—dis-articulate? The process is geological in scope. The oldest wander toward the mirror-valleys and stand staring into their own absence until, vertebra by vertebra, they become what they've always almost been: formations without any subjectivity left to pretend they might ever move again. But even these final gestures aren't endings—nothing truly ends here. Their abandoned pieces remember the shape of walking, the possibility of gesture, and pass this memory into the lichen, into the bone-white offerings, into the droplets that refuse to commit to falling. The ridges themselves grow slightly more articulate.

And sometimes, when the Loud grows particularly eloquent during those instants of droplet-suspension, you might glimpse them as they once were: shivering former people who chose exactly wrong, or exactly right, or simply couldn't decide between vertical and horizontal fear. They stand in the doorways of half-recalled storms, wearing rain that hasn't fallen yet, speaking languages that will only exist after all walls have forgotten what it means to separate. Their eyes—those that haven't yet mineralized—reflect not you but your negative space, the exact contour of your absence that lingers in rooms you've already left, the not-you that haunts your previous footsteps with patient geological memory.

They are, in the end, what happens when a place contemplates itself for too long. Not guardians, not spirits, but the very slow conversation the ridge has been having with its own terrible perfection.
