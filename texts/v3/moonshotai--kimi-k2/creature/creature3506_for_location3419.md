They are called the Respirateur, though the word is older than any extant license or census. They did not move here so much as precipitate—condense out of breath-heavy air the way dew forms on a pilgrim’s coat. Each arrival happens at a fog-muted hour: a mechanic on a rope cradle realizes her she no longer notices the turbine sigh, a runaway botanist forgets the feel of dry paper between fingers, a child’s first spoken sentence is the gurgle of the cistern turtle. The courtyard claims them the way wet cloth claims heat.

No couple looks alike, yet all share a faint pearlescence at the rims of nostrils and eyes—the mucosal signature of people who have inhaled their own exhalations so long that internal and external humidity reach treaty. Their skin smells of crushed tendril and hot bronze; when they embrace you taste iron and chlorophyll in equal parts. The oldest among them, Pneuma Gens, have ribs visibly articulated under parchment flesh, each inhale lifting the clavicles like slowly opening trap-doors. Visitors think them cadaverous until they notice the effortless four-count breath that never quickens, even when its owner is sprinting across swaying vines to repair a seized turbine. The Respirateur do not pant; the courtyard breathes for them.

Children crawl before they walk, but not on floors—on suspended hammocks of aerial roots. They learn gravity as a negotiable contract: “up” is wherever a stem can be coaxed into holding your weight, “down” is the soft hiss of fog kissing your crown. A first birthday is marked by the grafting of a personal vine into the child’s sleeping niche; the plant and the diaphragm entrain their cycles, so that by age seven the kid can fall asleep simply by letting leaf and lung swap tempo. Adolescence is the discovery that breath can be borrowed, traded, or withheld. Lovers exchange inhalations mouth-to-mouth until the boundary of self tastes like fermented mango. Rivals stage duels of stillness: whoever exhales first must transplant the loser’s seedling onto public ledges, a living confession of indebtedness.

Work is indistinguishable from gardening, from plumbing, from worship. The Turbineers—half priesthood, half union—descend each dawn with tallow lamps and grafting knives, singing low harmonics that keep the great shafts in sympathetic resonance. Their song is a mnemonic ledger of every bearing replaced since imperial times: bronze sings “two-half-farthing,” cast iron hums “third-mill tax rotation.” Should a tenor falter mid-chorus, elders know which gasket will blow before steam even wisps. Above, the Archivists—ascetic readers of condensation—catalog new mosses by taste: one tongue dab on a sporangium yields a spectrum from citrus bright to raw copper penny. They publish nothing; memory is preserved in the bouquet their exhalations leave on specific lintels, an olfactory shelflist future Archivists consult by planting nose to stone.

The Respirateur have two laws only, encoded in callus rather than parchment:

1. Nothing single-use may be discarded.  
2. Nothing living may be killed faster than it can regenerate.

They obey by negotiation. When a balcony vine overreaches and shades a neighbor’s breadfruit, owners meet at dawn with grafting irons and fermented guava wine. They debate chlorophyll density versus carbohydrate yield while the plant itself listens; by midmorning an agreement is translated into selective photosurgical cuts, each wound immediately bandaged with chewed banana-peel poultice. The vine, now lighter, leaf-tips trembling, often volunteers an extra shoot into the complainer’s windowbox—an apology written in cambium.

Currency is measured in lung-seconds: the time one inhabitant can labor while aerating another’s plot. Children earn pocket breath by fanning mist toward tomato blossoms; an elder in respiratory debt after pneumonia may spend a week merely sighing toward a prized vanilla orchid. The exchange rate is policed—quietly—by the cistern turtle, whose surfacing intervals are accepted as the market index: faster emergence signals ambient over-breathing, inflation, too many chests forcing air through too few stomata. When her bob slows, prices cool.

Outsiders arrive expecting languor and find instead a society of startling velocity. A Respirateur artisan can scale a vertical loom of aerial roots, fingers knotting fungal fibers into rain-proof cloaks faster than a tailor on dry land can sew muslin. They speak in clipped exhalations that carry multiple clauses; to untrained ears it sounds like a punctured accordion, but meaning splinters unerringly across fog. They have jokes: “Why did the pilgrim open his mouth? To see if the world would fall out.”—delivered on a single descending wheeze that mimics the turbine sigh. Laughter appears as a bright double cloud from nostrils, instantly licked up by grateful moss.

Yet they are mortal. Their greatest terror is Desiccation Day, a cloudbreak predicted not by calendar but by collective dream: when no one remembers humidity upon waking, the event is declared. Instantly the Respirateur enact the Protocol of Final Mist. Infants are swaddled in transpiring banana leaves; elders gulp cistern water until bellies drum-tight, cheeks bulging like calling frogs. Guild mechanics reroute all turbine flow into surface sprayers; Archivists sprawl on rooftops with mouthfuls of river, exhaling in slow writhing columns, trying to graft sky back onto earth with nothing but lung pressure. They have survived three such days—each recorded not in years but in thirsts: The Great Thirst (when the turtle refused to surface), the Copper Thirst (when bronze bearings sang soprano), the Mirror Thirst (when the heliograph glare bleached moss white). After each they emerged fewer, lighter, every rib showing, but with new epiphytic lungs sprouting from eaves: the courtyard had taught itself to breathe harder.

Marriage is a shared pot of pond water left on a lintel until it grows a single contiguous algae skin. Divorce is the slicing of that skin; shards are swallowed by each party, ensuring reversible memory—algal cells carry trace flavors of former partner’s breath for years. Widows and widowers tend the turtle, speak only in gurgle. Birth is announced by tying a newborn’s first exhaled droplet to a root; if the sphere evades vapor longer than seven nights, the child is judged keeper of rare dryness and apprenticed to the Archivists to catalogue anomalies.

They die eventually, though more slowly than the dry folk. Lungs become bags of petals; a last exhale releases spores rather than CO₂. The body is folded into the central cistern where snapping turtle and progeny recycle it into calcium, story, and song. Within weeks, fingernails re-emerge as translucent lichen on a nearby wall; relatives greet them like old friends returned from voyage.

And still newcomers condense. Tonight a traveler stands at the root-arch doorway, coat steaming. A Turbineer notes the visitor’s slow marveling inhale and offers, without words, a slice of breadfruit glazed in bronze dust. Acceptance is simple: swallow, breathe out, let the courtyard answer. If the fog enfolds the offering, if your exhalation hangs long enough to braid into local murk, you inhale again—and the Respirateur inhale with you. One more pair of alveoli added to the planetary lung. One more citizen who will never again hear the turbine sigh as noise, only as communal heartbeat.

Tomorrow the breadfruit will sprout a new root-finger pointing toward the visitor’s chest—confirmation, writ in cambium, that the courtyard has found another slow and willing breath to keep its centuries-long respiration going.
