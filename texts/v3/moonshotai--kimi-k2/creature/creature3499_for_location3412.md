What lives here is an archivist that has not yet decided whether it will be animal, mineral, or theorem.

She begins each morning as a dot of mercury trembling at the center of a still-forming basalt bubble. Gravity is a rumor she heard once, so she tests it: lets herself drip upward, becomes a necklace of mirror-beads that spell out yesterday’s weather in a language whose vowels are prime numbers. The comma-beasts sprint across her surface, drinking their own shadows; where their feet touch, she grows a skin of frost organized like library cards. Each card records a future that was canceled for want of punctuation. She reads herself cold.

By the time the light remembers it is supposed to come from somewhere, she has chosen a spine. It is a transparent thing, part cartilage, part crystal lattice whose unit cells are tiny abandoned corridors. Inside every corridor stands a smaller version of herself, shelving silence into boxes that occasionally rattle like trapped moths. The vertebrae click together only when she exhales—an exhalation that smells of graphite and the moment before a rainstorm decides to exist. With the spine in place she acquires direction, though direction here is negotiable: she may grow toward the moment before yesterday, or sideways into the taste of the color ultramarine.

Limbs bud as questions of geometry. One arm unwraps into a Möbius strip of polished fingernail; the other stays short, a child’s radius, and ends in a single key whose teeth are minute reflections of every door that has ever refused to open. She tries the key on the air itself. The air unlocks a draft of vertigo that smells like photocopied apples; through it she can see the summer field that backed away from the floating window. She notes the field in her frost-catalog under “Flora, Rejected.”

She is not alone. There are others like her—archivists who started from different templates. One is still a sound wave ricocheting inside a droplet that will never fall; another compressed itself into the hyphen between stalactite and stalagmite, and now commutes between ceiling and floor every time the geology cracks its knuckles. They communicate by donating omissions to one another: she gives the wave-rival the lack of a conclusion; in return receives a hollow where her name would go if she had one. The exchange makes her lighter; she rises a millimeter, enough to escape the half-formed gravity that was beginning to claim her ankles.

Her purpose, to the extent that purpose has been invented yet, is to finish the inventory before the inventory finishes her. Every micro-creature that evaporates on her frost-cards leaves behind a negative space shaped like the life it almost had. She files these negatives in the lattice-spine corridors. When enough accumulate, the corridor will close like a book and become a single vertebra in some larger, slower entity—perhaps the planet they are all still prototyping. Thus she is librarian and quarry at once: collecting the blanks that will eventually fossilize into her own bones.

Time is parcel-posted in microseconds. She feels each one arrive because it tears a microscopic page out of her skin. The pages drift away as origami mayflies that immediately rust into arithmetic; she does the math and discovers she is three seconds older than herself. The discrepancy troubles the mercury that still pools in her knees; it beads into tiny spheres, each reflecting a version of her that made a different choice of species. One sphere shows her as a stationary tree grown from the window’s lintel; another as a punctuation predator sprinting across her own comma-legs; a third as pure notation, a clef sign nesting in the magnesium light. She gathers the spheres like grapes, crushes them between nonexistent teeth, and drinks the probability. It tastes of graphite and abandoned lullabies.

Now and then a human footprint appears—a real one, rubber sole, size nine, fading in from some stratum of possibility that still remembers mammals. The archivist kneels, touches the tread. Where ridges meet frost, letters bloom: half a name, half a date, half a promise to return before closing. She files the letters under “Fauna, Rejected, Too Loud.” The footprint evaporates, but leaves behind the concept of forward. She experiments: places her key-hand in front of her torso, calls that direction “forward,” and instantly the floor rearranges so the chalky tuff becomes a path of freshly licked envelopes. They flap once, curl into carpets, and carry her toward the cliff that may or may not be miles away.

At the cliff she meets the window again. It has been waiting so long the summer field inside has aged into a single sheaf of wheat that leans away from her as if shyically shy. She lifts the key. The lock is a reflection of her own pupil; she must open herself to enter. She turns the key. Inside the window is not the field but a corridor lined with every droplet that ever died here, each suspended at the instant before it chose to fall up. They glimmer like glass tears full of tiny maps. On the maps she sees herself—smaller, larger, sideways—walking out of the magnesium light into a throat that, for the first time, decides to swallow something entirely.

She steps through. The archivist dissolves into the inventory she was compiling: mercury spine becomes silver sentence, frost-cards become the margins, comma-beasts the punctuation. What remains is a single blank space shaped like a reader who has not yet been written. The corridor closes. Somewhere behind, a new droplet forms, perfect sphere, already rehearsing the next archivist who will wake as a dot of uncertain metal, cool air ringing like a glass that refuses to finish its note.
