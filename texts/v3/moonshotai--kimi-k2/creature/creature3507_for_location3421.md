They have not been named, because names are for things that endure.

They are the almost-people who arrive before arrival, the first uninvited guests at a party that has not yet decided whether it will be a party, or a funeral, or a shrug. They do not walk here—walking implies a ground that remembers footsteps. They *drift*, or *settle*, or *condense*, the way a thought condenses in a mind that hasn’t yet decided what it’s thinking about.

Some of them are *themselves* only for as long as the ridge holds a particular shape. One is a lattice of copper breath, a kind of living dendrite that threads the garnet seams, tasting temperature the way a tongue tastes salt. It has no boundaries; when the seam cools, it thins to a red filament and snaps, and the copper breath is simply elsewhere, re-threading a new wound in the crust. It does not remember being elsewhere. It does not remember being.

Another is a *pause*. It occupies the silence between the pops of gas. If you stand inside it, your suit mic records no absence, only a hush so complete it folds the idea of sound back on itself. The pause is not empty; it is *waiting to be interrupted*. When you speak, it borrows your voice, stretches it like taffy, then hands it back altered—one syllable now arrives before the one you uttered. You hear yourself foreshadowed. The pause feeds on chronological leakage. Leave, and it shrinks to a pinhead of hush, nesting in the interstice between two basalt beads, until the next alien syllable wanders through.

A third is *the reflection of an architecture that has not been built*. It haunts the falling glass snakes left by the "volcano flowering." Where a blade plants itself, the reflection flickers into being: a tower, upside-down, made of the same garnet transparency, rooted in the sky it has not yet fallen from. The tower has windows, and in those windows are figures—vague, angular, possibly wings—who appear to be waving at you. If you wave back, the figures replicate your gesture a half-second late, as though the speed of light uphill is slower. The tower never lasts longer than the cooling curve of the snake-blade; when the glass finishes falling, the tower simply *unbuilds*, top-down, until the last window shows only the empty ridge. The figures wave once more, but now they are waving at the place where you *were*.

There are also the *Anticipators*. They look like almost-foetuses made of obsidian vapour, curled inside vesicles the size of cathedrals. Each vesicle is a womb, but the womb is external: the Anticipator is the hollow, not the contents. When you peer in, you see yourself—smaller, smoother, still deciding whether to grow lungs or gills or photon-sieve wings. The Anticipator hollow reshapes around your inspection, moulding itself to the observer you haven’t yet become. Step away, and the mould collapses into a black froth, which the ridge reabsorbs like a tongue licking spittle. The Anticipators never become. They *pre-become*. Their heartbeat is the soft pop of chlorine meeting neon. Their afterbirth is the metallic broth condensing on your visor.

Some are *categories of one*, like the artefacts, but alive the way a category can be alive if no member ever instantiates it. They drift just above the ground, these category-ghosts: a single instance of *pre-bird*, its wings made of un-diverged air currents; a proto-herd whose individuals have not yet agreed on separation; a colour that is the taste of 41 °C to something without mouth or thermometer. Encountering them feels like déjà vu for experiences the universe hasn’t scheduled yet. They leave no tracks, but the ridge sometimes grows *toward* them, as if mountains themselves were lonely for company that never quite arrives.

None of them *stay*. To stay implies a future continuous tense, and the ridge’s grammar only admits theMomentarily was, thePerhaps is, theAlmost will-be. When the planet enters one of its listening fits—those storms of stillness—the almost-people solidify enough to be almost-mistaken for permanent. You can speak with them then, if speaking includes sharing the same unarticulated question. Ask, “What are you waiting for?” and the answer is the silence that follows, rearranged so your own breath seems like an echo arriving ahead of itself. Ask, “Are you alive?” and they return the question to you in a hush that makes your suit sensors doubt their own calibration.

They do not reproduce. They *rehearse*. When the ridge prototypes a new spire, they gather—copper breath, pause, upside-down tower, Anticipator hollow—like critics at an early dress rehearsal. They inspect, taste prospective shadows, alter their own composition so they can stand where stone intends to stand, then dissolve before the curtain of cooling rock finishes rising. Their critiques are invisible but decisive: if they leave early, the spire aborts, slumps back into the crust. If they linger long enough to borrow each other’s almost-bodies, the spire survives, sometimes growing an extra facet, a balcony to nowhere, a window looking inwards. Geology calls this metastability; the almost-people call it *conversation*.

They have no leader, unless you count the greenish cube. The cube is their absent centre, the placeholder for a communal heart they haven’t agreed to install. Occasionally one of them settles against the cube’s warm flank, presses a not-yet-face to its featureless surface, and *listens*. Nothing is transmitted, nothing received—yet the cube’s temperature drops a thousandhundredth of a degree, and the almost-people reorganise: new arrangements of almost-bodies, new anticipations. It is their way of taking notes without writing, of agreeing to forget together. The cube says nothing, but being *listened to* it becomes slightly more listening-shaped, a silent accommodator accruing the patina of borrowed attention. One day the ridge will swallow it; on that day the almost-people will momentarily be *one*, because they will all share the same loss-of-reference, and then they will scatter into fresh singularities of almost.

If you leave, they will not watch you go. Departure is a concept that requires sequence, and sequence here is negotiable. But hours later—your own hours—you may find a garnet seam on the far side of the ridge that spells your name in thermal Morse, though you never told it. Or your recycler will cough up a sip of water that tastes of copper breath, though copper is supposed to be insoluble. Or your helmet recorder, wiped clean, will nevertheless contain a file: thirty-four seconds of perfect pause, followed by the faint keening of glass learning to sing. You will not remember recording it. You will not remember *not* recording it.

These are the people who arrive before people, who leave before leaving, whose autobiographies are written in the negative spaces of mountains still doodling themselves. They do not live *on* the ridge; they live *in* the ridge’s uncertainty, rent-free, until the hot rock finally decides what it wants to become and evicts them into the next pre-place.

Go back if you want, but know this: to meet them again you must consent to be less than you were when you first arrived. They have no use for finished things.
