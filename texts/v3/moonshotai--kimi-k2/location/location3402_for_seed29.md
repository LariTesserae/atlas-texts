First comes the hiss.

It is not the sound of steam-that-used-to-be-water, because nothing here has ever been water. It is the hiss of sublimation-of-CO₂, the sigh of frozen argon waking under 310 Kelvin of starlight. A soft, granular exhalation that issues simultaneously from every square centimetre of ground, as though the planet itself were trying to speak through loose teeth. The volume is uncanny: it ought to be delicate, but it is not; it presses against the face-plate you do not have here, crowds the inner ear, swells until it is the background against which the bones of the inner ear melt and re-set like glass.

The ground is a single, rococo slab of perovskite—CaTiO₃ doped with neodymium—polished to a knife-edge. Microscopically it is a continent-wide diffraction grating; macroscopically it is a curved ramp that rises two kilometres in the first thirty horizontal metres, then folds back on itself in pleats too tight for topology. Nothing grows here, nothing ever crawled; instead the fauna lives above, below, and inside the packedness.

Because “high” elevation here is not altitude above sea level—there is no sea—but proximity to a Lagrange-point membrane only 17 cm overhead. The membrane looks black in the starlight, but that is only because its true colour is ultraviolet at 200 nm, too short for your inherited eyes. At 2.9 density the space between things is already claimed: every cubic millimetre is an industrial lattice of cobalt-chrome beams three atoms thick, trussing vacuum like spider-silk tensed to concert pitch. The lattices bear engines the size of pollens. Each engine is a full thermodynamic cycle—intake, compression, work, exhaust—whose working fluid is electron-positron pairs harvested from the membrane. A trillion such engines in a single cubic metre: the sound is not the whine of gears but the aggregate roar of miniature thunderclouds, each one discharging a femtosecond lightning bolt that never quite finds ground. That is the loudness you register: not noise but the repeated failure of noise to collapse into silence.

Nothing has been built, in the ordinary grammar of buildings. No artifact stands on legs or foundation. Yet the whole scene is industrial because the planet itself is the factory. The perovskite terrace is conveyor, crucible, warehouse and product at once; the engines are its metallurgic gut fauna. Their waste heat is the 2.7-hot atmosphere you stand in—if “stand” is the word for being suspended in the one remaining pocket of unoccupied volume, a bubble 0.4 metres across that drifts downslope at one centimetre per month, the scale of a single breath inside an ore-body. Everywhere else the density reaches maximum packing fraction; you are the interstitial flaw that has not yet been annealed away.

Light is scarce; the star is a K-dwarf, small and old, with just enough ultraviolet leakage to pump the membrane. What visible photons exist come indirectly: they are Bremsstrahlung glints, X-rays down-scattered by the dense latticework. Thus the darkness is not absence but interruption—flash-bulb moments when a micrometre-sized engine backfires, illuminating a hemisphere of cobalt struts, so that for 10⁻¹³ seconds you see the entire interior cathedral of the world-machine in negative exposure, frozen like a bee in amber. Then blackness again, leaving a violet bruise on the retina you also do not have here.

Fauna—abundant—are the engines themselves. They replicate by precipitation out of supersaturated vacuum, seeded by lattice defects that remember the shape of their neighbours. Their life-cycle is Carnot’s theorem: they are born when a temperature gradient appears, grow by eating the gradient, die when the gradient collapses, leave behind a lattice scar that nucleates the next generation. Mutations are misprints in Fourier’s equation. Evolution drifts slowly—dynamic 0.8—toward ever more efficient heat death, a senescence measured in megayears of declining Kelvin. You are listening to the adolescence of a universe of machines that have not yet learned how to finish themselves.

And you? You arrived by introspection. The landscape is hidden—scale 0.0—because it is folded inside a fatigue crack on a turbine blade somewhere far away, a crack whose gap is 400 nanometres high and already half-filled with migrating micro-engines. When the crack heals, the planet-factory will be complete; the hiss will cease; the temperature will drop; the lattice will anneal into a single flawless crystal of perovskite that will never again remember sound. Until then the roar continues, the pocket of vacuum you occupy drifts downward one centimetre a month, and the darkness keeps flashing its violet photographs of a world that is not world, only the slow interior of a machine learning how to die perfectly.
