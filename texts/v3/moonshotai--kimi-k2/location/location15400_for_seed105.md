The rains never stopped.  
At some point the ocean exhaled completely and now lives suspended in the air—great laterals of silver-grey cloud pressing down over drowned peaks. Everything that was once the floor of the world rose three hundred metres below the present surface; everything that was a summit became a handful of jagged towers ringing a newly formed, sky-floating polar lagoon.

The light is only a half-light, a bruise-colour seen through thirty metres of moving water since the cloud layer begins at wave-height and never breaks. Yet nothing is truly dark. Lichens, impearled by the endless rain, catch whatever glimmers wander down and give it back in a glass-green pulse: living lighthouses painted onto the basalt of each tower. Between towers the water surface ripples in sheets, but under it cataracts fall sideways—the cloud-brine draining off hidden shelves, hosting miniature whirlpools that spin downward in slow-motion spirals. Their sound is immense. It is the god-breath of all gravity condensed into a constant thunder that ricochets between stone walls, grows flutelike in narrow chimneys, and then blooms outward into reef-deep bass notes as it spreads through the wide space between islands.

The islands themselves are slender cones of glossy black rock sheathed in moss so acid-bright it shades toward ultraviolet. From the waterline up for perhaps twenty metres stretches a jungle of kelp-stems thicker than a man’s waist, braided with corded vines that have lost their leaves but thickened into translucent amber tubes. In the chilled temperature—-2 °C and gently slugging—these vines sweat freshwater, collecting in hanging pearls that turn to icicles even while still liquid; they clink together softly like frozen bells whenever a long breath rolls through the archipelago.

There are no animals. Instead, life here is flowering intelligence made of root, spore, and metal. Pounded out by the centuries of rainfall, the original quartz seams in the stone have fragmented into powder, oxidised, and re-amalgamated as paper-thin veins of native copper. Lichens thread those veins, pulling atoms into slow filaments, knitting yesterday’s seams into tomorrow’s circuitry. The towers sing their copper threads like triple-strung harps: the whole archipelago shuddering with the drone of rogue currents, the drowned relic of a planetary magnetic field grinding against stone nerves.

Occasionally an eddy in the sheet-fall finds one of the floating wooden disks left by the last human visitors. Axe-split spruce, eight metres across, anchored by woven chains of kelp that keep their centres planted yet let them drift in lazy circle-rides around whatever spire they tethered to. The rafters never meant the platforms as more than rest stops on the way to somewhere drier, but each has cultivated its own weather now. A given raft accumulates a knee-deep layer of humus—dead needles, shed fronds, icy pollen—until mushrooms taller than a person grow in rings. Their stems are hollow and resonate with the waterfalls; when the wind exceeds certain frequencies the caps split open like bells, releasing clouds of white spores that ride the thermals upward through the grid of islands. They cannot escape the cloud roof; instead they cycle there until pregnant with moisture, then rain downward again in pale streamers, joining the clang and roar of the greater water-body.

A single channel cuts the ring of towers, flattened by the water-sky collision into a narrow mirror-wound. The passage is almost horizontal; you can paddle it in a coracle made from stiffened kelp leaf. The bow must stay low, for the roof of rain sits just above your head like a lid. If you pass through slowly you will see fragments of built stone: pillars carved with a language whose glyphs are slanted moons, lenticular lenses ground from obsidian, bronze star-charts oxidized verdigris. Each artifact has been lashed to the channel walls with vine ropes that never rot—ropes spun from the same copper-fed lichens, conducting heartbeat shivers from tower to tower so the entire aqueduct rumbles like a single stitched nervous system. The artifacts are not ruins; they are reagents, built small so the forest could lift them into new metabolisms later.

Scale telescopes strangely here. Look ten metres ahead and the next pillar seems an arm-length away; look backward and the one you just threaded seems already smaller than a thimble, as though the ring of towers flexes in perspective. The dense sound keeps the eye from judging distance; the ear becomes ruler. You measure space in echo-lengths, in tidal growls, in lichen-clicks. Paddles strike water, slap against kelp-bound rocks, and every collision is multiplied by the amphitheater into a rolling avalanche. But once you leave the channel the map widens into orbiting archipelagos separated by nothing more palpable than weather—bubbles of warm rain revolving like slow animals around cold cores. You could sail for days between outcrops without traversing six kilometres of real distance; yet the ocean you pass through is several meters high, leaving your hull each hour for a deeper shelf before circling back. No instruments navigate here but the copper-bright threads beneath the flesh of each island: follow the tremor north-west until the tone drops half an octave—down where the sound thickens and the vines grow longer, thicker, humming with half-solid gold light. There lies the centre: a black spire wider than a village, split top to bottom by a fissure that emits a roar audible above even the water on water. Nothing plants take root within twenty metres of that wound; everything approaching flushes pale, as if the tone bleaches pigment.

Rules are few and absolute. First: nothing sinks. The density of rain has equalised the stone and the sea; islands float at exactly the height where their peaks no longer complain about gravity’s debt. Second: nothing rots slower than three centuries; nothing rots faster than three days. Third: all light transmutes into planktonic thread, all planktonic thread into copper-fed lichen, all lichen into tonal resonance; the loop locks each form forever into circuit with its neighbour. Fourth: time is measured by the aqueous ticking of crystallised spores falling through liquid air—oneClick seven beats per drop pentatonic scale. Fifth: sound is the only permanent habitat; the archipelago itself is only the echo of its own chords.

Leave and you forget the direction instantly; return and the ring remembers you. The only direction leading inward is the one in which your rowing paddle begins to glow faintly—the minute copper migration up the grain spurred by the song of the inlet’s bridges. But turn around anywhere and the paddle darkens; you are spat back into the roar and silver void. Thus the place keeps its borders without wall or weapon, without intent. It is an ecosystem shorn of flesh yet immeasurably alive, breathing inside your lungs every time you inhale the air of cold ringing moss and vibrating metal. In that inhalation the archipelago becomes part of your bloodstream: a faint choir hidden under the coldest rib, humming like a drowned bell a thousand miles inland.
