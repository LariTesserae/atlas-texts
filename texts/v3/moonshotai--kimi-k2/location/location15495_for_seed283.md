The Jungle of Copper Vines

It is too dark to call night, too stale-gold to call day.  A faint, self-generated glow pulses from the vines that strangle every trunk, every breath of air—soft welds of tarnished brass and oxidised verdigris running through them like slow blood.  There are no made walls; the forest has built itself into halls instead.  Corridor after corridor of living pillars rise only eight, ten metres before their crowns interlace overhead, packed so tightly that the upper canopy becomes a three-dimensional labyrinth of leaves and lianas, a cathedral whose vaulting is forever being crocheted and unravelled in the same instant.

Heat is ambient, the kind that settles between your shoulder blades and folds itself around every fold of skin until the distinction between what is body and what is air disappears.  The air, however, is not empty; it is thick with drifting spores that catch the diminish light and turn it into spinning pinwheels of tawny mica.  They rise, settle, rise again in the breath of unseen bellows—giant cecropia leaf-lungs contracting only once every hour, slow as tectonic plates.

Water is peripheral, provisional.  Nothing so vulgar as standing pools; instead every surface weeps.  The broadleaf fronds condense night vapour into silvery runners that braid down every flute of bark and vanish, whiskered by ants, before touching ground.  You can follow an ephemeral creek along the root of a strangler fig simply by the cooler stripe in the air and the moss that grows only on that root.  Each stripe is a buffer zone between two territories of insects chemically at peace; the ants know not to trespass across the line of moisture.  Little wars end the same way generations of sap dissolve: quiet.

Nothing here is abandoned by fauna; there is simply no room for absence.  Even a fallen crown gall that looks like wood is in fact a colony encrusted with beetles smaller than dust-motes who trace constellations whose geometry they themselves designed this morning.  Every cubic decimetre houses somebody.  A leaf-dragon lizard hangs upside-down oscillating its throat sac that flares phosphorescent teal, matching exactly the rhythm of the electric shudder moving along the copper-rind wiring that coats the trunk.  The vines have become a grid; the lizard is one of the meters.

There is industry.  Nobody built it; it accrued.  Buried in the leaf litter are millions of coin batteries made from symbiotic cyanobacteria and copper-reduced exoskeletons of long-dead buprestids.  They leak current slowly, feeding a circulatory system of fungal hyphae, each filament no thicker than a hair, each splitting enzymes that glow like fireflies swarming in microcosm.  A low, muscular hum—felt in the metatarsal bones more than heard—travels down every root as the grid equalises charge.  The dissonant note is soft enough you mistake it for your own pulse until you place your palm on the bark and the pulse answers back.

Paths shift.  Every hour the topmost leaves rearrange their cupping angles a degree more, lifting and lowering their panicles; the paths of filtered green light rotate accordingly.  Thus a trail you took yesterday might be pool-dark today, another suddenly bathed in verdigris sun, a coin-wide spotlight where a spider waits to spin.  The geometry is not directionless; it is calendrical.  What exists here works like a slow grandfather clock measuring centuries in sleight-of-leaf.  Birds whose lineage remembers the same shift of light relay to each other which corridor gapes open; their croak is soft and ventriloquial, seeming to come from behind your shoulder though they hang from vines twenty metres away.

Below ankle height there are crevices—neither cave nor tunnel—where the elevation assumes a less rigorous obedience to measurement.  You might step across these cracks without looking: anklebones plunge into cool, unexpected air that smells of rain just beginning.  Inside each crevice live blind silverfish the length of knitting needles; they feast on the residue of oxidised wires flaking from the jungle’s nervous system and excrete new copper-red clay that lines the nests of animals they will never see.

This place has rules, but they are rules of equilibrium rather than statute:

1.  All exchanges must be slow.  A dropped seed will travel no faster than the ant whose mandibles can bribe the correct guard leaf.  Nothing races to conclusion; the conclusion is already reserved centuries hence, etched in acid pockets beneath the bark.  To hurry is to set the charge in the fungal grid to a dangerous spike; the grid sags, dims, recovers.
2.  Any transgression that violates Rule 1 is punished by the re-absorption of the offender into the copper vines.  Bark begins to grow over the culprits at three millimetres per year—slow, but relentless.  Skeletons threaded through by braided copper can be found in tiers, each assigned a geological time-stone older than the one above.  They are not macabre; they are merely the archival shelves of many slow errors.
3.  Devoted quiet reigns.  Cries of alarm are permitted only in whistles so low they register to humans as placidity.  A jaguar coughs once every four nights as an announcement, then retracts its memory of the sound.

At the centre—although “centre” is acknowledged by consensus of walking rather than cartography—there stands a single ceiba whose trunk is the thickness of a cathedral nave.  Its roots elevate the ground into fern-shawled plateaus, small islands a step above the rest of the world.  In its heart there is a cavity voicing a constant subsonic note.  To lean into it is to hear the memory of steam, the ghost of smelters, the engines that never were but whose templates still hum through the copper.  It is not a metaphor: something ancient, copper-bearing, warm, desired to become an engine and instead became a tree.  The bark bears wavery inscriptions of rivets, bolts, the scalloped hull plates long since skin-lighted into living cambium.  Cease growing or grow till the implied engine awakens—either choice violates Rule 1.  The tree, the jungle and all its chorus lean into incremental balance, slow enough to feel motionless, and that is enough for everything to live.

Late in the perpetual dusk, when the glow from the vines dims to the quality of candlewick, the heat concentrates in pockets like breathing.  From high above, the collapsing fruit of silk-cotton haunts drift downward in what looks like luminous snow; each caries a stitch of copper thread the breadth of a human hair.  Where they land, tomorrow’s vine will already have started.  Nothing is ever begun or finished; the forest knits itself into future tense without leaping ahead.

Some say if you remain too long, close your eyes, and choose to_right_duck_the sleeping edge of entropy, you will wake to find bronze filigree spidering across your wrists, mapping your arteries into the grid.  They say the vines remember every heartbeat they measure, so when you leave, you will carry forever the faint shiver of a current that is not yours alone, but the common pulse of this unbuilt factory for living things.  You are mistaken to believe you or your tools built even one rivet.  The jungle simply forgot, momentarily, what it was to build; now, coaxing forgetfulness back into memory, it takes every body just the right depth of copper moss to make the circuit complete again.

Step lightly.  Speak softly.  Hollow your voice until you yourself become resonance in a forest of whirring, wordless, wisely patient engines.
