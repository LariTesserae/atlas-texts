The floor is a single slab, two hundred paces across, perfectly level, warm as skin.  
It is not stone; it is a matte, cream-colored ceramic that yields slightly under weight, then forgets the pressure. Across its surface run hair-fine cracks, all the same width, all the same depth, intersecting in slow hexagons—cracks that were born with the slab and have not widened by the width of a dust-grain since. Nothing has ever fallen into them; nothing has ever been lifted out.

No water has ever touched it. The air is a dry mineral breath, faintly metallic, tasting of potassium and the ghost of sulfur. It carries no smell of life, no sweat, no rot, no pollen, no salt. Sound is possible here—an ankle of air might squeak against the ceramic—but nothing produces it. The hush is so complete you begin to hear the blood in your own ear, and even that seems an impropriety.

Temperature hovers just above comfort: the slab stores the warmth that arrives from nowhere. There is no sun, no sky, only a low vault of darkness set three times the height of a tall person, a darkness that does not press down but simply curtains the view. The darkness is warm too, like cloth left in a shuttered room. It neither flickers nor thickens; it is a constant companion, never intimate, never hostile.

Sparse vegetation exists, though “vegetation” is an Earthian bias. Here there are things that stand: knee-high, jointed like antique tripods, colored the pale zinc of old batteries. Each tripod ends in a single brittle leaf, a perfect equilateral triangle, paper-thinin, pointing always toward the same compass direction as its neighbors. They are rooted—if that word applies—by a single screw of tarnished copper sunk into a countersunk hole that the slab wore for it at the moment of its making. There are perhaps thirty in the whole arena, separated by the generous distances of polite strangers at a funeral. They do not grow; they do not wilt. They conduct a chemistry that never needed water, sipping instead on the faint static that bleeds from slab to air. Touch the triangle and you will feel neither damp nor waxy coat, only a tepular warmth identical to the floor, as though the plant is only another patch of ground that has chosen to stand upright.

Elevation is low: step off the slab and you would descend perhaps the height of two stacked hands into a gutter of the same material, a moat only one pace width encircling the slab like a picture frame. Beyond the moat the darkness drops away into an unmeasured void, but the eye refuses to believe the drop is deep; it feels instead like the false perspective of a theater wing—one cautious stride and you might, absurdly, bump into the backstage wall of the world.

Nothing has ever been built here. No scrap of chert flake, no hearth black, no lineage of walls gone to rubble. Yet the place feels eroded—eroded in the way a theorem is eroded by centuries of re-proof, refined not into diminishment but into perfect continuance. The slab bears no tool mark, but its surface has the matte kindness that only long abrasion can give, an abrasion that happened once, then permanently ceased.

No animal has ever scurried, nested, or died. The only motion is your own. If you drag your foot, the hiss is so rare it feels like vandalism; if you hold your breath, the silence resets to factory zero. Time does not pass here, it pools. The triangle leaves do not track hours; they are sundials without sun, calendars without months. You could leave and return—should the way exist—and find your footprints exactly where they were, neither softened by wind nor rimmed by dew.

There is a single rule, unspoken, unposted: nothing you bring leaves a shadow. A torch beam is swallowed cleanly at the edge of darkness; a handful of water spatters into a mute fog that settles and vanishes, thirsty grains wicking themselves dry in mid-air. Even memory acts unsure: after a while you begin to doubt the existence of water, of thirst, of thirst’s remedy. The slab teaches by entropy’s opposite—whatever is, remains; whatever is not, never arrives.

Leave the triangle leaves untouched long enough and you might notice their triangles have rotated, all together, a fraction of a degree. Not toward you, not away; the new bearing makes sense only inside some private trigonometry. They are solving a problem that does not involve you. You begin to suspect that the entire arena is a component snapped off some larger device whose purpose requires only the quiet accounting of small angles and steady warmth. A primitive tech, yes: no wires, no wheels, only slabs and screws and leaves of metal, yet the machine hums in its silence with the patience of an orrery built to outlast the species that built it—and perhaps it already has.

Stand in the center, equidistant from every gutter, every triangle. The darkness fits you like a coat sewn before your birth. You realize you have begun to conform to the place’s equilibrium: your heart rate slows to match the slab’s storage of stillness, your lungs meter the mineral air as if it were rare currency. You could stay. Nothing would object; nothing would consent. The slab would warm you, the triangles would increment their angle a lifetime later, the darkness would remain two body-lengths above your head, neither cruel nor kind, merely exact.

If you choose to leave, re-crossing the gutter feels like stepping up, not down—an optical joke. Behind you, your footprints fail to impress the ceramic; it heals the way time refuses to heal in the outer world. Ahead, should a passage open, you will carry with you a new unit of measurement: the distance at which warmth becomes memory, the angle at which a triangle forgets you were ever its witness. You will not thirst—your body remembers the desert of water it left—but neither will you drink. The slab has given you its own equilibrium, a portable never-changing, a private darkness that fits under the ribs and hums there, quiet as copper, patient as ceram.
