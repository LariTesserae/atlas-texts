Above the last livable shelf of the Lung-thrust Plateau, the sky is so sun-blaze bright it feels thicker than the air itself, a white-hot syrup that pours over everything and drips as liquid glass from the cornices of the old Upper Works. Water is everywhere, yet never standing: it runs in molten strings down the flanks of still-smoking slag walls, it pearls on the bronze ribs of abandoned cooling fins, it streams out of fractured condensers in a perpetual horizontal mist that never quite reaches the ground. Everything glistens as if freshly licked.

The plateau is a lakebed drained backward in time, a city disassembled into its component veins. Trace the aqueduct girders and you find they have become aquifers, seeping. Walk the boulevard grids and your boots splash through thermal runoff patterned like tessellated marble. What little vegetation once grew here was porcelain pipe-work and steel rebar; now even those are gone—volatilised, rust-flaked into ochre snow. Only heat-loving pigments remain, baked onto brick like petroglyphs, the faintest rouge of lost signage.  

From the fallen clocktower at the center spreads a honeycomb of former workshops, each cell scoured smooth by decades of acidic steam. The corners round over; bolts and rivets droop like candle-wax. Nothing stays sharp for long. In the high windless heat, eddies of metal vapor dance low, ankle-high ghosts that reach clawed fingers toward ankles before condensing back into boiling droplets. The sound of their spit and hiss is so constant it is, in practice, silence—an auditory snow that covers every lesser note.

Yet life seethes. No green things, no seeds, no roots. Rather, animal—if that word still fits a creature that drips mercury instead of blood. Bottle-cats: cat-sized things with skin like blown glass, full of swirling grey liquid, padding among the beams on splayed mercury-slick paws. Whenever one mews, the sound is a soft tintinnabulation heard less by ear than by the bone’s own watery resonance, a wet bell struck under skin. They lap at the same runoff that pools in sump drains, each sip making them briefly opaque, then clear again, like breathing. Higher above dog-sized serpents of braided copper hang from swing hooks and conveyor struts, kinetic sculptures that spontaneously uncoil, somersault through the white air, and refasten themselves further on; their pulses along the galleries sound like muffled turbines starting up somewhere far away on a day you cannot quite remember. Bright osprey-sized manta kites of micaceous film ride the vertical thermals, steered by minute electric arcs—spark wings—gliding from one heat vent to another, photophores flashing signal codes no botanist taught them because there are no botanists left and nothing to pollinate.  

Rules of the place settle emergent yet strict:
- Moving water is sacred. Any liquid channel left unclaimed for five minutes is annexed by the fauna: even a spilled droplet births a mirror-snail that scatters in six directions; whole alleyways crystallise into temporary frescos of living solder.
- Touch-bearing must be reciprocated. Brush against a copper serpent and it will carefully lick the metal skin of whatever tool or limb you offer, depositing a thin coat of alloy that cools into an involuntary wedding ring. The gesture is neither affection nor ownership; merely ledger-balancing mineral karma.
- Heat bends scale. Small things become large. The single loosened rivet a month ago is now a smooth monolith taller than a man, oxide petals opening like iron blossoms at noon; by dusk it may be pebble-small again, having exhaled its mass into the wind.
- Light ends at mirrors. Any flat reflective surface (there are many—the polished fronts of power-stations turned tanning mirrors) becomes, at the moment solar zenith passes, a soft vertical loch that ripples. Step into the reflection and you will descend ankle-deep into liquid luminance; some bottle-cats vanish this way and are never seen again, others re-emerge days later with copper bellies they didn’t have before.

The settlement itself is only half human. The surviving gantries and valve-trees house nesting shelves for mercury birds—fist-sized droplet flocks that migrate in perfect spheres, splitting and rejoining like thoughts across rooms. Windows were blown out ages ago; storms of bright air enter and exit unimpeded. Intact doors are greeted with bemusement; a few have been laid flat to serve as dew collectors for nighttime rituals—because even here there is night: the city tilts slowly east-west in great stately sighs, a twelve-hour rolling wobble whose shadow could smother a hemisphere of stars if any dared appear. They do not. The sky stays cursor-white, bright as midday candle-wick even when the sun itself has slid behind the western rift.

Peoples—descendants of shift engineers, copper brokers, canal-divers—move shade to shade in relic respirators repurposed to cool rather than filter. They’ve learned not to plant, only to shepherd flow. Every rooftop is a flat basin for harvesting dilute runoff; siphons dangle like lazy IV lines into subterranean storage. Children born here develop a translucent membrane on their backs, a double skin that distills their sweat back to drink in emergencies. From a distance they resemble glass-blown angels with oily halos.

Down below the inhabited shelf lies the Twilight Ledge, already cooled, already night. There skeletal apartment blocks hang over precipices like stunned bats, their windows all open mouths leaking black lichen—primitive lichen, the first creeping martyr edge of life before chlorophyll. But it cannot endure the climb; the thermocline shrivels it each dawn. So the Upper Works stay ungreen, flushed clean hourly by the lash of steam, displaying only mineral muscles in a fever dream.

Listen: no single voice rises above the simmer. Even when two bottle-cats fight over a drip, the clash is soft light tapping open hollow glassware, lost immediately in the collective croon of condensers high overhead. And yet the city is active, breathing. Every minute the mercury snakes slither along new catwalks, reshaping bent Conduits like dough; every hour the great cooling fins crackle and shift a finger’s breadth; every night-whilst-day the panorama re-curves itself like a sleeping gull folding and unfolding one wing at a time.

Stand at the parapet, scalp prickling, and look out. Beyond the Works, the plateau falls away in shelves of paler and paler stone until distant alabaster flats vanish through watery haze. The horizon glints with departing manta kites, each trailing a photograph of sunlight the size of a metropolis. One final regulation: should the wall behind you begin to weep-not-condense but actual clean tears, taste them. They will be human saline, thank-yous from machines that used to dream they were lungs.
