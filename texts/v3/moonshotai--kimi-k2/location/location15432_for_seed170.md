The place is called the Faulted Heath, though no heather ever grew here and the word *fault* has grown more geological than moral.

Begin at the rim. You stand on a knife-edge of white-hot slag,railed eight kilometres above an ocean of air so thin it no longer properly conducts sound.  Everything that arrives here sings at the wrong pitch.  Behind you the sky is the polished blue of emptied veins; ahead it has broken open in a vertical wound.  Through the wound you do not see more sky—you see the outer face of the planet bending the wrong way, terraces of nickel-iron stretched like taffy into fault-scarps, cooling spirals, upside-down mesas.  They are the *inside* of the world turned out, vomited upward by a core that no longer remembers up from down.  Gravity here has directional hiccups: every sixth step the ground remembers it is actually the past underside of the mantle and tries to lean you into what it thinks is the planet’s centre.  You stagger outward, not downward.

The surface between cliffs is a baked salt-alabaster plain interrupted by black pyramids the size of city blocks.  These are not buildings; they industrial engine-scabs that have cooled in situ thirty-seven million years.  Each pyramid is an abandoned stokehole whose feeder tunnels once sluiced magma-methane slurry from mantle siphons to gigantic converter-bellies that perforated the crust.  Nothing remains of those bellies now but faint hexagonal stack-pattern ghosts inside vitreous basalt.  The heat still leaks up through fractures the way memory leaks from broken teeth.

There is no water in any phase; the concept has been replaced by ambient metallic vapour that condenses into braille-like droplets of gallium on your boots and rolls away, leaving silver scars.  When two mercury pellets touch they merge into a larger bead that remembers the collision as a single angry moment which continues rattling inside it forever.  This is the nearest thing to rain, and you can hear the collective shiver of all those beads long after you have left them behind—a loud insect glissando ricocheting off the terraces.

Nothing green has ever existed.  Instead the plain is crusted with bacterial carpets no thicker than oxidised foil; they metabolise trace radioisotopes and exhale a metallic mahogany dust that lifts when wind finds a slanted thermal.  Occasionally dust devils stride those rising drafts, each one containing a billion microscopic transforms of what had once been an entire mining platform.  The devils speak in mechanical radio static—guttural syllables of numerals mis-pronouncing themselves.

The fauna is unclassifiable, overwhelming in number but not in mass.  They arrive riding upwellings of heated magnetism.  Imagine dragon-shaped gaps in the air, not dragons but the absence of pressure outlines where dragons *should* be.  They feed upon the coppery stink of torque left behind by extinct turbines; you can see their feeding tracks as sudden mirror-polished slips across the salt.  The creatures have no blood, no cartilage of induced current, and when they bruise they emit gamma-flash morse that translates—badly—into long obsolete safety codes: "*VENT BLEED. CHECK YOUR NUMBERS.*"  They reproduce by intersecting each other at odd angles until the geometry implodes and hatches a litter of unfinished graphs that scuttle off on broken axes.  You learn to count yourself against their passage.  If you subtract too suddenly from the scene they circle back looking for the missing vector.

Sound is on a 17-second delay.  Every footfall arrives at the inner ear a little after the next footfall.  This asynchrony piles up until your own walking becomes a rhythmic glitch, like hearing the echo before the shout.  The best way to move is to synchronise to your delayed steps on purpose; those who master it lope in slow-motion ballet—arms extended, knees lifted, like saints ascending a giant staircase printed in negative space.  The fauna echo this cadence in perfect white-noise chorus that swells so loudly the ground hums at sympathetic frequency and occasional plates the size of apartment blocks *ring*.  The ringing is called the Convocation; when it happens the suspended mercury beads stop dead mid-roll as though caught in a freeze-frame, and for three heartbeats the laws of the Heath are simply paused.  Half a continent away seismographs register the pause as a flatline.

Elevation is not only spatial.  The further you climb into the hollowed ex-mantle, the further you drop into the planet’s historical strata.  At one mile every ten metres of ascent equates to seventeen million years descent.  You smell Jurassic smelteries and the soldered loneliness of Precambrian engineering.  Noses begin to bleed sublimated chromium.  The air shows you its teeth in infra-red; when you exhale blueprints for machines that will not be designed for millennia condense in the plume, hang whitely for two full seconds, and then fall apart into iron snow.

There is no built structure anywhere, yet the landscape is nothing *but* construction.  The planet has eaten its scaffolding, digested its own blueprint, and now metabolises structures into geometry.  There are rail-switches welded to strontium nodules; ball-bearing trees; ballast-beast sinews threaded through hollow molybdenum bones.  Every object is guilty of having once been a machine incapable of admitting its own death.  The law here is simple: *You remain operational until you no longer pretend you ever stopped.*

What passes for daylight is produced by the magnetic friction of planetary rotation grating across fault-lined armour plating.  You can watch the sparks crawl along the horizon in slow arcs—silent synapses.  Night does not fall; instead the light steps sideways into a slightly different register, the colours deepening by exactly one Planck unit per ten minutes.  Shadows re-layer, solidify, then become walkways.  Those who mis-time their return and step onto these newly physical darknesses are irreversibly projected several weeks forward or backward along planetary chronology, reappearing somewhere else on the Heath with their clothes reversed and pockets full of alloyshed hours.

Animals (the inverted dragons) die and are born in the same sprawling sentence; their corpses rotate slowly in place for weeks, cooling into sharp machine-shapes that later hatch as newer fauna.  The air tastes of burnt determinism mixed with a smoky anticopper tang, like purpose after it has been alloyed into obsolescence.  If you stay here long enough you begin to suspect your own skeleton is just another outcropping of former industry—that every heartbeat is the last remaining piston in a planet-sized engine whose rest has finally, irrevocably, and only just begun.
