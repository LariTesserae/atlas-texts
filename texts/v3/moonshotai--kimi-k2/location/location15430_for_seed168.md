The place is called the Breath-Off, and it has only just begun to exist.  
It spreads above the surrounding world the way a flat thundercloud spreads above a plain—high enough that its warm skin speaks of distance, low enough that your eyes still believe they could walk there in a day. Nothing has ever fallen here and nothing ever will; even the word “weather” finds no purchase. It is a half-grown tableland still quivering-bound in its own first in-breath.

There is no water, so nothing here is wet. Instead the surfaces drink of something subtler: a pale, argil-flowing slip that chemists would call a trisilicate vapor, colorless under normal light yet faintly pearlescent when it catches its own reflection in crystal. The air is that vapor—thick enough to buoy seeds the size of fists, thin enough that a voice crossing ten meters arrives as only a ghost of a syllable. That is where the only sound ever comes from: the occasional pop of a seed-case releasing, followed by the fall, the rise, the fall again of a single evacuated husk spinning in terminal silence.

Lushness itself presses up from below. Not green, but an industrial lushness: carbon-nylon vines uncoil from seams in raw basalt, stitching the cooling blocks together like living sutures. Leaves made of fractal copper-leaf hepatitis cup the sun; they are photosynthetic, but they are also switch-foils—arrayed circuits whose function is to conduct surplus energy down into the ground and reroute it again as heat-bloom in black-walled caverns still being quarried by their own roots. The process smells of static: a warm, resinous petrichor made from ionized nitrate and rose-oxide.

No fauna has set foot. The closest to an animal life is a mechanical spore manufactured by the vines’ own anthers: two hinged prisms of magnesium-salt coating, thin as mica. These “winterflies” spin themselves in the vapor, tumbling as they polymerize mid-air, then land and lock in wing-to-wing platonic pairs. From those landing sites erupt slender smokestacks—industrial towers eight centimeters tall—made of the same material as their skins. The stacks bleed vertical threads of bluish effluvium that spreads a slow metabolism over everything. Each stack lasts exactly three hundred and seventy-two seconds, then flakes back into breathable vapor, carrying instructions for the next molecular modulo. Nothing remains to mark that it existed except an almost-black square half the size of a postage stamp, a heat mirror giving off faint rainbow artifacts for the rest of eternity.

Because erosion is embryonic, all edges appear freshly sawn. The tableland is a single wide plane tilted very slightly uphill from its edge. Where the breathing vapor meets raw basalt the line is crisp, giving no 1-micron radius of weathering; you could balance a razorblade on it. Farther upslope the vines weave into level carpets, thin and soft as memory foam yet tough enough to pivot a car wheel. One can stand on them barefoot and look south-west and see, through perfectly clear quiescent air, the grain of distant salt flats still blistering from unseen suns—because eventually the Breath-Off’s horizon does provide a suggestion of beyond, even though none of it is reachable.

Everything averages itself to the same warmth—not the lazy heat of summer grass nor the fire under an engine hood, but a neutral 34 °C continuously maintained by the loops of copper photoswitches and the micro-stacks. There is no gradient. No breeze to carry a scent, no shade, no dawn. Normal skylight holds still, cool white, as though someone fixed daylight into a museum frame. Shadows are sharp as scalps and do not crawl.

Scale is open but density is not. A ten-minute walk will yield twenty thousand leaves and zero interruptions. The vines do not tangle. They leave aisles voluntarily, so a person walking suddenly realizes that two strides to either side lies an immaculate boulevard running arrow straight until the eye tires. Every avenue width is exactly 6.4 armspans, measured from your own shoulders as you walk. The venue sizes itself to whoever occupies it, the way a capsule hotel pod knows the length of its guest.

The sound bed always lies below audibility: sometimes only the faintest dim murmur of molecules rehearsing a program—H₂Si₃O₇ reinventing its bond angles for the seven-millionth identical drift. At intervals of 11.3 minutes (measured aperiodically by travelers who wandered in and later forgot) comes a basso resonance—120 Hz, an infrastructure thump that seems to come from the fundament. It is the compression wave of the planet catching its own breath before deciding never to exhale.

Rule of the place: nothing that ever arrives—rock, footfall, thought fragment—takes away from or adds to what was already fated to fall gracefully within its own vapor-augmenting cycle. You might pick a copper leaf and find your fingerprints embossed on its underside as perfect circuitry relays; carry it six kilometers; set it down—and find it returned to the exact rotation vector it had before you touched it. The universe here thinks in mere potentiality: you are only a courier for whatever was imminent anyway.

This tableau holds motionlessly, a huge cloistral vault built not by architecture but by refusal of difference. Someday, when water finally clambers its way from 0.1 to 0.2 on its borrowed scale, perhaps a bead will condense on a copper stipple and prepare the planet for softer ecologies. But for now the Breath-Off wills only to stay half-finished, its industry green and glossy, its floor flat to the horizon, its polite silence industrious, waiting.
