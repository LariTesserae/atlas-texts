Seven granite ridges rise like knuckles of some buried giant, knuckles worn smooth by centuries of brine-laden wind. They stand high above a misty bowl the size of a small city—exactly high enough that waves crashing at their seaward base sound only as a continuous, ceaseless roar when you stand on the edge, like standing inside a shell gone deaf.

Between the ridges runs the Porphyry Breach, an old river turned metallic sluice. Its surface is only visible in irregular flashes: one moment the shine of lead-colored water, the next moment a flat sheet of factory runoff amethyst-blue, catching the sun without glitter because anything loose—pollen, dust, gull feathers—has already been pulled under and plated to the bed. The river arcs inland and fingers uphill, defying ease, teased skyward by thick cast-iron pipes that look like veins trying to break free from their own walls. Those pipes are mossless, oiled by condensed steam that drips and trickles and beads along the seams, never having the mercy to evaporate. Every hour on a schedule no one remembers setting, there is a tremor as each sluice gate exhales a low, wet blast that sounds unsurvivably loud—sixty-three seconds after the previous. Visitors sometimes count the pause between breaths; locals sew it into their heartbeats.

The city itself—its formal name never caught and so is simply called the Works—occupies the long platforms of granite between the ridges, stacked in architectural codons: cast-iron warehouses the color of blood once dried; windowed dormitories ringed with fluting cooling towers that blow a perpetual warm fog; a central plug of black basalt rubble once natural but now mined smooth except for its veins of copper wire running like capitals in stone. Streets are conveyor belts over wood-block gutters where seepage collects before it can stream. The gutters groan under the weight of water-laden bearings; they have for long enough that the bearings are now part of the stone. These conveyors move people and pallets at the same dignified crawl: five kilometres a day, no more, no less. Cabins built onto the belts serve as shops and homes; a lamplighter walks alongside with a canister of carbon gas that turns his lantern blue so he looks like a ghost inside a fire.

There are few plants. What does grow sprouts from cracks in belts gone still: tepid-trunked succulents that taste of rust and faint spice, called “glassloaves,” whose leaves secrete clear honey that crystallizes into thin sheets at night. People peel these off in the morning and trade them as transparencies for factory windows when a storm shatters a frame. Another species, “hood wicks,” push single pale cylinders into the air until they open like umbrellas too-heavy to lift. Their cores smoke slowly, feeding a particular beetle that has never been found living outside the Works and which, by day, scuttles too deep beneath the platforms to be seen. At night the beetles roam the hood wicks, making a sound like dropped marbles on metal; crowd noise tuned down until the clicks overlap into ocean static.

The people of the Works walk in loose constellations—never alone, rarely in pairs, always in half-lit intermediates. Conversation is brief because voices don’t traverse the blanket of mechanical thunder. Instead they use stamped tin signs on neck chains: flip one way for yes, the reverse for no, tap twice for hours of daylight remaining. Machinery produces every necessity, so the signs suffice. In flannel-drab coveralls everyone resembles blurred paintings left too near the sea. They queue at clamp-hooks to exchange emergency breath-masks, thick rubber against the rising metallic steam, then let the belt carry them to the next duty. Children born here learn the lullaby first as vibration through hipbones pressed to conveyor planks; words come only later and mostly in interrogatives.

Above all this floats a giant net of brass whistles, each tube humming at the same pitch as the loudest compressors. The net exists for sound, not air: the city commissioners planted it to resonate with their machinery and soften the frequencies that shred lungs. When it works, as it does on most days, the net spreads an uneasy hush over conversation, like talking in a library that happens to roar. Sometimes a whistle corrodes and a shard drops—a fluted dagger arcing down to lodge point-first between two planks, shivering in resonance. People avoid walking under that exactly landed blade; they have long agreed that the vibration in the iron a few feet away is the safer path, though no one can prove why.

Water is always within ten steps, but never freely collected. The upper crust seeps steadily through joint seams in every building: condensation sliding—iridescent phases of violet, bruise-blue, sea-foam—into ceramic gutters diverted into spouts that hiss down hollow towers. At the base of each tower a brass faucet pours steady, lukewarm hitch into thirsty buckets. A municipal ordinance decrees that a person may take only as much water as they can keep in their own mouth while walking ten paces, so newcomers learn to stretch jaws and throats. Anything they cannot hold runs off into the sluice ahead of schedule, turning the Breach brinier by parts per million every decade; the Works is slowly pickling itself, but too slowly to be called ruin.

Every hundred years, give or take a midsummer, the entire network of belts reverses direction. When it does, librarians wheel racks of compressed moss rolls down their strip-book corridors, offices repaint the symbols on their doors, and the statue of the original machinist rolls onto its mirrored plinth where it will face the opposite direction for the next century. No one ever reveals how they know the century is ending; an emergent, clockless accord. During this reversal the water lightens to a mineral grey, the whistles drop three tones, and people feel a curious lack. It lasts nine days, like an eddy in deep thought, then the belts resume their old direction—except a single ratchet somewhere inside the scaffolding always ends up one tooth behind. Engineers shrug; the Works drifts minutely, imperceptibly, toward the ocean rhumb line beneath the ridges. Somewhere far below, a beetle hiccups.

At night the city turns that epoch-forward sound downward. With normal light now absent, the sky offers its own flat greyscale; only the stars of the shipyard dock-barges appear larger because they have been fixed to masts that sway just above eye-level. The view east is a narrow rift where the granite can no longer contain riverlight—the Breach widens into a surf malevolent as punched tin. There, phosphor foam climbs and collapses in a cadence matched by the slow lift of the city itself, as though both water and architecture share the same enormous respiration. Standing on the outermost ridge, the place feels settled, balanced, in its own state of erosion yet hopelessly in love with its own weight. Somewhere on a conveyor nearly at the far edge of the Works, a miller lays a hand on a sack of pulverized glassloaf razors destined for polishing lenses, and counts the beats in his wrist the way priests once measured paternosters against hearts.

It is slow here, yes, but it moves.
