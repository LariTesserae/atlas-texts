In a high cleft above any river—so high that the clouds themselves must bend and thicken to fit between the towers of black basalt—there exists a shelf of ground no wider than three joined living rooms. Yet it is a complete world, because everything happens at once inside it and nothing happens afterward. Gravity has long since come to peace with the water here; droplets simply float or hover the way dust hangs in a sun-beam a second before it moves. The air is cool enough for breath to frost on uncut quartz teeth along the rock, cool enough that stone watches for you, quietly.

Everything is saturated. Moss grows not in cushions but in furrows so precisely filled that their margin stands knife-thin; a careless hand would draw blood from brushing them. Ferns, the height of hares, stand shoulder to shoulder without even one spare crevice of dark air between them; their fronds overlap like herring-bone until any single blade is indistinguishable, yet you sense the individuality of each because the lighter undersides endlessly flick open and shut, breathing in slow semaphore. Every gap is claimed by the velvety blackapes of liverwort and by ghost-white lichens that pattern the cliffside skeletal. The whole grove-room is the color of rainy forest compressed, harder light held within dark.

Nothing built touches this ground. Still,, some pattern older than hands make circles. Stones older than their strata have taken seats—egg-shapes worn to thin bowls—and each bowl always holds a thimble of water. No one put them there, and they never spill. The geology has pindled its own dinnerware.

The beasts are invisible but tactile. When you push into the thicket your shins and knees feel the nudging of muscular forms no seeming warmer than the air, bodies without edges pushing air aside as water parts for submerged fish. They breathe—you feel the pressure—but you never catch a whisper of fur or scale. These are the Telgira, a name one learns the first time land silk touches ten-dril tongue to skin. They are dense in population; in the two strides ahead three muzzles jostle endlessly; extra antler-splinters crosshatch space without belonging to anything solid. Their droppings are pale lichen pellets indistinguishable from seed cases, adding nutrients in perfect equilibrium so the vegetation neither gains nor loses. They never walk out of this shelf; even to turn is unnecessary because space folds around them in short layers.

Static—nothing changes. That declaration holds the same authority the stone holds silence. Moisture droplets may drift but they repeat yesterday’s drift and will repeat tomorrow’s; dew beads swell to precisely the same weights, gather beneath the same maidenhair frond at precisely the same angle before dropping. One constant bead descends, lands, reforms at the tip—a syrinx loop keeping a note in suspension forever. Ferns do not uncurl a millimeter beyond yesterday’s cured spiral; moss fur stands still like fluffed cat under a dislodged snowflakes no one notices landing.

At no hour does light improve; a charcoal luminance, like moonlight diluted further in tone by violet ink forever, outlines simple surfaces without ever revealing grain. The cliff tunnels a faint phosphor inside its own cavity so the viewer can always tell dark from darker. Every shadow—there are thousands—is a texture stitched to the next; no wind taunts them. The single sound is the slow rolling of a single droplet against labradorite, tick . . . tick . . . a beat so perfectly spaced that once heard it lodges in the marrow and becomes the meter against which silence is heard thereafter.

Technology appears only in the shed exoskeleton of dragonflies: a lacy lattice like early sorghum stalk stripped of pith—delicate, the color of old ice. They remain because nothing erodes, nothing decomposes, nothing is born, thus nothing is lost. They tangle in the fern spokes exactly where they sloughed one scaleless yesterday. One could take the dried casing and absent-mindedly roll it, discovering how its plates interlock like the first finger-counting, inventing scarabs of thought never yet considered—but your hand stops midway. There is no future for newness here, and even curiosity feels immodest, like uprooting a petrified order.

The water saturates unseen capillaries. After your heart listens for forty breaths, you notice wetness oozing from the pores in moss itself: bead upon bead in a steady roe gather until each fern pinna drips into the next. What looks like ground is a mossy mattress suspended above a low cavern of black air. Stand long enough and you feel the mattress rise and fall, centimeter-pulse, as if the world itself snored; the shelf sits on the chest of the mountain and the mountain does not wake. Deep beneath, water maramures for eternity through conduits as wide as swallow holes, quietly rinsing rock so gently the cavern never widens, never narrows: a streamed forever in perfect drainage.

If you stay so long the cold bites cartilage, you might notice the Telgira gathering nearer. Their breath fogs in flat disks against your bare wrists, small chilled platters of vapor the color of quartz dust. They mean welcome the way lichen means color—without intention beyond being there. You perceive their weight as warm pressure against knee hollow, hip, shoulder blade, until the small grove feels the size of a single body, an organism in which you are a wandering cell finally caught between membranes. They would keep step with you, even though no step is required. They would drink from you, yet nothing is required. They merely press, to remind you the world has no edges under this high ledge.

There exists no history to this place, though it contains everything any story would need. Cycles have locked so perfectly that beginnings and ends circulate within one another like water mint-shapes in an hourglass laid on its side where both bulbs become one wide tube. No bird perches, no seed lands from elsewhere, no storm drives change from sky to stone. Perhaps once—long ago—one footstep arrived and, seeing there was no footprint past it, no footprint beside it, stood still until the memory of motion bled into the moss and froze forever at zero velocity. That footprint is now the mossy bowl you might sit in. Any new footfall would pollute the equation, but the place refuses newcomers; you only visit, and even your memory of the grove-room feels over-corrected to fit the pattern once you step away at the lip. All you carry out is that uneven heart-beat, the drip-tick that continues in the hollow behind your ears­—immutable.
