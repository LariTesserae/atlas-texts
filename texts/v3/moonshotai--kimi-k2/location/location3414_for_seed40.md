The Skyfold Terraces rise through fog that is not fog but solid crystal, a lattice of frozen breaths ex- terruded upward by machines that never stop.  Every surface is plated with riveted copper, yet the copper is alive—veins of sap bleed through the seams and drip into gutters that sing in falsetto.  No ground exists; instead there are only balconies, catwalks, spiral ramps stapled to an openwork cliff of iron that was never rock.  The structure remembers it was molten once, and on windless midnights it softly recounts that dream in low pulses you feel in the shinbones more than hear.

Water is rationed as ornament.  It appears in rotating globes—spherical droplets the size of melons—suspended by weak electro-static hooks.  They orbit the handrails slowly, evaporating a skin each hour so that the air tastes of pennies and petrichor.  When one sphere is drunk dry the hooks collapse with a sigh and the remnants rain downward, pattering on tin awnings far below; the sound is the closest thing to weather the terraces allow.

Vegetation is not refused but translated.  Thin black birches have been flayed, carbonized, and re- laminated into pipework; their cambium still pulses with green code, pushing out brassy leaves that open like camera shutters at dawn and close with a gun-cock at dusk.  The leaves are not for photosynthesis—they exhale a fine dust of zinc oxide that keeps the rust in check.  Gardeners in oilskin coats walk the gantries with magnetized boots, whispering coordinates to the trees so the roots know which rivets to tighten.

Coolness is engineered.  Great radiator wings project from the outer strings, dulled red on their underside, silver on top, harvesting the chill that leaks from an upper sky no eye has seen.  It is said the sky itself was removed early in construction, rolled back like tarpaulin to make room for brightness.  What remains is a scrim of polished reflector plate, hammered into shallow parabolas so the light rains straight down in white columns, too brilliant for shadows to survive.  Squint long enough and you can spot the rivet ghosts—circular after-images that swim across the retina like microbes.

Fauna thrives because nothing is allowed to die properly.  Sparrows are riveted into the copper at the moment of expiration; their tiny bodies become hinge joints that still twitch when the wind gybes.  Rats wear leather dirigibles stitched to their spines—hot-air bladders that lift them to the rafters where they chew data-cables for salt.  Every twenty years the cables remember they are bones and briefly try to walk; the rats ride the undulation like sailors, eyes bright with election.

Density is the rule: catwalk above catwalk, balustrade behind balustrade, until perspective gives up and folds into fretwork.  You can stand still and be pierced by five separate draft vectors, each bringing its own perfume—creosote, ozone, beeswax, ether, albumen.  There is no private volume; even your pockets are inventoried by small polite levers that depress when you sit.  Yet the terraces are also open: the eye finds vanishing points multiplied to nonsense, distance looped back on itself, so that a friend waving from two platforms away might appear both above your head and inside your mouth.

Erosion here is arrested at the adolescent phase.  Surfaces carry the acne of fresh stamping: burrs, flashes, milling scars.  Edges remain preternaturally sharp; if you cut yourself the wound will weld shut overnight, because the copper remembers the forge and tries to return.  Only ideas erode.  Speak aloud and the sentence oxidizes, turning verdigris where it brushed the banister; tomorrow you will see your words hanging as a brittle vine that snaps when touched.

The dynamic is slow but inexorable.  Once a decade every railing elongates by the length of one rivet, a growth that happens in a single groan at 3:14 a.m.  Clockkeepers gather to listen; they claim the note tells which districts will prosper, which romance will collapse.  No one has ever proved them wrong.  Elsewhere, engines the size of cathedral bells remain always at half-crank, their pistons inhaling but never completing the exhalation.  The compression sweetens the air, keeps it thick so sound travels poorly; conversations arrive incomplete, scrambled like eggs dropped down a stair.

At the nadir they keep the Fold Lamp, a lantern whose glass is cracked reality itself.  When its wick is trimmed, laws relax: copper conducts emotion, rust grows backward, dead sparrows sing the numbers between three and four.  The lamp is lit only when repairs demand impossibility—when a terrace must be removed yet stay, when a citizen wishes to be unborn.  Each lighting costs one color that the world can never show again; already indigo is missing, and recent infants point at the sky asking for a hue their parents cannot see.

Visitors arrive by falling upward: you step off the world below, descend three seconds, then find yourself rising toward the terraces at a polite speed.  No one explains the inversion; explanation would require admitting somewhere else exists.  At the lip a steward greets you with a sponge soaked in warm mercury.  Press it to your temples; it erases the memory of flat horizons, installs a map of hinges instead.  From that moment on, every footstep contributes torque to the great structure; you walk, therefore it tightens.  The quiet is part of the contract—your throat receives a small velvet collar that muffles all but the most necessary syllables.  Most newcomers learn to speak in comma-length whispers, language becoming Morse tapped fingernail to railing.

Exit is rumored but not demonstrated.  Some say the terraces turn themselves inside out every equinox, and if you stand at the correct intersection of struts you will be everted back into a nature that never missed you.  Others claim departure is vertical: keep climbing the maintenance ladders until the light grows kind enough to die, then step through the panel that was once your shadow.  No story ends; they merely oxidize into banisters.  Grip hard enough and you can feel the narrative veins, still pulsing, still cool, still patiently adding one rivet-length every ten silent years.
