It is called the **Hollow Works**.  
It is a cavern the size of a chapel nave carved at the crest of a newborn mountain ridge where no mountain has any right to be. The rock itself is only six days older than the people who first mapped it; an upthrust piston of nickel-iron and glass still venting heat the color of old copper. Everything inside that cavity is fabricated. Nothing grows that was not poured or bolted or extruded. Yet the air is never dry; condensate courses along every seam, as though a storm has just finished bleeding itself out on the ceiling and refuses to be evicted.

The shell  
A lattice of black steel ribs lines the irregular walls like the inside of a piano stripped clean. Where the ribs cross they leave narrow cisterns, copper gutters no wider than a finger. From these gutters a fine drizzle sifts down at all hours—warm water smelling faintly of machine oil and salt. It polishes the floor, thickens the air, and finds every ear. Cock your head and the interior becomes a snapped snare drum made of distant surf. The sound magnitude is constant, eighty-four decibels, a stranger rumble you feel in your lungs rather than merely hear. The engineers who laid the Works called it the “heartbeat” and installed it on purpose, a vibrating diaphragm somewhere far up in the rock feeding off steam pressure. They believed absolute silence was unbearable at altitude. They were wrong about many things, but not about that.

The floor  
Walkways of perforated checkerplate hover three centimeters above a continuous sheet of water that never grows deeper than the ankle. The plates hum. They tremor in sympathetic motion with the distant heart. Anyone pausing long enough will notice the water is creeping upward—one millimetre every forty-three minutes. Projected failure point in six centuries. By then the Works will be a tidy drowned archive.

Machines  
There are exactly thirty-two spindles disguised as standing stones: cylinders of grey alloy throat-high, topped with unlit striker collars. Each is cored by a wire of superconducting ceramic that exits the top, arcs in a bright rigid hoop twelve metres overhead, then dives back toward the floor and disappears through the grating into the water. The wires vibrate but do not touch. Every nine minutes the circuit conducts an impulse the engineers called a pulse-packet. When the packet travels through a wire it lights the entire strand magnesium-white for two seconds, throwing violent silhouettes of whoever stands between them onto the wet walls. Thirty-two flashes in series created what the first observers named the day-in-miniature: a staccato 64-second “sunrise” that repeats, regular as a stopwatch, no matter the hour outside. Despite this illumination, average lux is low—never above a yellowed library bulb—because the flashes are too brief to register as true light.

Objects  
Scattered agents:  
– A stack of oblong crates that sweat beads of Antarctic-blue glaze; none have been opened since arrival six months ago, and they tick like cold bones whenever a spindle fires.  
– One door of acid-yellow polymer set flush into the east wall, leading nowhere the survey teams can fathom; in recent weeks the hinges have begun to drip rust as if infected by tears.  
– A single brass valve the size of a lunch table jutting up through the water sheet. When opened (rare), out rolls a ribbon of jelly-slick fluid that oxidizes to cork-brown crystals within seconds. Labeled “Preservative Matrix, Type IV” by tags half dissolved.  

Feel of the space  
The air is cool, just shy of requiring a coat; oxygen content is fractionally elevated. Breath comes sharpened by potassium chloride and a whiff of ozone from the spindles. Humidity is saturation plus one percent—no textiles quite dry. Visitors report skin stinging as if dusted in lunar salt.  

And yet nothing lives here. Engineers wore filtered masks, deposited no spores, carved no burrows for fauna. Microbial counts register as sterile glass. The only movement is the dripping, the shiver of water breathing between holes in the plates, and the purposeful electric convulsions running through the sculptures. What looks at first like forest understory of slender silver reeds proves, upon rubbing, to be hollow aerogel fibrils—non-sentinel sensor lines feeling for tremors in the newborn mountain skin surrounding the Works. Each node spits telemetry into a silent datalight grid. The grid has no keeper now; it is as self-maintaining as weather, and as careless.

Scale among giants  
Mountain wall outside rises a shattered 2,400 metres—sharp ridgeline still sizzling with basalt needles that pierce low cloud. But the Works itself is a nutshell. A man can walk across its greatest diameter in forty-five nervous paces. From entrance lip you gaze back at the storm-wet floor the way an ant stares into a kettledrum. Size contradicts location. You are standing, compressed, inside the marrow of a peak that feels bigger than countries.

Motion cycles  
Minute-scale: drizzle droplet grows on rib until it weighs exactly 0.46 grams, detaches, plinks into mirror layer below—each drop reporting temperature to neighboring spindles.  
Hour-scale: pressure pulses shift a few megapascals, causing the entire checkered floor to rise or sink a millimetre—so slowly no ankle notices—but over days the change adds quarter-coin tilts reported to visitors via software later in quiet dormitories miles downslope where they play the Works in dreamlike timelapse.  
Month-scale: rusted hinges weep enough iron that the water gains taste, becomes a faint saline that promises future corrosion, thus begins the long, patient making of an inland sea under a cupola no wider than a ballroom.

Rules  
1. Any sound you make of your own accord becomes indistinguishable from the heartbeat within seven seconds. Attempts to shout leave you feeling you have merely continued the echo already at work.  
2. The floor will buoy a human body; should you fall asleep on the plates, slow capillary action draws you downward until you wake chin-deep in iron-scented perfume. Yet drowning is impossible—the water sheet is exactly shoulder height beneath the plates at deepest—so drowning is replaced by a different terror: being trapped at waterline, ears full of the industry of humming alloy, voice already part of the heartbeat.  
3. No flora can root; any seed arriving on clothing is instantly deliquesced by osmotic shock. In consequence, the Works is allergic to green; should a leaf arrive it becomes a blackened smear within minutes.  
4. Outbound signals jam themselves. Equipment radios refuse carrier; compasses oscillate. The Works does not permit itself to be spoken of except at the remove of memory. Even barometers seal up their dials as if embarrassed.

Future  
In one decade the dripping will have raised the water level to the first hoop-wires. Then every spindle flash will ignite brief corona halos across the surface—sunlight salvaged in rings. In three decades the yellow false-door will dissolve from the inside, pouring out more preservative matrix until the cavity glazes amber and the Works becomes a vitreous, echoing honeycomb. In fifty years the mountain will have weathered enough to sound different—heartbeat softened by moss rumoured to have learned how to germinate in blue-glazed crate runoff—but the Works itself will press on, sealed and saturated, a miniature drowned cathedral humming with the same 84-decibel lullaby. And somewhere in the drip-music a final piece of orchestration: two words, already recorded, waiting to be spoken again only when the last oxygen cylinder cracks and the Works at last lies truly empty.

Until then, the mountain stands newborn, and inside its crest this pocket forge continues manufacturing the single condition it understands: water on metal, warmth in cold, silence wrapped around a roar.
