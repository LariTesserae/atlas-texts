The place is called the *Sky Reef*, though no one living remembers why.

Imagine an ocean peeled upward—an impossible tide that hangs in the air like a second sky. From a distance it resembles the upside-down mirror of open sea: a rolling, living ceiling of dark ultramarine suspended two hundred metres above the valley floor. Its lower surface ripples with slow-motion waves, each crest taking minutes to form and collapse. Droplets the size of fists hesitate, then fall with lazy dignity, drumming on leaves that should not be able to hold their weight.

You stand on a newborn ridge that still steps from the bath of its own emergence. Stone is salmon-pink, vesicular, so fresh that when you press your palm to it you feel retained warmth of the planet’s deep pulse. No lichen has found time to root; the rock exhales a faint metallic breath, like rainwater on a gun barrel. Every ridge spine is knife-sharp, every fault a clean tear—gravity and water have not yet fatigued the edges. But the wet is already at work: threads of condensed sky-ocean seep into fractures, germinating impossible chlorophyll overnight.

Upward-growing trees answer the downward ocean. They rise in close colonnades, trunks swollen with stored rain, bark threaded by translucent veins that carry light as much as sap. Their leaves are broad, rubbery, inverted umbrellas flipped to catch the sky-drip. Where foliage overlaps it forms horizontal gutters, braided aqueducts that route water back into the air as a fine warm mist. Vines leap between branches without waiting for sunlight—because light here is moderate, even, filtered through the aqueous ceiling—so they chase gradients of humidity instead, braiding living cables that hum when the wind moves.

Between trunks, space is scarce. Ferns shoulder broad blades into neighbour stems; aerial moss drapes like curtains, heavy with absorbed sky. Epiphytic lilies unfold their petals inside the dusk of the understory, glowing faintly—bioluminescent warning or invitation, too early for biology to have settled the distinction. The overall feeling is of standing in the lung of something still deciding whether to breathe.

And the quiet: not mute, but reserved. Rainfall exists as a tactile experience more than sound; droplets arrive with the hush of wet fingers on skin. Now and then a gas bubble escapes the inverted sea above—an irregular *blop* that becomes a word in no language before it dies against foliage. Even your heartbeat seems an intrusion; the place listens to itself grow.

Elevation keeps you high enough that horizon is a saw-tooth of younger ridges, each one just born, still counting heartbeats since eruption. Between them the valley floor is a mist meadow where water accumulates ankle-deep, an infant lake learning to be mirror. Now and then stone needles pierce through, creating hundreds of tiny islands, colonised already by acid-green algae that etch minerals into the first soil this region will ever own.

Animals, if you can call them that, appear seldom and reluctantly. A glassy salamander, no longer than your thumb, climbs a vertical sheet of water as if the droplet were solid. Its skin shows slow galaxies of moving pigment; when it blinks, you feel you have forgotten something important. High overhead something drifts—a translucent bell, part-jellyfish, part-thistledown, trailing root-like intestines that sip moisture. It pulses once every half-minute, propelling itself a few centimetres through the air, tasting chemistry of brand-new stone. Nothing bites, nothing sings; merely witnesses.

There is no built trace anywhere. No campfire, no flint, no carbonised story. If you mark a boulder with your knife, the wound will heal under mineral sweat before nightfall; the ridge refuses memory. Tools feel awkward here, oversized, like trying to adjust a dream with a wrench. Technology slips from the mind the way a name dissolves in fever. Even fire would doubt itself confronted by this much wet.

The governing principle is vertical counterpoise. Sky pulling water upward into itself; land pulling life upward into its own new pores. Two immensities learning the habit of meeting halfway. The exchange is slow—entire afternoons pass while one leaf fills like a reservoir, one stone pore drinks its first litre. Seasons, here, will last centuries; by the time autumn invents itself, these peaks may have sagged into grandmother hills gentled by rain grandsons.

But in the moment you are drenched, warm, and utterly listened-to. The Sky Reef floats above you like a second chance at drowning. Step carefully; every footfall is a signature on wet paint, the ink of geology not yet set. Stay long enough and you become part of the lush density—another dripping presence among the leaves, half plant, half witness, evaporating upward in infinitesimal increments toward the patient inverted sea.
