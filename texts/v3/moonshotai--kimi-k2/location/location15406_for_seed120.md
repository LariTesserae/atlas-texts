Six inches above the workbench of the worldmaker, hanging in air so dense it drips, spins a droplet-continent no larger than a dew pearl. It is being built while it is already scorched.

Hot-silver vapour rises from its rim like breath from a kiln yet refuses to disperse; instead it sticks, forming a micrometre-high skyline of curling fern-towers and arched gill-bridges. Nothing here is permitted to erode because the very idea of erosion has not been written into it yet: the tiniest fragment of time it can hold, one biologic heartbeat long, is time enough to terraform. The meshwork of newborn stones clicks outward on primitive hinges—lignified shell, hammered bronze atoms, one chitin plate fitted against the next—until the entire sphere becomes an interlocking armillary of tower-corals, guttering tongues, and gutter-roofed stamens.

Water (hot, mineral, aseptic) does not flow but condenses in relentless sheets on every ledge. It starts as vapour and ends as rivulets the width of capillaries yet loud—cathedral-echo loud—because the continent is a whistle carved by giants: every passage, canal, air-shaft tuned to a single tone, a braying A-sharp that never decays. The sound keeps the sphere buoyant in the humid air of the artisan’s studio; the sound *is* its weather cycle. Every eleven seconds the tone palpitates, pressure falls, new droplets precipitate, slide down rails, and the landscape heaves half a degree hotter from their friction.

The flora has no names because it needs none. You simply see the colour of wanting. Growth is so dense that stalk, petal, and architecture collapse together—spiral taproots hanging like rigging, leaves that are also shutters, flowers the size of pinheads that swell into corridor-lamps when they open. They open rarely. When they do, the dim amber glow they release is subtractive darkness—every lumen pulled out of nearby objects, so the leaves themselves momentarily print inverse silhouettes on your retina.

Fauna appears less often than the flowers open. There is nothing with vertebrae here. Instead, in two places—once at the equatorial ridge, once on the dark side—tiny brass beetles guard galleries. Their carapaces begin life as pollen and are rolled into shape between two armoured jaws. They wear barley-seed armour over lithium-stuffed abdomens. When two beetles meet they clang so loudly they make new storm cells; the water immediately gathers, climbs their shells and begins to design a new gallery for them, parthenogenic architecture budding straight from song. So far only four have ever met, therefore there are only four galleries. A fifth collision is overdue, rumoured, feared.

Nothing in this dripping clave can be touched on the outside; the artisan’s calloused fingertip is a continent-sized moon pressing down with more gravity than any mountain could survive. The only way in is to read the surface like Braille. Trace the etched axon of climbing jasmine-titanium vines, pluck one note of its permanent horn blast, then step inward through the wound the tone tears in reality.

If you manage this and do not dissolve, you will find the only open square in the entire land: a plaza exactly four blood-warm droplets across. Beneath the plaza thrums the moist, embryonic engine of impending erosion. Positive ions collect here, longing to become erosion, practicing by releasing steam that has nowhere to go. They hurl themselves against the plaza’s floor, leaping in suicidal arcs to feel what destruction might be like; each time they reassemble as the same stray mist. In their jumping they have begun to etch the first false chronicle: the story that someday—cycles hence, millennia hence—this bedroom planet will age, crack, and spill into an ordinary sea. But that would require subtraction, and subtraction is currently mythical.

At the sphere’s south pole stands a single palm-sized minaret made of spun sugarglass protecting a drop of absolute night. From this drop the one colour forbidden to the interior leaks out: violet so saturated it casts a second shadow backward into the preceding three seconds. Beetle 3 found the minaret once, scraped it, stole a microgram of violet, and vanished. The sound bent when it happened, became a triad, then forgot the middle note. Ever since, the space beneath the minaret seems to overprint faint after-images of futures that could belong to another continuum entirely. The air occasionally condenses into a tiny glass staircase that rises three rungs and is gone before it is climbed.

In another quadrant an entire district has already outgrown the sphere. When the humidity hits 101 % (which is always) a slow-motion avalanche of vegetation spills over an invisible brink, folds back on itself, and becomes next second’s inner scaffold. Thus the continent is a Möbius strip of volume, curving back through the fourth dimension its own primitive tool-kit never dreamt of. It ends at its beginning but it will never notice.  

Standing inside it—if you can keep your lungs from crystallising moisture—you feel the rules:

Rule 1: Anything you name solidifies and cools; once cooled it cannot grow. Hence the beetles remain unnamed.

Rule 2: Memories greater than eleven seconds long coalesce as brass, become beetles, clang into one another, and are absorbed. Human visitors rarely last nine.

Rule 3: The louder you are, the smaller the space you can occupy. Shout, and the corridors pinch until you stand inside your own throat. Whimper, and they swell into amphitheatres where droplets fall like bombs.

Most visitors, unable to leave a footprint, resort to apology here. The sound of their apology approaches the continual drone and is gently drafted into the next coat of shell-lacquer on the rim.

And still the sphere poises, hot, wet, already old by its own standards but newborn by ours, a candlewick burning down before it is lit. A smear on the godworker’s finger, a planet in its evening gown of condensates—a teacup that holds its own monsoon—a secret heart pounding right above the workbench, there for the next careless breath.
