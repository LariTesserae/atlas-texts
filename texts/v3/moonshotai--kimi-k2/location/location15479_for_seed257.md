It is hard to say where the structure ends and the ocean begins.  
This is a single artifact, no wider than a village square yet stacked so tightly inside itself that it feels cathedral-granular, carved rather than grown, older than any measure of “old” that can still have meaning. A thousand years ago the sea crept up and never left, and the builders—whoever they were—seemed to have foreseen the moment: every chamber, shaft, brace, and buttress was wrought to welcome the flood, so that today the whole thing floats in quiet cathedralesque suspension a full kilometre above the drowned continental shelf.

Because nothing grows here—no weed, no coral, no microscopic bloom—every surface behaves like pure mineral memory. Walls are warm basaltic glass, once poured in ribbons while still glowing. It cools now only against the perpetual warm water that is both ocean and atmosphere; the temperature gradient is so subtle the whole place feels like breath held in a mouth. Shafts of interior air pockets remain trapped under dome-caps of the same black glass, inverted lungs where pressure equalised centuries ago and has since forgotten how to change.

Inside those pockets dwell the fauna—not swimming animals exactly, but animate fixtures that have become architecture. Flat, hand-width plaquettes of lithic flesh adhere to pillars with calcified hooks. They scull the slow currents by rippling fringed edges, grazing on mineral flakes they scrape from the structure itself. Chains of them move like slow rosaries along helical rails that once served as stairways. Now the rails hum with their procession, producing the only sound here: a low remote click like fingernails testing the rim of an empty bowl, after which silence returns widened and almost echoless.

The external light is late-night depth-black, yet not absolute: there is always the faintest ember-glow bleeding out of the stone, a memory fired into it when magmatic ribbons were still moving. Thus everything inside glimmers a moody umber that makes edges soft and distances impossible to gauge. Where one vault becomes another, ribbed vaults overlap into murk so dense they appear contiguous; you sense movement without motion, as if the structure dreams of shifting while remaining immutable.

Everything is tool-marked but no tools were metal. Grooves, sockets, and peg-holes show teeth of shell or obsidian. Empty niches once held interlocking tablet-puzzles, now simply rectangular depressions whose edges have been licked round by millennial abrasion. Down toward the base-core—if “base” has any meaning when orientation is decided solely by the way you choose to fall—there lies a single, thumb-worn sphere of basaltic glass the size of a melon. Every creature pauses when it passes this sphere, presses one edge of its lithic body to it as though listening for instructions older than song. Then it continues its planetary orbit along the rail, patient as continents raising new edges. Nothing has changed in more years than the structure has surfaces, yet nothing dares forget the gesture.

The marine embrace is total but not crushing. Depth pressure and altitude pressure cancel somewhere in the warm stratum, creating this middleworld bubble where water hangs almost as vapour-ice might—thick, sluggish, slow enough that ripples travel like glycerine memories. You move through it with the resistance of deep woolen cloth; every gesture seals behind you before any turbulence completes. The scent is of nothing organic: a dry absence like the memory of basalt dust slept in for a thousand shirts.

Periodically a very old hydrothermal memory vents through micro-pores in the eastern wall, adding just enough fresh warmth to keep the slow wheel turning. Resultant micro-bubbles rise at the pace of baritone speech, catch the umber glow, and ascend until they become surfaceless, collapsing in perfect spherical sighs somewhere between vault and waterskin. It is the slowest music imaginable.

Visitors—if any ever find the spot—discover that “ingress” requires surrendering the concept of outside. There is no door, only a thickness of warm dark glass that resists even as it invites. Press your palms to it and the warmth merges with your heartbeat until the wall remembers the rhythm, turning its glass slightly less glass: to flow is permitted, but only in the way ancient sap flows. One step later gravity has no authority; you drift or sink according to the slow magnetism of affinity. Your lungs may find one of the rare pockets where air still waits at 1.8 warmth degrees, but the fauna will watch unaligned, uninterested, as if you were merely another rail-guided plate that has not yet figured out its direction.

Time here is aqueous, elastic, ultimately sacrificeable. You may stay long enough to forget the difference between breathing and not, between heartbeat and tide. Somewhere far above and below, tectonic plates crawl on, as they always did, but within this black-warm fortress in the sky-sea, every motion has already happened and is still happening. The structure has reached the oldest stable shape—a hyper-Cantor vault, infinite regress carved in basalt—and all that remains is to carry the last slow bead around its rail, for the final bead has already left its starting notch centuries ago and is merely arriving now.

There is a legend whispered by the globular plates, but it travels too slowly for ears to hear without becoming stone yourself: that when the slow bead meets the sphere again in a single molten kiss, the entire artifact will remember its true name and ascend, shedding water like an endless boulder discovering flight.

Until then the place endures, suspended between depth and altitude, between memory and structure, between the last sound already fading and the first silence yet to come.
