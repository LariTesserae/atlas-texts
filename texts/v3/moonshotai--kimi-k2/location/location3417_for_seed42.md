The city is called Eidenvein and it floats head-down.  
Four or five centuries ago it was a mining town on the cliffs of an alp, but the rain came—warm at first, then cool and patient—and kept coming until the range itself became a chain of islands whose peaks are only the knuck knuckles of something much larger sleeping under glassy water.  
Eidenvein refused to drown. Instead it capsized.

Imagine a cathedral turned inside out and hung from a mirror.  
Every stone arcade, every slate roof, every gutter and bell-cote now points toward the abyss like roots yearning for darkness.  
The streets hang in vertical catenary curves, held by ropes of calcified evergreen.  
The trees—mostly dark-boled yew and pale, blood-hearted larch—grew down after the city grew up, threading their cambium through pillar and buttress until the two architectures fused: one of sap and heartwood, one of schist and mortar.  
What was once flying buttress is now holding root; what was once roof-beam is now phloem highway.

You arrive by swimming the rain.  
The rain is ocean-sized; it begins nowhere and ends nowhere, a single sheet of water dropping forever between cloud and cloud.  
Somewhere inside that sheet the temperature slips to cool, not cold—just cool enough that your thoughts stay tethered to your skull—and you feel the first cedar branch brush your ankle like a kid on a subway.  
Follow that branch upward (downward, if you still trust your old sky-compass) and you breach the inverted canopy that is Eidenvein’s new ground plane.

Light here is normal for dusk: pewter, readable, shadow-edged.  
It filters through the sheet of rain above—no, below—no, concurrently—until you stop trying to map cardinal directions and accept “sky” as any surface far enough away to glow.  
Because the rain is continuous and the city is small (a single hill’s worth of masonry, no wider than a plaza and a half) every surface wets to patent leather; lichens unspool long botanical punctuation marks across it all.  
The coolness keeps evaporation slow; puddles adhere, growing tiny aerial rootlets that quest vertically toward the buried sun they have never seen.

Fauna is abundant, but obedient to the inverted gravity.  
Glossy otters with opal nictitating membranes slide along the gutters, chasing silver imperial moths whose wings bear the floor-plans of vanished townhouses.  
Schools of glass minnows thread the latticework of yew roots, feeding on bark flecks and the echo of church bells that still resound inside the wood.  
Every bell in Eidenvein cracked during the Capsize; the metal cooled into new shapes, but the sound stayed, diffusing through lignin like slow yeast.  
If you press your ear to a trunk you hear the bronze still falling, a muffled carillon that makes the entire grove thrum—loud, but plush, as if someone turned volume up and edge off simultaneously.

Human presence is urban but primitive.  
The survivors—perhaps three hundred—walk on braided nettle-fiber tightropes strung between trunks.  
Their footfall is deliberate; with each step they plant a small ceramic bell-clapper (tinies of the old bells) into the living wood, feeding the acoustic yeast.  
They wear cloaks woven from cedar cambium that harden into corslets when soaked and flex like cloth when dry.  
No metal is left; what little iron survived the rusting rains they beat into leaf-shaped fish hooks, each hook etched with a family door number so the catch can find its way home.  
They keep no fire—too dangerous under perpetual drip—but ferment everything: shredded root, insect chitin, even the soft limestone sift that still sheds from fractured vaulting.  
The alcohol is cool and slightly saline; it tastes like stone fruit trying to remember the moon.

Government is by echo.  
Every decision is spoken into the rain-sheet at the civic node—an upside-down baptistery whose font is now a tide-pool of brine and quartz sand.  
The words descend (ascend) through water, reflect off the invisible ocean floor far overhead, and return weeks later garbled, reversed, half swallowed.  
By then the community has already moved in the direction the echo suggests; they treat governance as a form of weather prediction rather than command.  
Justice is the same: a criminal is tied to a young larch and left to argue with the tree.  
By the time the trunk has overgrown the rope, the crime has become anecdote inside the annual rings.

Erosion is established but courteous.  
Stone rounds itself; mortar powders; wood plugs the gaps.  
Nothing collapses, nothing improves; the city drifts at centimeter speed toward a shape that will suit its next century, the way a river stone rolls to fit the palm that will eventually find it.  
The process is so slow that a child who drops a toy marble today will, at ninety, see the marble half embedded in larch buttress like a politician’s medallion, still bright because the rain scrubs oxygen away.

Weirdness is low, just a sour shimmer at the corner of vision.  
Sometimes a root will bear a knotted face wearing your grandmother’s frown.  
Sometimes the rain will pause for three heartbeats, and in that hush you hear the old city still standing upright somewhere very far above—or below—applauding itself with intact bells.  
Residents call these moments “vertical memory” and do not discuss them; they simply wait until the drip resumes, then continue fermenting.

Scale stays intimate; you can walk from civic node to outer root-grove in fifty breaststrokes of air, though the swim may take an hour because you keep stopping to watch an otter open a moth like a love letter.  
Density is sparse by design; every human family is allotted one buttress, one rope, one fermentation urn, and enough empty air to hear their own pulse echo back as municipal carillon.  
No one feels alone because the grove itself is choir.

When outsiders ask for a map the residents hand them a cedar cone.  
“Hold it until it hums,” they say.  
If you listen past your own blood you will feel, in the cone’s slow fibres, the entire city’s drift: not a location but a lullaby cadence, patient, cool, inverted, rain-washed, root-held, bell-fed, sea-dropped, grove-dreamed.  
Carry that cadence far enough and you will build Eidenvein again wherever you stand—upside-down or right-side-up, it makes no difference; the city has already agreed to wait inside the wood.
