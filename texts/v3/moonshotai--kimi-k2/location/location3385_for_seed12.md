The city of Nereid’s Birth floats three vertical miles above an ocean that has no horizon—only a slow, silver-black skin that reflects nothing because there is nothing yet to reflect. The water is the eldest thing here; it remembers no shore. From below it looks solid, a planet of liquid mercury cradling its own curved sky. From above it is weather: a suspended rain that never quite falls, droplets the size of melons hanging in the warm air, touching one another, merging, birthing new droplets that ascend again. A three-dimensional ocean, saturated, 2.9 out of 3—an atmosphere that behaves like a tide.

Above this airborne sea, the newborn mesa-city is still learning its own shape. It is a tableland of living basalt, extruded only yesterday by some infant tectonics, still exhaling heat. Its edges are lace where cooling stone has curled back on itself, forming gothic ribs, drip-castle buttresses. No erosion worth the name has had time to act; every angle is knife-fresh, every surface a record of the moment it cooled. Yet the stone is already colonised: vines have found purchase in faults that opened within hours of formation, their seeds arriving on warm updrafts that smell of iron and kelp. The plants germinated while the rock was still plastic; some stems are half-included, glassy skins of basalt still glowing faintly around them like amber with an insect at its heart. Vegetation 1.8, lush but young, still uncertain which way is up, growing downward into sky just as eagerly.

Elevation 2.4 means the air is thin enough for moonlight to feel sharp, yet the temperature lingers at 2.5—blood-warm, almost feverish. Nothing cools here; the ocean-rain hoards heat the way a whale hoards breath. When a droplet touches your arm it is warmer than your own skin, a reverse sweat, the world feeding you its metabolism. You do not dry; you become damper inside your clothes until fabric dissolves into a felt of algae. The dimness 1.1 is not absence but postponement: daylight is filtered through two miles of hovering water, prismatic, delayed. Sunrises arrive six hours late and spread sideways, a bruise that opens like a hand. Colours exist in the ultraviolet vocabulary of plankton; when you look at a blossom you see patterns that bees normally keep secret.

Fauna 1.2—rare, but what is present is oversized and soft. Nereid moths with swallowtail wings fifteen metres wide trail silk that hardens into basalt thread where it touches stone; they repair the city’s cracks faster than they can widen. Their caterpillars are mobile fountains, bodies a lattice of capillaries through which airborne ocean is pumped, irrigating rooftop gardens that have no soil. They move so slowly 1.1 that lichen grows on their backs while they graze, so a herd appears as a chain of green hills sliding an inch a year.

Density 1.6: the city is crowded but not by people—by itself. Every courtyard is three courtyards occupying the same coordinates, separated by mere minutes. The stone keeps remaking yesterday’s walls a handspan to the left, so you inhabit a blur of architecture. Houses are grown, not built: families plant a seed of geode that draws calcium from the rain, unfolding rooms like origami in slow motion. By ordinance 1.6 built—urban—streets are negotiated corridors between competing growths. A roof today becomes someone else’s floor tomorrow; property is measured in probability clouds. Primitive tech 0.6 is mandatory: only tools that can be dropped without shattering are allowed. Bone, cured kelp, cooled lava floss. Hammers are living boulders trained to respond to song; you persuade rather than swing. There is no metal yet; the crust of the mesa is still deciding what ores it will consent to be. When you need a knife you ask the caterpillar to crystallise a glass fang from its own blood; it obliges, then regenerates, and the blade warms to body temperature, tasting faintly of salt and gratitude.

Weirdness 1.8 leaks from the schedule of the world. Gravity itself is unfinished: in some plazas it points outward a few degrees, so you drift toward the edge and have to lean back like a sailor into wind. Children play by launching themselves uphill along this tilt, gliding in slow arcs, laughing—a sound absorbed so quickly by the water-air that an echo arrives before the original, a backward giggle that makes adults dizzy. Time zones shift by altitude; your feet age slower than your head. Citizens carry hourglasses necklaced between clavicle and hip to keep personal clocks.

Sound 1.3—mostly the hush of droplets grazing leaves, a wet percussion felt in the lungs more than heard. Occasionally a tectonic sigh moves through the basalt and every droplet shivers, a collective inhale, then releases a chord that is neither major nor minor but tectonic. The city answers with the low wooden thrum of balcony railings grown hollow for exactly this resonance, an architecture that sings only when the earth remembers to breathe.

At night—difficult to define—bioluminescent plankton condenses into mile-wide spheres that drift between mesa and ocean, temporary moons. Under their lavender glow you see the underside of the city pregnant with roots, each vine trying to decide whether to dive the remaining miles and complete the circuit, to drink the sea that birthed it. No one knows what will happen when the first root touches water; some say the city will finish forming and erosion will finally begin, others that the ocean will climb the root like a rope and flood the sky. Either way it is slow, dynamic 1.1, a slow-motion countdown measured in fathoms and centuries.

Visitors arrive by being born here—they climb out of oversized seedpods that form overnight on the warm stone, already damp, already knowing the rules: Do not ask for iron. Do not build faster than stone can cool. Do not drink the rain— it drinks you. Speak quietly; sound is expensive. Carry your house gently; it is still learning who it wants to be. Above all, trust the weirdness 1.8: the city is stranger than you can balance on, and that is why it floats.
