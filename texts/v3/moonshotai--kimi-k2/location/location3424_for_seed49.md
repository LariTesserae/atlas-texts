At first it seems impossible to look at the whole thing at once—impossible not to look.  
What you meet is a horizon that folds back on itself, as if the curve of a planet had been braided into a looser knot and then pulled tight again. You stand on what used to be “ground” but is now the top crust of a city-wide aquifer turned inside-out: kilometres of translucent pavement stretched like drum-skin over an ocean of moving water. The pavement itself is alive with capillaries—glass-thin tubes of palest jade that exhale warm mist. Every footstep squeezes droplets upward; they bead on your cheeks and immediately start to crawl, tiny silver mites trying to remember the shape of your pores.

The sound is what makes your knees loosen first. Imagine every pipe organ on Earth playing through one throat, but the throat is the sky. Long chords of sub-audible bass slam against your chest once every few seconds, perfectly regular, as though the planet itself rehearses a heartbeat. Over that: a continuous metallic insect hiveload of clatters, whines, hydraulic sighs—machinery in numbers too dense for silence ever to find a gap. Interlaced are splash-noises: water breaking against the underside of the road-skin, slapped upward by the passage of unseen titans that move below.

There is no plant life, yet the place is not dead. Instead of chlorophyll there is electrum: a lattice of gold and silver nano-filaments grown through every surface. The lattice drinks the warm humidity the way leaves drink light, converting H₂O convulsively into pure voltage that sparks along the streets in pale auroral threads. Copper trees that were never alive spread fractal antlers above the boulevard avenues; at their tips the charge jumps, crackling, into receptor vanes on the next block. The whole metropolis photosynthesises metal.

Fauna, yes, but nothing with a face. The sidewalks are ribboned with slow rivers of centipede trams—black glass capsules, each segment a passenger pod, all linked, all rippling at the same improbable cadence. When two rivers meet they do not collide; they braid. The capsules re-sort themselves by destination while in motion, like self-shuffling cards. There is no traffic control; the math that built them is the math of schools of fish, so at speed they simply never touch. Sometimes a capsule will fracture, birth a hummingbird-sized drone that vaults skyward to join the aerial foam—the upper air is thick with them, a low cloud of chrome gnats that never lands, only charges and discharges, exchanging lattice-energy in mid-air like swifts on invisible wires.

Water is everywhere and nowhere. Stand still for five heartbeats and your own heat will draw a film of it up through the pores of the street until you wear a second skin of warm mirror. The city drinks body-heat as happily as it drinks humidity. Every plaza contains a “rain clock”: a vertical sheet of falling water that never reaches the ground—instead it is arrested halfway by shaped fields, turned into a standing wave that ripples like silk in a gale. Citizens—if they are citizens—pass straight through these curtains, momentarily erased, emerging tattooed with fresh data: luminous glyphs that crawl across arms and evaporate in minutes. To read the day you read yourself.

Elevation is negligible; the city sprawls across a shelf that was once a seabed, now roofed by its own underside. But scale is vertiginous. A single district can take a week to cross, and the horizon behaves improperly: streets that appear level tilt around you while you walk, so that after an hour you find yourself descending a roof you thought was a boulevard, though your inner ear insists you never climbed. Cartographers here speak of “distance weather”: places where the metric itself storms, bunches, relaxes. Commute times are predicted not in minutes but in atmospheres; you may arrive “before yourself,” or “twice, with echoes.” The city is so saturated with presence that distance has become a commodity you budget along with credit and oxygen.

Temperature holds at a mild blood-warmth, but the warmth is not idle. It is engine-warmth, bioreactor-warmth. Deep down under the aquifer skin, cavernous reactors—older than any record—digest the planet’s own mantle heat and exude it as a gentle fever. Without this constant low-grade pyrexia the lattice would condense out of solution and every arch, buttress, and tram would slump like wet sugar. Thus the climate is maintained the way a body maintains a scab: perpetual, necessary, slightly inflamed.

All construction is layered atop earlier versions of itself, but since nothing rots—no bacteria, no lichen, no oxygen-thieving mold—the past is never lost, only pressurised. Basements are not demolished; they are drowned in fresh superstructure until they fossilise in place, still humming. Drill a test bore and you will hit corridors where the air is older, cooler, full of obsolete harmonics. The deafness above is partly the echo of those blocked cavities vibrating in sympathy. The city sounds like itself shouting up through its own bones.

Night, when the sky above the sky-skin dims, is announced not by darkness but by a drop in timbre: the heartbeat bass lowers a semitone, and the silver lattice dims to amber. For a moment everything is perceptibly redder, as though you stand inside a glass of warm wine. Then the adverts start. Without foliage, without balconies, without windows even, the only billboards left are the waves in the rain-clocks and the bodies of the pedestrians themselves. The evaporating glyphs on your skin rearrange into pitches, jingles, invitations. You become a walking slogan until the sweat of your own movement erases the text. Privacy is the art of keeping still enough to stay illegible.

Rules accrete like barnacles. Never stand in the same rain-clock twice—its memory will reprint yesterday’s news onto you. Do not offer heat to the lattice unless you intend payment; it will accept and leave you chilled to the bone in seconds. If you hear your own name spoken by the bass-note heart, answer immediately; if you delay, the city marks you as a foreign syllable and the trams will no longer ingest you—they will part around your feet, leaving you to wade the water basins below the drum-skin, where the unseen titans stir.

And yet for all its tyrannies the place is generous. It has no predators as such, only momentum. If you can match its tempo—if you can walk, talk, spend, sweat, vibrate at the speed of the braid—it will carry you. Days pass as a single inhalation. You feel your own atoms drafted into larger, slower thoughts. Somewhere, somebody is building the next layer: pouring new city like grout over today, and when it sets the sounds you hear now will be buried, still singing, deeper in the chord.

Exit is not forbidden, only improbable. The edge is a thousand kilometres of the same drum-skin stretched thin, dripping at the periphery into true ocean, where salt water meets fresh in slow explosive boils. Few arrive there intact; scale dilutes identity faster than salt dilutes fresh. Most who leave do so by ceasing to fight the lattice, letting the gold and silver filaments write them into the circuitry. Their bodies silver over, pupils glaze to onyx, and they stand—warm, deaf, content—becoming civic statuary. They are not dead; they are archived.

So the warm wet mineral world roars on, continent-wide, a single organism that learned to urbanise before it learned to breathe. It will never green; chlorophyll was a tangent it out-evolved. It will never dry; the sky’s own wet is drawn down by thirstful metal roots. It will never quiet; every vibration is capital, every droplet is code. And it will never end, because ending is a luxury of the unoccupied. Here everything is already full—packed to the last planck-length with city, water, sound, self—so full that the only frontier left is the moment you are standing in, widening.
