The Sump Crevasse is not, properly speaking, a city; it’s the city-shaped wound that New Babbage left behind when the crust gave way.  
A full district—ironworks, row-houses, pneumatic post, three chapels and a stock exchange—dropped overnight into the wet throat of the world.  Now it hangs, head-down, five storeys below the old street-line, still glowing like coal in a grate that has been left to die.

Water is the first fact.  lives only a hand-span below the surface: condensate drips from every joist, gutters sweat, and the old steam mains sputter hot ropes of brackish mist.  Rainwater that once vanished into drains now perches on splintered beams, then loses its nerve and falls in slow, deliberate dribbles.  Nothing is ever truly dry; paper curls, skin glistens, iron blooms orange overnight.  The air tastes of nickels.

Temperature keeps pace with rumor: 38 °C at midnight, 42 by dawn.  The heat does not roar; it accumulates, like debt.  Every cast-iron wall stores it, every brick exhales it back at dusk.  Workers long ago learned to walk with the shallow tread of thieves, lest friction push the thermometer past endurance.

Vegetation is almost a joke.  Between tilted flagstones a few pioneer weeds—fat-leafed, metallic green—have worked roots into the lime-rich slurry.  Their stems are translucent and show the rust moving upward like slow arterial blood.  One bough-root found purchase in a wrought-iron newell post and has since strangled it in a vegetal half-nelson; no one intervenes.  The plants behave like reluctant colonists, arriving only to discover they dislike the neighborhood.

Fauna, on the other hand, arrived in carnival numbers.  Heat and seepage brewed an insect monsoon.  Midges braid the lamplight, cockroaches skate across standing water on surface tension alone, and a whitish troglob rat—as long as your shin—has learned to shinny the vertical pipes without ever touching the ground.  Their combined breathing is the loudest thing: a constant soft roar, the way surf sounds when you put your ear to a shell held against a pillow.  Individual voices vanish inside the chorus; human speech rises an octave unless augmented by tin speaking-trumpets bolted to every tenth corner.

The geometry is urban but embryonic, half-born.  Streets that used to meet at tidy angles now buckle into infant ravines.  Buildings tilt like seedlings toward the drip-line.  The old #4 Foundry, still upright, has had its basement filled with calcite-laden water; inside, stalactites younger than a year hang from conveyor beams, dripping onto the assembly line below.  The factory never officially closed; management merely decreed that anyone who could fit into the lower levels without drowning might clock in.  Workers in waders now sand-cast piston heads under a cathedral of dripping stone, each drop hissing when it hits the iron.

Light is rationed.  Sunlight reaches only the uppermost balconies at noon, a coin-sized bright spot that migrates across shattered walls like the ghost of a watchful child.  Everywhere else is lamp-oil twilight:  fishtail burners bolted to gas lines that still, miraculously, hold pressure.  They burn with a low, irritated sigh, painting the wet brickwork the color of dried blood.  Where lamplight meets water the colors invert:  brick looks lilac, steel looks gold, human skin acquires the waxy pallor of old soap.

Sound stacks, layer on layer.  Beneath the insect surf you can pick out the slow drip-dap-dap of water wearing through mortar, the clank of a shift foreman testing an unseen rail, the wet hiccup of steam pistons bleeding off into cul-de-sacs.  Every twenty minutes the city’s arterial mains attempt to equalize; somewhere in the dark a valve flutters like a dying bird, then gives up.  Residents can set their pocket-watches by the hush that follows.

Scale is domestic and impossible at once.  No vista exceeds the reach of a shout, yet every alley ends in a culvert that seems to remember oceans.  Corridors were built for horse-carts but now admit only one tentative pedestrian at a time, turning sideways-on to let the damp pass like a polite stranger.  There is space, but it is accountable: every cistern, every sagging girder, every wayward balcony is named, catalogued, insured by scavenger cooperatives whose ledgers stay underwater-proof in tin coffers nailed high on the wall.

Technology survives as etiquette.  Pneumatic tubes still snake between offices; packets filled with requisition slips slither overhead, dripping like syrupy clocks.  Once a week you hear the hollow donk of a cylinder arriving at a station long since sunken underwater.  The capsule floats; clerks retrieve it with bent wires, read the mold-flecked invoice, then file it in cabinets that have become aquariums.  No one has the heart to tell upper management that the orders are three years late.  The system keeps going because etiquette demands continuity, not usefulness.

Erosion is present tense.  You can stand at the lip of Sump Square and watch grit leave a wall in real time:  each second a grain, each minute a freckle, each hour a face you used to recognize.  Residents have grown philosophical.  They prop timbers against nothing, cast buttresses into puddles, knowing tomorrow the brick will decide its own angle of repose.  The civic motto, stenciled on washed-out hoardings, reads: “We Are Still In The Blueprint.”

The civic law is simple:  nothing heavy may be left on the ground overnight.  Couches, safes, corpses—all sink.  Graveyards migrate up walls; coffins are roped to the upper window-frames like swollen hammocks.  Deceased property is taxed by the drip:  the city assessor measures the water level beside your ancestor’s coffin each month and levies accordingly.  People pay gladly; it proves the deceased still possess volume.

outsiders arrive expecting spectacle, but the Crevasse refuses climax.  Change is gradational, tectonic in miniature.  A balcony detaches and tilts—over weeks, not seconds—until it kisses the flood, becomes a bridge, a pier, finally a reef of splintered teak on which new stalagmites grow.  No moment feels decisive; history is a slow leak.

And yet the place is alive.  Children born here think of horizontal ground as mythical, like the equator.  They cobble together stilts from broom handles and teeter above the soup, playing “Delivery Clerk,” imitating the solemn tread of adults balancing payroll sacks on their heads.  Their parents, meanwhile, distil the condensation that streaks iron beams—collect it in thimbles, boil it with tea-leached of tannin, trade it for coal that someone farther up still hauls out of the seam.  A micro-economy of droplet, flame, and echo.

Night is only a darker shade of dim.  Lamps stay lit; insects thicken the air until breathing feels like sipping broth through a cloth.  You learn to time your inhalations with the drip of a distant eave, to walk heel-toe so the water knows you’re coming.  Conversation drops to a murmur because the roar of fauna swells to meet silence; hush itself becomes loud.

Some evenings the city’s inversion fogs, and the temperature momentarily swaps: cool air slides down the walls, hot air climbs past your ankles like escaping cats.  For three minutes you shiver.  People pause mid-stride to savor the anomaly, collars raised, eyes closed, listening to the drip change its key from G to B-flat.  Then the heat settles back, amused, like a landlord collecting rent.

Above, original New Babbage continues its business with the oblivious cheer of a corpse at a funeral who refuses to lie down.  Trams clang, brokers shout, bakeries issue forth cinnamon clouds that drift to the cracked edge and stop, embarrassed, at the drop-off.  From below you can sometimes see the bellies of those clouds—soft, cinnamon-colored, and unattainable as heaven.

The Sump Crevasse neither collapses nor heals; it simply accrues.  Brick by brick, drop by drop, contract by mold-fuzzed contract, it becomes more exactly the sump it already is:  a civic embryo, a district-sized organ still digesting the meal of itself.  And one day—centuries hence, or tomorrow, time here keeps its own counsel—the drips will have hollowed out the final lintel, the last lamp will sigh out, a rat will teeter on the last upright, and the city will finish falling upward into the idea it has been rehearsing all along:  a place that exists only in the act of becoming something else, forever half-written, forever half-drowned, forever politely, patiently, loudly, sizzling in its own warm broth.
