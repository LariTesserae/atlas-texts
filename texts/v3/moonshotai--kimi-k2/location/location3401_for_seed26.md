You descend through a wound in the earth that never quite scabs over.  
The lift cage rattles down a shaft lined with riveted copper plates already sweating in the cold—condensation forming, dripping, re-forming—so that every metal sheet weters its own reflection. You smell ozone and wet rust before your boots touch bottom.

Welcome to the **Tideplant Underworks**, a newborn industrial territory clawed out of subsurface rock only three seasons ago and already half-drowned in its own breath.

1.  THE FLOOR  
It is not ground. It is decking: catwalk gratings bolted to stubby pylons driven into raw basalt. Between the slats you glimpse black sump water ten metres down, rocking gently like a sleeping child. That water is the real floor—cold as meltwater, salt as tears, and climbing. The pumps are louder than the furnaces; they speak in diesel hiccups that echo up the cavity’s throat and return as wet vowels.

2.  THE WALLS  
Fresh-cut gneiss, still livid with sparkle, rings the space in a steep natural amphitheatre. Nothing is smooth—everything sheared yesterday. The stone vents heatless moisture; capillary films slide downhill, carrying glittering sulfates that bloom into white frost-flowers overnight. When shifts change, miners snap these flowers off for luck, crunching them like meringue while the stone begins to weep again.

3.  THE CEILING  
An acre of low-built iron roofing stretches from wall to wall, every seam sealed with tar and prayer. Steam hits it, condenses, and falls back as a perpetual indoor drizzle—cold rain on a factory plain that exists entirely underground. Workers call it “the indoor weather.” Umbrellas are uniform issue.

4.  THE VEGETATION  
There shouldn’t be any, yet pale abortive tendrils finger up through decking joints: a saprophytic orphan plant that learned to root in condensation alone. It has no chlorophyll; its leaves the colour of skim milk, veined with manganese blue. It lives fast, flowers like a whisper, drops spores that rattle across tin like insect legs, then dissolves into grey jelly. Nothing eats it; nothing mourns.

5.  THE FAUNA  
Rumour credits albino lamp-eels slipping through the sump, feeding on lubricant grease. You hear them rarely—sudden metallic belches, a tail slapping a hollow pylon. Management denies them, but welders leave sacrificial gobs of lard on the grating just in case.

6.  THE LIGHT  
A haze of sodium globes droops from cables, each in its wire birdcage. They swing whenever a compressor kicks, so illumination sways between forge-orange and guttering blood. Shadows lurch; droplets become molten beads mid-air. Workers strap forehead torches, but the dark is a gas: it fills every pause.

7.  THE MACHINES  
At the centre stands the Thaw-Stack, four storeys of brass and cruel green paint designed to drink the frigid groundwater, heat it in thunderous coal-fuel guts, and jet live steam back into the galleries. This keeps the roofs clear of ice and the stone from contracting too fast, which would shear the bolts. The Stack never sleeps; it sighs like an old whale, coughs cinders that hiss out in the drizzle, and every sixty minutes excretes a clot of iron-red sludge that slides down a chute into the waiting sump—fresh land being born backwards.

8.  THE WORK  
Crews in quilted canvas and vulcanised aprons wrangle expansion joints, hammer ice from the rail tracks that go nowhere yet, and catalogue new cracks. There is always a new crack. Geologists say the chamber is still “relaxing” into its shape, as if the planet regretted this incision and tried to shrug it closed. The noise therefore is polyphonic: pump throb, hoist clank, the high keen of safety valves, the lower groan of rock under strain, and through it all the constant tap-dance of water hitting tin.

9.  THE RULES  
Nothing organic leaves. Boots, tools, even cigarette filters are accounted for: a single rag jamming a pump could drown the yard in a shift.  
Stay on the yellow painted mesh; the white alloy plates are frost-slick and end in sump teeth.  
Never trust a silence; if the pumps hush, the water is rising faster than suction can swallow.  
Report pale growths—botanists want samples, but the shift-bosses want them burned; whichever finds you first decides.  
And if the lamps flicker toward ultraviolet for three blinks, evacuate: the Stack is about to hiccup superheated gas. No alarm will sound—management hates shutdown fees—so the light itself is your only herald.

10.  THE OVERFLOW  
Some evenings, when the drizzle thickens to a cold monsoon, the whole cafteria of echo turns suddenly cathedral. Steam rolls in layered banks; headlamps become rose windows; pump rhythms align into a single heartbeat. For a minute you feel the Underworks as a living riddle: architecture pretending to be geology, industry pretending to be weather, people pretending to be stitches in a wound that refuses to heal. Then a compressor misfires, the cadence breaks, and you remember you are a guest inside the planet’s half-scabbed incision, helping it bleed in a controlled fashion.

11.  THE FUTURE  
Engineers talk of a second tier—decks sunk even lower, wider galleries, maybe an underground canal to float ore barges beneath the continental shelf. Every blast of gelignite they sink is pregnant with that ambition. But stone is still adolescent here, quick to bruise, quick to swell. It drinks their steam and answers with seep, seep, seep. If the pumping faltered for one whole day the sump would rise, the roofs would bow, the pale ghost-herbs dissolve, and the Tideplant Underworks would complete its reverse birth—sealing itself back into the dark womb of the world, taking every rivet and rumor with it.

Until that day, the pumps maintain a bright, brute heartbeat. And the wet confetti of drizzle keeps falling on a town that forgot the sky—an inverted, iron, newborn marsh where industry and groundwater write their unfinished covenant in rust.
