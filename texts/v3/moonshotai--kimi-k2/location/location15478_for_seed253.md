The world is an exhale that never ends: a single breath held since the memory of any creature remembers. Everything here floats inside an immense, sunless ocean whose surface is far too high to imagine—an elevation defined not by rising land but by the simple fact that pressure itself has a ceiling, and that ceiling is the top of the water. There is no bottom. There is only the deep, cool corridor of the place‐without‐shore.

Water is not merely the medium; it is the wall, the floor, the vault of roof. Light—what little exists—arrives as a dead, beaten silver, pushed through endless fathoms until only the faintest ghost of direction remains. You see by echoes: each movement launches slow ripples of phosphorescence that ripple along unending kelp-bones. Sound is everywhere—bone-deep and perpetually loud—because nothing here ever stops growing or dying, and every cell breaks its sugars with an audible snap that accumulates into a rolling susurrus like surf inside the skull.

Vegetation alone is architecture. The space is a sparse arboreal cityscape of living trunks that grew, were drowned, kept growing. Each separate tree—if the word still fits—is a single organism as thick as cliff faces and as old as erosion itself: a pillar of lignin and chlorophyll slowly petrifying into a sort of vegetable jade. Their butts once rooted on some Precambrian landmass, but that continent is long gone; the roots have spent immensities refining themselves into a lattice of woody filaments dangling like slowly churning chains. Macrobarnacles of perennial fruiting bodies bob under every branch, pulsing in slow bioluminescent tideclocks. Entire meadows—if you drift between them—consist of curtains of red algae that cling to the tree-boles like cloaks, autumn vermilion sustained eternally by an equilibrium with the chill. Every organism exhales oxygen in such abundance that the water itself has become viscous, syrupy, an ointment against coldness.

Density is sparing: between each colossal trunk the blue-black empties for half a league or more. In that void, spheres of jellylike algae hang still as moons, each sphere hatchery and cemetery for the fauna. And the fauna! Transparent lobsters with lungs made of leaves paddle through halls of darkness, their auditory setae trembling like wind chimes. Worm-eels, fat as pythons, slide along branches rasping off yearly layers of bark the way grazers once cropped upland meadows. Sharks—blind, milk-pale—circle like bishops around cathedral trunks, teeth honed on wood instead of bone; their flesh exudes antifreeze proteins so ancient that fossils share the same shapes found in these encircling bodies. From far upcurrent drift pods of jelly-cetaceans: entire whales remade into coelenterates, roiling bells fifty feet across, their songs a booming, discordant drone that strikes the densest tree-wood and sets it vibrating like a whale-bone flute.

All is constructed, yet nothing is built deliberately. The phrase “no-tech” means that every evidence of design is evolutionary. Structures are coral, but of cold cellulose: orgasmic cysts age into caverns; fiber bundles twist into bridges; bark peels in deliberate plates, edges worn to a glassy smoothness over epochs. Spongy, woody foam—lighter than the water yet stronger than stone—has filled every niche as air once filled lungs. You could hold an entire spirolite cathedral in your hands; if you let go, it would simply drift upward until it tangled with the next cathedral a mile above. And yet nothing drifts far; the current variability—the dynamic that might have broken this equilibrium—long ago disappeared into the steady respiration of planet-ocean. Everything here floats at the layer where its buoyancy equals its weight; nothing joins or leaves, only cycles lingeringly.

Erosion is ancient to the point of stillness; trunks are vertical topographies of history. Their cores read like rings in reverse—wood disappearing inward, eaten by anaerobic fungi that leave lacy libraries of lignin and stone. On the outer rind, centuries accrete not in matter but in pattern: epiphytes melt their signatures onto the parent tree, and the inscription grows halos of succeeding species, each halo the exact color that the water was when that ancestor seeded itself. You are walking backward through color time; if you press your hand to the bark, you meet the chromatic age of a floorwalker that lived three hundred million years ago. These halos never blur; dynamics have all but ceased. The place itself has flattened into an exquisite tedium, the last form any state can achieve before nothingness.

The only measurable temperature is a slow cold, but that cold is utterly uniform. Metabolisms adapted to it conduct life at the gait of granting wishes. You can age a month between two heartbeats of the fauna, a geological era within one of the trees. Light is never brighter or darker; it is the residue of energies trapped centuries before, rebounding between algal mirrors until only the over-and-over is left. Sound alone refuses to taper; the everlasting munching of barnacular lichens, the sonar peals of the jelly-whales, and tiny detonations of seed pods cracking like capguns under pressure build up into an omnipresent choral dirge.

Rules of this world:

1. What remains in suspension, remains forever: matter has lost all routes of intake or release except living metabolism.
2. Buoyancy equals destiny: every organism floats at its net-neutral zone. To rise or descend is adaptive death.
3. Age writes itself upon the epidermis of the world, but no record is ever lost. Past color encysts in present flesh.
4. Movement kineticizes light: any displacement rescues trapped photons, so motion itself becomes an ambiguous generosity—too much and the world burns with a faint cyan edge; too little and it recedes into dishonored darkness.
5. Names evaporate; there is no language except the naming of layers—water, light, heartwood, buoyancy, cold—each term dragged behind each explorer like a safety line of one word kept in mind to haul back a single memory of home. Even that thread frays.

Between the roots that hang like stalactites of lignin from trunks unseen, you float. Your lungs have become pocketed gill-sacs that breathe without effort; you no longer know how long you have been drifting. Occasionally you scrape a fingertip along a hardwood fascia and forests of papery proto-orchids open like umbrellas. They put out sporeheads that glow for exactly seven heartbeats, then vanish again. You watch one such efflorescence and in the space of its glow you feel you have become the entire history of one continent. When it fades, the memory fades with it. You keep only the taste—not of brine, but of living cellulose hammered thin into syllables too old for mouths.

This is the last cathedral: architecture not by intention but by consensus, crystallized out of the long exhalation of a planet gone fully aquatic, fully arboreal, fully cold. It is a place drowned in itself, plant-filled to satiation, teeming yet sparse, ancient past comparison, technically generated from the body of the living earth yet without intention or artifact. It rings with sound undiminished and undiminishable, a loud pouring forward of forever.
