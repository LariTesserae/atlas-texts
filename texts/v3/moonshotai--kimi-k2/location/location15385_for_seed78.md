It is called the Vertical Night.

You perceive it before you see it, the experience carried by the crystalline groan that rolls across half a continent of black air. The sound is insect-wing hum layered under kilometre-long oak timbers being dragged over ice. At this altitude the atmosphere is so sparse that every collision between leaf and wind becomes a struck gong; the local phrase for “alive” is literally “to be semi-audible.”  
A flute bone of rain—only 1.3/3, only a suggestion—floats instead of falls, meandering upward and downward in defiance of gravity as though the droplets have forgotten their allegiance to real fluids. When they alight on a surface they do not wet; they bead, float off again like confused fireflies, carrying their chill.

Vegetation equals architecture, substrate, armor, and immune system. A single organelle can become a cathedral arch. The lowest fronds—lacy ruffs of glaucous blue—float fifteen kilometres beneath you, drifting like continents turned to feather. Above them, higher and higher until even the term “elevation” loses currency, rise successive belts of plant-towers: redstone mangroves riveted with ice needles, pale cartilaginous vines braided into hollow trunks ten kilometres thick, fronds splayed so wide they darken the already weak starlight like the ribs of failed moons. Their trunks are cooling hearts; you can press an ear against the bark, hear a slow diastole sucking sap toward orbit at temperatures so low that the living flesh crystallises then thaws with each heartbeat. Between the great plants are thousand-kilometre voids, desert gaps whose official cartography reads “light-year echo,” yet those caverns are themselves colonised—fine strings of alga slung like harp-strings, bioluminescent particles drifting snow.

The rule here is inverse gravitation for metabolisms: growth extends away from any local centre of mass rather than toward it. Trees grow up, sideways, down again, depending on where warmth—the counterpoint to cold—can be metabolised. Every organism is simultaneously balloon and root; every loop of vine is a lung 2 km long.

Temperature 0.5/3 means the ice on every leaf edge is alive, and will braid migrating spines of frost across your cheeks if you stand still in the black. Yet the titan bodies deep at the core are warm—thermally at least. Touch their cambium and it yields like hot water under parchment; pull your finger away and your print remains a steaming bruise that soon bergs over into white.

Density 0.1/3: you never walk so much as swim. Solid surfaces appear by consent only; twigs fold away like polite clocks. Most of the Vertical Night is the room between sounds. Occasionally you encounter “stations” whom travellers call guide-seeds: half-barn owls, half air-anemones that anchor long silk threads to passing trunks. They retie the weave nightly, singing in 14 Hz throbs that travel the vascular tubes.  

Fauna abundant yet discrete: peregrine millipedes hundreds of metres long accordion through the meshes, motionless in visible time, lunging thirty metres in heartbeat instants you cannot catch. Aerial whales—sheet-nosed leviathans of mycelium—pulse like lazy deltas between frond-reefs, filtering stray protons for energy. The apex predator is thought to be a sound itself: the agreeable music of colonies of glass cicadas whose resonance liquefies sap and harvests it later from volatilised cones. Yet no one has seen a cicada; only the empty tuning forks of old.

Built: only tiny artifacts, because tools are patient. A leaf-knife marks a hollow hemisphere out of a root-flute; a fire no larger than a thumbnail burns for centuries, tended by hereditary linnet-folk no heavier than dust. Once every eighty-three years they let the flame blossom, and a glowing copper-red sphere rises, drifting upward through the night—its destination is always “farther.”

Erosion 1/3: nothing stands long enough to grow old. Surfaces shear and sheathe nightly; what looks like stratified geology is only yesterday's lightning converted into lignin. The landscape is therefore young but ancient at once, renewing its morning skin every midnight while holding memories engraved as pheromonal glyphs in the heartwood. To read them you must split the trunk at absolute zero and breathe the code in, exhaling later to plant the story in another direction of growth. Histories travel vertically, never laterally.

Light 0.2/3: the Vertical Night is lit by afterglows. The plants fluoresce weakly in the ultraviolet range, permitting black-red silhouettes and a suggestion of peach-blue veins. Cosmic rays, arriving unfiltered at thirty kilometres, strike carbohydrate auroras out of the floating pollen, discharging brief purple ladders across which nerve-like flames climb for minutes at a time. Human eyes adjust until what was darkness turns grainy red, then ultraviolet grey-scale; your iris muscles ache from learning unfamiliar diameters.

Weirdness 0.9/3: the farther downward you descend, the colder it paradoxically becomes; heat is stratified upward in this sky-grove. Directionality itself is plant-mediated. Each tree exudes a field that defines “vertical” locally; the angle shifts like compass bearing. Navigate by tracing the growth hormones in the air. If you inhale a single molecule it will feed you for twelve days; but if you overdose you root where you stand and sprout colossal lenticel leaves white as moonlight. Dreamers do so deliberately, planting their bodies as spore-bridges six kilometres long, crossing abysses too vast to measure.

You could spend an eternity here and remain forever on the front stoop. There are stairways you cannot ascend without growing new bones; there are mornings condensed into hollow bead-seconds where an entire biome rises into orbit upon thermal paths of its own exhalation. Every traveller leaves footprints that, instead of indenting, protrude as tiny translucent mushrooms three days later. Cartographers map these footprints in reverse, arguing that memory proceeds downward, not back in time. The correct way to orient a map is upside-down, roots above.

In the distant centre of this sky-canopy, nestled inside the pulsing umbilicus of the tallest celadon bole, sleeps the Place That Is Not Temperature. No thermometer placed there has ever returned the same reading twice; some results are numbers, others are letters, or smells of hometown bread. Occasionally the structure cracks with a noise loud enough to register as predicted rain on planets three star-systems away. When that thunder folds inward, whole biomes swap places—mangrove clouds become coral archipelagos of frost, herds of echo-mammals invert their lungs and begin exhaling starlight.

If you speak aloud, your sentence seeds future forests whose shapes are phonetic. The hereditary linnet-folk collect these phonemes, burn them in the thumbnail fire, and release them back as silent pollen that insists on falling upward once it cools. In this way language is transmuted into weather. You may hear your own long-dead words drifting past as sleet.

To leave, tilt your body ninety degrees from the closest trunk’s axial preference, evaporate the mountain-cold breath in your lungs, and exhale until the Vertical Night spins you out through one of the uppermost holes into true stratosphere where ice galaxies turn. You will arrive on a surface that only minutes ago might have been the ceiling of someone else’s dream.

You will carry back a faint tint in your vision: a bruised green glowing through purple, the afterimage of the tallest plant breathing.

And you will never know whether the sound you still hear—the long groan—is wind across leaves, or the planet remembering the day it chose to stand upright and grow into its own sky.
