The place has no name yet because the survey shafts were drilled before anything had settled into place.  It is a cavern-city still inside the cocoon, already alive with industry but still held in the hush of prebirth.

Entry is down a vertical service shaft bored through fossil salt that tastes of metal and does not dissolve on the tongue–the mouth dries instead, packing itself with crystal dust that crackles when you breathe.  The shaft corkscrews for three kilometers and opens into air that is cool, slightly resinous, and almost too still.  Each footfall lands on a living floor of plated leaves the size of pillows; the tissue is rigid enough to bite out clear, dry clicks like thin porcelain.  Every step writes a fracture line of violet sap, but the sap film scabs almost instantly into a crystalline scar and the architecture heals as quickly as it is wounded.

Above this floor grows the city.

Ivy with iron in its veins climbs in webs as thick as sewer mains.  The ivy is the sewage: its hollow trunks carry lubricants and compressed gases that taste faintly of anise.  Every fifty meters the vines swell into bulbous furnaces seeded with slow-growing pollen engines.  Bright dielectric filaments spread outward like industrial ivy blossoms; when they flower they trip copper brushes that stroke off man-sized sparks.  The sparks are harvested by living trusses–tower trees whose trunks are made of boilerplate steel but whose burls contain liver-red cellulose chambers trembling with stored charge.

Between these metal-trees the settlement platforms are already spreading, but they are scaffoldings rather than finished works: catwalks of knife-edged leaf-blades woven into trellis ribs, floors of chitin laminated over cable veins, vertical streets only half ossified and still sweating mist.  Anything left unattended is either overgrown or digested; abandoned wrenches already wear moss that curdles into chrome-colored feathers.  Meanwhile brand-new mechanisms sprout wherever gardeners have spilled organic catalysts: cone-shaped vent turbines that spin like dandelion clocks, their vanes actual seeds sheathed in vanadium.

There is water only in the sense that vapor exists.  A mantle of desiccant roots draws every last picoliter out of the air; moisture is stripped, bound to sugar polymers, and marched away in living conduits the diameter of freight shafts.  Anything approaching moisture–sweat, spit, machine coolant–is hoarded instantly by stoma that bloom like charcoal poppies in the walkways.

Faunal voices appear in clusters.  Finned lamp-eels loop between the sparkling tangles and graze on static discharge.  Their hides are matte black and leave after-images of phosphor lime on the retina.  Each eel, discharging, sings at 17 kilohertz; the sound ripples as thin glass threads between canyon walls.  Larger grazers are beetle-oxen with back plates shaped like freight barges; their locomotion is slow and frictionless on oiled mycelial rails.  The rails are sprouting new track even now, though no one laid them.  An ox-beetle noses up to an inventory stack of ceramic fruits, bites the pallet apart, and flares its antennae until the stack’s seeds turn to steam.  The steam is harvested by a nearby leaf-roof which unrolls like an awning and funnels the vapor directly into a still-beating industrial heart that is itself the size of a town hall.

Despite the abundance of life the population density is low; each inhabitant must carry enough metal and circuitry to deny the plant intestine’s constant attempt to reclaim them.  People walk in lacquered partial exosuits whose elbows sprout antenna blades.  Their breath rattle-hisses through filter petals.  No one speaks aloud; a modified cricket stridulates inside the ear canal and allows bone-conduction speech.  The cricket’s organ-like resonator dampens ambient noise so that a whisper crosses a hundred meters.  Consequently the city hums with thousand-voice silences: one hears only leaves clacking, furnace valves sighing, distant grinding as another ventricle door births its cargo of compressed aggregate fertilizer bars.

Streetlights are eye-like clusters of crystal bananas grown along trunks; at dusk they unfold spontaneously and drink charge from the ivy batteries.  Once saturated they glow the cold yellow-green of harbor fog.  The light is intentionally even, produced at every meter height so that shadows are minimized; shadows, here, are pockets where parasitic fungi can germinate.  Every evening half the workforce marches out armed with fulgur prongs, stabbing fungal bloompoints into steam until the district steams like a kettle bath.  Then the moisture is siphoned again, the cycle closing on itself.

Above, the cavern roof is studded with stalactite roots still dripping the last soluble minerals from the long-dead planet surface.  They hang too high to touch, glowing pale aquamarine in the plant-light like half-melted chandeliers.  Every new moon cycle the roots shorten by a measured millimeter: the city is tugging the sky downward.  In three centuries, administrators claim, the ceiling will be low enough that lamp-eels will nest there and the top layer of settlement will hear the clatter of their fins.  By then the cavern will be filled–a single organism burrowed into itself, breathing electricity, excreting alloy ingots, dreaming to the tick of furnaces modeled on seed ovaries.

Rules:

1. Nothing wet is allowed to remain free; every droplet must be owned, transmitted, or married to a chemical elsewhere.  
2. Metal is not imported: it is farmed from lianas whose leaves smelt themselves into plates every third year; these plates are peeled, quenched, and riveted while still warm.  
3. Light must be shared horizontally.  Any device, organic or artificial, that casts a dense vertical shadow is prosecuted as sabotage.  
4. Speech is reverence for the near-silence.  
5. All tools must yield biomass value at end of life; if a drill bit is too corroded to reprint, it is planted and within a month blossoms into a tiny organ that hums a single pure tone.  The tone is registered, harvested, and sold as a memory block that never degrades.

At night, the insect whispers stop, the furnaces tick down to a cardiac hush, and the only motion comes from seeds of architectural ivy crackling open one shell-layer at a time.  In each fracture line the aluminum core unveils itself and catches the distant aquamarine glimmer of the descending roots–a promise the place will one day be entirely enclosed, a sealed cathedral of vegetable metallurgy where the sky is a floor you can walk on.  

The desert above has forgotten this; the desert has no memory of water.  Below, the stillness is filled with slow cicada pulses, each blast a blue-white bud that burns a single perfect second before satisfying the silence again.
