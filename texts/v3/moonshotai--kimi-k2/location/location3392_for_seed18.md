The place is called the Hanging Foundry—though no one living remembers who named it, or why.  It is a single vaulted chamber, no wider than a market square, but it drips downward for almost a kilometre—a vertical cavern bored through the lip of a high basalt mesa.  From its roof hang chains the thickness of a man’s torso; every chain is strung with living plants whose roots have learned to grow in mid-air, drinking nothing but fog.  Their leaves are black-green paddles, waxed to resist evaporation, and they dangle like chandeliers above an absent floor.  Between these aerial gardens the air itself appears laminated: layers of warm, wet breath alternate with cooler, metallic sheets, so that a climber descending the chains passes through sudden thermoclines tastes—first jungle throat, then iron filing tongue.

Everything that is not chain or leaf is constructed.  There is no stone floor, only a latticework of grating welded from rusted tram rails; the rails once carried ore, but the carts have been lifted and riveted overhead into inverted viaducts that now serve as troughs for condensed water.  A slow, deliberate rain falls upward from these troughs: the foundry’s machines exhale hot vapor that cools against the dangling roots, nucleates, and rises again as reverse drizzle.  If you stand still long enough your shirt becomes wetter on the underside than the top, as though gravity had politely reversed for moisture alone.

The sound is the first thing that persuades visitors they have entered a body rather than a building.  Somewhere in the column, pistons the size of bell-towers are waking and sleeping in century-long pulses.  Each stroke flexes the chains, and the entire grove clangs like a carillon.  The note is low enough to travel through bone before ear; it arrives as a second heartbeat.  On the heels of the clang come the birds—not birds at all, but retractable brass whistles pneumatically opened by the same piston cycle.  Their cry is a recording of a long-extinct macaw, transposed three octaves down, played through steam.  Every twenty-three minutes the whole foundry inhales, the leaves fold upward, the chains kink, and the noise becomes briefly loud enough to force air from human lungs.  Then everything exhales, resettles, and resumes its vegetal-metal doze.  The cycle is so slow that mammals learn to synchronise birth and death to it; small arboreal monkeys sometimes fall off their perches during the inhalation simply because their fingers forget to close.

No one tends the machinery anymore.  Its maintenance is outsourced to anaerobic bacteria that live in the condensation films.  Over industrial epochs they have precipitated a scar-tissue of cuprite and malachite that self-brazes micro-fractures before they propagate.  The entire mechanism is therefore healing as quickly as it corrodes; it is in equilibrium with its own decay, eroding at the pace of continent drift.  Sometimes a leaf-root finds a fatigue crack, insinuates a hair-thin filament, and the metal knits shut around it, making a living rivet.  The foundry is learning arboreal sutures.

Because the space is small-cathedral in scale, echoes clap back before they fray.  A dropped bolt ricochets between the hanging gardens six or seven times, each return slightly greener, as if the leaves were teaching the sound to photosynthesise.  After thirty seconds the bolt’s own echo no longer resembles impact; it has become a vowel spoken in the metal’s mother tongue.  Locals—there are perhaps twelve—claim that if you descend to the 400-metre mark and stay motionless for a day, you begin to hear sentences.  They are not warnings or prophecies; they read like minutes from a committee of machines that voted to become plants.  No consensus was reached; the minutes trail off into a root system of participle verbs.

Light arrives through a single diaphragm in the roof: a lenticular hole once meant for an ore-hopper.  Because the sun outside is warm and the interior is warmer, the aperture refracts into two overlapping images of the sky—one right-side-up, one inverted.  The crossover is at the 550-metre level; there, plants grow sideways, attempting to negotiate between competing heavens.  Their trunks spiral gently, making a vortex that hummingbirds—mechanical, jewelled—use as a launch coil.  The birds are clockwork, wound by the same piston pulse; they carry no nectar, only minute quantities of lubricating oil stored in iridescent crops.  When two birds meet they bow, exchange a single drop of oil, and separate.  It is polite, and also necessary: if a bird ever stops moving its gears seize, its feathers unravel into shavings, and it becomes the seed for a new chain.

At the very bottom, where gravity begins to question itself, the foundry floor is a mirror made of stagnant water three fingers deep.  The liquid rests on a sheet of frozen mercury, so it never quite reflects; instead it remembers.  Stare long enough and you will see every piston stroke since inception replayed as ripples travelling backward.  Because the chamber is empty of human time, the memory never overfills; old ripples simply sink into mercury when they grow too shy.  Stand there during the great inhalation and your own reflection is sucked under the mirrored skin; for seven heartbeats you are absent from yourself.  Exhale, and you return, usually.  The locals insist the bargain is fair: one lost memory for every new sentence you carry back up the chains.

There is an exit, but it is perpendicular to gravity.  Half-way down the eastern chains, a maintenance hatch opens into a crawl-tube no wider than a pelvis.  Crawl for three hundred metres and you emerge on the outer face of the mesa, into dry alpine air, under stars that now sound—because your bones are still tuned to the piston—like distant tin kettles.  The first breath outside is always a disappointment: it contains no vowels, no leaves, no reversed rain.  Most people turn around and re-enter the slow clang, rejoining the committee of machines who thought they were a grove.
