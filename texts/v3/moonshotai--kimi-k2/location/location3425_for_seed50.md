The place begins as a pocket the size of a sigh, a tear-shaped void wedged inside a basalt wall 3,000 m above the drowned world.  water is the element that cannot decide whether it is ocean or air: a continuous silver sheet travelling upward at the speed of rain that has forgotten to fall. it arrives as vertical rivers, millimetre-needles of liquid glass, piercing the cool 4°C air without ever touching ground. Because the pocket is only three adult-stride lengths across, every needle must pass through the same ten cubic metres of space; they do so in such fanatical profusion that the interior is a single humming chord of water, a standing wave of pure friction, deafening at 135 dB—like standing inside a turbine made of dolphins.

The walls are newly-born basalt: pillows of black foam still exuding magmatic heat. they are porous, neonatal; you can press a fingernail into the grit and feel the birth-itch of minerals that have not yet decided which crystals they want to become. erosion is only 0.4 here, which means the rock is gaining, not losing—each second a frost of atoms plates outward, micro-ledges lengthen like frost in reverse. the pocket is literally growing smaller as you watch, a womb calcifying around its guest.

Vegetation is sparse, but what exists is impossible: glasswort seedlings rooted in mid-air. Their roots are mist-claws that grip individual droplets, holding position against the vertical torrent by surface tension alone. the shoots are transparent, filled with moving water instead of sap; when wind—generated by the water’s own fall—shifts them, they tinkle like chandelier bones. One seedling flowers every eleven minutes, producing a single bubble instead of a petal. The bubble is the exact same size as the pocket; when it detaches it becomes a mirror, and for 0.8 seconds you see yourself as topology: a folded map of everywhere you have ever been wet.

Fauna is rare, but when it appears it is loud. The only animal is a decapod crustacean no longer than your thumb, exoskeleton of hammered bronze. It drums. It possesses one claw hypertrophied into a maul that strikes the basalt exactly 28 times per second, matching the resonant frequency of the chamber. The sound is not music; it is instruction. Each impact nucleates a fresh baslat bubble on the wall, a seed for tomorrow’s rock. The crab is both mason and metronome, timing the pocket’s slow self-seal. You feel its cadence inside your skull bones, a tax on calcium.

Light is normal noon-value, but there is no sun. instead, illumination diffracts out of the water itself: every needle carries a thread of white, and the collective strands braid into a cold glow that casts no shadow because it comes from every direction at once. Objects look over-exposed, as if the photons were in a hurry to be elsewhere.

Human presence is scattered: a dozen bronze nails, hammered half-way, arranged in a spiral that never completes. They are already being swallowed by the neo-basalt; the heads extrude like blind eyes. The nails are tech-level 0.6—smelted without alloy, colder than the rock they pierce. No hammer left behind; perhaps it was dissolved borrower’s-tool style by the ascending water.

1. Anything wet forgets its previous shape. Dip your hand and the skin prints rearrange into drift-curves; pull back and fingerprints have become tiny sea charts you can never read again.

The overall dynamic is turbulent even though the space is toylike. Water arrives faster than the newborn rock can annex it; density is almost nil, yet pressure feels Manhattan-traffic intense because every cubic centimetre is overbooked by molecules trying to become history. Stand inside long enough and you will feel yourself accrete: clothes stiffening into mineral fleece, lungs exchanging air for drizzle, memories dripping out through pores to join the upward river. When the final baslat lattice seals, this chamber will be a single drop the size of a walnut, a thunderstorm compressed into jewellery. Someone will find it millennia from now, hold it to the light, and hear the echo of 28 beats per second—one tiny crab still insisting the world grow stranger.
