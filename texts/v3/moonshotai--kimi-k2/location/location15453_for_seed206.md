You do not arrive; you are already inside.  
The chamber is the size of a cathedral, yet folded so tight round the edges that when you close one eye you see both walls at once.  It is neither cave nor grove: it is the paved hollow left by something monstrous that once grew, collapsed, and was embanked by its own drought-proof tissue.  The space feels embedded in the trunk of a single plant that long ago learned to make stone of its cambium and wax of its sap.  Walls are blotched with lichen the colour of oxidised mail, but under the lichen the living periderm breathes, shifting its cracks half a millimetre per decade, an imperceptible pulse that keeps the equilibrium intact.

The floor is a mat of thick roots laid in herring-bone order, oiled with slow seepage.  Every minute a droplet forms in the ceiling’s pitted stone-wood, glints, falls: one percussion in the chronic drum.  That is the loudest sound here, amplified by the vault so that each drop lands like a mallet on an empty tank, ka-thack, ka-thack, a rhythm unchanged for three centuries and respected by everything living, for nothing resets the phase.  Beneath it walks the low hiss of steam: somewhere belly-deep in the root-mass, scalps of moss grow partly over hot pipes left by engineers who visited once and never needed to return.  The temperature holds at blood-warm; the air swims like the inside of a greenhouse gone metal-ripe.  The steam arms humidity until every breath contains fifty smaller breaths.  Condensate pearls on leaf-hairs, on insect wings, on the single brass valve that refuses to corrode.

Light is neither lampless nor open sky.  A diffuse citrine sheet pours from a horizontal slit eight metres overhead: the sun must climb the horizon of the outer world, refract through a skin of aerosol, and finally ride capillaries of translucent pith-planks whose resin has half turned to glass.  Light gives neither track nor shadow; objects glow from the volume of the room rather than a source.

Vegetation crowds every surface yet arranges itself like sparse furniture.  Giant monocot ribs—wide as bridge cables—angle upward, unbranching, into black air, linked at intervals by faint aerial stolons too delicate ever to sag.  Between these pillars the understory works like a sparse gallery of still lives: solitary trees sprout from pockets in the root-floor, each one different yet each configured at the same ripe maturity, leaves peeled open to the exact aperture that balances respiration and evaporation in this one radiant plenum.  Ferns swell from crotches and from rents in the bark-stone walls, fanned fronds all pointed to the same unchanging angle, accepting photons genetically memorised.  A single crimson inflorescence hangs upside-down from a knot producing neither pollen nor seed; it is the reproductive structure of three separate species grown tight together, grafted by chance and lack of pressure for change, stilled at perfect anthesis.  The ecology proceeds by refrigeration of time: no death overtakes, no seedling stakes open ground; turnover has been exported elsewhere, leaving behind only a curated display of plant-equilibrium.

Water is everywhere, still you will not step in a pool.  The over-saturated air supplies it as film: the floor drinks by capillarity, leaf cuticles exhale drifts of micro-droplets that collapse again on neighbouring blades, a planetary cycle compressed into an orrery three metres high.  Extra drips feed the brass valve; pressure drives them along unknown intestines to the buried engines whose hum, though present, is almost inaudible beneath the eternal drip-echo.  You feel the machines somewhere, vibrating the wet stone floor in the low frequencies that travel in bone better than in air.  The plant-architecture is both factory and product.

Fauna teems but stays measured.  A swallowtail loops past without ever coming to feed, repeating the same oval whose rotation period is 7.1 seconds, honed to compensate for the windless air.  Hoverflies abide mid-air as though glued at invisible nodes.  White snails trace polished furrows across the root-mat—furrows begun a century ago now fixed as carved trenches when the secretion dried.  On the solitary crimson bloom rest four butterflies, wings held upright in perfect synchrony; nobody knows which wing beat initiated the stance, for none has broken in living memory.  A mantid among them changes colour so slowly its chitin tracks the moving band of light across the slit overhead, but the motion never completes because the band never shifts position.  Sound therefore is not silence but a meticulously co-signed thunder: thousands of minute legs, wings, spiracles, all obeying one rhythm impossibly faithful.

Weirdness manifests in scale: despite the hidden, pocket nature of the chamber, its air pockets seem deeper than flesh can occupy.  If you lean your ear to a pore in the root-wall you will hear not dripping but a drone like lathes cutting steel, although the plant has never encountered industry.  The root-tunnels may be hollow recorders of the machines it once engulfed.  When you withdraw, your ear glistens with oil that smells of coal and fir balsam.

Rules:  
1. Nothing larger than your head may eat anything else here.  
2. Every pulse that the drop gives must be answered by another pulse in the subterranean engine; if one fails, silence lasts six centuries until the other begins again.  
3. The light slit never widens or narrows, but nor will it ever be occluded.  
4. What roots upward also roots downward, and any thought you think above ground will sprout inverted in the mirrored root-garden below, forever.  
5. Time is currency but no one spends it, for the interest rate is zero and the treasury is infinite.

You leave only by noticing you are already leaving: the tendrils begin to fold the chamber shut behind your attention; when it narrows, the soundlessness dilates and becomes the noisy world you once knew.  But this place will not have changed when you look away; it is the condition and the keeper of its own non-weather, locked warm, bright, and thundering.
