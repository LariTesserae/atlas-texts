You reach the summit only to discover it is not a summit at all, but the lip of a wound.  The air is cold—so cold it tastes metallic on thethe tongue, like licking a copper pipe—and every exhale falls downward in perfect glassy threads, too heavy to drift.  Above you the sky is a single sheet of polished tin, bright enough to bleach the shadows out of everything yet curiously depthless; it offers no warmth, only reflection.  You have walked up forever, yet the whole arena is no larger than a ruined abbey: a tilted basin of black stone barely a hundred paces across, cupped like a hand ready to catch something spilled.

There is no soil, no moss, no blade of grass.  The stone itself feels half-alive, a kind of fissured glass-ceramic veined under pressure.  Where it is broken you see not crystals but pores: hexagonal vents no wider than a needle, each exhaling a breath colder than the air, so the whole basin glimmers with descending ribbons of fog.  The moisture never quite becomes rain; instead it lacquers the walls with damp that instantly freezes into a clear, flexible rind.  If you tap it the rind rings like porcelain, but lean too hard and the whole sheet sighers downward in one reptilian motion, revealing a darker vein beneath.  The sound it makes is a wet cracking, distant, as though the mountain itself quietly disapproves of being touched.

Across this small arena stand a dozen cairns, but cairns built by people who had forgotten what graves were for.  None is taller than your hip; each is stacked from wafer-thin slates of the native glass-rock, balanced so precariously that the thin perpetual breeze (itself only the ghost of a breeze, colder than the draught from a deep freezer) makes them tremble and click.  No two cairns share the same geometry: some are spirals, some are nested cubes, one is simply a vertical queue of identical plates waiting to topple.  They are obviously built, yet the builders seem to have stopped mid-thought, walked away, and let the cold finish the design.  Between the cairns the ground is littered with their discarded tools: cobbles flaked to three edges, antler picks fossilized into stone, hammerstones cracked open by their own repeated blows.  The tools are arranged in casual little constellations, as if the workers set each one down merely to stretch their fingers, then forgot to return.  Nothing rusts; nothing rots; the cold keeps everything in unfinished suspense.

The only moving thing is a single column of water at the very centre.  It rises from a hole the width of two palms, climbs twenty times your height, and holds there, perfectly cylindrical, never breaking into drops.  You realise it is not liquid but a very slow liquid—supercooled, reluctant, a creek frozen mid-leap.  The column wavers only when your body heat drifts near; then a dimple travels up its glossy skin like a shiver, and the basin answers with a low soft pop you feel in your molars rather than hear.  This is the loudest sound for miles.  The rest is the hush of absolute winter: your blood in your ears, the faint ceramic tinkle of the cairns, and—if you stay long enough—the metronomic drip of your own eyelashes thawing and re-freezing.

Time behaves strangely here.  You can watch a single piece of frost grow a beard of needles, but when you blink three cairns have subtly changed their orientation, as if the plateau rotates a hair’s-breadtch each time your vision is off-duty.  You feel no hunger, no thirst; the cold itself seems to metabolise you, burning your heat as tribute.  The elevation should starve your lungs, yet breathing is effortless—only the breaths turn to ballast and pool around your boots, so that with every minute you stand rooted deeper in your own grey exhalations.  If you stayed long enough you would become a cairn: a still observer constructed from laminated breath and laminated regret.

There is no life larger than whatever it is that keeps the water column suspending itself against gravity.  Still, the place feels crowded, almost stewarded.  Step too close to a cairn and you catch—at the limit of peripheral vision—the momentary suggestion of hands the colour of basalt, withdrawing into the stone like turtles into shells.  They leave no prints, yet the tools on the ground rearrange themselves into neater arcs, as if tidied by polite, disappointed hosts.  The sense of company is so insistent that you find yourself speaking under your breath, not from fear but courtesy: “I’m only passing through.”  The plateau answers with another soft pop inside the rock, maybe agreement, maybe laughter.

You notice then the rules: nothing may complete itself here.  Water never finishes falling, builders never finish building, visitors never finish leaving.  Every object is allowed to approach its final form but must stop one gesture short—an elegant mercy, because in this cold a finished thing would shatter of its own perfection.  It is a landscape of held breath, of almost.  The weirdness is not that it disobeys physics, but that it withholds physics’ denouement.

So you step backward, careful not to scuff the frost-rind.  Your heel dislodges a single chip of glassy stone; it skitters, tinkling, and the column of water quivers in sympathetic resonance, shedding a spiral of ultra-thin ice that travels upward like film reversing in a projector.  The cairns tilt, listening.  The sky keeps its tin-coloured brightness, but for a moment its reflection in the basin dims, as if the wound were thinking of closing.

You leave the summit-that-is-not-a-summit exactly as you found it: incomplete.  Behind you the wet cracking continues, patient, geological, like something quietly knitting itself a body out of black glass and interrupted intentions.
