The cliff garden hangs a mile above the sea, but the sea is still here—inside the rock.  
Water has not finished becoming itself. It beads on every face of pale-green feldspar, condenses from air too hot to breathe without shivering, runs in hair-thin threads that join and re-split like nervous capillaries. When you stand still you can feel your own pulse echoed in the drip-drip against your ankles. No rivers, no pools—only this sweat of stone, this perpetual birth of liquid from vapor. Touch a wall and your palm comes away filming into droplets that roll uphill, drawn by polarity fields the garden inherited from whatever excavated it.

Elevation is high enough that the sky has turned bruise-purple even at noon, yet the air is thick as broth. You climb through it rather than walk. Every fourth breath tastes of metal aerosols the garden exhales to keep its lattice alive. The garden itself is small—three interlocking terraces no wider than a village commons—but it repeats. Perspective buckles: step through an arch of living basalt and the same trio of terraces re-appears below you, tilted 31° left, as though someone folded space until the folding forgot to stop. Locals (there are a few) call the place *mirloch*, “mirror that does not imitate.” They claim if you meet yourself here, the other one is younger by the number of droplets that have passed since you arrived.

Vegetation occupies only the interstices, but it grows as if to hide the fact that it is outnumbered by emptiness. Ferns stitched from copper foil unfold overnight, their fronds hot enough to scorch fingerprints. Vines of transparent cellulose lift their own roots like balloon strings and drift a hand’s breadth above the ground, searching for airborne nitrates. Leaves are rare; photosynthesis happens in the mist itself, cultured by symbiotic phytoplankton that give the fog its faint peppermint glow. Look closely and you’ll see each droplet is a tiny green sun. The garden is lush, yes, but only if you count by chemistry instead of green matter.

Stone remembers yesterday’s shape only reluctantly. Corners are still sharp, drill-scars un-blurred; the terraces were machined, not quarried, sliced away by something that could hold a plasma blade as thin as a silk thread. This happened recently—geologic seconds ago. Magma still mutters under the lowest terrace; you can hear it if you press your ear to the basalt, a cat-purr that threatens to become a roar. Seismic adolescents, the locals joke, always knocking the furniture about before they learn grace.

Fauna failed the entrance exam. A single species made it through: glass-wings, thumb-long creatures like dragonflies cast in quartz. They have no mouthparts; they live on stray voltage, alighting on copper ferns to sip photoelectrons. Their wings beat at 88 Hz, the same frequency the garden hums when it thinks no one is listening. When two glass-wings mate they fuse into one double-winged hyphen, fly a vertical figure eight, then split again—each carrying half the memory of the other. Scientists (there are some, though they prefer the title *curious guests*) debate whether this counts as death or merely conversation.

Built artifacts are scattershot, half-digested by the climate. You’ll find a chair of carbon lattice balanced on a parapet, still warm as if vacated minutes ago. No tables, no walls, just chairs—each a different epoch’s idea of comfort. One is scaled for a body with three knees; another sighs when sat upon, exhaling ozone. No one has seen whoever leaves them. Around dawn the chairs migrate, inching millimeters across the wet stone as rootlets of mineral grow from their legs and then retract, tasting for structural flaw.

Technology here is not worn but exhaled. The entire garden is a lung for something vaster buried deeper in the plateau. Nanoscale splines ride the condensation upward, harvesting heat differentials, using the copper ferns as heat-sinks. If you speak aloud the splines coalesce into hovering Braille—admonitions, recipes, half jokes in dead languages. The text disintegrates when read completely; literacy is punished by entropy. Guests soon learn to leave sentences unfinished, an etiquette of deferral.

Quiet dominates, but it is an aggressive quiet—sound folded into gaps between water beats. Every so often the basalt flutes release a pressure sigh, a three-note minor chord that makes glass-wing swarms momentarily sync. Otherwise you hear only your own hydration: spit fizzing, blood surfing, cerebrospinal fluid sloshing when you tilt your head to stare at the impossible repetition of terraces. In that tilt you may notice your sense of up has become negotiable; vertigo is the local currency.

Weirdness is stitched into utility. Gravity decreases by 2% for every meter you ascend the main stair; visitors arrive believing the climb will get easier and instead feel their hearts drifting up against collarbones. Time at the cliff edge runs a quarter-second fast; if you extend an arm outward and pull it back, fingernails have grown older than knuckles. The garden exports nothing but postcards addressed to people who will be born next week; the postage cancel is always yesterday.

Seasons cycle in hours, not months. Hot-wet-growth, hot-wet-decay, repeat. Afternoon brings a storm that exists only as sound: thunder without clouds, rain that falls as silence. During this acoustic deluge the copper ferns close like pray-ing hands and all the chairs turn to face inward, polite parishioners. Stand at the center and you become the axis; the garden rotates instead of you. Tourists report the sensation of being the pin in a mobile, a stillness everything else momentarily agrees to orbit.

There is exit, but it is shy. Descend the east stair and you will reach the original height; keep descending and height accumulates, as though someone balanced gravity’s books with compound interest. Most people leave by accident: they blink and find themselves on the coastal plain, clothes soaked, pockets full of copper-wick seeds that will grow into perfect miniature replicas of the terraces overnight. Plant one and by dawn you have a foot-tall cliff, sweating. Authorities (there are none) advise against orchards.

And yet invitations keep condensing on clear nights when light is mathematically normal: holograms addressed to you, spelled out in dew, urging return. They promise that next time the garden will show its root—the buried lung, the machine that exhales terraces, the reason water climbs uphill toward heat. Accept, and the fog remembers your name; decline, and the chairs miss you audibly, creaking in a wind that has not arrived yet. Either way, you are already part of the erosion it has planned for itself—another droplet learning how to leave the rock, how to hang for a moment in hot thin air before deciding what to become next.
