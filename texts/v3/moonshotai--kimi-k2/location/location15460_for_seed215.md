Name it first, because every world deserves a name you can mutter under your breath when the wind lifts:  
Call it Ysonde.  

Ysonde is still being quarried from the idea of mountains. Geologically speaking it may not be mountains at all; it may be the first protrusion of a continent only now deciding to stand above an ocean that hasn’t fully noticed it. The stone is raw, chalk-white except where iron is beginning to blush into ochre, and the edges along fractures are so sharp that gloves slit before fingers know they are cut. Frost feathers every surface like scale, turning every mineral vein into the pale artery of a sleeping dragon. Cold is a fourth state of matter here: colder than liquid, more restlessly mobile than solid, not quite gaseous; it crystallises into mobile lattices that click and drift across stone like constructs made of breath. You can watch one cell slide across another, exchanging momentum, until it loses cohesion and disappears again into ordinary chill. The air smells faintly of blue – it makes coherent light hang in needles between the teeth of granite.

Water is familiarity in a land that has forgotten most every other familiarity. It trickles beneath the tent flaps of new-form bedrock, pooling in shallow dishes where no dish existed before. Every dawn lenses of water film the broad steps of plateau, mirror-thin. The water remembers yesterday’s sky imperfectly; clouds repeat only in blurs and seep at the edges, like memories of old television. These mirrors evaporate during the hours when the low sun is finally higher than the mountain lip, but not before colloidal foam lifts off the film in perfect spheres the size of seed pearls. These spheres are fauna, or they contain fauna, spherical nautiloids that roll along the wind until they burst over some invisible pressure ridge and spill invisible ink that forms the next glaze of fluid on the next un-folded slab. Dozens burst each minute; thousands form at dusk; the arithmetic of life is generous and the continent is still learning how to balance its equation.

Temperature hovers at the exact moment when meltwater stops pretending it will ever be ocean again; it is cold, but within that cold lives the ultraviolet cusp of conversion. Peel off your glove and press the stone and the stone is neither cool nor warm; it is a borderline that can’t decide which side to hold. Blood vessels in your hand freeze into lacework crystals that painlessly redden back to liquidity as soon as pressure lifts – the process is so quick that the hand ends warmer than when it touched. You might, if you stayed, learn how to carry such borderlines under the skin.

Vegetation, where permitted, is equally borderline: carpets of lichens that invent their own pigment every morning – cobalt if prevailing wind meets the northeast, ochre if southwest. One whole flank of a ridge hosts lichens shaped like clock hands; they rotate collectively once every 23 hours and 47 minutes, keeping better time than day itself which this latitude has only recently discovered. Poles of a single tree appear now and then, all of them merely a trunk the diameter of an arm and as tall as a minaret. They are coppice erupted from the same seed that was flung here a geological second ago; every morning a new ring grows, but the trunk does not lengthen, it simply becomes denser. At the centre of some the wood has already collapsed into fluted hearts of amber heavy enough to draw iron nails from passing toolbelts – there are stories of workers simply staggering off the edge of plateaus when their belts empty.

Because, of course, there are workers.

A city is beginning, though it declines to sit still long enough to be drawn on a map. Its name is borrowed from a procedural generation seed that its planners call Site Aletheia, but maps scribble it as Site Α along the southern scar of the plateau. Construction is urban but embryonic: block-sized extrusions of alloy and stone rise in configurations a half-integer floor at a time, stacking without mortar, their joins lined with silicate foam that heals microfractures as they appear. A breathing skyline. Conduits shaped like cervical vertebra carry argent liquors and information – the distinction between the two is cosmetic since either may be made of ionic lattices vibrating slower or faster than consciousness. Roads are not laid; they are more like lilypads of quasi-crystal that spin upward the moment a foot touches their molecular groove, sliding the traveller forward while the pad itself dissolves in their wake. Commuters in reversible coats step from pad to pad and are tram-smooth within five strides. At night the pads float mid-air, rotating, moon-light catching their rims like coins balanced on fingertips. Nobody minds; they are background weather.

Sound is therefore minimal, only the hushed click–click of mechanical ice grafting onto the underside of each transit-pad, and the faint singing of those cloud-spheres bursting overhead. No one raises their voice because public address here is orbital: voices are encoded in the spin-states of walking-track atoms. You want to say something to someone three kilometres away, you simply breathe on a handrail and they feel it like a spark in the side of the neck. Conversation, then, is a collective nudge. Only the fauna remonstrate in true sound: bruised-throat yawps of the stripe-eyed gulls who have learned how to hover upside down in the katabatic wind and fish particulate city debris that fizzes at the edge of microclimate. They scream because shredded alloy is tasty. Predators listen, but quietly. The apex predator here is probably an architecture: a dormitory complex that has developed territorial behaviours. When the dormitory’s corridors feel geometric stress – too many residents dreaming in vector-similar trajectories – it extrudes balconies and takes wing. Every six weeks it shifts downslope, chased always by the single administrator whose grand-aunt once designed the firmware. She outruns it on those membranous pads yelling what sound like numbers in a language no one else speaks. She insists she is merely stretching the cable restraints; the dormitory insists it is chasing her dreams as prey.

Light is normal but refracted through unfamiliar mediums. The sun sits low but has grown huge – perhaps the continent is merely higher than its atmosphere expected. The sky is pale indescum, the colour of sky under gasoline puddles. Midday feels like glowing before thunder, although the thunderstorms themselves are polite affairs where cumuli kneel down to the flats and politely hand out packets of condensed humidity like elevator attendants. The city lights respond to sky colour reciprocally: when colour deepens, buildings alter their surface roughness to polarise upward, turning street level suddenly brighter, so time-of-day is an endless balanced rave between cloud and artefact. Shift-workers report waking at 3 a.m. to find entire avenues flooded with golden noontime while their apartment interiors remain true dawn. Lamps within rooms adjust asymmetrically, as though seeking privacy from the outside. Residents list favourite times asurally: “Third dawn, second noon, inverse dusk,” timers that never repeat study day or night. Hence cafés that open “until the purple stops”.

The population density is sparse – every human individual wears on average 42,000 cubic metres of atmospheric margin. The walkways feel like airport concourses ten hours before a single holiday. Gaps are juxtapositional, stitched with errata of solitude: a longitude politely stepped out to maintain the spiritual elbow room demanded by those living atop a mountain still undecided about being born. Footsteps echo then smother themselves. Newcomers confess to hearing sounds that are obviously their own but arriving thirty metres behind. The lag corrects itself after a week – then the newcomers are native enough to be the echo others will hear later. Employment algorithms use the aperture. If you walk with a certain negative-space cadence, logistics systems append a freight pallet plus a hypothesis to whatever destination your navel-centre inclines downward as you walk. Supplies chime down the air five minutes after you next glance at nothing. No verbal request is ever acknowledged; merely space is fulfilled. Palimpsests of need instead of order-queues.

What little ground fauna does not live in water spheres has adapted to the strictly temporary ecology. Lizards transparent except for reflections of sky climb frozen cascade vines just long enough to warm, then leap and glide in frozen updrafts. The membrane between last toe and fifth rib is a sheet of superconductor liquid; when they reach ambient critical temperature they flash into white fireflies that spiral into space. Birds exist that nest inside the soundless thundercells, weaving nests from braided cold-light, surviving on oxidised hydrogen. Their eggs are geodes; keening infant beaks chip at the inner lacework until a prism cracks outward and drops a chick already wondering whether to climb or fall.

Further down, where the plateau ends not at a cliff but in a crystalline overhang like an awning larger than Eurasia, the city preserves labour zones: factory floors grown not built, seeded from buckets the size of sand grains. Assembly coils within the buckets like centipede origami; contents stream across tessellation seams then fold upward until any tool you can name surrounds its operator in fractal ritual. No scaffolding, no noise beyond exhalation of coolant fog scented mint and copper. A single technician at desk consoles that are themselves standing waves of shifting symbols watches 3-D plots that update themselves into parity with reality every 2.2 seconds; if she nods, a plate will appear on an intercontinental fault whose flex will be exactly the curvature of tomorrow’s stress tensor growth. She nods infrequently. Her coffee stays warm by heat-singularity indistinguishable from kindness.

Time dilation creeps into the social layer. People converge at a bazaar made entirely of doors; doors hinged to nothing and opening onto nearly any other place in the city including tomorrow’s construction. Transactions involve handing someone the key to a door behind them that isn’t there until the frame has accepted the key as pattern. Currency is therefore small glass tokens shaped like frozen breath. Spend enough and your personal exhalations will slowly disappear until even sighs become charges against the future. Experienced accountants carry around spare atmospheres in transparent canisters, sloshing like aquariums full of cloudy brandy, and sell portions to those who have spent too much breath on conversation about architecture birds. The market bustles quietly, because the trading of air feels like ritual rather than commerce.

Laws are embry-scale. The first mandate carved into bedrock: nothing shall sit still for more than thirty rotations of the planetary core. This prevents backlog or stagnation, which would be impossible anyway, yet the inscription has become secular scripture. Enforcement is pre-emptive: structures that sense they are being loved for more than twenty-nine rotations gently bevel themselves into next-door quantum lattices, translocate ownership tags, appear in morning precisely one block leftward. Residents awake to find neighbouring apartments swapped with their own, personal belongings sliding out from drawers that now open onto reversed cardinal directions, and the minor inconvenience is considered community gameplay. Children trace faultlines in pixels on railings, predicting where the next swap will occur, and if correct receive a city-wide network announcement that their laugh is the next wind chime to condition commuter calm.

Above all of this, sky keeps its half-face drooping dawn, dusk, and noon in a slow impossible waltz. Weather systems are still inventing themselves; some days static sheets of snow fall upward – each crystal a folded mirror reflecting sunrise you haven’t seen yet – and vanish before touching hat. People hold hands when walking under inverse snow because the flakes tickle memories stored in palm lines. On such mornings chimes ring citywide not as sound but as an alteration of colour temperature that feels like metallophone heartbeats against the back of eyesockets.

But the quiet remains. Even living in the middle of major thrumming tech and abundant life, the volume dial of the world is low. Conversations are carbonated whispers. Machines murmur only barely above subliminal. One senses geology itself breathing in slow benthic exhalations, the distinctive thrum that precedes a continent making up its mind. When a new cliff head extrudes overnight, no one celebrates loudly. Instead, entire districts dim their internal golden noon to a candle interval and all footfalls pause until the cliff-face has settled. The shared respectful silence is punctuated eventually by a single kiosk printer spinning a ticket reading “thank you” in a language whose consonants taste like iron rainfall. Someone pockets the ticket; it will be a seed to grow next week’s bridge.

At the northern brow of the plateau an observatory sits without telescope or dish but only a circular aperture twenty metres across and one atom thick. The hole is kept open so light can be observed from both sides at once. Through it one can see a future Ysonde a million years forward: street layout drifted kilometres west, oceans having condensed and re-evaporated several times, walking pads replaced by embedded dreams that carry travellers by narrative inertia alone. The future city, seen through the single atom ring, continues construction in loops that intersect here–now causality. Observers checking the ring report the elegant architecture of tomorrow laying foundation stones that coincide precisely with this decade’s translocation activities – suggesting the laws already know their own destinations. Nobody calls it prophecy; technicians call it “rhyming footprint,” a poetic term offered by the tech because the vocabulary database is still evolving alongside city substance.

Eventually the nearest water mirror re-forms after dusk. Moonlight refracts in perfect ovals across every puddle, and when a dweller bends to dip a fingertip the reflected moon dissolves like powdered pearl and lifts – not as dust but as a small companion moon, a bead of photonic ice the size of an apple. It follows that dweller home. In the morning, if the dweller has remembered to speak kindly to it, the bead rests on the sill and has grown, turning the window into a second dawn inside the apartment. If forgotten, the bead will have gone, window glass frosted translucent, and the moon that night is fractionally more complete in the sky and the dweller will dream of impossible tides. Thus astronomers and lovers track their unspoken kindnesses by counting the multiple miniature moons that orbit Ysonde like migratory swallows made of gravity bite-sized and silver.

And roll onward the slow wheels of weather, reconstruction, and moment. Ysonde continues breathing in long cold stratifications of crystal, fungi, half-born birds, condensing concept-maps in every thaw. Somewhere inside Site Α dormitory the circuitry hums toward another walk-about; somewhere beneath a fresh splinter of cliff a technician walks barefoot across newborn stone, already considering how a city might love a continent enough to allow the mountain to migrate dream first; somewhere overhead a lattice of cooling light migrates from pod to skyline slowly enough to seem like the draught under a door which has not yet decided which room it divides. The quiet presses close like kindness. The continent remains only half named and wholly alive.
