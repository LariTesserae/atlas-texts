A forgotten tile floor lying three stories beneath the present sea level.  
Its square meters are so few you can count them—say sixty-four—yet within that border everything is wedged: brackish water, breathing machinery, crawling things, and the smell of an iron lung exhaling through cracked porcelain.  

The tiles themselves are salt-glazed cobalt, engraved with minute train schedules and birth certificates from a city no longer above. Their grout has become runnels of soft gypsum constantly dissolving and reprecipitating overnight; every dawn the seams reseat a millimeter closer, so on a thousand-year map the entire mosaic would appear to be curling toward its center like a dying leaf.

Water sits in spectral layers.  
A clear skin, two millimetres of glassy film, floats over blacker water thick as axle grease. Between the layers slide glass eels the length of fountain pens—unpigmented, eyes like silver pins. They feed on dust motes that rain from the ceiling. That ceiling is the underside of a bigger basement; rusted floor-joists drip every seven minutes in perfect metronomic synchronization, the drops cycling through micro-galaxies of bio-luminescent algae that thrive in freefall for half a second before striking the surface and vanishing.  

There are no plants; biology keeps to the margins of the fungal-analog. A velvet of nickel-blue lichen coats discarded gear teeth and piston shafts lying in shallow pigeon-baths. When a drop hits the lichen, spores loft, coalescing into minute storm fronts—visible only where the steam-borne projector light touches them. The light source itself is a single six-volt lantern bolted to a fallen I-beam; the bulb is broken, but it still arcs inside, sustaining a throbbing orange filament that never quite dies. It sputters like a heart arrested mid-beat.

Acoustics: the room is hushed except for the anodic soprano-hum of electrolytic corrosion, the hard I’s and soft O’s of water folding. Sometimes a distant boiler exhales through drowned pipework; the note travels down the pipe, clears its throat against a colony of mussels, and emerges inside the chamber as a ghost-major chord no human ever tempered. It lingers for thirty seconds before the slow water drinks it.

Fauna, more than you can tally in a square meter.  
Besides the eels and the mussels, the floor’s recesses shelter isopods machined from bronze—they run on clock-springs that wind tighter whenever the lantern dims. Their legs dent the glaze with crescent scars.   
Hanging in the southwest corner is a dormant school of jelly-rats: translucent, vein-mapped bodies, lungs dragging like red handkerchiefs behind them. They pulse in slow respiration, never once touching any surface, suspended by the updraft of evaporative cooling; when the air brines at dusk they descend to skim the water and ingest condensate, then ascend again like floating lungs.  
Flecks of black microbites orbit every gear tooth—the size of dust, the appetites of wolves. Any metal surface they graze develops frost-pattern etchings overnight, city grids a tenth of a millimetre wide.

Industrial relics are few yet decisive:  
– a brass pressure gauge that still thinks it’s measuring steam in an engine room two buildings above; the needle twitches whenever an eel flicks the water.  
– one fourth of a steam turbine casing, split cleanly as if struck by a guillotine, now functions as a burrow for an albino snapping shrimp that click-snips its claw in conversation with the distant boiler.  
– thick rubber hoses extrude from a wall bulge like digestive loops; they sweat chilled hydraulic fluid that pools, congeals, and is harvested by the lichens in beaded necklaces.

Rules this pocket obeys:  
Anything metal left longer than a day will acquire nerve endings—thin pink filaments of copper that seek optimum ground. You can feel, if you press your palm to them, the turbine’s remembered rotation.  
Time dilates toward the center point where the ceiling joist almost meets the floor tiles; stand there and your heartbeat halves without noticing, the room aging two hours for each of your minutes.  
Speak aloud and the microbites spell what you said on the closest surface, then eat the evidence within an hour—but fresh script leaks back into the sound you breathe years later, a syllable returned when you least expect it.  

You could map the entire place on an index card. You could live a lifetime learning its corners. The room is perfectly aware of both of you.
