You tube your breath out slowly because the air keeps trying to tear it from your lungs.  
You are standing on a needle’s eye, a single rampart of rock no wider than a chapel aisle, thrust so high that the sky below it glows paler than the sky above. From here the chasm is a cracked glass: silver water stands inside the fracture, a lake turned on its side, clinging to the cliff as if gravity had relinquished its claim on liquids long ago. It leaks continually—a garden of ropes and sheets of water unspooling upward, spattering back onto itself, beading, pooling again, then cascading down a few yards only to climb once more. This constant exchange is the loudest thing: a thunderous whisper you feel in your teeth as much as your ears. Water chatters, drips, slaps, occasionally gongs against hollow stone.

The rock itself is black marble veined with white arsenic; centuries—these particular centuries, anyway—have etched gashes that carry the wandering water, but the stone is now honeycombed so evenly that the entire fin looks more like a fossilised sponge than a blade of mountain. A cool, steady breeze funnels along the ridge, tasting faintly of salt and metal.

Life occupies the holes: pale mosses like upholsterer’s velvet packed into crevices, ferns that droop green over voids, still drinking although their roots dangle in air. Lichens of antique robin-egg blue bloom outward on damp faces, mapping where the spores have explored for centuries without erasing one another. Each colony edges the next instead of fighting; the plateau has arrived at a slow-motion detente. Here and there a shrub shaped like a seated heron grows from cracks no thicker than twigs; its seeds must have followed bird-airways centuries ago and mutated to dwarf stature, because their trunks knot exactly to the width of the clefts. Their leaves are purple on the underside, silver on top, flipping color as the wind turns them over in waves.

No animal larger than a cat has footing, but the air is thick with them. Gliding lemurs have evolved to kite-bones: skin-membranes running from fingertips to ankles, giving them the silhouette of chewed paper lanterns. They yaw and bank through the ascending water, snapping moth-clouds whose wings bear the same lichen blues, carrying spore on fur back up the cliff face. Below the ridge, swifts spell arabesques in flocks too tight to track, screaming sociably. Their voices cut through the water-roar so that the sky feels threaded with language. Occasionally one will snatch a globule of airborne lake, slake its thirst without landing.

There are remnants of people, or beings that once had hands: stones mortised together without mortar form a shelf twenty paces long, supporting nothing now but a single, enormous carved seat. The seat is too narrow for human hips, deeper than forearm length, and its back is graven with concentric rings that imitate the eye of a storm. Someone once sat here watching the ungravity lake, perhaps attempting to read its slow letters. A few hand-tools—basalt hammer, a copper chisel now green as lizard tails—lie scattered, mineralised into the surface like insects in amber.

The day’s light is day-coloured but clenched; sun passes through screens of moisture and reappears broken into prisms, every colour caught in its own sheet of droplets. Whenever the breeze quickens the air fills with frail rainbows, momentary cathedral windows. At dusk the cliff throwbacks two shadows: one falling upward into the sky, one downward into the blue-ink depths. During these cross-fading minutes the lemurs settle in the lee of the throne, clasping their long fingers across their bellies, and the lake swap directions, a quiet reverse peristalsis, pulling its threads of water home for the night.

You can still walk the spine—eight careful paces—before the ridge narrows to a wrist. From there, kneel and wait: sound grows so pure it becomes silence, then you hear the mountain’s own bloodstream, the minute shuffle of crystal grains resetting their grip beneath your boots. The noise is geological tide, louder now because the air has emptied of wings.

Rules: Stay until your exhalation chooses its own direction; do not drink the climbing water; never sit on the throne, only beside it; leave before the lemurs begin to hum.
