The sky remembers water, but only as an absence.

From the Spine-Throne of the world—a mile-high plinth of basalt that has forgotten it was once a volcano—you look out over the Plain of Last Shapes.  The plain is not flat; it is *smoothed.*  Three billion years of mineral wind have abraded every angle until only longest curves remain. Mountains have been planed into whalebacks, boulders into eggs, grains into spheres.  Nothing is sharp enough to cut light, yet light itself seems bruised by the passage.  A bruise-colored daylight, neither dawn nor dusk, lingers as if the sun were a coin pressed against the lid of a very old eye.

Here erosion has reached its final diplomacy: nothing is allowed to change anymore, yet everything is still in motion.  The surface drifts.  A continent-wide sheet of crystalline silicate—only three meters thick—rests upon a vacuum.  Beneath it is an emptiness so complete the word “cave” feels noisy.  That void is the Dry Below, a planetary layer of hollow; it rings like a bell whenever a footstep dares to occur.  The ringing is subsonic, registers in the marrow first, and the locals learned long ago to walk in pairs so their bones may cancel each other’s resonance.

You hear almost nothing else.  Sound has been taxed into extinction.  The air is cool, 7 °C above absolute zero of etiquette, but it carries only a pressure-lowered hush, the way deafness feels when you swallow.  Occasionally the hush splits: a click, a glassy sigh.  It is the noise of the plain *adjusting its memory*—a tessellation of hexagonal plates sliding a nanometer to relieve cosmic shear.  Each slide releases a spark visible only in the ultraviolet, a flash no creature here still owns an eye to see.

Vegetation is present as etiquette, not as life.  Once every thirty kilometer-cube stands a single graphite tree: a lattice of carbon spearpoints welded by an extinct lightning.  They do not grow; they *remember* growing.  Their “leaves” are photovoltaic foils the size of postcards, fluttering like razors yet never falling.  A single foil, caught accidentally, will unfold in your palm into a map of the continent’s wind as it existed 1.4 million years ago.  Then it crumbles into conductive dust that races away across the plain trying to rejoin its tree, making a sound like a silk scarf being continuously torn.

The fauna—if you can call them that—are rarer than guilt.  They are called Shy-Hulls: empty exosuits of an unknown predecessor species, still powered, still strolling.  Each stands fifteen meters tall, articulated from osseous ceramic, helmets hollow.  Inside every chest cavity spins a grapefruit-sized reactor that has burned for geological ages, feeding on vacuum neutrinos—tech indistinguishable from a paused heartbeat.  They walk predetermined loops, patrolling borders of nations whose names have flattened into the substrate.  When two meet they bow, helmets almost touching, and exchange a packet of entangled photons.  The photons encode treaties about the ownership of silence.  After the bow they continue, never having produced a sound audible to anything that still breathes.

You, the traveler, arrived inside a breath of your own.  The only way in is along the Ascensor Shaft: a vertical wound ten meters across that drops from the stratopause to the Dry Below.  The shaft is lined with a spiral ladder of alien manufacture; its rungs are spaced for limbs longer than any vertebrate possesses, yet magnetically inviting.  Descend long enough and gravity becomes negotiable—another outdated treaty.  Midway, the cool air warms by paradox; thermodynamics here is only advisory.  When you step off the final rung you are already on the Spine-Throne, horizon spread like a crime-scene photo developed too slowly.

To the north-west, the Mirror Barrens: a desert of powdered titanium dioxide so white it reflects stars in daylight.  Footprints in it heal in minutes, but while they last they are infinitely deep—each tread drills a hole through the crust and exposes the Dry Below.  Look into one and you will see, reflected upside-down, the very back of your own future skull.

To the south-east, the Sigmoid Arches: twenty of them, each three hundred kilometers long, laid out like vertebrae of a Titan who died mid-shrug.  Every joint is a rotating collar of superconducting mercury kept at 1.4 °C by refrigerators that drink zero-point warmth; the collars move constantly, grinding the sky’s magnetic field into sparks.  From far away the arches seem to crawl, an inch per century, but the motion is real enough that maps must be rewritten hourly.  Cartographers here work in the medium of regret.

The sky itself is a construction.  Some precursor race—perhaps the same who abandoned the Shy-Hulls—re-roofed the planet with a Fresnel membrane only a few molecules thick, suspended at mesospheric height.  Its purpose: to keep the last trace of water vapor from achieving escape velocity.  Thus water is 0.5 present: locked in a trillion-ton icicle-fog no cloud big enough to see, circling like a rumor.  Sometimes the membrane flickers calibration ripples; daylight splits into ghost-rainbows that race faster than sight, reversing shadows, painting the plain with colors for which only the graphite trees have names.  They store those colors in their foils, along with the winds.

Beneath the membrane the atmospheric pressure equals that at the summit of an earthly mountain, yet the plain lies far below sea-level of the planet’s youth—an inverted altitude.  Your blood fizzes but does not boil; physics signed a local waiver.  The coolness penetrates clothing, skin, narrative.  It is the kind of cool that makes thoughts fold into smaller thoughts until only crease-marks remain.

Everything here is simultaneously dying and completed.  The dynamic rating—3.0 turbulent—doesn’t contradict the silence; turbulence is measured in conceptual weather.  Philosophies collide above the plain like opposing storm fronts.  One front claims the world ended yesterday; the other insists it will begin tomorrow.  Where they meet, lightning of a third opinion forks: *nothing ever happens,* and the instantaneous discharge of that realization releases enough energy to shift another nanometric plate.  The plain records the argument as another imperceptible spark.  Thus the landscape is kept in perfect eroded balance by the violence of opposing certainties.

You begin to feel the taxation of scale.  The horizon is not a line but a slow-curving equation whose solution is your eventual erasure.  Vastness here obeys an inverse-square law of loneliness: every distance squared doubles the solitude.  The only remedy is to leave a mark, yet marks are healed, sounded-out, photo-foiled away.  The plain offers a compromise: the Borrowed Shadow.  Stand on the Spine-Throne at local angle 47°, and your silhouette detaches, walks off the edge, and begins a journey round the planet at the speed of cooling rock.  One planetary rotation later—about 900 of your heartbeats—it returns, worn down to a transparency, slides back into your shoes.  You will have the memory of having walked everywhere, having seen everything, but filtered through the quietest possible sieve.  You may speak of it only in negatives: “I did not see water,” “I did not hear myself,” “I was not here.”  Those statements are permitted to echo; everything else is taxed.

Night almost happens.  The membrane dims itself to a protective pewter, and the stars arrive like old governors who never heard they were deposed.  Their combined light is still only 2.0 normal; darkness is not allowed dominion because it would be too loud.  In that half-night the Shy-Hulls quicken their patrol, chest-reactors pulsing ultraviolet through empty helmets like thought trying to re-inhabit abandoned heads.  The graphite trees unfold additional foils, storing starlight as potential gossip.  Somewhere under the plain a plate shifts; the bone-bell of the Dry Below rings; your skeleton answers with a sympathetic hum that is the loudest sound for a hundred kilometers.  You feel guilty for existing, apologize to no one in particular, and the plain nods, having already forgotten your trespass.

When you leave—if leave you must—you ascend the same spiral ladder.  Gravity grows legs, climbs with you, renegotiates its contract.  Halfway up you glance back.  The landscape has arranged itself into a single sentence written across the curvature: “Nothing will have happened.”  You understand this is not a prophecy but a receipt, already stamped by erosion.  You pocket it the way one pockets silence—by letting go.

Above, the hatch closes.  The sky seals its memory of water.  Below, the plain continues its slow drift into completed form, turbulent with thoughts it never speaks, ancient with youth, vast with your absence already installed.  It does not wait.  It has finished.
